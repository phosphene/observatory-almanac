# Biodiversity and Conservation: Where the Popular Consensus Breaks Down

The popular narrative around biodiversity conservation runs roughly like this: species are disappearing at alarming rates, but if we create enough protected areas, support rewilding projects, and hit the "30x30" target (protecting 30% of land and ocean by 2030), we can reverse the crisis. The IUCN Red List tells us which species are at risk, and species recovery programs show that conservation works when funded and applied.

This narrative contains genuine elements — the biodiversity crisis is real, some protected areas work well, and targeted species recovery has produced measurable successes. But the gap between conservation announcements and conservation outcomes is substantial. Many protected areas exist only on paper. The IUCN Red List systematically undercounts the species most at risk. "Rewilding" has become a fundraising brand faster than it has become an evidence-based practice. And the 30x30 target risks becoming an area-counting exercise rather than a biodiversity outcome exercise. This document maps where the conservation narrative diverges from the evidence.

## The Weakest Popular Claims

### "Protected areas are protecting biodiversity" ⚠️ Mixed evidence — many are inadequately managed or enforced

A March 2026 Mongabay commentary ("What paper parks reveal about the limits of conservation policy") documented that while marine protected areas cover roughly 8% of the world's oceans and protected lands account for nearly a fifth of terrestrial surface, many lack the enforcement, funding, and management to produce conservation outcomes. A 2024 study in *Nature Communications* ("Mixed effectiveness of global protected areas in resisting habitat loss") provided a more nuanced picture: it found that the majority of protected areas do curb human pressures compared to surrounding areas, but effectiveness varies enormously by region, governance, and management capacity. The "paper parks" problem is most acute in tropical developing countries where biodiversity is richest and institutional capacity is weakest. England's own 30x30 assessment acknowledged that "land inside these areas is no more likely to be managed for wildlife than land outside them" — with almost all uplands still used for intensive sheep grazing. The count of protected area *hectares* is a poor proxy for actual conservation *outcomes*.

### "The 30x30 target will reverse biodiversity loss" ⚠️ Area target — not an outcome target — and deadline is essentially unachievable

The Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework's Target 3 calls for protecting 30% of terrestrial, freshwater, and marine areas by 2030. As of 2024, approximately 17% of land and 8% of oceans are formally protected. Reaching 30% by 2030 would require protecting an additional area roughly the size of China on land alone — in six years. The real risk is that the target incentivizes quantity over quality: rapid designation of areas that count toward the percentage without the management, enforcement, or ecological connectivity required for biodiversity outcomes. A UN progress report at COP16 (October 2024) found "glimmers of promise" but called for "boosted efforts," and Key Biodiversity Areas showed large gaps in coverage. The 30x30 target is valuable as a political mobilization tool but risks becoming a metric that is optimized rather than a goal that is achieved.

### "Rewilding is a proven conservation strategy" ⚠️ Theory-led, not evidence-based — outcomes variable

A 2023 scoping review in *Biological Conservation* ("A scoping review of the scientific evidence base for rewilding in Europe") found that "the science of rewilding has been criticised for being largely theory-led rather than evidence based." Rewilding encompasses a wide range of activities — from reintroducing apex predators (wolves in Yellowstone) to passive land abandonment — with inconsistent definitions and variable outcomes. A 2025 paper in *Ambio* warned that "approaches aiming for strict non-intervention to restore 'wilderness' may be conceptually inconsistent when applied to human-shaped landscapes." In Mediterranean and European cultural landscapes, centuries of human management have created habitats that depend on continued management — and rewilding can actually reduce biodiversity in these systems. The Yellowstone wolf reintroduction is frequently cited as a rewilding success, but the "trophic cascade" narrative (wolves changed the rivers) has been significantly challenged by subsequent research. Rewilding can work in specific contexts — but the popular narrative treats it as a universal conservation strategy when it is context-dependent and under-evidenced.

### "The IUCN Red List tells us what species are threatened" ⚠️ Systematic gaps — biased toward large, charismatic, commercially important species

A 2024 paper in *Biological Conservation* ("IUCN Red List criteria fail to recognise most threatened and extinct species") found that Red List data is "heavily weighted towards species that are large or commercially important, biasing societies' understanding of the true magnitude and footprint of the ongoing biodiversity crisis." A 2024 paper in *ICES Journal of Marine Science* documented that IUCN assessment criteria "overlook genetic variation within species" — meaning genetically distinct populations can be lost without triggering a threat category change. A 2025 paper in *Biological Conservation* noted that the Red List has been criticized for "failing to assess species with inadequate data, ignoring genetic diversity, and lacking timely updates." Of the estimated 8–10 million species on Earth, the IUCN has assessed approximately 160,000 — less than 2%. The vast majority of invertebrates, fungi, and microorganisms have never been evaluated. The Red List is the best tool available, but treating it as a comprehensive inventory of threatened biodiversity dramatically understates the crisis.

### "Species recovery programs prove conservation works" ⚠️ Survivor bias — successes are real but not representative

High-profile species recoveries — bald eagles, California condors, humpback whales, giant pandas — demonstrate that targeted, well-funded conservation can prevent extinction. These successes are real and important. But they represent a tiny, biased sample: large, charismatic vertebrates with dedicated funding streams, legal protection (e.g., the U.S. Endangered Species Act), and public support. The median species facing extinction is an insect, a plant, or a freshwater mollusk that receives no dedicated funding, has no recovery program, and may not have been formally described by science. A 2022 analysis in *PLOS ONE* found that Endangered Species Act recovery plans are chronically underfunded — actual expenditures average less than 20% of what recovery plans themselves identify as needed. Success stories are real; extrapolating them to the broader extinction crisis is survivorship bias.

### "Biodiversity loss is primarily a protected area problem" ⚠️ The primary driver is land use change outside protected areas

The IPBES Global Assessment (2019) identified land use change as the single largest driver of biodiversity loss, followed by direct exploitation, climate change, pollution, and invasive species. The majority of land use change — agricultural expansion, urbanization, infrastructure development — occurs outside protected areas. Protecting 30% of the planet while the remaining 70% undergoes intensifying land use change is a conservation strategy with a structural gap. The Observatory's article on Afro-Descendant communities in the Amazon documents how community-managed territories — not formal protected areas — provide effective conservation through traditional land management. Approaches that address land use across the entire landscape, not just within designated reserves, are structurally more complete.

## What Actually Holds Up

**The biodiversity crisis is real and accelerating.** The IPBES Global Assessment documented that approximately 1 million species face extinction, and the rate of species loss is tens to hundreds of times higher than the average over the past 10 million years. This conclusion is supported by multiple independent lines of evidence including population trends, habitat loss data, and extinction rates.

**Well-funded, well-managed protected areas do reduce habitat loss.** The 2024 *Nature Communications* study confirmed that the majority of protected areas outperform surrounding landscapes on habitat retention. The key variables are governance, funding, and enforcement — not designation alone.

**Indigenous-managed territories are among the most effective conservation areas globally.** Multiple studies, including work documented in the Observatory, show that Indigenous-managed lands retain more forest cover and biodiversity than equivalent non-Indigenous areas, including some government-managed protected areas. The Kunming-Montreal Framework explicitly recognizes Indigenous and community-managed areas for the first time.

**The Endangered Species Act has prevented documented extinctions.** A 2019 analysis found that 99% of species listed under the ESA have not gone extinct — though many have not recovered either. The law functions primarily as an extinction prevention tool rather than a recovery tool.

## Practical Framework: Navigating Biodiversity Under Uncertainty

| Risk Tier | What to Watch | What It Means |
|---|---|---|
| **Track outcomes, not area designations** | Habitat condition within protected areas; species population trends; enforcement budgets | Hectares protected ≠ biodiversity conserved. A well-managed 5% is worth more than a poorly managed 30%. |
| **Evaluate rewilding by context and evidence** | Species composition changes; ecosystem function metrics; timeframe | Rewilding works in some contexts and fails in others. Demand evidence of outcomes, not just narratives of intention. |
| **Look beyond charismatic species** | Invertebrate surveys; plant assessments; freshwater biodiversity monitoring | If conservation attention is concentrated on large vertebrates, the majority of biodiversity is invisible to the conservation system. |
| **Support landscape-level approaches** | Agricultural land management; buffer zones; connectivity corridors; working lands conservation | Biodiversity conservation that operates only within reserves while ignoring the surrounding matrix is structurally incomplete. |
| **Recognize Indigenous land management as conservation** | Community-managed territory outcomes; deforestation rates on Indigenous lands vs. government reserves | The evidence is clear that Indigenous management produces conservation outcomes. Policy that excludes or undermines Indigenous land rights is counterproductive for biodiversity. |
