# Environmental Health and Toxics: Where the Popular Consensus Breaks Down

The popular narrative around environmental health assumes that chemical safety regulation keeps people safe: substances are tested before they reach the market, "safe levels" of exposure are established by regulatory agencies, and if a product is legal to sell, it is safe to use. When problems emerge — lead in water, PFAS in drinking water, pesticides in food — they are treated as failures of enforcement rather than failures of the system itself.

The reality is structurally different. The regulatory framework for chemical safety in most countries, particularly the United States, is based on assumptions that endocrinology has progressively undermined. The "dose makes the poison" paradigm — that there is a safe threshold below which any substance is harmless — does not hold for endocrine-disrupting chemicals, which can produce effects at extremely low doses through hormone-mediated pathways. The chemical industry has systematically shaped the regulatory science that determines what is tested, how it is tested, and what level of evidence is required for restriction. The Observatory's Jimmy Videle article on air pollution documents that air pollution alone is "killing millions" annually and "rising exponentially." Understanding where the regulatory apparatus actually protects health and where it fails is essential for anyone making decisions about environmental exposure.

## The Weakest Popular Claims

### "Regulatory 'safe levels' of chemical exposure protect public health" ⚠️ Endocrine disruptors violate the threshold assumption — no safe dose may exist

A 2026 paper in *Environmental Health* (Springer Nature) concluded that "the assumption that there is no threshold or safe daily exposure below which this special category of systemic toxicants, EDCs, do not cause adverse effects" is supported by current endocrinology and toxicology research. The paper specifically characterized the FDA's continued use of threshold-based risk assessment for endocrine-disrupting chemicals as "a policy failure." A 2024 IPEN report on endocrine-disrupting chemicals stated that "there may be no safe dose for chemicals that disrupt the endocrine system." The fundamental problem: regulatory agencies set "acceptable daily intakes" and "reference doses" based on traditional toxicology's dose-response curve, which assumes effects decrease linearly as dose decreases. Endocrine disruptors often produce non-monotonic dose-response curves — effects at very low doses, no effect at medium doses, different effects at high doses — because they operate through hormone receptor pathways where the body's own hormones are already present above any possible threshold. This means the entire regulatory framework for setting "safe levels" may be structurally unsuited for the fastest-growing category of chemical concern.

### "Chemicals are tested before they reach the market" ⚠️ The burden of proof is inverted — most chemicals are presumed safe until proven harmful

Under the U.S. Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA), even after its 2016 Lautenberg amendment, the EPA has completed risk evaluations for only a small fraction of the roughly 86,000 chemicals in commerce. The original 1976 TSCA grandfathered in approximately 62,000 existing chemicals without any safety testing requirement. The 2016 reform required EPA to evaluate chemicals on a prioritized basis, but by 2024, EPA had completed final risk evaluations for only about 10 chemicals. In 2025, The Regulatory Review reported that EPA proposed rollbacks to chemical regulation, arguing that the 2024 final rules were "not consistent with the best reading of TSCA." The European Union's REACH regulation (Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of Chemicals) requires manufacturers to provide safety data — shifting the burden to industry — but REACH has also faced criticism for slow implementation and insufficient enforcement. The practical reality in most jurisdictions: the vast majority of chemicals in commercial use have never been subjected to comprehensive safety testing for chronic, low-dose, or endocrine-disrupting effects.

### "PFAS contamination is being addressed by regulation" ⚠️ Regulation is decades behind contamination — PFAS are already ubiquitous in human blood

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) — "forever chemicals" that do not break down in the environment — have been detected in the blood of 98% of Americans tested by the CDC's National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey. The EPA set a maximum contaminant level for PFAS in drinking water in April 2024 (4 parts per trillion for PFOA and PFOS), representing the first federal drinking water standard for these chemicals — more than 20 years after the class was identified as a major environmental contaminant. 3M, the primary manufacturer, agreed to a $10.3 billion settlement in 2023 over PFAS contamination of public water supplies — while maintaining that it acted responsibly. DuPont's history with PFOA (C8) contamination in Parkersburg, West Virginia was documented in the 2019 film "Dark Waters" and extensive investigative journalism by The Intercept and The New York Times. States have moved faster than the federal government: Minnesota and Hawaii banned PFAS in certain consumer products starting in 2024. But the structural problem remains: PFAS are in water, soil, food packaging, and consumer products globally, and no technology exists to remove them from the environment at scale.

### "Indoor air quality is a minor concern compared to outdoor pollution" ⚠️ Reversed — most people spend 90% of their time indoors where pollutant levels are often higher

The EPA's own research has documented that indoor air pollutant levels are frequently 2–5 times higher than outdoor levels, and can occasionally exceed 100 times outdoor levels. Sources include volatile organic compounds (VOCs) from building materials, furniture, cleaning products, and cooking; particulate matter from gas stoves; formaldehyde from pressed wood products; and radon from soil gas. A 2022 Stanford-led study published in *Environmental Science & Technology* found that gas stoves leak methane even when turned off, and that nitrogen dioxide levels during use frequently exceed EPA outdoor air quality standards. Americans spend approximately 90% of their time indoors. The popular narrative focuses overwhelmingly on outdoor air quality — smokestacks, vehicle exhaust, wildfires — while the environment where most exposure actually occurs receives comparatively little regulatory attention.

### "The precautionary principle would solve environmental health problems" ⚠️ Conceptually sound but politically difficult — implementation requires structural reform

The precautionary principle — the idea that substances should be restricted when there is credible evidence of harm, even without complete scientific certainty — is embedded in EU environmental law but largely absent from U.S. regulatory practice. The U.S. system generally requires demonstration of harm before restriction, creating a decades-long gap between scientific concern and regulatory action (as documented with lead, asbestos, PFAS, and endocrine disruptors). However, the precautionary principle in practice faces its own challenges: defining what constitutes "credible evidence," determining who bears the cost of precaution, and preventing the principle from being used to block beneficial innovations alongside harmful ones. The EU has applied precautionary restrictions (e.g., on certain pesticides, certain PFAS uses) but enforcement remains inconsistent, and industry pressure has delayed or weakened many proposed restrictions. The precautionary principle is structurally sound but requires institutional independence from the industries being regulated — a condition rarely met.

### "Air pollution is mainly an outdoor, developing-world problem" ⚠️ Indoor pollution kills millions; developed-world air quality is deteriorating

The Observatory's Jimmy Videle article documents that air pollution is "killing millions and rising exponentially." The WHO estimates that 4.2 million people die prematurely each year from ambient outdoor air pollution, and an additional 3.8 million from household air pollution (primarily from cooking with solid fuels in developing countries). But developed countries are not exempt: wildfire smoke has dramatically worsened air quality in the western U.S., Canada, and Australia in recent years. The 2023 Canadian wildfire season produced hazardous air quality across the eastern U.S. for weeks. U.S. progress on criteria air pollutants under the Clean Air Act — real and significant — has been partially offset by increasing wildfire frequency and intensity linked to climate change. The framing of air pollution as primarily a developing-world problem obscures real and worsening exposures in wealthy nations.

## What Actually Holds Up

**Endocrine disruption is a real, well-documented mechanism.** The Endocrine Society's scientific statements (2009, 2015) summarize extensive evidence that synthetic chemicals can interfere with hormone signaling at low doses, affecting development, reproduction, metabolism, and neurological function. This is not speculative — it is supported by decades of in vitro, in vivo, and epidemiological research.

**PFAS persistence is a genuine environmental crisis.** PFAS do not degrade in the environment, bioaccumulate through food chains, and are associated with kidney cancer, testicular cancer, thyroid disease, and immune suppression. The science is robust enough that major manufacturers have settled billions in litigation.

**Lead regulation demonstrates that removing toxic exposures works.** The phase-out of leaded gasoline in the U.S. (completed 1996) reduced blood lead levels in children by over 75% and is associated with measurable improvements in cognitive outcomes. This is one of the clearest examples of regulatory intervention producing population-level health benefits.

**The Clean Air Act has prevented millions of premature deaths.** EPA estimates that the Clean Air Act prevented 230,000 premature deaths in 2020 alone through reductions in particulate matter, ozone, and other criteria pollutants. The law works because it sets enforceable standards with penalties for non-compliance.

## Practical Framework: Navigating Environmental Health Under Uncertainty

| Risk Tier | What to Watch | What It Means |
|---|---|---|
| **Minimize endocrine disruptor exposure where feasible** | Avoid heating food in plastic; filter drinking water (activated carbon reduces many contaminants); choose personal care products without phthalates and parabens | You cannot eliminate exposure to endocrine disruptors in modern life. But reducing the highest-exposure pathways (food contact plastics, unfiltered water, certain personal care products) is a reasonable precaution given the evidence. |
| **Prioritize indoor air quality** | Ventilate during cooking; use range hoods vented outside; test for radon; avoid unnecessary VOC sources | Indoor air is where most exposure occurs. A kitchen range hood vented to the outside is one of the highest-impact single interventions for residential air quality. |
| **Do not assume "legal" means "safe"** | Regulatory status of a chemical tells you about the political economy of regulation, not necessarily about its safety | Chemicals can remain legal for decades after credible evidence of harm. The lag between scientific evidence and regulatory action is structural, not accidental. |
| **Support structural reform, not just consumer avoidance** | TSCA reform; PFAS restrictions; burden-of-proof reform; independent regulatory science | Individual avoidance strategies are valuable but insufficient. The chemicals are in the water, the soil, and the air. Structural reform of the regulatory system is the only intervention that matches the scale of the problem. |
| **Track PFAS specifically** | Local water quality reports; EWG's PFAS contamination map; state-level restrictions | PFAS are the most consequential class of environmental contaminants for which regulatory action is currently accelerating. Know your local exposure. |
