# Soil & Sun: The Living Foundation

*From The Observatory Almanac — Living Environment*

---

> *"To forget how to dig the earth and tend the soil is to forget ourselves."*
> — Mahatma Gandhi

The garden begins not with the seed, but with the ground that receives it. Soil is not merely dirt — it is a living ecosystem of bacteria, fungi, minerals, water, air, and organic matter working in concert. Understanding what lies beneath your feet, how the sun touches your land across the seasons, and how to work with the natural rhythms of moon and zone transforms gardening from guesswork into an informed, joyful practice.

---

## Part One: Knowing Your Soil

### The Six Primary Soil Types

Soil scientists classify soils by texture — the relative proportions of sand, silt, and clay particles. Each type has distinct characteristics that affect drainage, aeration, nutrient retention, and root penetration.

**1. Sandy Soil**
Sandy soil is composed of large, coarse particles with significant air space between them. Water moves through quickly, which means nutrients leach out rapidly. It warms up fast in spring, making it attractive for early planting, but it requires frequent watering and fertilization. Sandy soils are ideal for root vegetables like carrots and parsnips that dislike compaction. Improve sandy soil by adding generous quantities of compost and organic matter annually.

**2. Clay Soil**
Dense, fine-particled clay holds moisture and nutrients exceptionally well but drains poorly and compacts easily when wet or walked upon. Clay soil is "heavy" — hard to work in spring when saturated and prone to cracking when dry. It warms slowly. However, once amended and managed well, clay soils are among the most fertile. Add coarse sand, grit, and compost to improve structure. Never work clay soil when it's wet; wait until it crumbles rather than smears.

**3. Silt Soil**
Finer than sand but coarser than clay, silty soil is smooth and floury in texture. It holds moisture better than sand and is more fertile, but can compact and form surface crusts. Often found near rivers and flood plains, silt is generally quite productive. It benefits from organic matter additions to improve structure.

**4. Loam Soil**
The gardener's gold standard. Loam is a balanced mixture of sand, silt, and clay (roughly 40/40/20 percent) that drains well but retains adequate moisture, supports microbial life, and has good structure. It's workable, fertile, and forgiving. Most gardening advice implicitly assumes loam — if you have it, tend it carefully.

**5. Peaty Soil**
High in organic matter and typically acidic, peaty soils are dark, spongy, and moisture-retentive. They're naturally low in nutrients because the organic material hasn't fully decomposed. Found in boggy areas and moorlands, peat soils need liming to raise pH and added nutrients for most crops. They excel for acid-loving plants like blueberries, rhododendrons, and heathers.

**6. Chalky/Limestone Soil**
Alkaline and often stony, chalky soils are free-draining and thin. They're prone to nutrient deficiencies — particularly iron and manganese — because the high pH locks these minerals away from plants. Visible chalk or limestone fragments are a telltale sign. Lower pH gradually with sulfur amendments and add organic matter liberally.

### Understanding Soil pH

Soil pH measures acidity or alkalinity on a scale from 0 to 14, with 7.0 being neutral. Most vegetables and flowers thrive in the range of 6.0–7.0, where nutrients are most available.

| pH Range | Classification | Common Plants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Below 4.5 | Extremely acidic | Cranberries, some ferns |
| 4.5–5.5 | Strongly acidic | Blueberries, azaleas, heathers |
| 5.5–6.5 | Moderately acidic | Potatoes, strawberries, most conifers |
| 6.5–7.0 | Slightly acidic/Neutral | Most vegetables, grasses, general gardening |
| 7.0–7.5 | Slightly alkaline | Brassicas, asparagus, clematis |
| Above 7.5 | Alkaline | Lilacs, lavender, some ornamentals |

**Raising pH (making more alkaline):** Add ground limestone (calcium carbonate) or dolomitic limestone. Work into soil in autumn; effects appear by spring.

**Lowering pH (making more acidic):** Add elemental sulfur, which soil bacteria convert to sulfuric acid. Pine needle mulch and acidic compost also help over time. Use aluminum sulfate for faster results.

### Soil Testing: What, Why, and How

A soil test removes the guesswork from gardening. A basic test measures pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (the primary macronutrients). A comprehensive test also covers secondary nutrients (calcium, magnesium, sulfur) and micronutrients (iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron).

**When to test:** Every 2–3 years for established gardens; before planting a new bed; when plants show persistent deficiency symptoms; when changing crops significantly.

**How to collect a sample:**
1. Use a clean trowel and a clean plastic bucket.
2. From 10–15 spots across the area, take a slice of soil 6–8 inches deep.
3. Remove grass, roots, and debris. Mix samples thoroughly in the bucket.
4. Take about 1 cup of the blended soil for the test kit or laboratory.
5. For different garden areas (vegetable beds, lawn, orchard), collect separate samples.

**Home test kits** use color-reactive chemicals to give quick, approximate readings. They're inexpensive and useful for rapid pH checks.

**Laboratory tests** provide precise results and specific amendment recommendations. Your local cooperative extension service often offers affordable testing. Send samples in autumn or late winter for best turnaround.

## Part Two: Moisture Management

Water is the lifeblood of the garden, but its management is as much art as science. Too little and plants stress and yield declines; too much and roots suffocate and disease flourishes.

### Reading Soil Moisture

**The squeeze test:** Take a handful of soil from 2–3 inches below the surface. Squeeze firmly, then open your hand. If it holds a ball and crumbles apart when poked, moisture is ideal. If it stays in a ball and smears, the soil is too wet. If it won't hold together, it's too dry.

**The screwdriver test:** Push a long screwdriver into the soil. If it slides in easily to 6 inches, moisture is adequate. Resistance means dry soil.

**Observation:** Wilting in the heat of the afternoon is normal stress and recovers by morning. Wilting in the morning means the plant is drought-stressed.

### Watering Principles

* **Water deeply and infrequently.** Shallow daily watering encourages shallow root systems. Deep, infrequent watering (1–2 inches per week) pushes roots down to more stable moisture reserves.
* **Water the root zone, not the leaves.** Drip irrigation and soaker hoses deliver water where it's needed and reduce foliar disease pressure.
* **Water in the morning.** This allows foliage to dry during the day. Evening watering leaves plants damp overnight, encouraging fungal disease.
* **Mulch generously.** A 2–4 inch layer of organic mulch (straw, wood chips, compost) reduces evaporation by up to 70%, suppresses weeds, and regulates soil temperature.

### Drainage Solutions

**For waterlogged areas:**
- Raised beds elevate the root zone above saturated soil.
- French drains (perforated pipe in gravel-filled trenches) redirect excess water.
- Building swales — shallow channels that follow contour lines — slows runoff and allows infiltration.
- Choosing flood-tolerant plants (willows, elder, iris, cardinal flower) turns a problem area into a feature.

## Part Three: The UV Index and Sun Mapping Your Garden

### The UV Index Explained

The UV Index (UVI) is a standardized measurement of ultraviolet radiation intensity at the Earth's surface, developed by the World Health Organization and World Meteorological Organization. It ranges from 0 (no UV) to 11+ (extreme).

| UVI | Category | Skin Protection |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0–2 | Low | Minimal precautions needed |
| 3–5 | Moderate | Sun protection recommended |
| 6–7 | High | Protection essential |
| 8–10 | Very High | Extra precautions required |
| 11+ | Extreme | Avoid unprotected sun exposure |

UVI varies by latitude (higher near the equator), altitude (UVI increases ~10% per 1,000 meters), season, time of day (peak 10 AM–2 PM), and cloud cover. Thin clouds reduce UV by only 20–30%, making overcast days deceptively risky.

**For the garden:** High UV accelerates bleaching of plant foliage, particularly in high-altitude or tropical gardens. Afternoon shade cloth (30–50% shade factor) protects sensitive plants in extreme UV conditions. Most vegetables actually tolerate and require high light levels; it's the heat and water loss associated with intense sun that typically causes problems.

### Sun Mapping Your Garden

Before planting, spend one clear day in early spring and one in midsummer mapping sun patterns. Walk your property at 8 AM, 10 AM, noon, 2 PM, and 4 PM and note which areas receive full sun (6+ hours), partial sun (3–6 hours), or shade (under 3 hours).

| Classification | Hours of Direct Sun | Suitable Plants |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full Sun | 6+ hours | Tomatoes, peppers, corn, most herbs, squash |
| Partial Sun/Shade | 3–6 hours | Lettuce, spinach, kale, herbs like parsley |
| Full Shade | Under 3 hours | Hostas, ferns, impatiens, some root vegetables |

**North vs. South exposure:** In the Northern Hemisphere, south-facing slopes and south sides of structures receive the most sun and warm earliest. North-facing areas stay cooler and shadier — ideal for plants that bolt in heat.

## Part Four: USDA Plant Hardiness Zones

The USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map divides North America into 13 zones based on average annual minimum winter temperatures. It's the standard language for describing plant cold tolerance. The most recent revision (2023) reflects climate data and shows many areas warming compared to previous editions.

| Zone | Average Annual Minimum Temp (°F) | Average Annual Minimum Temp (°C) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Zone 1 | Below -60°F | Below -51°C |
| Zone 2 | -50 to -60°F | -46 to -51°C |
| Zone 3 | -40 to -50°F | -40 to -46°C |
| Zone 4 | -30 to -40°F | -34 to -40°C |
| Zone 5 | -20 to -30°F | -29 to -34°C |
| Zone 6 | -10 to -20°F | -23 to -29°C |
| Zone 7 | 0 to -10°F | -18 to -23°C |
| Zone 8 | 10 to 0°F | -12 to -18°C |
| Zone 9 | 20 to 10°F | -7 to -12°C |
| Zone 10 | 30 to 20°F | -1 to -7°C |
| Zone 11 | 40 to 30°F | 4 to -1°C |
| Zone 12 | 50 to 40°F | 10 to 4°C |
| Zone 13 | 60°F and above | 16°C and above |

**Important caveats:** Hardiness zones describe only minimum winter temperatures. They do not account for summer heat, humidity, soil type, drainage, wind exposure, or the length of the growing season. A gardener in Zone 7 Georgia faces different challenges than one in Zone 7 coastal Oregon. Supplementing zone information with knowledge of your specific microclimate — frost pocket, wind corridor, urban heat island — is essential.

## Part Six: Moon Phase Gardening

The tradition of planting and harvesting by the phases of the moon stretches across cultures and millennia — from the agricultural calendars of ancient Babylon to the biodynamic system formalized by Rudolf Steiner in the 1920s and still practiced by farmers worldwide today.

### The Basic Framework

**New Moon:** The period from the new moon through the first quarter is associated with rising sap and moisture in plant tissue. Traditionally used for planting above-ground crops that produce seeds outside the fruit: lettuce, spinach, celery, broccoli, cabbage, grains.

**First Quarter to Full Moon:** Sap is still rising; moisture is high. Traditional for planting crops that produce seeds inside the fruit: tomatoes, squash, beans, peppers, cucumbers, melons.

**Full Moon to Last Quarter (Waning):** The pull of the moon decreases; sap flows downward toward roots. Traditional time for planting root crops (carrots, beets, potatoes, turnips, radishes) and bulbs. Also favored for transplanting, pruning (said to reduce regrowth), and harvesting root crops for storage.

**Last Quarter to New Moon:** A resting period. Traditional for soil cultivation, composting, weeding, and applying fertilizers. Not generally recommended for planting.

### The Science Behind the Tradition

Research on moon gardening is limited and results are mixed. A meta-analysis by the scientific journal *Chronobiology International* found some statistically significant effects of lunar cycles on germination in certain studies, but effect sizes were generally small and methodology inconsistent.

The more plausible mechanism is gravitational: just as the moon exerts tidal forces on Earth's oceans, it exerts minute gravitational effects on all water — including soil moisture and plant sap. Whether these forces are significant enough to affect plant physiology meaningfully remains a genuine scientific question.

**The pragmatic view:** Moon gardening provides a useful scheduling framework. Whether or not the lunar mechanism is causal, gardeners who follow lunar calendars tend to garden more attentively — observing rhythms, planning ahead, and acting decisively within planting windows. That attentiveness itself produces results.

## Part Seven: Composting — The Complete Guide

Compost is decomposed organic matter — the alchemical transformation of kitchen scraps and garden waste into black gold that feeds soil life and grows extraordinary plants. Three methods serve different needs and timelines.

### The Fundamentals: Carbon, Nitrogen, and Microbes

All composting requires a balance of **carbon-rich "brown" materials** and **nitrogen-rich "green" materials**, along with moisture and air. The ratio by weight is roughly 25–30 parts carbon to 1 part nitrogen.

**Carbon-rich (Browns):** Dry leaves, straw, cardboard (non-glossy), paper, wood chips, sawdust (untreated wood), corn stalks, dried plant stems.

**Nitrogen-rich (Greens):** Kitchen vegetable scraps, fresh grass clippings, coffee grounds (and filters), tea leaves, fresh plant trimmings, livestock manures.

**Never compost:** Meat, fish, bones, dairy, oils (attract pests; can introduce pathogens), diseased plants, invasive weeds with seeds, pet feces (dogs, cats — pathogen risk), treated wood products, synthetic materials.

---

### Method 1: Hot Composting (Active Composting)

Hot composting produces finished compost in **4–12 weeks** through active management. Microbial activity heats the pile to 131–160°F (55–71°C), killing weed seeds, pathogens, and many pest eggs.

**Setup:**
1. Build a pile at least 3×3×3 feet — small piles can't generate sufficient heat.
2. Alternate layers of browns and greens (roughly 3:1 by volume, not weight).
3. Add a thin layer of finished compost or garden soil as a microbial starter.
4. Moisten each layer as you build. The pile should feel like a wrung-out sponge.

**Maintenance:**
- **Turn the pile** every 3–7 days with a pitchfork, moving outer material to the center.
- **Monitor temperature** with a compost thermometer. A properly hot pile will register 131–160°F within 48–72 hours of building.
- **Check moisture** at each turning. Add water if dry; add browns if soggy.
- The pile is finished when it no longer heats up after turning, smells earthy (not ammonia or rotten eggs), and looks dark and crumbly with no recognizable original materials.

**Troubleshooting:**
- *Pile not heating:* Too dry, too small, or insufficient nitrogen. Add water and green materials.
- *Ammonia smell:* Too much nitrogen. Add more browns and turn.
- *Rotten egg smell:* Anaerobic — too wet, or not enough air. Turn and add browns.
- *Pest activity:* Bury food scraps, exclude meat/dairy, use a lidded bin.

---

### Method 2: Cold Composting (Passive Composting)

Cold composting requires minimal effort but takes **6 months to 2 years**. Simply accumulate organic materials in a heap or bin, adding as material becomes available. Turn occasionally if convenient. This method doesn't kill weed seeds or pathogens reliably, so avoid adding diseased material or weeds gone to seed. Ideal for low-maintenance gardens with plenty of time and space.

---

### Method 3: Vermicomposting (Worm Composting)

Vermicomposting uses **red wigglers** (*Eisenia fetida*) — not common earthworms — to convert kitchen scraps into rich worm castings (vermicast). These castings are exceptionally high in plant-available nutrients, beneficial microbes, and plant growth hormones.

**Getting started:**
- **Bin:** A wooden, plastic, or purpose-built worm bin. Minimum size: 1×2×1 foot for a household of 2–4 people. Drill small drainage and ventilation holes.
- **Bedding:** Moist, shredded newspaper; cardboard; coconut coir; or partially finished compost. Fill the bin about half-full. Bedding should be as moist as a wrung-out sponge.
- **Worms:** Start with 1 pound of red wigglers per pound of weekly food scraps. Order from reputable suppliers or take from an established bin.
- **Location:** Indoors (basement, under the sink) or outdoors in mild climates (50–77°F optimal). Worms slow dramatically below 50°F and die above 84°F.

**Feeding:**
- Bury food scraps under bedding in different sections of the bin each time.
- Feed vegetable peels, fruit scraps, coffee grounds, tea bags, crushed eggshells.
- Avoid: citrus and onion in large quantities (worms dislike the acidity and oils), meat, dairy, oils, spicy foods.
- Feed 1–2 times weekly; start slowly until worm population establishes.

**Harvesting castings:**
Every 2–4 months, harvest finished castings using one of these methods:
- *Migration method:* Move finished material to one side; add fresh bedding and food to the other. Over 2–3 weeks, worms migrate toward fresh food. Remove and use the depleted side.
- *Light method:* Dump the bin contents under bright light. Worms burrow away from light. Scrape top layers until only worms and some casting remain at the bottom.

**Using vermicast:** Apply as a top dressing, mix into potting soil (up to 25% by volume), or steep in water to make worm casting tea — a powerful liquid fertilizer.

### Direct Sow Outdoors

Some crops dislike transplanting and should be direct sown in the garden:

**Direct sow as soon as soil can be worked (4–6 weeks before last frost):** Peas, spinach, carrots, radishes, beets, turnips, kale, chard, arugula, cilantro, dill.

**Direct sow after last frost when soil is warm (60°F+):** Beans, corn, squash, cucumbers, melons, sunflowers, nasturtium, basil.

**Fall crops — count back from first autumn frost:** Succession plant lettuce, spinach, and root crops 8–10 weeks before your average first fall frost for a second harvest season.

## A Note on Observation

No guide can substitute for the particular knowledge of your own land. Mark your frost dates, sketch your sun maps, test your soil, and watch what grows and what fails. A gardener who observes with genuine curiosity — who walks the beds at different hours and in different seasons, who notes which corner dries first and which stays wet — builds knowledge that no book can provide.

The garden teaches through experiment and patience. Begin where you are, with what you have, and tend it carefully. The rest follows.
