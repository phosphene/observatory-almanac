# This photo's real, but doesn't show 'Great Pacific Garbage Patch'


## 1. The Claim

The claim, which has circulated on social media in multiple waves since at least 2021 and most recently in early 2025, holds that a striking photograph of a vast open-ocean surface blanketed with floating plastic refuse shows the "Great Pacific Garbage Patch"—the marine debris concentration in the North Pacific Ocean. Accompanying text typically amplifies the image with alarming statistics, the most common being that the garbage patch has "grown to be twice the size of Texas," framing the photograph as proof of an accelerating environmental catastrophe of planetary scale.

The image itself is viscerally compelling: a mass of multicolored plastic bags, packaging, bottles, and consumer debris extending to every visible horizon, with a scuba diver visible in the frame who appears dwarfed and overwhelmed by the surrounding waste. The photograph has been widely republished not only in viral social media posts but in environmental advocacy materials and news coverage, typically paired with claims about the North Pacific accumulation zone. In its various iterations the caption geography has shifted: some posts place the scene in the North Pacific, others describe it more vaguely as "the ocean," while still others use it to illustrate general claims about global marine plastic pollution. All versions share the implicit or explicit claim that this is the famous garbage patch of scientific and media renown.

The claim is false in its geographic framing and compoundly misleading in its description of the target phenomenon, because the "Great Pacific Garbage Patch" is itself not what popular imagery typically portrays.


## 2. What's Actually True

The photograph is authentic and was taken by underwater photographer Caroline Power in 2017. Power is a British photographer based in the Caribbean who has documented marine plastic pollution extensively in the waters of Central America and the wider Caribbean Basin. In October 2017, she shared a series of photographs on her personal Facebook account depicting what she and her dive team encountered approximately 15 miles off the coast of Roatán Island, Honduras—an encounter that Power described in a Telegraph interview that same month as traversing "nearly five miles" of floating garbage en route to what was intended to be a pristine dive site. The specific image widely shared in the Great Pacific Garbage Patch claims was not included in Power's original Facebook post, but a companion photo from the same dive shows the same male diver with matching hair, clothing, and diving equipment, confirming the photograph's provenance. Power confirmed via Facebook message to Snopes fact-checkers that the photograph is hers and that it was taken off Roatán.

Roatán is an island in the Bay Islands chain off the northern coast of Honduras. It lies in the Caribbean Sea, not the Pacific Ocean. The Caribbean Sea is separated from the North Pacific by the entire landmass of Central America and Mexico. There is no geographic mechanism by which a photograph taken in Caribbean waters near Honduras could depict the Great Pacific Garbage Patch.

The Great Pacific Garbage Patch is a real marine phenomenon but is itself substantially different from what most popular imagery—including Power's photograph—depicts. The term refers to a pair of debris accumulation zones in the North Pacific Ocean: the Western Garbage Patch, located near Japan, and the Eastern Garbage Patch, located between Hawaii and California. These zones form because of the North Pacific Subtropical Gyre, a large system of rotating ocean currents that slowly concentrates floating material toward its relatively calm center. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's National Ocean Service has explicitly addressed the popular misconception generated by the term in its public communications, noting that the "Pacific Garbage Patch" naming "has led many to believe that this area is a large and continuous patch of easily visible marine debris items such as bottles and other litter—akin to a literal island of trash that should be visible with satellite or aerial photographs. This is not the case" (NOAA National Ocean Service, 2024).

The scientific consensus, developed through multiple large-scale ocean sampling surveys, is that the majority of debris in the GPGP consists of very small plastic fragments—microplastics and mesoplastics—suspended throughout the upper water column rather than floating as a coherent surface mat. A comprehensive survey conducted by the Ocean Cleanup Foundation, using aerial surveys combined with surface trawling, estimated the GPGP accumulated a total mass of approximately 79,000 metric tons in an area of roughly 1.6 million km², with plastics occurring at concentrations much higher than surrounding ocean waters but not forming the dense visible surface layer depicted in photographs like Power's (van Sebille et al., 2015, *Nature Communications*; Lebreton et al., 2018, *Scientific Reports*). The NOAA statement continues: "It is possible to sail through 'garbage patch' areas in the Pacific and see very little or no debris on the surface." A vessel traveling through the GPGP would likely encounter elevated concentrations of small plastic fragments but would not encounter the wall-to-wall surface coverage visible in Caroline Power's Caribbean photographs.

This distinction is critical for both accurate environmental reporting and effective policy communication. The conditions that generated Power's photographs—heavy surface accumulation of intact consumer plastic items—are characteristic of coastal ocean zones receiving direct nearshore runoff or riverine plastic discharge, not of mid-ocean gyre accumulation zones. Coastal accumulation is governed by entirely different dynamics, primarily the rate of land-sourced plastic entering the marine environment via rivers and storm drains, and the short-range drift currents near coastlines. Research by Jambeck and colleagues estimated that 8 million metric tons of plastic entered the global ocean annually as of 2010, with river-mouth coastal zones bearing the heaviest instantaneous concentrations (Jambeck et al., 2015, *Science*).


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of the miscaptioned photograph across years and multiple platform generations reveals a specific cluster of epistemological vulnerabilities that deserve careful examination.

**The Image–Concept Mismatch Problem**: The Great Pacific Garbage Patch is a genuinely difficult concept to visualize accurately. It consists primarily of particles smaller than a grain of rice, distributed through millions of square kilometers of open ocean. This reality is scientifically significant but visually inaccessible—you cannot photograph it in a way that conveys its scale or composition to a lay viewer. When environmental advocates, journalists, and social media users seek to illustrate the concept in a compelling way, they face an epistemic problem: the accurate visual representation of the GPGP is an expanse of open ocean that looks mostly like open ocean, punctuated by occasional small fragments. This accurate image would not and does not generate the emotional engagement needed to communicate the severity of the problem. Caroline Power's photograph, conversely, is maximally legible as evidence of plastic catastrophe. The result is a systematic selection pressure toward misrepresentation: the photographs that best communicate environmental urgency are the ones that most diverge from the actual appearance of the specific phenomenon being named.

**The Naming Effect and Geographic Compression**: The phrase "Great Pacific Garbage Patch" combines multiple concepts—magnitude ("Great"), location ("Pacific"), material type ("Garbage"), and spatial coherence ("Patch")—into a single familiar label. This compression serves communication efficiency but enables geographic slippage. Once the label is internalized as a shorthand for "ocean plastic crisis," the actual location becomes secondary; any dramatic photograph of ocean plastic appears to confirm the label's referent. The Caribbean location of Roatán is not part of the cultural schema activated by "Pacific Garbage Patch," yet the photograph's emotional content fits the schema so perfectly that many viewers process it without registering the geographic impossibility. This is a known feature of schema activation in memory psychology: incoming information that fits a pre-existing mental model tends to be assimilated to the model rather than used to update it (Bartlett, 1932, *Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social Psychology*).

**Epistemic Novelty—The Multiple-Myth Layering Problem**: This case exhibits an unusually interesting epistemic structure that has not been adequately discussed in standard fact-check treatments: it is a double myth. The first myth is that the photograph shows the GPGP. The second, more fundamental myth is that the GPGP looks like what the photograph shows. Most corrections of the miscaption appropriately address the first myth (wrong location) but leave the second myth intact, which means that viewers who encounter the correction may update their belief about photograph provenance while retaining their fundamentally incorrect mental image of the target phenomenon. Effective correction of this claim must address both layers simultaneously: the photograph is from Honduras, and the GPGP would not produce a photograph like this even if photographed directly. The two corrections together change not just the attribution of one image but the entire visual schema through which ocean plastic accumulation is processed.

This layering dynamic appears to be underappreciated in misinformation research more generally. When a false claim is built on a second false premise that is never explicitly stated, single-layer corrections may leave audiences with a partially corrected but still systematically distorted model of reality. The effective debiasing literature suggests that full narrative replacement—substituting the accurate complete picture rather than merely removing the false element—produces more robust correction effects than targeted refutation alone (Lewandowsky et al., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*).

**The Real Problem and Its Obscured Severity**: A further and practically important consequence of the image-GPGP conflation is that it obscures where the most severe visible plastic pollution actually occurs. Power's photographs document conditions that are substantially worse than anything visible in the GPGP—dense, primarily intact consumer waste accumulating rapidly near actively populated coastlines. The environmental emergency captured in those photographs is not the gentle mid-ocean accumulation of drifting microplastics but the explosive, high-volume coastal discharge of waste from urbanizing societies with inadequate solid waste infrastructure. Misidentifying the location as the GPGP redirects attention and policy concern away from the riverine pathways and coastal discharge systems responsible for the conditions Power photographed, toward a mid-ocean phenomenon that requires different and more technically challenging intervention strategies.


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: MISCAPTIONED**

The photograph is authentic and documents a genuine and severe episode of open-ocean plastic pollution. It was taken by underwater photographer Caroline Power in 2017 near Roatán Island, Honduras, in the Caribbean Sea. It does not show the Great Pacific Garbage Patch, which is located in the North Pacific Ocean roughly 8,000 kilometers away. All social media posts attributing this photograph to the GPGP are incorrect in their geographic identification and should be treated as miscaptioned rather than fabricated.

The GPGP is also not accurately characterized by the description "twice the size of Texas" when applied to this photograph, for two reasons. First, the photograph was not taken there. Second, the GPGP does not resemble this photograph: it is primarily a zone of elevated microplastic concentration in open ocean, not a surface mat of dense consumer waste. Existing size estimates for the GPGP are real (surface area estimates range from approximately 700,000 km² to over 1.6 million km² depending on methodology and boundary definition), but applying them to a photograph of a Caribbean coastal trash event creates a compound misleading impression.

**Gap Category: Miscaptioned Real Photo**

This case belongs to a significant and growing category of environmental misinformation in which authentic photographs are systematically reattributed to higher-profile phenomena to amplify their rhetorical impact. The underlying environmental concern (ocean plastic pollution) is legitimate and severe; the miscaptioning is harmful precisely because it distorts the geography and nature of the problem in ways that impede accurate public understanding and policy response.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Great Pacific Garbage Patch has become one of the most loaded symbols in contemporary environmental communication, functioning as a cognitive anchor for the scale of human plastic pollution of marine ecosystems. That symbolic function is warranted—marine plastic pollution is a genuine crisis with documented consequences for marine biodiversity, food web dynamics, and potentially human health through microplastic ingestion. Kosuth and colleagues documented microplastics in human drinking water sources globally, finding plastic contamination in 83% of tap water samples across fourteen countries (Kosuth et al., 2018, *PLOS ONE*). The pathway from ocean surface plastic through fish consumption to human dietary exposure has been empirically traced, with Rochman and colleagues documenting plastic ingestion in fish sold at Californian fish markets (Rochman et al., 2015, *Scientific Reports*).

The scientific picture of plastic's marine fate is substantially more alarming than popular imagery of floating refuse conveys. Photodegradation—the process by which UV radiation and mechanical wave action break down large plastic items into progressively smaller fragments—means that the "floating island" conceptual model is wrong in a direction that understates the problem. Large visible plastics are early-stage material; the older and more pervasive pollution is the microplastic fraction (<5 mm) and nanoplastic fraction (<1 μm) that now permeates the global ocean at every depth level, including the deep sea abyssal zone and polar ice cores. Research on deep-sea sediment cores from the California margin documented increasing plastic deposition reaching back more than forty years, with exponential accumulation rates tracking global plastic production statistics (Brandon et al., 2019, *Science Advances*).

The policy architecture emerging to address marine plastic pollution—the UN Global Plastics Treaty negotiations, national extended producer responsibility frameworks, single-use plastic prohibitions—is being shaped by public environmental perception, which in turn is heavily influenced by the available symbolic imagery. When that imagery consistently misrepresents the geographic distribution, physical form, and origin pathways of marine plastic, the resulting policy intuitions are miscalibrated. Large mid-ocean cleanup initiatives, while scientifically interesting, address a fraction of the problem that receives disproportionate media attention precisely because the GPGP is a known cultural reference point. The riverine pathways and coastal discharge points responsible for the bulk of plastic input—the conditions actually documented in Power's photographs—receive comparatively less policy attention.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published a full fact-check of this specific photograph attribution in March 2025, authored by Madison Dapcevich, rating the claim Miscaptioned. The fact-check correctly identifies the photograph's provenance (Caroline Power, 2017, Roatán Island, Honduras) and distinguishes the Caribbean location from the North Pacific location of the GPGP. It includes the NOAA clarification about the GPGP's actual character as a primarily microplastic zone rather than a visible surface mat of debris. The fact-check does not address the second layer of the misconception—that the GPGP would look like the photograph even if photographed directly—but this is a common limitation of single-claim fact-check format rather than a specific editorial failure.

Fact-checking treatments of geographic miscaptions face a particular structural challenge: the correction of a photograph's location requires the audience to accept that a photograph can be simultaneously authentic and misleadingly contextualized, a distinction that is less intuitive than simple fake/real binaries. Audiences habituated to "this photo is false" as the operative fact-check verdict may reconstruct a corrected version of the claim ("well, it shows a real place that has real trash") while still processing the photograph as evidence for the GPGP. Full debunking requires not only establishing the correct provenance but providing a clear replacement narrative about what the GPGP actually looks like and why, which requires more explanatory real estate than standard fact-check formats typically allocate.

The broader archival challenge this case illustrates is one of image persistence. Photographs, once associated with a named concept in public memory, are extraordinarily difficult to disassociate through subsequent text-based corrections. Research on continued influence effects demonstrates that corrected misinformation continues to influence reasoning even after explicit rejection (Johnson & Seifert, 1994, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*). For vivid photographs paired with accessible captions, this persistence is likely amplified. Effective mitigation strategies must include not only caption corrections attached to the original image but alternative visual anchors for the phenomenon being incorrectly depicted—in this case, accurate scientific visualizations of GPGP microplastic concentrations from research cruises.
