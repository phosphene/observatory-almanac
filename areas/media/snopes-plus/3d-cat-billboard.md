## The Claim

In early July 2021, a video began circulating online of what appeared to be a colossal cat perched on the upper corner of a building in Tokyo, Japan. The animal was rendered in extraordinary detail — fur textures, whisker movements, blinking amber eyes — and its size was staggering. The cat looked three-dimensional, as though a genuine giant creature had decided to take up residence several storeys above Shinjuku's bustling streets. Pedestrians stopped and looked up. Social media accounts shared the footage in the millions.

The claim that accompanied much of the initial sharing was one of genuine wonder mixed with uncertainty: was this some kind of real architectural installation? A massive animatronic creature? CGI somehow projected onto a building surface? And then a secondary claim emerged, framed as a debunking: the cat, supposedly three-dimensional and seemingly alive, was actually just a flat billboard.

The implied assertion of the "debunking" framing was slightly deflating — as though knowing the cat was a screen somehow diminished it, as though the proper response to discovering it was a billboard was a mild let-down. But the actual answer to the question is more interesting than either pure wonder or disillusionment. The cat was not a free-standing animatronic. It was not CGI overlaid on a building. It was a real, physical installation: a high-resolution 4K LED billboard mounted on the exterior of a building adjacent to one of the world's busiest train stations. The "3D" quality was achieved through sophisticated anamorphic visual design — a technique that produces compelling three-dimensional illusions for viewers positioned at the right angle, while being clearly flat from any other viewpoint.

Both parts of the claim are, in their own way, true. The effect is real and physically achieved. And the underlying technology is, technically, a flat screen. Understanding how both these things can be simultaneously true is a lesson in visual perception, advertising technology, and the way viral content packages reality into digestible form — often stripping out the genuinely interesting technological and artistic detail in favour of a simpler "it's not what it looks like" frame.

## What's Actually True

The Shinjuku Station 3D cat is a genuine, real installation. It is not a hoax, not CGI post-production inserted into a real street scene, and not a fake video. The installation uses a high-resolution 4K LED billboard screen mounted on the corner of a building at Shinjuku Station's East Exit, operated by Cross Space Co., Ltd., the outdoor advertising company that manages the display. The billboard is enormous — the screen itself spans multiple storeys of the building and is designed to be viewed from street level.

The cat animation — a tabby named "Shinjuku's 3D cat" — appears periodically throughout the day, rotating with more conventional flat advertisements. When it appears, it is created through a technique called anamorphic forced perspective, or more precisely through a style of motion graphics designed for L-shaped or corner-mounted screens. The Shinjuku billboard wraps around the corner of the building, with two faces meeting at approximately a right angle. By presenting slightly different images on the two faces simultaneously, carefully calibrated so that the geometry of the images aligns when viewed from the correct angle — roughly straight-on from the east side of the street — the display creates a powerfully convincing illusion of three-dimensionality.

The key mechanism is the same one that makes classic anamorphic street art deceptive: the human visual system interprets the imagery as three-dimensional when viewed from the target angle because all the spatial and perspective cues present in the image are consistent with three-dimensionality. The brain does not receive a signal labelled "this is a flat image trying to look 3D" — it receives a set of visual cues that it processes through its normal depth-perception mechanisms, and those mechanisms return a verdict of "three-dimensional object."

Snopes journalist Bethania Palma confirmed the installation's authenticity through the Japanese news blog SoraNews24 and technology news site Gizmodo, both of which covered the billboard extensively in July 2021. Gizmodo in particular demonstrated the flat reality of the billboard by publishing video taken from a different angle — one where the two faces of the corner screen are visible simultaneously without the designed alignment. From that angle, the cat appears clearly shown on a flat surface, the illusion broken by the change in perspective. This side-angle footage was the primary evidence for the "just a flat billboard" framing that circulated as a counter-narrative.

The installation was designed as a promotional centrepiece for Cross Space Co., Ltd. and was positioned at Shinjuku Station very deliberately: Shinjuku Station is the world's busiest train station by some measures, handling millions of passengers per day. The station serves as a convergence point for dozens of rail lines and is embedded in one of Tokyo's dense commercial and entertainment districts. The billboard placement guaranteed a massive audience of passersby, and the 3D cat was designed to stop them in their tracks — which it did.

Subsequent to the cat's debut, Cross Space announced plans to expand the types of 3D content on the billboard, with additional animations to launch from July 12, 2021. The billboard has since hosted a range of anamorphic content and has become one of Tokyo's recognised visual landmarks, inspiring similar anamorphic billboard installations in other cities globally. The Korean city of Seoul installed comparable anamorphic displays, as did locations in London and several Chinese cities. The Shinjuku cat is therefore not just a viral moment but a catalytic event in outdoor advertising history, helping to popularise a technology that has since spread significantly.

## Why People Believe This

The initial surprise — widespread, genuine, shared by millions — is not irrational, and the range of belief-responses the installation generated is worth examining in detail.

**The optics of impossibility.** Nothing in ordinary experience prepares a viewer for a giant cat resting on a building corner. Our visual systems are calibrated for the natural and built environments, and neither contains objects of this type. The response to the cat was therefore not a lapse in critical thinking — it was the appropriate response of a perceptual system encountering genuine novelty. The fact that videos of the cat generated immediate and widespread sharing reflects accurate processing of an unusual and striking stimulus: this really is worth seeing. The virality of the content was earned.

**The magic trick dynamic.** Part of the cultural fascination with the billboard concerns the pleasure of the trick itself. Anamorphic illusions have a long history — from Renaissance paintings using perspective tricks to 20th-century street art traditions to modern digital installations. The pleasure of an anamorphic illusion is double: first the initial perception of the impossible, and then the moment of understanding the mechanism. Both phases are enjoyable. The "is it flat?" question was already embedded in viewer psychology from the first moment people saw the cat, because sophisticated viewers could sense something was being managed, even if they couldn't immediately identify what.

**The "just a billboard" deflation.** One response to the cat — and to viral content involving technological spectacle generally — is a kind of deflationary debunking reflex: "it's just X," where X is the simpler technical explanation. This reflex is partly healthy (it reflects accurate information-seeking and resistance to being fooled) and partly a misapplication of scepticism. The cat is genuinely extraordinary. The technology that creates it is sophisticated, artistically compelling, and represents a genuine intersection of optics, motion graphics, architectural installation, and mass media. Saying "it's just a flat billboard" is technically accurate but phenomenologically incomplete — it doesn't capture what is interesting about the installation, which is precisely the engineering and artistry used to make a flat screen feel like something more.

**Uncertainty about digital fabrication.** By July 2021, audiences had been exposed to an increasing volume of digitally fabricated content in news contexts — deepfakes, CGI-enhanced footage, AI-generated imagery. The question "is this real?" had sharpened into something with higher stakes than it previously carried, because the range of fabrication technologies had expanded substantially. The cat arrived in this environment and was evaluated through a framework shaped partly by awareness of digital manipulation. Some viewers initially suspected CGI post-processing. The confirmation that the cat was physically real — a genuine installation, viewable in person, captured by multiple cameras from multiple angles — was therefore itself newsworthy and somewhat reassuring about the reliability of visual evidence.

**The cross-cultural dimension.** Many Western viewers who saw the cat had limited direct experience of Japan's urban advertising environment, which includes some of the most sophisticated and visually arresting public display technology in the world. Tokyo's Shibuya Crossing, Akihabara's densely-layered electronic storefronts, and Shinjuku's concentrated entertainment-district visual density are not fully legible from outside without context. The cat's installation made immediate sense as part of a visual culture that has invested heavily in public display technology and has high public tolerance for extravagant visual spectacle in commercial contexts.

## Verdict

**The claim is true in both its apparent forms, and the apparent contradiction between them is the interesting part.**

The 3D cat near Shinjuku Station is a real physical installation. It is not CGI, not a hoax, not a digitally manipulated video. It genuinely appears three-dimensional from the intended viewing angle because of sophisticated anamorphic visual design on a corner-mounted 4K LED billboard. It is also, technically, a flat screen. These two facts are not in tension — they are both accurate descriptions of the same installation from different angles, literally and figuratively.

Snopes rated this claim as "True" — the cat is indeed a flat billboard — while confirming that the installation itself is genuine and physically real. The full picture requires holding both halves of the story simultaneously: extraordinary as it looks, it is achieved through media technology; exactly because it is achieved through media technology, it is extraordinary.

The billboard represents a genuine technological and artistic achievement in outdoor advertising, one that has influenced subsequent installations worldwide. It is worth understanding in full, rather than flattening into either uncritical wonder or knowing deflation.

## The Wider Picture

The Shinjuku 3D cat sits at the intersection of several expanding technological and cultural trajectories, each of which has implications beyond the immediate wonder of a cat on a building.

**Anamorphic display technology.** The optical principle underlying the Shinjuku billboard — anamorphosis — has a history stretching back to the 15th century. Renaissance painters used it to embed hidden images or correct for perspectival distortion in ceiling paintings. Hans Holbein the Younger famously embedded an elongated, unrecognisable skull in his 1533 painting "The Ambassadors," which resolves into a clear skull image when viewed from a specific oblique angle. Street artists in the late 20th and early 21st century popularised anamorphic chalk art and painted installations that create convincing three-dimensional effects on flat surfaces. Digital billboard technology has extended this tradition by adding motion, interactivity, and extraordinary resolution, and by embedding the technique in commercial contexts that achieve massive public reach.

The technical challenge of anamorphic digital content on corner screens requires careful calculation of the perspectives from which the display will be viewed, the precise geometry of the screen panels, and the rendering of imagery that will create consistent depth cues across both panels when seen from the target position. The result is an engineered illusion — one that exploits the known properties of human visual perception with precision. This is not magic, but it is sophisticated science and art working in concert.

**The urban media landscape.** Tokyo's advertising environment is remarkable even without the 3D cat. Japan's outdoor advertising industry is among the most sophisticated in the world, reflecting both technological investment and a cultural aesthetic that places high value on visual novelty, precision engineering, and public spectacle. The placement of the Cross Space billboard at Shinjuku Station's East Exit was not accidental — it was a calculated deployment of maximum-impact advertising technology in the highest-footfall outdoor retail and transit location in Japan. The 3D cat was both a demonstration of what anamorphic LED technology could do and a sophisticated piece of brand marketing for the display company.

The spread of similar technology to other cities following the Shinjuku cat's viral success reflects a global advertising industry pattern: compelling demonstrations of new display capabilities function as proof of concept for clients in other markets. The cat — by being genuinely astonishing and generating enormous unpaid media — served as the advertising industry's most effective advertisement for anamorphic display technology. Seoul's 3D Wave installation and similar projects in London and Chinese cities followed in its wake.

**Perception and reality.** The Shinjuku cat raises, in a commercially accessible form, deep questions about the relationship between perception and reality that philosophy and cognitive science have grappled with for centuries. We perceive the cat as three-dimensional. The cat is not three-dimensional. Yet describing the cat as "just" a flat screen undersells the phenomenological reality of the experience — the cat genuinely looks three-dimensional, and the experience of looking at it is genuinely extraordinary. The gap between "how things appear" and "how things are" is a classic philosophical territory, but the billboard makes this gap vivid and immediate in a way that abstract philosophical examples rarely do.

This connects to broader questions about what we mean by "real" in a heavily mediated visual environment. The cat is not real in the sense of being a biological organism or a physical three-dimensional structure. But the visual experience of seeing it is real. The delight and surprise of passersby is real. The technical achievement of the display is real. The cat's cultural and media footprint is real. In what sense, exactly, is the cat "not real"? The question deserves more careful treatment than the simple "it's just a billboard" framing provides.

**Viral dynamics and visual culture.** The cat's viral spread was driven by the same mechanism that drives most viral visual content: it showed people something they had never seen before and that was immediately, viscerally arresting. Unlike AI-generated fake imagery (such as the Indonesian beach octopus discussed elsewhere in this series), the Shinjuku cat was entirely honest about what it was — a genuine installation, viewable in person, confirmed by multiple sources. Its virality was not driven by deception but by genuine wonder. This distinction matters: not all viral visual content is problematic, and the spread of the cat across social media was a reasonably healthy instance of public information-sharing about an interesting real-world event.

**The future of public display.** The cat also gestures toward a rapidly developing future of public display technology. Higher-resolution displays, improved corner and curved screen technology, real-time rendering capabilities, and the integration of audience sensing (knowing where viewers are standing in real time to optimise the anamorphic effect for live viewers) are all areas of active development. Future large-scale public displays may be responsive, interactive, and customised for specific viewers in real time. The Shinjuku cat is, from this perspective, an early and somewhat primitive ancestor of what public visual media will eventually become — though it already represents a substantial degree of technical sophistication by 2021 standards.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check on the Shinjuku 3D cat is an example of a fact-check performed on a claim that is true, presented with sufficient context to be informative rather than merely confirmatory.

**Verification methodology.** The fact-checker, Bethania Palma, confirmed the existence and nature of the installation through Japanese-language and English-language media coverage. SoraNews24 and Gizmodo both covered the billboard in detail with video and photographic documentation. This multi-source confirmation is appropriate for a claim of this kind. The installation is genuinely new and was genuinely confusing to many viewers; providing sourced confirmation of its nature and mechanism serves a clear public information function.

**The role of the angle-revealing video.** Gizmodo's publication of video taken from the non-target angle — showing the flat reality of the billboard when viewed from the side — is the key piece of evidence in this fact-check. It demonstrates the mechanism of the illusion clearly and efficiently. A viewer who has seen both the target-angle video (cat looks 3D) and the side-angle video (screen clearly flat) has, in effect, seen how the trick works. This demonstration-of-mechanism approach is good fact-checking practice: it doesn't just assert a conclusion but shows the evidence that produces the conclusion.

**What "True" means here.** The Snopes rating of "True" confirms the specific claim that the cat is a flat billboard. This is a narrowly but accurately scoped verdict. The fuller picture — that the installation is a genuine technological achievement, that the 3D effect is real-in-the-sense-of-being-physically-produced rather than digitally inserted, that the technology is sophisticated rather than simple — is present in the article's text but somewhat subordinated to the rating. This is a limitation of the fact-check verdict format: binary or near-binary ratings inevitably lose information that the full article contains.

**The limits of the debunking frame.** The "is it really just a flat billboard?" framing of the claim treats the installation as a puzzle to be solved rather than a phenomenon to be understood. Once the puzzle is solved — yes, it's a screen — the framing's work is done. But the more interesting questions — how does the anamorphic technique work? what does the installation tell us about urban advertising culture in Japan? what does the experience of the illusion tell us about perception? — fall outside the debunking frame. Richer engagement with these questions requires contextual and explanatory journalism beyond the fact-check format, of which this entry is an attempt.

The 3D cat is one of those pieces of viral content that rewards returning to after the initial wonder has settled. The more you know about how it works, the more impressive it becomes. The anamorphic technique is elegant; the installation engineering is precise; the cultural context is rich. What looks like a simple trick is actually a dense intersection of optics, art, technology, and media strategy. Knowing it's a flat billboard should increase rather than decrease your appreciation for what it is — which is, on any honest accounting, extraordinary.
