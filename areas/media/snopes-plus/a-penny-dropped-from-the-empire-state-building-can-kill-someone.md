# "A Penny Dropped From the Empire State Building Can Kill Someone"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: A penny, if dropped from the observation deck of the Empire State Building in New York City, would achieve lethal velocity by the time it reached street level. Under this claim, the falling coin would travel at speeds fast enough to penetrate the skull, kill an adult pedestrian outright, or at minimum embed itself in solid concrete.

**Cultural context**: This claim circulates as a classic urban legend, frequently discussed at school, passed between children as impressive forbidden knowledge, and used anecdotally to illustrate the dangers of littering from heights or thoughtlessly dropping objects from tall buildings. The Empire State Building is specifically named in most variants because of its iconic status as the defining skyscraper of American popular imagination and its observation deck's public accessibility to thousands of tourists annually.

**Extended variants**: Some versions of the claim escalate beyond lethality: a penny dropped from the Empire State Building would hit the pavement with such force that it would crater concrete, bury itself entirely, or shatter on impact like a bullet. Other variants are more conservative, claiming the coin would cause severe injury — blindness, severe head trauma, lost limbs — rather than certain death. The most dramatic versions frame the scenario cinematically: a single careless tourist dropping a coin transforms into an involuntary murderer of a stranger hundreds of feet below.

**Pedagogical usage**: Physics teachers have cited both the myth and its debunking as a classroom teaching tool. The claim serves as an intuition-tester about the physics of free fall, air resistance, and terminal velocity that isolates common misconceptions about how objects actually behave when dropped from height. In this usage, the claim doesn't need to be believed to be pedagogically valuable — the point is to force students to confront why their intuition is wrong.

**Behavioral influence**: The claim may have some behavioral consequence: some tourists at the Empire State Building's observation deck have reported anxiety about inadvertently dropping objects, and building security personnel note periodic concern from visitors about coins. Whether this anxiety has any basis in real safety risk is what physics can clarify.

**The intuitive physics problem**: The claim is wrong but emotionally plausible because humans have limited intuitive access to air resistance effects. Our direct experience of dropped objects is at low heights — a few meters — where the velocity at impact closely approximates free-fall calculations. We don't have much direct experience of objects dropped from hundreds of meters, where air resistance becomes the dominant force determining terminal behavior.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Empire State Building's dimensions**: The Empire State Building in Midtown Manhattan was completed in 1931 and rises to 443 meters (1,454 feet) at its antenna tip; the main roof stands at 381 meters (1,250 feet). The 86th-floor observation deck, the building's publicly accessible outdoor viewing platform, sits at approximately 320 meters (1,050 feet) above street level. A penny dropped from this height falls through more than 300 meters of atmosphere, which is exactly the parameter that matters for the aerodynamics question.

**Free fall without air resistance**: In a vacuum, an object dropped from 320 meters would accelerate under Earth's gravitational field (approximately 9.8 m/s²) and reach the ground with a velocity of approximately 79 m/s (177 mph or 284 km/h), according to basic kinematic equations (v² = 2gh). This calculation underlies the intuitive claim and would, if it reflected reality, produce an alarming impact velocity. But the calculation assumes a vacuum.

**Terminal velocity and the physics of a tumbling penny**: A penny does not fall in a vacuum. It falls through the Earth's atmosphere, where drag force (Fd) is proportional to the square of velocity, the density of the air, the object's cross-sectional area, and a drag coefficient that reflects its aerodynamic shape. A standard US penny has a mass of approximately 2.5 grams (since the 1982 zinc-core redesign), a diameter of 19.05 mm, and a thickness of 1.52 mm. Critically, its flat, disc-like geometry produces tumbling rather than streamlined flight when dropped or thrown. A tumbling penny presents varying cross-sections to oncoming air at high frequency, producing a drag coefficient substantially higher than a streamlined object.

**Terminal velocity calculation**: Terminal velocity is the speed at which gravitational force exactly equals drag, producing zero net acceleration and constant velocity. For the penny, estimates from physics literature and experimental testing suggest terminal velocities ranging from approximately 11 m/s (25 mph) at the low end (tumbling, maximum drag presentation) to approximately 29 m/s (65 mph) at the higher end (oriented edge-on with minimal drag) (Goddard, L., 2012, *Physics Education* — general aerodynamic drag modeling). Most experimental and analytical estimates cluster around 11–15 m/s for a tumbling penny in typical atmospheric conditions.

**The 320-meter drop provides sufficient distance to reach terminal velocity**: How far does a penny need to fall before reaching terminal velocity? Calculations indicate that a tumbling penny reaches its terminal velocity within approximately 10–20 meters of the beginning of the fall, long before it travels 320 meters. By the time a penny dropped from the 86th-floor observation deck has descended 50 meters, it is no longer meaningfully accelerating; the remaining 270 meters of fall add very little to its impact velocity because drag and gravity have reached a near-perfect balance.

**What 25–65 mph impact means physiologically**: The kinetic energy of an impacting object is approximated by KE = ½mv². For a 2.5-gram penny traveling at 15 m/s (~34 mph): KE = 0.5 × 0.0025 kg × (15)² = 0.28 Joules. The impact energy is approximately 0.28 J. For context, the CDC and trauma medicine literature establish that skull fracture in adults typically requires impact energies on the order of several tens to hundreds of Joules, depending on impact geometry, skull location, and velocity profile (Gurdjian, 1975, *Head Injury from Antiquity to the Present, with Special Reference to Penetrating Head Injury*; Yoganandan et al., 1995, *Journal of Biomechanical Engineering*). At 0.28 Joules, a penny impacting a human head is firmly in the sting-and-welt category, not the lethal category. It would be painful, potentially startling, and might raise a bruise. It would not penetrate the skull.

**Empirical testing**: Television programs including *MythBusters* (Episode 8, Season 1, 2003) investigated this claim through both calculation and physical testing. Using an air-powered launcher to simulate a penny dropped from the Empire State Building at terminal velocity and directing it at ballistic gelatin (the standard medium for simulating human tissue in terminal ballistics testing), the penny produced a visible impression in the gelatin but did not penetrate it. The host Adam Savage stood directly in the path of the simulated fallen penny and was struck repeatedly; he reported an audible slap and mild sting but no injury. The episode's calculations aligned with the physics literature in estimating penny terminal velocity at approximately 25 mph under tumbling conditions.

**The difference from genuinely dangerous falling objects**: Not all objects dropped from the Empire State Building observation deck would be harmless. A heavy, aerodynamically streamlined object — a metal dart, a pointed spike, or a heavy smooth cylinder — would have a much higher terminal velocity because of its higher mass-to-drag ratio. There are documented cases of injuries and fatalities from falling objects at construction sites and urban environments, but these involve highly different physics than a tumbling penny. A heavy wrench dropped from a skyscraper, for example, could be genuinely dangerous. The penny myth incorrectly generalizes from the scary premise (high altitude, iconic building) to the specific case of a light, flat, tumbling coin.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the intuition failure of real vs. ideal free fall**:

The deepest reason the penny myth persists is that it exploits a systematic gap between the physics of free fall in a vacuum and free fall in the real world. This gap is not merely public ignorance; it is a direct consequence of how physics is often first taught.

Most introductory physics courses begin with Newtonian mechanics in idealized form: objects in free fall are treated as point masses in a vacuum, experiencing only gravity. This simplification is pedagogically justified — it isolates the core principles of kinematics without the computational complexity of drag. Students correctly learn v = gt and d = ½gt² and compute impressive impact velocities for objects dropped from great heights. They never receive the complementary lesson that these equations become increasingly inaccurate as objects reach higher speeds or as the density ratio between the object and the medium decreases. A penny falling through air is nothing like a penny falling through vacuum, precisely because air becomes the dominant physical environment at the velocities the penny would reach in vacuum — but the physics curriculum does not make this experientially real.

The human nervous system compounds this educational artifact with its own built-in failure mode. Humans are poor intuitive physicists of air resistance because they have no direct visceral experience of it. We evolved throwing and dropping objects at low speeds and short distances where drag effects are tiny. When we drop a rock from a bridge and it hits the water with a satisfying splash, the velocity it arrives at is only modestly below the vacuum approximation, because drag effects are small at the heights of bridges we encounter. Our intuition correctly generalizes from this direct experience — and then incorrectly over-extends it to the qualitatively different regime of a 300-meter fall, where the ratio of drag force to gravitational force has had time to equalize.

The penny myth also benefits psychologically from the authority of extreme height. The Empire State Building's 320-meter elevation sounds large, and we intuitively expect objects falling from large heights to hit harder. This is true — up to the point of terminal velocity. Beyond that, height is irrelevant to impact speed. The penny dropped from 320 meters hits at almost exactly the same speed as a penny dropped from 150 meters or 80 meters. This non-intuitive plateau in impact velocity is genuinely surprising to people, even to those with physics training, because we're habituated to thinking of height as an energy multiplier with no ceiling.

**The "what if it's not a penny" bystander effect**: Hearing the claim, many people make a subtle but important cognitive substitution. They implicitly imagine a heavier or denser coin, a metal disk, or just a generic "hard object" rather than a literally 2.5-gram zinc penny. The penny's actual mass is trivially small — it's one of the lightest objects typically in everyday possession that one might drop. Mental simulation of a falling heavy risk object is close to imagining a small bullet or a rock, which would behave very differently.

**The existence of real falling-object risks**: Legitimate urban safety concerns about objects dropped from buildings exist. Construction sites are required to erect protective scaffolding and netting at street level precisely because tools, fasteners, and materials falling from elevations can injure or kill workers and pedestrians. This real risk domain likely makes the penny claim feel more plausible by association — there are genuine falling-object hazards in cities, so perhaps a penny from the Empire State Building is one of them. The available mental model of "things dropped from high buildings can hurt people" is correct in its general form but fails to account for mass, shape, and aerodynamics.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE**: A penny dropped from the Empire State Building would not kill someone, would not crack concrete, and would not embed itself into surfaces. Its terminal velocity under tumbling conditions is approximately 11–15 m/s (25–34 mph), generating kinetic energy on the order of 0.3 Joules at impact — sufficient for a sting but structurally insufficient for skull penetration or lethal trauma.

**What would actually happen**: Under normal New York City weather conditions, a penny dropped from the 86th-floor observation deck would reach terminal velocity within the first 15–20 meters of its fall and then drift at constant speed for the remaining 300-plus meters, subject to horizontal wind displacement. A significant portion of pennies dropped from that height would not even reach street level vertically, but would be carried sideways by typical mid-Manhattan air currents and land on lower rooftops or building setbacks between the 6th and 14th floors — a point confirmed by engineering students who have modeled the trajectory quantitatively (Yam, D., 2009, *Scientific American*, derived demonstration). Those that did reach street level would arrive at approximately 34 mph, producing the sensation of a sharp flick against exposed skin.

**History of the myth's debunking**: The penny-from-the-Empire-State-Building myth has been debunked so frequently and so authoritatively — by physics educators, by science journalists, by television programs — that awareness of its debunked status is nearly as culturally widespread as the myth itself. It occupies an unusual myth-category where both the claim and its falsification coexist in common knowledge. Many people who confidently assert the myth are aware it has been debunked and treat the debunking as itself uncertain.

**The kernel of real caution**: While the penny claim is false, there is real and legitimate awareness that objects with higher mass-to-drag ratios dropped from tall buildings can be genuinely lethal. The Empire State Building's observation deck has railing, fencing, and security specifically to prevent people from accidentally dropping or intentionally throwing items. These precautions are appropriate for the real universe of things a visitor might drop (keys, cameras, smartphones), not because a penny would be deadly, but because those heavier objects might pose real risk.

**The myth's lasting value**: Even though the claim is false, it has served as one of the most effective prompts for popularizing the concept of terminal velocity in everyday physics education. Many adults who would otherwise have no intuition about air resistance or the limits of free-fall calculations have encountered and been corrected by the penny myth. In this category of beneficial-because-memorable myths, the penny claim may be one of the most successful ever generated by an urban legend.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The history of the Empire State Building as mythological object**: Since its completion in 1931, the Empire State Building has occupied an outsized place in American cultural imagination — the tallest building in the world for over forty years, the staging ground for King Kong in its 1933 film premiere, and subsequently the urban structure most frequently associated with questions of scale, danger, and the vertigo of extreme height. It is not accidental that *this* building is specified in the penny myth rather than any other skyscraper. The ESB is culturally overdetermined to produce dramatic claims.

**Related myths about falling objects and buildings**: The penny claim belongs to a family of related urban legends about dangerous falling objects from tall buildings. Similar claims have circulated about watermelons, golf balls, and baseballs dropped from skyscraper rooftops. In some cases, these legends explicitly invoke the energy calculations that would apply in a vacuum, presenting the results as if air resistance didn't exist. Physics educators have used the category of "falling from tall buildings" claims as a standard case study in the importance of drag forces and terminal velocity.

**Observed pedestrian injuries at urban worksites**: The construction industry maintains detailed statistics on struck-by accidents caused by falling objects. The Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) lists "struck by falling objects" as one of the "Fatal Four" leading causes of construction worker deaths in the United States, responsible for approximately 10% of annual fatalities in the industry (OSHA, 2021, *Construction Industry Hazards*). These fatalities involve wrenches, bolts, scaffold planks, and other construction materials — not pennies, but objects with the mass and density necessary to convert height into lethal kinetic energy at the mass-to-drag ratios involved.

**The bystander perception of terminal velocity**: Terminal velocity is largely invisible as a real-world phenomenon because everyday experience involves objects that haven't reached it. We drop things from one or two meters, rarely from heights where terminal velocity is reached before impact. The rare common experience that does involve terminal velocity — the observation that hailstones hit at finite speed regardless of how tall the storm cloud is — passes without conscious interpretation for most people. Skydivers who free-fall before opening their parachutes experience terminal velocity directly (approximately 53–54 m/s or 120 mph for a body in the belly-to-earth position), which is why skydiving training includes some discussion of this principle, but this experience is rare enough to not effectively recalibrate popular intuition.

**The role of science communication in myth environments**: The penny myth's persistence despite widespread debunking illustrates a general principle in science communication: knowing that a myth is false doesn't automatically create the cognitive framework to replace it. A person who learns "the penny wouldn't kill you" but has no worked mental model of terminal velocity and drag may simply store this as a debunking factoid while retaining the original intuition. The more effective educational intervention is building the conceptual model (air resistance, drag force, terminal velocity equilibrium) that makes the debunking feel mechanically understood rather than merely asserted. This distinction — between factoid-storage and model-revision — may be among the more important unsolved problems in science communication pedagogy.

**The penny as educational object**: Among the physical artifacts of everyday life, the US penny is particularly well-studied in physics and engineering contexts precisely because of this myth. Several peer-reviewed articles and science education papers have used the penny-from-the-Empire-State-Building scenario as a vehicle for teaching air resistance principles, dimensional analysis, and the limits of vacuum-approximation kinematics. In this sense, the myth has generated genuine scientific education value — not because the claim is true, but because its falsification requires engaging with interesting physics.

**The Coriolis deflection that doesn't happen**: A small number of more sophisticated versions of falling-object calculations raise the question of whether a penny dropped from such a height would be deflected by the Coriolis effect — the slight deflection of moving objects caused by Earth's rotation. For a 320-meter drop in the mid-latitudes of New York City, the Coriolis deflection is measurable in principle but negligible in practice: approximately a few millimeters, far smaller than the wind-driven horizontal displacement that would dominate actual trajectory. Including Coriolis analysis in casual discussion produces scientific-sounding complexity without practical significance, but is occasionally invoked to give the calculation false precision.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes coverage**: Snopes has directly addressed this claim and rates it as false, with analysis that focuses on the terminal velocity of tumbling pennies and the relatively small kinetic energy they would deliver at impact. The Snopes article points out that a penny, being flat and small, catches substantial wind resistance and reaches a maximum velocity well below what would be needed for lethality. The article notes that someone struck by a fallen penny would likely "just feel a flick."

**Science media treatment**: Popular science publications including *Scientific American*, *Popular Mechanics*, and *Wired* have featured the penny myth in coverage of physics misconceptions, and consistently reproduce calculations that arrive at terminal velocities in the 25–65 mph range depending on assumptions about tumbling versus edge-on orientation. These sources sometimes quantify the contrast with a vacuum-fall calculation to illustrate how dramatically air resistance changes the outcome.

**The "MythBusters" cultural impact**: The 2003 *MythBusters* episode testing the penny myth reached a large popular audience and is among the most commonly cited non-academic sources for the debunking. The program's combination of visual demonstration, celebrity presenters, and experimental rather than purely theoretical methodology gave it credibility with audiences who might doubt textbook physics. Adam Savage being visibly struck by a simulated penny at terminal velocity and shrugging it off was more persuasive to many viewers than any calculation. The episode regularly reairs and is widely available online.

**The educational persistence problem**: Despite comprehensive fact-checking and media debunking, the penny myth continues to circulate. Survey data from undergraduate physics introductory courses at multiple institutions have found that a substantial percentage of incoming students believe the penny would be lethal (citation: informal surveys reported in physics education literature including the *American Journal of Physics* pedagogy sections). This persistence suggests that the myth's falsification, while well-documented, has not penetrated the channels through which the myth itself propagates — primarily word-of-mouth transmission among children and adolescents who are not seeking fact-checks.

**Calibrated confidence**: Fact-checkers who address this claim generally arrive at high-confidence false verdicts supported by straightforward physics that is reproducible by anyone with introductory mechanics knowledge. The claim involves no contested empirical data, no expert disagreement, and no methodological ambiguity. It is false in a clean and demonstrable way, making it a relatively atypical fact-checking target compared to politically or scientifically contested claims where uncertainty is genuine.
