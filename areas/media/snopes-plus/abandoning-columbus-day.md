# More cities and states are abandoning Columbus Day for Indigenous Peoples' Day


## 1. The Claim

A widely circulated claim holds that cities and states across the United States are increasingly replacing Columbus Day — the federal holiday observed on the second Monday of October, commemorating Christopher Columbus's 1492 arrival in the Americas — with Indigenous Peoples' Day, honouring the Native American peoples who inhabited the Americas before European contact. The claim appears in news reporting, advocacy materials, political commentary, and social media posts, typically framed either as evidence of a necessary historical reckoning or as an example of the overreach of cultural progressive politics, depending on the perspective of the source.

The claim is stated as a trend description: the direction of change is from Columbus Day to Indigenous Peoples' Day, the trend is ongoing, and the number of jurisdictions making the change is increasing. Variations of the claim emphasise different aspects: the number of cities or states that have made the change (specific figures ranging from dozens to over a hundred, depending on the date of the claim); the participation of large major cities including Los Angeles, Washington D.C., and Denver; the federal government's actions under the Biden administration in issuing presidential proclamations acknowledging Indigenous Peoples' Day; and in some versions, the resistance of Italian-American organisations and other advocates for maintaining Columbus Day in its traditional form.

Understanding whether this claim is accurate requires examining the historical arc of the movement, the current legal and administrative status of the change across jurisdictions, and the nature of the ongoing controversy — which is principally political, not factual.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The historical origin of Indigenous Peoples' Day.** The movement to replace or supplement Columbus Day with a holiday recognising Indigenous peoples did not emerge spontaneously in the social media era. Its institutional origins trace to a 1977 proposal issued by participants at the International Conference on Discrimination Against Indigenous Populations in the Americas, held in Geneva, Switzerland, which called for replacing Columbus Day with a ceremony honouring Indigenous Americans. The proposal did not immediately generate legislative action in the United States, but it established the conceptual framework that later advocates would adopt.

The first formal recognition of Indigenous Peoples' Day in a US jurisdiction came from Berkeley, California, in 1992 — the 500th anniversary of Columbus's 1492 voyage. The Berkeley City Council voted to establish Indigenous Peoples Day as a counter-observance to the Columbus Day quincentennial. This was a deliberate act of counter-programming: 1992 marked nationwide celebrations of the 500th anniversary of Columbus's landing, and the Berkeley measure was partly conceived as a protest of those celebrations and of the historical narrative they implied.

For roughly fifteen years after Berkeley's action, the movement made limited legislative progress. A small number of universities and communities adopted similar measures, but Indigenous Peoples' Day remained a minority observance confined to sympathetic communities. The broader political landscape did not encourage rapid change: Columbus Day had been a federal holiday since 1937, the date having been championed in part by Italian-American civic organisations who saw it as recognition of Columbus's Italian heritage and by extension of the Italian-American community's place in American civic life.

**The acceleration of change after 2014.** The pace of change accelerated significantly beginning around 2014. Seattle and Minneapolis both adopted Indigenous Peoples' Day ordinances in 2014, and Minneapolis's action attracted national media attention partly because of its political prominence and partly because the framing was explicitly about replacing rather than supplementing the Columbus Day observance. Portland, Oregon; Albuquerque, New Mexico; and a growing number of other cities followed in subsequent years.

By the late 2010s, the cumulative number of US cities, counties, townships, and local jurisdictions that had formally adopted Indigenous Peoples' Day or renamed the second Monday of October from Columbus Day to Indigenous Peoples' Day had grown to more than 100, with major cities including Los Angeles (2017), Denver (2019), and Washington D.C. (2019) among the adopters. The list is not static, and its precise enumeration depends on how formal adoption is defined (some jurisdictions passed symbolic resolutions without changing official holiday designation).

States began adopting changes at a slower pace, reflecting the greater political complexity of statewide policy. Alaska, Vermont, New Mexico, Maine, Louisiana, Iowa, Michigan, Wisconsin, and Oregon are among the states that had formally replaced Columbus Day with Indigenous Peoples' Day (or a similarly designated holiday) at the state level by the mid-2020s. South Dakota has long observed a "Native Americans' Day" on the same date, predating the modern movement.

**Federal actions.** The federal holiday Columbus Day, established by Congress and signed into law by President Dwight Eisenhower in 1937, has not been abolished or renamed by federal legislation. However, beginning in 2021, President Joe Biden issued a presidential proclamation acknowledging Indigenous Peoples' Day on the same date as Columbus Day — a symbolic action that did not change the official federal holiday name but represented the first presidential-level recognition of the counter-observance. Biden continued this practice in subsequent years of his administration. Whether subsequent administrations would continue or discontinue the practice became a political question following the 2024 elections.

**The claim's accuracy.** The specific claim that "more cities and states are abandoning Columbus Day for Indigenous Peoples' Day" is factually accurate as a description of a trend. The trend is real, documented through the legislative and administrative records of the jurisdictions concerned, and involves a numerically significant and growing number of US entities. The word "more" implies a comparison over time, which is supported by the historical data: the number was one (Berkeley) in 1992, remained very small through the 2000s, and grew to over 100 cities and roughly a dozen states by the mid-2020s.

The word "abandoning" carries an evaluative connotation — it implies something is being lost, and it frames the change from the perspective of those who view it as a loss. A neutral description would use language like "replacing" or "renaming" or "observing an alternative to." The use of "abandoning" in popular media coverage, particularly coverage sympathetic to the Columbus Day tradition, is a framing choice that shapes reader interpretation without constituting factual inaccuracy about the change itself.

**What the change does and does not mean.** The legislative change in a given city typically works in one of several ways. Some jurisdictions formally rename the holiday — the second Monday of October is officially Indigenous Peoples' Day, with Columbus Day no longer recognised as an official holiday. Some jurisdictions add Indigenous Peoples' Day as a co-equal observance while retaining Columbus Day on the ceremonial calendar. Some issue symbolic resolutions acknowledging Indigenous Peoples' Day without changing official holiday designations. The practical effects — whether it is a paid day off for city employees, whether city offices close, whether schools observe it — vary by jurisdiction and by the administrative category under which the change was made.

**The Italian-American dimension.** Opposition to the change has been most organised within the Italian-American community, through organisations including the Order Sons and Daughters of Italy in America, the Italian American One Voice Coalition, and various local Italian-American civic associations. Their argument is that Columbus Day serves as the primary civic recognition of Italian-American heritage and contributions, and that replacing it removes a form of representation that the community has historically valued. The holiday was championed as a federal observance partly as a response to anti-Italian discrimination in the early twentieth century, including the 1891 lynching of eleven Sicilian immigrants in New Orleans — then the largest mass lynching in American history — and it carries historical significance within the Italian-American community beyond its nominal subject of Columbus.

Proponents of Indigenous Peoples' Day argue that the holiday's original framing obscures the historical record of Columbus's expeditions, which included enslavement, violence, and the introduction of colonial systems that devastated Indigenous populations; that observing Columbus as a civic hero sends signals to Indigenous students, communities, and citizens about their status in American civic life; and that the Italian-American community's heritage can be honoured through mechanisms other than a holiday honouring an individual whose historical significance is inseparable from the beginning of European colonial conquest.

This is a contested value question in which both sides are arguing from genuine historical claims and legitimate interests; it is not a dispute about factual accuracy in the way that many Snopes-style claims are.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Because it is verifiably occurring.** The primary reason the claim circulates is that it accurately describes a documented legislative and policy trend. Media coverage of individual city and state adoptions creates repeated exposure to the fact of the trend, and the cumulative effect of that coverage creates widespread awareness that the change is happening.

**The claim functions as political signal-sending.** In political discourse, citing the trend toward Indigenous Peoples' Day serves different rhetorical functions depending on perspective. For advocates of the change, citing the trend demonstrates momentum and normalises the choice. For opponents, citing the trend frames it as a threat requiring response. Both uses depend on the claim being accurate — and both use accuracy in service of partisan framing.

**Media coverage emphasises moments of change.** Journalism tends to cover the adoption of new policies rather than the continuation of old ones. Each city's or state's adoption of Indigenous Peoples' Day generates local and sometimes national coverage. The cumulative effect of this coverage in the aggregate makes the trend prominent in public awareness even though any given reader may have encountered only a handful of individual adoption stories.

**The framing language varies by political context.** Whether the change is described as "abandoning," "replacing," "renaming," "celebrating," or "recognising" is a framing indicator that signals the political orientation of the source. Readers who primarily consume media from sources sympathetic to Columbus Day preservation will encounter different linguistic framings than readers primarily exposed to media sympathetic to the change, even when they are reading accurate descriptions of the same legislative actions.

**Historical revisionism debates generate broader cultural attention.** The Columbus Day/Indigenous Peoples' Day debate is one of several parallel debates about historical commemoration that intensified in the late 2010s and followed the post-2020 discussions of Confederate monument removal, Juneteenth recognition, and other commemorative controversies. The debate does not exist in isolation but is part of a broader cultural and political conversation about whose history is honoured in public life and what that honouring signifies. The visibility of that broader conversation amplifies awareness of the specific Columbus Day debate.

**Italian-American political engagement sustains media coverage.** The organised opposition of Italian-American civic organisations gives the debate institutional weight on both sides. Formal statements, political lobbying, and court challenges from Italian-American organisations ensure continued media coverage of the controversy, which in turn keeps the trend itself in public awareness. A policy change that encountered no organised opposition would attract much less ongoing media attention than this one does.


## 4. Verdict

**Accurately described trend, contested on political rather than factual grounds.** The claim that more cities and states are abandoning Columbus Day for Indigenous Peoples' Day is factually accurate as a trend description. The number of US jurisdictions that have formally adopted Indigenous Peoples' Day in place of or alongside Columbus Day has grown substantially from the 1992 Berkeley action through the mid-2020s, encompassing over 100 cities and at least a dozen states. The trend is documented, verifiable, and ongoing.

The claim is not contested factually in any serious way. The controversy it generates is about whether the change is desirable, whether it accurately represents historical complexity, whether it appropriately weighs competing community interests, and whether it is the right mechanism for the cultural objectives its proponents seek. These are genuine and legitimate political and value disputes, but they are not disputes about whether the trend the claim describes is real.

The EARC classification is R: the claim requires context to be properly assessed, because the evaluative framing embedded in the word "abandoning" and the political polarisation of the topic make neutral understanding difficult from the claim alone, even though the factual core is accurately stated. The relevant context includes the historical origins of both Columbus Day and the Indigenous Peoples' Day movement, the Italian-American community's specific relationship to Columbus Day, the legal mechanisms through which changes have been made (many of which are symbolic or partial), and the federal holiday's continued legal status.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Columbus Day/Indigenous Peoples' Day debate is a case study in how commemorative politics function in plural democracies, where public symbols and official holidays are sites of contested historical meaning rather than neutral markers of shared heritage.

**Columbus Day's own contested origins.** Columbus Day was first proposed as a federal holiday by Colorado in 1907 and was championed by Knights of Columbus — a Catholic fraternal organisation — partly as a demonstration that Catholics could participate in American civic nationalism. The choice of Columbus as a figure was partly because he offered a Catholic, Italian (Genoese) explorer who could serve as an alternative national founder narrative to the dominant Protestant Anglo-Saxon framing of American origins. The holiday was established as a federal observance in the context of post-World War II American identity politics and Cold War cultural diplomacy as much as as a straightforward historical commemoration.

This origin means that Columbus Day was itself a politically constructed observance serving specific community and political interests, not a neutral historical celebration that later politics disrupted. The Indigenous Peoples' Day movement is, in this sense, a competing political construction of the same date rather than an external challenge to an apolitical tradition. Recognising this genealogy makes the current dispute less anomalous: the date has always been politically contested terrain, and the current contest is a new phase of a long-running negotiation about whose history American public life celebrates.

**The comparative international context.** The Columbus discovery narrative and its relationship to Indigenous history is contested across the Americas, not only in the United States. In many Latin American countries, October 12 is observed as "Día de la Raza" (Day of the Race), emphasising the mixing of European and Indigenous ancestry that produced contemporary Latin American identity. Venezuela under Hugo Chávez renamed October 12 the "Day of Indigenous Resistance" in 2002. Some Caribbean and South American nations have moved toward ceremonial acknowledgments of pre-Columbian history. The American debate is part of a hemispheric negotiation with Indigenous history that is occurring across the former European colonial world.

**Monument and memorialisation as political acts.** The Columbus Day debate is continuous with broader debates about Confederate monuments, colonial-era statues in European cities (including the 2020 toppling of the Edward Colston statue in Bristol and debates about Belgian monuments to Leopold II), and the naming of public institutions after historical figures whose legacies are contested. These debates share a common structure: a commemoration established in a particular political moment for particular political purposes is later challenged by people who were not adequately represented in the original political context. The challenge is resisted by those who have developed attachments to the commemoration and who interpret the challenge as an erasure of their own historical recognition.

**The pedagogical dimension.** Part of what is at stake in the Columbus Day/Indigenous Peoples' Day debate is the message sent to students, particularly Indigenous students, about whose history and whose suffering are considered worthy of public acknowledgment. Research in educational psychology and multicultural education has examined how symbolic representation in official holidays, school curricula, and public commemorations affects the civic belonging and academic engagement of students from marginalised groups. This research provides some empirical grounding for arguments that the symbolic content of public holidays has consequences beyond their ceremonial function.

**What tends to happen after adoption.** In cities and states that have adopted Indigenous Peoples' Day, the practical effects have typically been modest. Most changes have been more symbolic than transformative. Few jurisdictions have radically restructured civic educational programmes or created substantial new investment in Indigenous cultural programming as part of the holiday change. Critics from within Indigenous communities have sometimes noted that renaming a holiday is a low-cost gesture that can substitute for more substantive policy changes regarding Indigenous land rights, sovereignty, treaty obligations, health, and economic disparities. This critique — that symbolic changes are used to discharge moral obligations that more substantive policy changes would more concretely address — is a standard analysis of commemorative politics that applies here as in other contexts.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The claim that cities and states are replacing Columbus Day with Indigenous Peoples' Day is not a typical subject for formal Snopes-style fact-checking because it is a verifiable factual trend rather than a contested claim about events or causation. Fact-checking organisations that have touched on Columbus-Day-related claims have typically done so in the context of specific disputed assertions: whether Columbus thought the earth was flat (a different claim addressed elsewhere in Truth Vault), whether he was the first European to reach the Americas (he was not; Norse contact with North America is documented), or whether specific statements made by public figures about Columbus Day policy are accurate.

The claim assessed in this entry falls into a category that fact-checkers sometimes call "contextually complex true claims" — statements that are accurate in their factual content but that carry evaluative or political framing that makes them difficult to use neutrally. The fact-checking task with this type of claim is less about verifying the factual content and more about establishing what the facts mean, which is ultimately a normative rather than empirical question.

Organisations seeking to verify the trend itself can do so through: (1) legislative tracking databases such as those maintained by the National Congress of American Indians (NCAI), which maintains a running list of jurisdictions that have adopted Indigenous Peoples' Day; (2) news database searches on city and state council actions in the relevant period; (3) direct review of the legislative records of specific cities and states for which claims are made; and (4) comparison of state holiday schedules before and after reported changes.

The broader media literacy lesson is that some verifiable trends are nonetheless politically charged in ways that transform accurate factual description into partisan communication. A neutral description of the same trend — "more jurisdictions are designating the second Monday of October as Indigenous Peoples' Day" — is technically equivalent to "more cities are abandoning Columbus Day" in factual content but produces a measurably different reader response based on framing. Recognising when accurate information is being used as political signal, and separating the legitimate factual accuracy of a claim from the political context that gives that fact its communicative function, is among the more demanding challenges in applied media literacy.
