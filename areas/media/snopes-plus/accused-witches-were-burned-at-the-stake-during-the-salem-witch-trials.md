# Accused Witches Were Burned at the Stake During the Salem Witch Trials


## 1. The Claim

Embedded in popular imagination as the defining image of the Salem witch trials of 1692 is the execution by fire: accused witches bound to stakes while flames climb around them, a mob bearing torches, the Puritan theocracy enacting lethal punishment through burning. The claim appears across films, novels, Halloween imagery, colloquial phrases ("burn the witch"), and classrooms. It is among the most widely held historical misconceptions in American cultural memory.

The specific assertion takes several forms. In its strongest version, the claim holds that accused witches were burned alive at the stake in Salem, Massachusetts in 1692. A softer variant alleges that at least some of those executed were burned. A broader variant conflates all early modern witch-trial execution into a single burning-at-the-stake template, treating Salem as merely one instance of a universal practice. All three variants share the same historically false core.

The claim does not exist in isolation. It is scaffolded by genuine historical facts — there were trials, there were executions, there was religious hysteria — which lend the burning detail a plausibility that makes its correction feel surprising or pedantic rather than substantive. The emotional logic is coherent: fire as purification, burning as the appropriate punishment for someone believed to be in league with a sulfurous devil. The detail feels right even though it is wrong.

The tenacity of the burning image also reflects the influence of European witch-hunt practices on American cultural understanding of Salem. Thousands of people, predominantly women, were indeed burned in Germany, France, and Scotland during the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries as condemned heretics and supposed witches. When Americans imagine Salem, they are frequently recalling images generated by that broader European template rather than the specific legal and penal practices of colonial Massachusetts.


## 2. What's Actually True

The historical record of the Salem witch trials is exceptionally well documented by the standards of seventeenth-century American history. Court records, depositions, petitions, letters, and contemporaneous accounts have been preserved and extensively analyzed (Boyer & Nissenbaum, 1974, *Salem Possessed: The Social Origins of Witchcraft*). These records are unambiguous on the method of execution: no one was burned.

Between June and September 1692, twenty people died as a consequence of the Salem proceedings. Nineteen were executed by hanging on Gallows Hill in Salem Town (now Salem, Massachusetts). One man, Giles Corey, was pressed to death by having heavy stones placed on his chest — a consequence of his refusal to enter a plea, which under English common law was the method of compelling a plea (peine forte et dure). Five others, including two infants, died in jail awaiting trial or execution.

The specific executed individuals are named: Bridget Bishop (June 10), Sarah Good, Elizabeth Howe, Susannah Martin, Rebecca Nurse, and Sarah Wildes (July 19), George Burroughs, Martha Carrier, George Jacobs Sr., John Proctor, and John Willard (August 19), Martha Corey, Mary Eastey, Ann Pudeator, Alice Parker, Mary Parker, Wilmot Redd, Margaret Scott, and Samuel Wardwell (September 22). Giles Corey was pressed to death on September 19. Each name is attached to court documentation (Salem Witch Trial Documentary Archive and Transcription Project, University of Virginia).

The legal mechanism also makes burning impossible under the specific jurisdiction applicable in 1692. Salem operated under English common law. In England, burning at the stake was reserved for heresy convictions under ecclesiastical courts, not for witchcraft prosecuted under the common law statutes. England's primary witchcraft legislation — the Witchcraft Act of 1604 (1 Jac. 1 c. 12) — specified hanging as the penalty. Massachusetts Bay Colony, operating under English authority, applied English common law procedure, which meant hanging. The colonists were not simply improvising; they were following codified legal practice (Rosenthal, 1993, *Salem Story: Reading the Witch Trials of 1692*, Cambridge University Press).

Snopes rates the burning claim as False and correctly explains the distinction. Their treatment notes that burning was the primary mode of execution in continental Europe, particularly in regions subject to the Roman and canon law tradition rather than English common law, and that this contrast explains how the conflation developed. Their analysis is accurate and historically grounded.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes treatment, while factually sound, functions as a correction without explanation. It tells us what did not happen and gestures at continental practice as the source of confusion. What it does not provide is an account of *why* this specific error is so durable — why the correction, repeated across decades, has not succeeded in displacing the burning image. This is the epistemically interesting question.

Collective memory research offers a precise framework for understanding this class of error. Jan Vansina's foundational work on oral tradition and James Wertsch's studies of collective memory as mediated action demonstrate that popularly held historical beliefs function less as stored information than as narrative schemas that organize identity and values (Wertsch, 2002, *Voices of Collective Remembering*, Cambridge University Press). The burning image encodes specific moral meanings — persecution, theocratic excess, the danger of religious zealotry, the victimization of women — that remain emotionally and politically salient. A hanging conveys punishment; a burning conveys the purifying violence of totalitarian religiosity. The schema is not chosen because it is accurate; it is chosen because it is semantically richer.

This is a case of what cognitive psychologists call a *schematic intrusion*: the replacement of a veridical memory trace with a semantically consistent but historically false one (Brewer & Treyens, 1981, "Role of Schemata in Memory for Places," *Cognitive Psychology*, 13(2), 207–230). The mental schema for "witch trial" already contains "fire" derived from European templates. When Americans encounter Salem, the schema activates and the fire detail slots in. The correction requires not just providing a true fact but restructuring the underlying schema — a significantly harder cognitive task.

The cultural transmission of the error is also structurally self-reinforcing. Cinema, the primary medium through which mass audiences form historical intuitions, heavily favored burning imagery because it is visually dramatic in ways that hanging is not. The 1937 film *Maid of Salem*, the 1957 *The Crucible* stage adaptation (and its 1996 film), and countless horror films set in the Puritan period defaulted to fire. Each representation reinforced the schema for the next audience. This is a case of mediated false memory operating at the cultural level: the error propagates not through individual forgetting but through the accumulated representational choices of storytellers responding to what audiences already expect to see.

There is a further dimension that neither Snopes nor most standard historical corrections address: the burning misconception is not merely a factual error but an index of a deeper uncertainty in American self-understanding about the nature and extent of Puritan religious violence. The Salem trials are a site of intense national ambivalence — simultaneously evidence of the republic's founding capacity for persecution and a cautionary tale about the dangers of theocracy. The burning image maximizes the horror and thereby maximizes the moral distance between past error and present enlightenment. Correcting the method of execution to hanging threatens to make the story slightly less extreme, which creates unconscious resistance. The correction is not politically neutral.

This resistance phenomenon is documented in what researchers call the "backfire effect," wherein corrections of politically or emotionally charged beliefs can temporarily increase confidence in the error (Nyhan & Reifler, 2010, "When Corrections Fail: The Persistence of Political Misperceptions," *Political Behavior*, 32(2), 303–330). While subsequent research has found the backfire effect to be less universal than initially claimed, it remains more pronounced in domains where the belief serves identity-protective functions — precisely the case with Salem's role in American historical self-conception.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** False  
**Confidence:** Certain  
**Epistemic Basis:** Primary historical record  
**Phenomenological Status:** Persistent false schema  
**Mechanistic Status:** Schematic intrusion + mediated cultural transmission  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** None (pure error in historical content)

No one was burned at the stake in Salem in 1692 or at any other point in the Salem witch trials. The historical record is unambiguous: executions were carried out by hanging, consistent with English common law. Giles Corey died by pressing. Five individuals died in prison. The documentation is extensive and has been continuously available to historians for over three centuries.

The error is nonetheless likely to persist because it serves specific cognitive and cultural functions that the truth does not serve as well. It is semantically richer, more visually dramatic, more consistent with existing schemas about European witch-hunting, and more useful as a moral emblem of theocratic excess. These are real forces maintaining the error's circulation, and they explain why factual correction alone has not been sufficient to displace it.

The appropriate response to this misconception requires not just providing the correct historical record but understanding and addressing the schema structures that generate and sustain it. Public history and science communication research increasingly recognize that schema-level errors require schema-level interventions — not just a different fact, but a restructured narrative that preserves the legitimate emotional content (the horror of false accusation being prosecuted with lethal force) while correcting the specific mechanism.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Salem witch trials of 1692 remain one of the most intensively studied episodes in American colonial history, and the scholarly record surrounding them is both rich and genuinely instructive about how communities, legal systems, and moral panics interact.

**The actual events in detail**: The crisis began in Salem Village (now Danvers, Massachusetts) in January–February 1692 when two girls — Betty Parris, daughter of the local minister Samuel Parris, and her cousin Abigail Williams — began exhibiting strange behaviors including convulsions, contortions, and the reporting of invisible attacks. A physician attributed their symptoms to the "evil hand" of witchcraft. Under interrogation, the girls and their acquaintance Ann Putnam Jr. named three women as responsible: Tituba (an enslaved woman from the Parris household), Sarah Good, and Sarah Osborne (Rosenthal, 1993, *Salem Story*).

Tituba's confession — likely coerced — sparked a widening spiral. She named accomplices and described a Satanic conspiracy, providing interrogators with a template they would repeatedly apply to extract further confessions. The colony had recently been without a proper legal charter following the revocation of the Massachusetts Bay Charter in 1684 and the upheaval of the Glorious Revolution. This political uncertainty contributed to a climate of anxiety that made the conspiracy interpretation credible to authorities (Demos, 1982, *Entertaining Satan: Witchcraft and the Culture of Early New England*, Oxford University Press).

The examinations and trials proceeded through a Special Court of Oyer and Terminer established by Governor Phips in May 1692. The court's acceptance of "spectral evidence" — testimony that the accused appeared as spirits to torment the witnesses — was the legal fulcrum that made mass convictions possible. Specters could not be cross-examined or refuted; the accusation was its own evidence. This epistemological trap meant that denial was itself suspect: only confession or death could end the proceedings.

By the time the court was dissolved in October 1692, following protests from clergy including Increase Mather (whose tract *Cases of Conscience Concerning Evil Spirits* argued against spectral evidence), 150 people had been imprisoned and twenty had died. Governor Phips issued a general reprieve, and the remaining prisoners were gradually released through 1693.

**Social history and the Boyer-Nissenbaum thesis**: Paul Boyer and Stephen Nissenbaum's *Salem Possessed* (1974, Harvard University Press) provided the most influential social-historical analysis of the trials by mapping accusation patterns onto documented property disputes and factional conflicts within Salem Village. Their analysis showed that accusers and accused generally fell along a spatial and economic fault line: accusers tended to come from the western, agricultural, anti-Parris faction while the accused tended to be connected to the eastern, commercially oriented pro-Parris faction and the port town of Salem Town.

Boyer and Nissenbaum argued that the witchcraft accusations were in part a vehicle for expressing pre-existing social tensions about economic modernization, the erosion of traditional community bonds, and resentment of mercantile prosperity. This interpretation has been criticized for overly deterministic mapping and for underweighting gender as a factor, but it remains an influential demonstration of how ostensibly supernatural claims can function as displaced social conflict.

**Gender and the pattern of accusation**: The Salem trials, while ultimately affecting both men and women, disproportionately targeted women — particularly older women who fell outside conventional family roles as wives and mothers. Carol Karlsen's *The Devil in the Shape of a Woman* (1987, Norton) analyzed the gender dynamics systematically, arguing that women who inherited property, who had survived multiple husbands, or who lived outside conventional social arrangements were particularly vulnerable to accusation because they disrupted expected patterns of female subordination.

The gender skew at Salem was less extreme than in European witch trials, where women constituted approximately 75–80% of those executed (Levack, 2006, *The Witch-Hunt in Early Modern Europe*, 3rd ed., Longman, p. 133), but the underlying dynamics had similar features. Accusations also ran along lines of community resentment rather than simply gender: people who were contentious, who had strained relations with neighbors, or who occupied socially marginal positions were at elevated risk.

**Comparison with European practice — where the burning actually happened**: The conflation of Salem with burning derives primarily from the very different legal and theological traditions governing continental European witch prosecution. In territories subject to the Inquisition or to Roman law traditions, heresy and devil-worship were ecclesiastical crimes whose standard penalty was burning. The Spanish and Italian Inquisitions, the French parlements, and German secular and ecclesiastical courts all treated witch-conviction as heresy and applied the heretic's punishment: fire.

The scale of European witch execution dwarfs Salem. Brian Levack's estimate puts European witch-trial executions at approximately 40,000–50,000 over three centuries, with the highest concentrations in the Holy Roman Empire, particularly in the southwest German territories, and in Scotland (Levack, 2006, p. 23). The Würzburg witch trials of 1626–1631 alone produced approximately 300 executions, including children as young as seven. The Bamberg witch trials of 1626–1631 produced approximately 300 further executions. These episodes involved mass burnings that were publicly witnessed and documented in contemporary engravings.

It is these images — documented in Flemish and German graphic art, transmitted into popular historical consciousness through illustrated histories and horror culture — that populate the mental template most people apply to Salem. A 1588 woodcut from Derenburg, Germany shows a mass burning of witches in a single bonfire; such images circulated widely and created the visual grammar of witch-trial representation that American audiences then projected onto the colonial event.

**Legal specificity — why the English hanged rather than burned**: English law distinguished sharply between witchcraft and heresy in ways that determined the method of punishment. Heresy in England was prosecuted by ecclesiastical courts and could result in burning (as in the Marian persecutions of 1553–58, when approximately 280 Protestants were burned under Queen Mary I). Witchcraft, however, was prosecuted under common law statutes as a civil crime — maleficium, the causing of harm through supernatural means — rather than as a theological offense.

The Elizabethan Witchcraft Act of 1563 and the Jacobean Act of 1604 both specified hanging for witchcraft conviction. Matthew Hopkins' witch-hunting campaign in East Anglia during 1645–47, which produced perhaps 200 executions — by far the largest cluster in English history — used hanging throughout, consistent with statutory law. Salem, as a Massachusetts Bay Colony operating under English legal authority, followed this same statutory framework. The legal specificity of hanging was not a colonial aberration; it was compliance with English law.

**The aftermath and exoneration**: The aftermath of the Salem trials is itself historically significant and partially explains the intense ongoing cultural investment in the episode. Samuel Sewall, one of the trial judges, publicly apologized in 1697. Tituba was sold out of the colony; her final fate is unknown. Ann Putnam Jr., one of the primary accusers, made a public apology in 1706 acknowledging that she had "been instrumental with others, though ignorantly and unwittingly" in bringing innocent people to destruction.

Formal legislative exoneration was slow. Massachusetts passed bills attainting the convictions in 1711, restoring some rights to descendant families. A 1957 resolution largely cleared the remaining names, and a 2001 act formally exonerated the last five individuals whose names had not previously appeared in earlier resolutions. The Governor of Massachusetts made a formal apology in 1992, the tercentenary of the trials.

The slowness of this legal remediation — more than three centuries for complete formal exoneration — reflects the ongoing political sensitivity of the episode in Massachusetts civic identity and American historical self-understanding. Salem is not merely a historical fact; it is an ongoing argument about the relationship between religious authority, legal due process, and mass psychology.

**Popular culture and the persistence of the burning image**: The burning image's cultural persistence can be traced through specific influential representations. Arthur Miller's 1953 stage play *The Crucible*, while scrupulously avoiding explicit burning imagery (the play accurately depicts hanging), deploys fire metaphorically throughout the text in ways that have led subsequent readers to unconsciously associate the play with burning. The 1996 film adaptation directed by Nicholas Hytner uses firelight extensively in its atmospheric cinematography, reinforcing the fire-Salem association.

Earlier films were less careful. The 1957 film *Les Sorcières de Salem* (French adaptation starring Yves Montand and Simone Signoret), the 1922 Danish documentary-drama *Häxan* (which influenced subsequent horror conventions), and numerous horror films explicitly depicting Salem-style trials default to fire imagery for its dramatic impact. Halloween culture, which adopted the witch as a central symbolic figure, firmly entrenches the burning-at-stake image as the expected visual representation of witch-trial punishment.

The consequence is a sustained loop of cultural reinforcement: schoolchildren formed on Halloween imagery expect burning; filmmakers meet those expectations; new cohorts of schoolchildren form on those films. The correction of this loop requires not just accurate historical education but active schema disruption that replaces the fire image with something equally vivid and morally resonant — which the accurate image of a gallows on a hill, however historically precise, has not so far managed to achieve.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates the claim as "False" and provides a well-sourced account of the actual execution methods used in Salem. They correctly distinguish between the English common law tradition of hanging and the continental European civil and canon law tradition of burning, identifying this legal-cultural difference as the origin of the conflation. Their treatment cites historian Richard Trask and references the *Salem Witch Trials Documentary Archive*, both legitimate primary and secondary sources.

The Snopes analysis succeeds in providing an accurate correction with appropriate historical sourcing. What it does not address is why the error persists despite widespread availability of the correction, what cognitive and cultural mechanisms sustain false historical schemas against factual debunking, and what the burning image encodes about American moral attitudes toward its own colonial history. These are questions that fall outside conventional fact-checking protocol but are necessary for understanding why this particular misconception is so resistant to correction.

Other fact-checking and reference sources including FactCheck.org, History.com, and Britannica handle the claim similarly — accurately identifying the error and providing the correct historical account — without engaging its persistence as an epistemological or cultural phenomenon. The consensus of factual resources is clear and has been for decades. The misconception persists not because authoritative corrections are unavailable but because the schema-level structures generating it have not been adequately addressed by information provision alone.

The Salem burning myth is a textbook case of why factual correction is necessary but not sufficient for changing deeply embedded historical beliefs. It demonstrates the limitations of an epistemological framework that assumes errors persist due to ignorance and would be resolved by accurate information — a framework that fact-checking inherits from Enlightenment epistemology but that does not adequately account for the emotional, social, and narrative functions that false beliefs serve in collective memory.
