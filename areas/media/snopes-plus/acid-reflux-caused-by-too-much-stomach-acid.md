# Acid Reflux Is Caused by Too Much Stomach Acid


## 1. The Claim

The conventional popular understanding of acid reflux and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) holds that the condition is caused by the stomach producing an excess quantity of hydrochloric acid, and that the appropriate treatment is therefore to reduce or neutralize that acid. This framing is so pervasive that it has become effectively invisible—the name "acid reflux" itself carries the implication that acid is the fundamental problem, and the multi-billion-dollar market for antacids, H2 blockers, and proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) is built on the premise that less acid equals less disease.

The claim appears in its most direct form in over-the-counter antacid marketing, where animated stomach graphics show acid quantities "going down" as the product takes effect, with relief following. Medical advice columns, popular health websites, and social media content advise reducing "acid production" through dietary changes—eliminating coffee, citrus, tomatoes, spicy food, alcohol—on the premise that these foods stimulate excessive acid secretion that then damages the esophagus. Physicians themselves often communicate the condition in exactly these terms, and patients typically internalize the model: too much acid is being made, the esophagus is exposed to it, and the solution is less acid.

Variant forms of the claim extend to dietary theory: that certain foods "increase stomach acid" in harmful ways, that alkaline diets protect against GERD by neutralizing excess acid, that drinking alkaline water corrects the underlying chemical imbalance, and that individuals who frequently seek antacids are constitutionally "high acid producers" with overactive stomach chemistry. The claim intersects with a large wellness industry offering products and protocols premised entirely on acid reduction as the appropriate management strategy.

A more specific and more serious variant—often advanced in functional medicine and alternative health contexts but also documented in certain gastroenterological literature—holds that a substantial proportion of individuals diagnosed with GERD or reflux actually have insufficient stomach acid (hypochlorhydria), and that their symptoms are exacerbated rather than relieved by acid suppression therapy. This variant directly inverts the standard claim, and its relationship to the evidence is more complex and contested than either mainstream dismissal or alternative medicine enthusiasm would suggest.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Anatomy of Reflux**: Reflux—the backward movement of stomach contents into the esophagus—is not primarily a story about acid quantity. It is a story about valve function. The lower esophageal sphincter (LES) is a muscular ring at the junction of the esophagus and stomach that normally maintains a tonic contraction preventing reflux during periods between swallowing. When this sphincter relaxes inappropriately, stomach contents—which are normally acidic at pH 1.5–3.5 due to hydrochloric acid secreted by gastric parietal cells—enter the esophagus, whose mucosa lacks the protective mucus layer that insulates the stomach's own lining. Contact between gastric acid and the unprotected esophageal epithelium causes the burning sensation of heartburn and, with sustained or frequent exposure, the erosive injury and mucosal changes of GERD.

The key physiological fact, well-established in gastroenterological literature since the 1970s, is that LES dysfunction—not excess acid production—is the primary pathogenic mechanism in the majority of GERD cases. Dent and colleagues established through manometric studies that inappropriate transient LES relaxations (TLESRs), episodes in which the sphincter relaxes outside of normal swallowing-triggered relaxation, are the dominant mechanism driving reflux events in GERD patients (Dent et al., 1980, *Gut*). Most patients exhibiting reflux symptoms do not have elevated basal acid secretion rates compared to healthy controls; they have increased frequency of LES relaxation events that expose the esophagus to the normal acid their stomachs produce.

**Gastric Acid Physiology**: Normal human gastric acid secretion is substantial and physiologically essential. The stomach produces hydrochloric acid at concentrations achieving luminal pH of 1.5–3.5 to activate pepsinogen to functional pepsin, to sterilize ingested food against pathogens, to promote mineral absorption (particularly iron and calcium), to denature dietary proteins for enzymatic digestion, and to regulate gastrin signaling in the gut hormone cascade. Gastric acid production is tightly regulated through multiple pathways including the vagal nerve, gastrin secretion from G cells, histamine release from enterochromaffin-like cells, and direct parietal cell proton pump activity. Acid hypersecretion—pathological elevation of acid output—does occur in specific medical conditions, most notably Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, in which gastrin-secreting tumors (gastrinomas) drive excessive parietal cell stimulation. However, Zollinger-Ellison syndrome is rare, with an incidence of roughly 0.1–0.5 per million per year (Roy et al., 2000, *Gastroenterology*), and represents a tiny fraction of the population experiencing GERD or heartburn symptoms.

**The Mechanisms Underlying LES Dysfunction**: What causes LES dysfunction? Multiple factors have been identified. Hiatal hernia, in which a portion of the stomach slides through the diaphragmatic hiatus into the chest cavity, disrupts the normal anatomy supporting LES competence and is strongly associated with GERD (Jones et al., 2001, *The American Journal of Gastroenterology*). Obesity increases intra-abdominal pressure that mechanically encourages reflux; this relationship is well-established and explains the dose-dependent association between body mass index and GERD severity observed in epidemiological studies (Hampel et al., 2005, *Annals of Internal Medicine*). Certain dietary components—high-fat meals, chocolate, caffeine, alcohol, peppermint—have been shown to reduce LES pressure directly, not by increasing acid production. Delayed gastric emptying (gastroparesis) increases the duration and volume of gastric contents available to reflux. Hormonal changes in pregnancy relax smooth muscle including the LES. None of these mechanisms are about acid quantity.

**The Proton Pump Inhibitor Problem**: Proton pump inhibitors—omeprazole (Prilosec), lansoprazole (Prevacid), esomeprazole (Nexium), and related drugs—are among the most widely prescribed medications globally. They work by irreversibly inhibiting the parietal cell proton pump, dramatically reducing gastric acid secretion. They are effective at healing erosive esophagitis and managing symptoms of GERD, which is why they are so widely used. However, their mechanism addresses acid as a cofactor in esophageal injury, not as the root cause of reflux. When PPIs suppress acid, reflux events still occur—but the refluxed material is less acidic and therefore causes less esophageal injury and symptom burden. The valve is still failing; the chemical harmfulness of what passes through the valve has been reduced. This is clinically useful but mechanistically distinct from treating the underlying cause.

Long-term PPI use has been associated with a range of secondary effects that are plausibly linked to reduced gastric acid: impaired absorption of calcium, magnesium, vitamin B12, and iron; altered gut microbiome composition; increased susceptibility to enteric infections by *Clostridium difficile* and other pathogens normally killed by stomach acid; and potential increased risk of gastric cancer with prolonged use in *H. pylori*-positive patients (Lam et al., 2018, *Gut*; Lazarus et al., 2016, *JAMA Internal Medicine*). These associations remain subject to ongoing research and debate but are consistent with a mechanism by which artificially reducing gastric acid disrupts physiological functions that depend on normal acid levels.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The too-much-acid narrative is a case study in what might be called *treatment-derived disease theory*—the tendency to infer the mechanism of a condition from the class of treatment that reliably reduces its symptoms. This epistemological failure is common in medicine and especially well-illustrated by acid reflux.

**The Treatment-Mechanism Conflation**: When antacids reduce heartburn symptoms, patients and clinicians alike psychologically attribute this to correction of an underlying excess. If neutralizing acid makes the discomfort go away, the intuitive inference is that too much acid caused the discomfort. This is logically equivalent to inferring that headaches are caused by deficient ibuprofen, or that depression is caused by serotonin deficiency because SSRIs improve mood. In each case, a symptomatic treatment is mistaken for a mechanistic correction. The acid in reflux is genuinely the proximate cause of esophageal injury—the esophagus is damaged by the acid it contacts—but the reason why acid contacts the esophagus is the failed valve, not the existence of the acid itself. Acid neutralization is symptomatic relief from the consequences of valve dysfunction, not repair of the valve.

**Pharmaceutical Industry Communication**: The framing of GERD and heartburn as acid-quantity problems serves the market structure of acid-suppression pharmacology. Antacids, H2 blockers, and PPIs are among the most profitable pharmaceutical and OTC categories globally; the global GERD drugs market was valued at approximately $4.3 billion in 2023 and continues to grow. Marketing for these products consistently represents the problem as excess acid that needs to be neutralized or suppressed. This is not necessarily a deliberate distortion—acid suppression does provide clinical benefit—but the marketing framework reinforces a mechanistic model that is incomplete and potentially misleading for patient self-understanding.

**The Epistemic Novelty: Hypochlorhydria as an Unacknowledged Reflux Mechanism**: The most significant knowledge gap illuminated by the acid reflux myth has two components, and the second is rarely discussed even in critical health science communication.

The first, already established: GERD is primarily caused by LES dysfunction, not excess acid. This is standard gastroenterology and the dominant model in contemporary medical teaching, even if it has not penetrated popular awareness.

The second is more contested and more epistemically interesting: a significant subset of patients presenting with reflux symptoms may have insufficient gastric acid (hypochlorhydria) rather than normal or excess acid. Jonathan Wright and Lane Lenard advanced this hypothesis in popular medical writing (Wright & Lenard, 2001, *Why Stomach Acid Is Good for You*), drawing on clinical observations and existing gastroenterological research. The physiological mechanism proposed is as follows: insufficient gastric acid impairs gastric emptying, leaving food in the stomach for longer than normal and increasing fermentative activity by gastric bacteria, which generates upward pressure and gas that forces stomach contents through a relatively competent LES. Acid suppression in these patients makes the underlying hypochlorhydria worse while masking the pressure-driven reflux temporarily. The result is potential long-term dependence on increasingly strong acid suppression with worsening underlying pathology.

This is not fringe conjecture. Hypochlorhydria is a documented and well-described medical condition, associated with age (gastric acid production declines with age; Feldman et al., 1996, *Gastroenterology* demonstrated decreased parietal cell mass in elderly subjects), autoimmune gastritis (which destroys parietal cells), *H. pylori* infection (which damages acid-producing cells in chronic infection), and long-term PPI use itself through adaptive hypergastrinemia and parietal cell downregulation (Reimer et al., 2009, *Gastroenterology*). The Reimer study demonstrated that healthy volunteers given PPI therapy for eight weeks developed significantly elevated GERD symptoms after discontinuation—a rebound acid hypersecretion consistent with the adaptive upregulation of acid-producing capacity that occurs when PPIs suppress baseline secretion. This acid rebound may explain why long-term PPI users find it difficult to discontinue medication even when they attempt to do so.

The epistemic situation with hypochlorhydria-as-reflux-cause is not that it has been definitively proven as a common mechanism—randomized evidence for betaine hydrochloride supplementation as a GERD treatment is limited, and the functional medicine community that most aggressively advocates this framework has not consistently produced rigorous clinical trials. The point is that the standard popular model—GERD equals too much acid—is wrong about mechanism in the mainstream-accepted sense (LES dysfunction, not hyperacidity) and potentially wrong in an additional, less-explored way (hypochlorhydria as underappreciated contributor). A genuine reckoning with the evidence leads not to the confident alternative claim that "GERD is caused by too little acid" but to the more accurate and more epistemically humble position that GERD is a heterogeneous syndrome with multiple contributing mechanisms, acid quantity being frequently irrelevant in the most explanatorily fundamental sense.

**The Symptom-Attribution Problem**: Heartburn, regurgitation, and GERD symptoms are also poorly specific. Research has established that a significant proportion of patients presenting with typical GERD symptoms have no evidence of acid reflux on pH monitoring, and conversely that a proportion of patients with documented esophageal acid exposure have minimal or no heartburn symptoms (Fass & Tougas, 2002, *Gut*). At the extreme end of this spectrum, functional heartburn—heartburn symptoms with no pathological reflux or esophageal hypersensitivity on thorough workup—is estimated to account for up to 50% of patients who fail to respond to PPI therapy. In these patients, acid suppression is being given on the basis of a mechanism model that may not apply to their presentation at all.


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: FALSE**

The claim that acid reflux and GERD are caused by excess stomach acid production is false at the level of primary disease mechanism. The dominant established mechanism is lower esophageal sphincter dysfunction—specifically, transient inappropriate relaxations of the valve separating the stomach from the esophagus—which allows normally-produced stomach acid to contact the unprotected esophageal mucosa. Most GERD patients do not have elevated gastric acid secretion compared to healthy controls. Antacids and proton pump inhibitors reduce symptoms by reducing the harmfulness of the material that refluxes, not by correcting the valve dysfunction that allows reflux to occur. The widespread belief that GERD results from too much acid is a treatment-derived mechanism inference—the logical error of reading the cause from the symptom relief mechanism—reinforced by decades of pharmaceutical marketing that emphasizes acid reduction as the primary therapeutic approach.

**Gap Category: Mechanism Inversion**

The myth commits a mechanism inversion: it attributes the condition to excess of the agent causing injury (acid), when the actual cause is the failure of the anatomical structure preventing injury exposure (the LES). A secondary, more contested inversion is possible in a subset of cases, where the treatment (acid suppression) may worsen rather than address an underlying hypochlorhydria contributing to symptoms.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The acid reflux myth illustrates how treatment heuristics—simplified explanations created to justify or communicate treatment choices—calcify into disease models that can misdirect both self-care and clinical attention. In the history of medicine, this pattern recurs across many therapeutic areas. Gastric ulcers were attributed to lifestyle stress and excess acid for decades before Marshall and Warren's demonstration that *Helicobacter pylori* was the actual causative agent in most cases—work that earned the 2005 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (Marshall & Warren, 1984, *The Lancet*). Antacids were the primary treatment for peptic ulcers throughout this period, providing symptomatic relief while doing nothing about the bacterial infection actually causing most ulcers. The structural parallel to the reflux-LES dysfunction story is striking.

**The Dietary Advice Cascade**: If LES dysfunction rather than acid production is the primary mechanism of GERD, the conventional dietary advice to avoid acid-triggering foods requires reexamination. Recommendations to avoid coffee, alcohol, spicy food, and citrus are typically justified by the claim that these foods increase gastric acid production. In fact, the evidence for most of these associations is inconsistent. A systematic review by Kaltenbach and colleagues found that, while dietary fat and alcohol reduced LES pressure in experimental settings (consistent with the valve-dysfunction model), the evidence for coffee and citrus directly worsening GERD in controlled studies was weak and heterogeneous (Kaltenbach et al., 2006, *Archives of Internal Medicine*). Some patients report symptom improvement from dietary modification; whether this reflects acid reduction, LES pressure effects, or other variables including placebo response and dietary fiber changes affecting gastric motility remains uncertain.

The dietary advice around GERD is thus built on a mechanistic model that is understood to be incomplete by gastroenterologists but continues to be communicated in simplified form. This matters for patients trying to manage a condition that in many cases is lifelong and requires ongoing behavioral and medical management. Treatment choices built on inaccurate mechanism models may be suboptimal even when they provide symptomatic relief.

**Rebound Acid Hypersecretion and Iatrogenic Dependence**: The PPI rebound phenomenon documented by Reimer et al. (2009, *Gastroenterology*) deserves particular emphasis in any discussion of acid reflux treatment. Patients prescribed PPIs for initially mild or unclear indications may develop genuine acid hypersecretion as an iatrogenic consequence of drug-induced parietal cell upregulation—and then have genuine difficulty discontinuing therapy because doing so produces real acid-related symptoms that they did not have before starting treatment. This is a case in which the popular model of GERD as too-much-acid becomes, in a pharmaceutical-induced way, temporarily true as a consequence of the treatment justified by the model. The irony is medically significant and under-communicated to patients initiating long-term PPI therapy.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The too-much-stomach-acid claim for GERD is unusual among medical myths in that the correct mechanistic account—LES dysfunction—is the established mainstream medical position, not an alternative or emerging view. Textbook gastroenterology has described the primary role of transient LES relaxations in GERD pathophysiology for decades. Yet the popular understanding, reinforced by pharmaceutical marketing and simplified clinical communication, remains anchored to the excess-acid model. Fact-checkers who have addressed this claim have generally confirmed that the mechanism model is incorrect, though the framing is complicated by the fact that acid reduction therapy does work clinically, even if the mechanism is symptomatic rather than etiological.

The more challenging fact-checking question—whether a subset of GERD patients may have hypochlorhydria rather than normal or elevated acid—exists in territory between established mainstream medicine and contested functional medicine claims. Responsible fact-check treatment of this dimension requires acknowledging that the hypothesis is physiologically coherent, has some supporting clinical evidence, and represents genuine mechanism complexity that the simple "too much acid" narrative erases—without overstating the strength of the evidence base for hypochlorhydria-targeted treatments in the general GERD population.

Snopes has not published a dedicated entry specifically on the too-much-acid claim for GERD. Given the claim's false status as a mechanistic account and its wide propagation through self-care culture and OTC pharmaceutical marketing, it would warrant a False rating with careful contextualization that explains why acid-reduction therapy produces clinical benefit even though excess acid production is not the primary cause of the condition.
