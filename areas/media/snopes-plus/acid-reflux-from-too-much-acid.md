# Acid Reflux Is Caused by Too Much Stomach Acid

## 1. The Claim

The widespread belief is straightforward: acid reflux disease (GERD) occurs because the stomach produces too much hydrochloric acid, which then backs up into the esophagus causing heartburn and damage. This explanation forms the basis for treating reflux with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and H2 receptor antagonists—medications designed to reduce or block stomach acid production. The logic seems unassailable: if acid is burning the esophagus, reduce the acid.

This understanding dominates both medical practice and public perception. Patients experiencing heartburn naturally assume their stomachs are overproducing acid. Physicians routinely prescribe acid-suppressing medications as first-line therapy. The pharmaceutical industry has built a multi-billion dollar market around this premise, with PPIs like omeprazole (Prilosec) and esomeprazole (Nexium) becoming some of the most prescribed medications globally.

The claim extends beyond simple overproduction to include related assumptions: that spicy foods increase acid production causing reflux, that stress directly stimulates excess acid secretion, and that neutralizing stomach acid with antacids addresses the underlying problem rather than merely symptoms.

## 2. What's Actually True

The reality is far more complex and counterintuitive. Most people with acid reflux actually have normal or below-normal stomach acid production. The primary mechanism involves dysfunction of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES)—a muscular valve that should prevent stomach contents from backing up into the esophagus—rather than excessive acid production.

Research by Jonathan Wright and Lane Lenard, detailed in "Why Stomach Acid Is Good For You" (2001), demonstrates that hypochlorhydria (low stomach acid) is more common in reflux patients than hyperchlorhydria (high stomach acid). When stomach acid is insufficient, several problems cascade: incomplete protein digestion leads to fermentation and gas production, creating upward pressure that forces the weakened LES open. Undigested food particles and bacterial overgrowth create additional pressure and inflammation.

The Heidelberg test, which measures actual stomach acid levels using a radio telemetry capsule, reveals that many reflux patients produce inadequate hydrochloric acid. This explains why some patients experience symptom relief with betaine HCl supplementation—the opposite of conventional acid-suppression therapy.

Helicobacter pylori infection, found in roughly half of all humans, contributes to this mechanism. H. pylori can reduce stomach acid production while increasing inflammation, creating conditions where reflux occurs not from too much acid, but from too little acid combined with bacterial overgrowth and delayed gastric emptying.

The lower esophageal sphincter itself requires adequate stomach acidity to maintain proper tone. Insufficient acid can lead to LES relaxation, creating a mechanical pathway for reflux regardless of acid volume. This explains why elderly patients, who naturally produce less stomach acid, often develop reflux symptoms despite decreased acid production.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The "too much acid" explanation feels intuitively correct because it directly correlates cause with effect: acid burns, therefore reduce acid. This linear thinking aligns with how we understand other inflammatory conditions—if something hurts, remove the irritant. Psychologists call this the **representativeness heuristic**: the burning sensation in the chest feels like a problem of "too much fire," so reducing the fire seems like the obvious fix. The symptom-cause alignment is so tidy that questioning it feels almost perverse.

This cognitive shortcut is reinforced by a deeper epistemic pattern: **explanation by analogy with familiar mechanisms.** Most people understand corrosion—rust eating through metal, bleach dissolving fabric—and stomach acid maps neatly onto that model. The esophagus becomes the damaged surface, acid becomes the corrosive agent, and the logical intervention is to reduce the corrosive. What this analogy obscures is that the problem in reflux is not the existence of acid (which is supposed to be there) but rather **where the acid goes** and **why it goes there.** The distinction between "too much acid" and "acid in the wrong place" is subtle but fundamental, and everyday causal reasoning tends to collapse it.

Pharmaceutical marketing has reinforced this belief through decades of advertising emphasizing acid reduction as the solution to reflux. Television commercials showing animated stomachs producing excess acid, with medications swooping in to neutralize the problem, have shaped public understanding. The visual grammar of these advertisements is remarkably consistent: roiling, churning stomach acid depicted in angry reds and oranges, followed by cool blue or purple waves of medication washing the problem away. This color-coded narrative—red danger neutralized by blue relief—bypasses analytical thinking entirely and lodges the excess-acid model at the level of visceral association. AstraZeneca's "purple pill" campaign for Nexium, launched in 2001, spent over $500 million in direct-to-consumer advertising in its first two years alone, making it one of the most heavily marketed drugs in American history. The campaign didn't need to explain gastroenterology; it needed to make patients ask their doctors for a specific brand, and it succeeded spectacularly.

The medical establishment has largely accepted this paradigm because acid-suppressing medications do provide symptomatic relief for many patients, creating the appearance of addressing the root cause. This is a textbook example of **confusing therapeutic efficacy with mechanistic validity.** Aspirin relieves headaches, but that doesn't mean headaches are caused by aspirin deficiency. PPIs reduce the acidity of whatever refluxes into the esophagus, so the burning diminishes—but the reflux itself may continue. Studies using impedance monitoring have shown that many patients on PPIs still experience non-acid reflux events; they simply don't feel them as acutely because the pH of the refluxate is higher. The symptom disappears, the dysfunction persists, and the patient and physician both conclude that the diagnosis was correct.

The success of antacids provides immediate experiential validation. When someone takes Tums or Rolaids and feels better within minutes, it seems to confirm that excess acid was the problem. This immediate feedback loop strengthens the belief despite the temporary nature of the relief. Behavioral psychologists recognize this as **operant conditioning through negative reinforcement**: the unpleasant stimulus (heartburn) is removed by the behavior (taking an antacid), which powerfully reinforces both the behavior and the causal belief underlying it. The speed of relief is critical—antacids work within minutes, which creates a tighter association than slower-acting interventions. By contrast, dietary changes or LES rehabilitation exercises might take weeks to show results, making them feel less "real" as treatments even when they address root causes.

Medical education has traditionally focused on the acid-damage model, with textbooks emphasizing the corrosive effects of gastric acid on esophageal tissue. The discovery of H. pylori's role in peptic ulcers initially seemed to support the acid-centric view, even though later research revealed the bacteria's complex relationship with acid production. There is also a historical dimension: the acid-centric model gained dominance during a period (the 1970s through 1990s) when pharmaceutical research was rapidly producing effective acid-suppressing agents—first H2 blockers like cimetidine (Tagamet), then PPIs like omeprazole. The availability of powerful tools shaped the framing of the disease they were designed to treat, a phenomenon historians of medicine call **therapeutic ontology**: the treatment defines the disease rather than the other way around. When the only tool you have is an acid suppressor, every digestive complaint starts to look like an acid problem.

The complexity of actual reflux mechanisms makes them harder to understand and communicate. Explaining LES dysfunction, bacterial overgrowth, and the paradoxical role of low stomach acid requires more nuanced thinking than the straightforward "acid causes burning" narrative. This is compounded by the **asymmetry of explanation complexity**: the false model can be conveyed in a single sentence ("Your stomach makes too much acid"), while the accurate model requires a paragraph covering sphincter mechanics, intra-abdominal pressure, gastric motility, and microbial ecology. In a seven-minute primary care appointment, the simpler model wins by default. Physicians aren't necessarily ignorant of the nuances—they're operating under time constraints that reward parsimony over precision. The result is that the oversimplified model propagates not because it's believed to be complete, but because it's efficient enough to be actionable.

Finally, the cultural authority of the acid-centric model is self-reinforcing through **social proof.** When virtually everyone you know—family members, coworkers, friends—describes their reflux as "too much acid" and treats it accordingly, questioning the premise feels eccentric. The shared understanding creates a folk medical consensus that is resistant to revision, especially when the people holding it include physicians. Patients who discover the low-acid hypothesis online often find themselves in the uncomfortable position of contradicting their doctors, which most people are reluctant to do. The social dynamics of medical authority thus serve as a secondary reinforcement mechanism, independent of the pharmaceutical marketing and cognitive biases already described.

## 4. Verdict

**Mostly False with Important Caveats**

While stomach acid does cause the burning sensation when it contacts esophageal tissue, the underlying cause of acid reflux is rarely excessive acid production. The majority of reflux cases involve normal or reduced stomach acid levels combined with lower esophageal sphincter dysfunction, bacterial overgrowth, or delayed gastric emptying.

Acid-suppressing medications work by reducing symptoms rather than addressing root causes, which explains why many patients require long-term PPI therapy and may experience symptom return when discontinuing these medications. Some patients even develop rebound hypersecretion when stopping PPIs, creating dependency on continued acid suppression.

However, a small subset of patients does have true hyperchlorhydria, particularly those with Zollinger-Ellison syndrome or other gastrin-secreting conditions. For these individuals, excess acid production is genuinely problematic and requires acid suppression.

The verdict recognizes that while the mechanism of tissue damage (acid contact) is correctly identified, the assumption about causation (excess production) is wrong for most patients. This distinction has profound implications for treatment approaches and long-term management.

## 5. The Wider Picture

This misconception reflects broader patterns in modern medicine's approach to symptom management versus root cause analysis. The pharmaceutical industry's influence on medical education and practice has created treatment paradigms that prioritize symptom suppression over addressing underlying dysfunction. The acid reflux case is particularly instructive because it isn't a story about corrupt science or suppressed evidence—it's a story about how a partially correct model can become so entrenched that its limitations become invisible. Stomach acid genuinely does damage esophageal tissue; the error lies not in that observation but in the leap from "acid causes the damage" to "excess acid causes the disease."

The reflux epidemic parallels other digestive disorders increasing in industrialized populations: irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, and small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO). These conditions share common threads of disrupted digestive function, altered microbiomes, and inflammatory processes that acid suppression may worsen rather than improve. The connections between these conditions are increasingly understood through the lens of the **gut-brain axis** and the emerging science of the human microbiome. Stomach acid is not merely a digestive reagent—it is one of the body's primary antimicrobial defenses, a gatekeeper that determines which microorganisms survive passage into the small and large intestines. Suppressing acid production with PPIs alters the microbial ecology of the entire gastrointestinal tract, with downstream consequences that are only beginning to be mapped. A 2016 study published in *Gut* found that PPI users had significantly altered intestinal microbiomes, with increases in oral bacteria colonizing the gut—organisms that would normally be killed by stomach acid. This microbial translocation has been linked to increased susceptibility to *Clostridioides difficile* infection, a potentially life-threatening condition that disproportionately affects elderly and hospitalized patients.

Long-term PPI use carries significant risks that weren't fully appreciated when these medications gained widespread adoption. Chronic acid suppression impairs mineral absorption (particularly calcium, magnesium, and B12), increases infection risk by reducing the stomach's antimicrobial barrier, and may contribute to dementia, kidney disease, and bone fractures. The FDA has issued multiple safety communications about PPIs since 2010, adding warnings for hypomagnesemia, *C. difficile*-associated diarrhea, bone fracture risk, and acute interstitial nephritis. A large observational study in *JAMA Internal Medicine* (2016) found that PPI use was associated with a 20–50% increased risk of chronic kidney disease. A separate study in *JAMA Neurology* (2016) reported a 44% increased risk of dementia among regular PPI users over age 75. These associations remain debated—observational studies cannot prove causation—but the accumulation of signals across multiple organ systems has prompted calls for more judicious prescribing. The irony is stark: a medication prescribed to manage a condition that is itself largely manageable through dietary and behavioral modification may be creating iatrogenic disease more serious than the reflux it was meant to treat.

The scope of exposure is enormous. As of 2020, PPIs were among the top ten most prescribed drug classes in the United States, with over 15 million Americans using prescription PPIs and millions more purchasing over-the-counter versions. Many of these patients take PPIs for years or decades, often without periodic reassessment of whether continued therapy is necessary. Studies consistently find that 25–70% of PPI prescriptions do not meet guideline-based indications—meaning a large fraction of users may be taking these drugs without clear medical justification. The problem is compounded by **PPI rebound hypersecretion**: when patients attempt to stop PPIs after extended use, the stomach's acid-producing parietal cells, which have been suppressed and have compensatorily upregulated, suddenly produce acid at above-normal levels. This rebound effect creates new symptoms that feel like a return of the original disease, convincing patients (and their physicians) that they "need" the medication. The drug thus creates the physiological conditions for its own continued use—a pharmacological dependency loop that is distinct from addiction but functionally similar in its self-perpetuating nature.

The economic implications are substantial. Americans spend over $10 billion annually on acid-suppressing medications, much of which may be addressing symptoms of a condition caused by insufficient rather than excessive acid. The global market for acid-suppressing drugs exceeded $25 billion in 2023. Alternative approaches focusing on restoring proper digestive function, addressing H. pylori infections appropriately, and supporting LES function could potentially reduce both healthcare costs and long-term complications. These alternatives include dietary modifications (smaller meals, reduced carbohydrate fermentation, avoidance of late-night eating), targeted supplementation with betaine HCl for patients with confirmed hypochlorhydria, weight management to reduce intra-abdominal pressure on the LES, and in some cases, surgical intervention to mechanically reinforce the sphincter (fundoplication). None of these approaches are as simple as swallowing a pill, which is precisely why they struggle to compete in a medical culture oriented toward pharmaceutical solutions.

The reflux story also illuminates a structural problem in how medical research is funded and disseminated. The vast majority of gastroenterology research on reflux has been funded by pharmaceutical companies with a direct commercial interest in acid suppression. This does not mean the research is fraudulent, but it does mean that the questions being asked—and not asked—are shaped by commercial priorities. Studies on the efficacy of PPIs are abundant; studies on the prevalence of hypochlorhydria in reflux populations are comparatively rare. The research agenda itself encodes the assumption that acid suppression is the intervention worth studying, which creates a self-reinforcing literature base. Clinicians who turn to the evidence for guidance find a body of work overwhelmingly oriented toward the paradigm they already hold. This is not conspiracy; it is the predictable result of allowing commercial interests to set the research agenda in a market-driven healthcare system.

This case illustrates how medical paradigms can persist despite contradictory evidence when they align with commercial interests and provide symptomatic relief. The challenge lies in shifting focus from suppressing natural digestive processes to restoring optimal function. More broadly, the acid reflux misconception serves as a case study in what philosopher of science Thomas Kuhn called **paradigm persistence**—the tendency of established frameworks to resist revision even in the face of accumulating anomalies. The anomalies in the acid-excess model are numerous: the prevalence of reflux in elderly patients with naturally declining acid production, the failure of PPIs to resolve reflux in many patients, the phenomenon of non-acid reflux, the association between low-acid states and reflux symptoms. Each of these anomalies has been individually accommodated within the dominant paradigm through ad hoc explanations, rather than prompting a fundamental reassessment of the underlying model. The shift from "too much acid" to "acid in the wrong place due to mechanical and ecological dysfunction" represents exactly the kind of paradigm revision that Kuhn described—not a minor adjustment but a reframing of the entire problem. Whether gastroenterology will undergo this shift in clinical practice, as opposed to merely in the research literature, remains an open question with significant implications for millions of patients worldwide.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Major fact-checking organizations have largely avoided examining this medical claim in depth, possibly because it requires technical expertise and challenges established medical practice. When gastroenterology topics appear in fact-checks, they typically focus on obvious pseudoscience rather than examining nuanced questions about treatment paradigms.

Medical fact-checking tends to defer to consensus guidelines from professional organizations like the American Gastroenterological Association, which continue to recommend acid suppression as first-line therapy despite growing evidence of the low-acid mechanism. This creates a circular validation where fact-checkers cite medical authorities who may themselves be operating under outdated paradigms.

The complexity of digestive physiology makes this topic challenging for generalist fact-checkers to evaluate. Understanding requires familiarity with gastroenterology research, biochemistry, and the ability to distinguish between symptom relief and root cause treatment—concepts that don't translate easily into simple "true" or "false" determinations.

When alternative medicine practitioners claim that reflux is caused by low stomach acid, mainstream fact-checkers often dismiss these assertions as pseudoscience without examining the underlying research. This creates a false dichotomy where any departure from conventional acid-suppression therapy is labeled as quackery, preventing nuanced evaluation of the evidence.

The most accurate fact-checking approach would acknowledge that while acid-suppressing medications effectively treat symptoms for many patients, the underlying assumption about causation (excess acid production) lacks strong evidence for most cases of reflux disease. This nuanced position requires fact-checkers to distinguish between therapeutic effectiveness and mechanistic accuracy—a distinction that challenges binary fact-checking formats.

Future fact-checking of this claim should examine the actual research on stomach acid levels in reflux patients, the physiological role of the lower esophageal sphincter, and the growing body of evidence supporting the low-acid hypothesis while acknowledging that symptom relief doesn't necessarily validate the underlying theory of causation.
