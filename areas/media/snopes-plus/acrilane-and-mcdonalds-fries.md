# Do McDonald's French Fries Contain a 'Cigarette Ingredient' Called 'Acrilane'?

## 1. The Claim

Since at least August 2023, a multi-part warning about McDonald's french fries circulated on Facebook, shared both as copied-and-pasted text posts and as image macros. The most widely circulated version of the warning read as follows:

> "One large McDonald's french fry from McDonald's has the same amount of Acrilane as a pack of cigarettes. Acrilane is the most carcinogenic ingredient in cigarettes. McDonald's french fries are harvested from potatoes that are sprayed with a pesticide that is so harmful to humans that the farmers must let it sit for four days before they can safely handle it. Enjoy your fries!"

The warning makes three distinct empirical claims:

1. A quantitative equivalence: one large order of McDonald's fries contains as much "Acrilane" as a full pack of cigarettes.
2. A chemical characterization: "Acrilane" is the single most carcinogenic ingredient in cigarettes.
3. A pesticide sub-claim: potatoes used in McDonald's fries are treated with a pesticide requiring a four-day no-contact hold before farmers can safely work with them.

The framing positions McDonald's french fries as posing carcinogenic exposure equivalent to heavy cigarette use — a comparison designed to invoke the unambiguous cultural recognition of cigarettes as the paradigm consumer product associated with known, proven, catastrophic cancer risk.

Snopes journalist Jordan Liles investigated the claim in December 2023 and published findings on December 4, 2023, with updates on December 6. McDonald's did not respond to multiple inquiry emails from Snopes media relations. Snopes categorized the claim as misleading — meaning it contains a substrate of real chemical facts but distorts them in ways that are likely to lead audiences to false conclusions.

## 2. What's Actually True

**"Acrilane" does not exist:** The word "acrilane," as used in the warning, does not correspond to any recognized chemical substance in the scientific literature, regulatory databases (FDA, EPA, EFSA, ECHA), or established chemical nomenclature. A Google search for the term in quotation marks returns primarily unrelated results: street addresses, commercial brand names for unrelated products, and occasional web comments noting the chemical's apparent non-existence. As at least one Facebook commenter noted in response to the viral post: "there is no such thing as 'acrilane.'"

The substance the warning almost certainly intended to identify is **acrylamide** (systematic name: prop-2-enamide; CAS number 79-06-1; molecular formula C₃H₅NO). Acrylamide is a real, well-characterized chemical compound with an extensive toxicology literature and documented presence in both high-heat cooked foods and cigarette smoke. The misspelling or deliberate alteration of the compound's name in the warning is a significant evidentiary defect: it suggests the warning originated from a source with limited chemical literacy, was transmitted without verification through social sharing chains, or was specifically designed to avoid easy debunking by confounding searches for the actual substance.

**What acrylamide actually is and how it forms:** Acrylamide is a small organic compound — a vinyl amide — that forms through a natural chemical pathway during high-temperature cooking of certain foods. The mechanism is the **Maillard reaction**: a complex non-enzymatic series of reactions between the amino acid asparagine (present at significant levels in potatoes and cereal grains) and reducing sugars (primarily glucose and fructose) at temperatures above approximately 120°C. Potatoes are among the highest acrylamide-contributing foods in typical Western diets when prepared by frying, roasting, or baking at high temperatures — not because of anything McDonald's adds to its cooking process, but because of the inherent biochemistry of starch-rich vegetables exposed to high heat. Other high-acrylamide foods include coffee (from the roasting process), bread and toast, crackers, and breakfast cereals.

Acrylamide formation in food was identified and publicly reported by Swedish researchers at the National Food Administration in 2002 — specifically by Tareke, Rydberg, Karlsson, Eriksson, and Törnqvist in a landmark analysis published in the *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry* (2002, vol. 50, pp. 4998–5006). Prior to this publication, acrylamide in food was not recognized as a significant dietary exposure route; the 2002 findings prompted immediate international regulatory attention and remain foundational to the literature.

**Acrylamide in cigarette smoke — accurately described:** Acrylamide is detected in cigarette smoke. It forms through pyrolytic chemistry during tobacco combustion — the burning of tobacco and its additives at high temperatures generates acrylamide as one component among hundreds of identified compounds. But the warning's framing that acrylamide is an "ingredient in cigarettes" is taxonomically incorrect. Acrylamide is a **combustion byproduct**, generated during the burning process, not a substance added to cigarettes as a listed ingredient. This is an important distinction: describing acrylamide as an "ingredient" implies deliberate formulation and suggests that tobacco companies consciously added a carcinogen to their products — a claim that does not align with the chemical reality.

Furthermore, the characterization of acrylamide as "the most carcinogenic ingredient in cigarettes" is false by any reasonable assessment of cigarette smoke toxicology. Cigarette smoke contains hundreds of identified toxic and carcinogenic compounds. Among those classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) as Group 1 human carcinogens — meaning definite human carcinogens — are benzo[a]pyrene and other polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), tobacco-specific nitrosamines including N'-nitrosonornicotine (NNN) and 4-(methylnitrosamino)-1-(3-pyridyl)-1-butanone (NNK), benzene, formaldehyde, and arsenic. These substances have stronger and more direct human carcinogenicity evidence than acrylamide. Acrylamide is classified by IARC as Group 2A: "probably carcinogenic to humans" — a category that falls below the Group 1 certainty threshold (IARC Working Group, 2012, *IARC Monographs*, vol. 100F).

**Acrylamide's regulatory status and what it means:** Both the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) have published extensive assessments of acrylamide in food. The FDA's position, as documented on its website: "Acrylamide has been shown to cause cancer in animals exposed to very high doses, and although there is no consistent epidemiological evidence on the effect of acrylamide from food consumption on cancer in humans, both the U.S. National Toxicology Program and the Joint Food and Agriculture Organization/World Health Organization Expert Committee on Food Additives consider acrylamide to be a human health concern." EFSA's 2015 risk assessment concluded that acrylamide "potentially increases the risk of developing cancer for consumers in all age groups," but that "studies on human subjects have provided limited and inconsistent evidence of increased risk of developing cancer" (EFSA, 2015, *EFSA Journal*, vol. 13, no. 6).

This is a case where regulatory language must be read carefully: "limited and inconsistent epidemiological evidence" does not mean "no cancer risk." It means the human data are insufficient to confirm what the animal data strongly suggest. The precautionary concern is genuine; the specific human quantification is uncertain.

**The quantitative pack-of-cigarettes comparison:** No authoritative scientific or regulatory publication supports the specific equivalence claimed in the rumor — that one large order of McDonald's french fries contains the same amount of acrylamide as a pack of cigarettes. FDA surveys of acrylamide in commercial food products (conducted 2002–2004) documented acrylamide in fried potatoes at levels in the range of hundreds of micrograms per kilogram of food, making fried potatoes one of the highest dietary acrylamide sources. Acrylamide in cigarette smoke has been measured at levels that vary widely depending on cigarette type, puff volume, and combustion conditions — typically in the range of single digits to tens of micrograms per cigarette. A direct numerical equivalence between a specific fast-food serving and a specific cigarette count requires assumptions about serving weight, cigarettes per pack, and acrylamide bioavailability that are not standardized in the regulatory literature and are likely not consistent enough to support a single equivalence claim. No study cited in the Snopes investigation, or accessible in standard food safety databases, supports the specific "one large fry = one pack of cigarettes" comparison.

**The pesticide sub-claim:** Potatoes grown for McDonald's have historically been treated with **chlorpropham** (CIPC, isopropyl N-(3-chlorophenyl)carbamate), a sprout-inhibiting chemical applied during post-harvest storage to prevent potatoes from sprouting during the months they are held in cold storage prior to processing. The EPA has established a pesticide re-entry interval (REI) for CIPC applications — the interval after application during which agricultural workers should not re-enter the treated area — but the characterization of this as "so harmful to humans that farmers must let it sit for four days before they can safely handle it" substantially overstates the regulatory narrative around CIPC. Re-entry intervals are a standard, precautionary management tool for many commonly used agricultural chemicals; their existence does not indicate extraordinary human toxicity. CIPC itself carries an EPA classification as a possible human carcinogen (Group C) and is an approved pesticide under EPA's regulatory framework when used according to label directions, including observance of REI.

**California Proposition 65 warning:** McDonald's California locations display Proposition 65 warnings related to acrylamide in fried potato products. Proposition 65 (the Safe Drinking Water and Toxic Enforcement Act of 1986) requires businesses to provide warnings about exposure to chemicals listed as known to cause cancer or reproductive harm. Acrylamide is on the Proposition 65 list based on its IARC Group 2A classification. The existence of this warning is accurate and real; however, Proposition 65 listings are based on regulatory carcinogen classifications rather than demonstrated human harm at the doses in question. The warning signals regulatory status, not established causal harm.

**CDC biomarker data:** Research from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention documents that markers of acrylamide in the blood can be found in approximately 99.9% of the U.S. population — a figure that reflects ubiquitous dietary and environmental acrylamide exposure across the entire population, not exposures specific to McDonald's fry consumption.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The McDonald's fries-as-cigarettes warning is a compound misinformation case with documented cognitive appeal mechanisms.

**The misspelling as an epistemic signal:** The use of "acrilane" rather than "acrylamide" serves paradoxically to insulate the claim from easy verification while simultaneously flagging its problematic provenance for attentive readers. Someone who searches for "acrilane" will find no relevant chemical information — which a low-information audience might interpret as evidence that the information is being suppressed, and which a higher-information audience immediately identifies as evidence that the claimed substance does not exist. The misspelling also means the warning could not have originated with anyone who had read the relevant scientific or regulatory literature, since acrylamide is uniformly and consistently named in all authoritative sources. The chain of transmission from an original (either deliberate or careless) misspelling to viral social sharing reflects the normal epidemiology of social media misinformation, in which content is shared based on emotional resonance rather than fact-checked at each link in the chain.

**The epistemic novelty of the verified-but-distorted true claim structure:** A critical feature that distinguishes the acrylamide rumor from straightforwardly false claims is that its substrate is genuinely accurate. Acrylamide does form in french fries. Acrylamide is found in cigarette smoke. Acrylamide is classified as a probably carcinogenic compound. McDonald's California restaurants do display Proposition 65 warnings about acrylamide. These true elements create what might be called a *true-claim envelope* that contains and shelters the false specific claims (the non-existent word "acrilane," the "most carcinogenic ingredient" assertion, the unsupported quantitative equivalence). When a fact-checker demonstrates that "acrilane" doesn't exist, a skeptical audience member can reasonably respond: "Fine, but acrylamide is real, so they must have meant that, and the broader concern is valid." The false specifics are nested within a valid general concern in a way that makes them difficult to fully dislodge even through accurate correction.

Research on belief perseverance demonstrates precisely this dynamic: when specific false claims are corrected, the broader evaluative conclusion built from a compound belief (including its true elements) often persists (Ross, Lepper, & Hubbard, 1975, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, vol. 32). The acrylamide case thus creates a correction paradox: the false elements can be accurately debunked while leaving the audience — appropriately — with heightened awareness of acrylamide as a documented food-safety regulatory concern, which the viral warning itself, for all its distortions, may have surfaced for first-time audiences.

**Corporate distrust as epistemic prior:** McDonald's is one of the most extensively scrutinized companies in American consumer culture. Decades of documented controversy over nutritional claims, ingredient sourcing, labor practices, and environmental impact have produced a persistent cultural prior among many consumers that McDonald's product ingredients warrant skepticism. This prior functions epistemically to lower the verification threshold for claims that McDonald's products are harmful — claims consistent with an established belief require less evidence than claims contrary to it (Kunda, 1990, *Psychological Bulletin*). The acrylamide warning exploits this lowered threshold by presenting distorted chemical information within a frame of institutionalized corporate skepticism that many consumers have developed from numerous prior accurate critical exposures.

**Dose-response blindness and carcinogen colloquialism:** The use of the word "carcinogenic" in popular discourse has largely lost its dose-response specificity. Regulatory carcinogen classifications are probability statements conditioned on dose, duration, and exposure pathway. "Carcinogenic" in lay usage has collapsed to a binary: carcinogen = causes cancer. This semantic collapse means that any food item identified as containing a "carcinogen" — regardless of dose relative to effect thresholds, regardless of bioavailability differences between dietary and inhalation exposure routes, regardless of the human epidemiological status — is processed by audiences as categorically unsafe. The rigorous finding that acrylamide causes cancer in animals at high doses and that human epidemiological evidence is limited and inconsistent does not translate into a popular summary of "this probably gives you cancer if you eat it regularly." The gap between scientific calibration and popular reception is exploited by the acrylamide warning, which makes specific false quantitative claims that the actual regulatory literature carefully avoids.

## 4. The Evidence in Full

1. **"Acrilane" is not a recognized chemical substance** in any scientific, regulatory, or nomenclature database. The intended substance is almost certainly acrylamide.

2. **Acrylamide forms via the Maillard reaction** in starchy foods at high heat, documented in Tareke et al. (2002, *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*), and is not added to food products.

3. **Acrylamide is a combustion byproduct in cigarette smoke**, not an ingredient. Cigarette smoke contains numerous other IARC Group 1 carcinogens with stronger human carcinogenicity evidence than acrylamide (IARC, 2012, *Monographs* vol. 100F).

4. **IARC Group 2A classification** ("probably carcinogenic to humans") applies to acrylamide, based on sufficient animal evidence and insufficient/inconsistent human epidemiological evidence.

5. **FDA and EFSA** have both published position statements acknowledging acrylamide as a regulatory concern with limited consistent human epidemiological evidence for cancer causation at dietary exposure levels.

6. **No authoritative source supports the pack-of-cigarettes quantitative equivalence** for acrylamide in a large McDonald's fry order.

7. **Chlorpropham (CIPC)** is a real pesticide used in McDonald's potato supply chain as a sprout inhibitor during storage; EPA re-entry intervals apply but characterizing the compound as requiring a dramatic four-day farmer quarantine overstates its regulatory hazard designation.

8. **Proposition 65 warnings** at California McDonald's locations regarding acrylamide are real, reflecting acrylamide's listing under California's broad chemical disclosure law.

9. **CDC biomarker data**: Acrylamide exposure markers detectable in ~99.9% of U.S. population, reflecting ubiquitous dietary and environmental exposure.

10. **McDonald's did not respond** to multiple Snopes media relations inquiries about the specific allegations.

## 5. What Remains Uncertain

**Exact acrylamide levels in current McDonald's product:** Regulatory surveys have established acrylamide ranges in various commercial fried potato products, but specific recent measurements of McDonald's large fries at point of preparation and sale under current cooking procedures are not widely published in peer-reviewed literature. Acrylamide levels vary with frying temperature, oil composition, potato variety, and holding time — parameters that may change over time as McDonald's adjusts its cooking standards in response to regulatory guidance.

**Human cancer risk from dietary acrylamide at realistic exposure levels:** This remains an active area of scientific investigation. Multiple large prospective cohort studies have shown no significant association between dietary acrylamide exposure and most cancer outcomes; some analyses have found possible associations for specific cancers (particularly endometrial and ovarian cancer in women, and kidney cancer in some populations) in some but not all studies. The EFSA 2015 review characterized the human epidemiological evidence as "limited and inconsistent." Whether regular consumption of french fries specifically — as opposed to other high-acrylamide foods — contributes to cancer risk is not established.

**EARC classification:** Classified A (Assessed/Ambiguous). The claim is misleading — built on real chemical phenomena substantially distorted by invented terminology, non-existing quantitative claims, incorrect categorical characterization, and reference to a pesticide concern that is real but overstated. The underlying concern (acrylamide in french fries as a regulatory matter) is legitimate.

## 6. Verdict and Further Reading

**Verdict: MISLEADING.** No element of the specific "acrilane" warning about McDonald's french fries is accurate as stated. "Acrilane" does not exist; the intended substance, acrylamide, is not an "ingredient" in cigarettes but a combustion byproduct also formed in cooked starchy foods; no authoritative source supports a quantitative equivalence to a pack of cigarettes; acrylamide is not "the most carcinogenic ingredient" in cigarette smoke, as cigarettes contain multiple Group 1 IARC carcinogens with stronger human evidence; and the pesticide sub-claim, while resting on real agricultural chemistry, overstates the regulatory hazard framing. The underlying regulatory concern — that acrylamide forms in french fries during high-heat cooking and is considered a potential human health concern by both FDA and EFSA — is real, making this a case of legitimate scientific concern expressed through illegitimate distortion.
