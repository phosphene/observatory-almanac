# "Humans Can't Create New Brain Cells (Neurons) in Adulthood"


## 1. The Claim

The traditional neurobiological doctrine holds that the adult human brain cannot generate new neurons—that neurogenesis, the process of creating new nerve cells, is restricted to embryonic development and early postnatal periods. According to this classical view, humans are born with their full complement of neurons, and the adult brain's capacity for change is limited to synaptic plasticity (strengthening or weakening connections between existing neurons) rather than the creation of new cells.

This dogma dominated neuroscience for most of the 20th century, based on early observations that adult mammalian brains showed no evidence of cell division in neural tissue. The claim suggested that unlike other organs with regenerative capacity—such as the liver, skin, or intestinal lining—the brain was essentially a static organ after maturation, capable of forming new connections but incapable of replacing lost neurons or generating new ones.

The implications of this doctrine were profound for understanding brain function, recovery from injury, learning, memory, and neurodegenerative diseases. If true, it would mean that cognitive decline, stroke damage, or neurodegeneration represented irreversible losses, as the brain could not manufacture replacement cells. This view shaped therapeutic approaches, research priorities, and fundamental concepts about brain plasticity for decades.

However, this claim has been challenged by mounting evidence since the 1960s, with particular controversy erupting around human hippocampal neurogenesis—the generation of new neurons in the hippocampus, a brain region crucial for learning and memory. The debate reached a crescendo in 2018 when two high-profile studies published within weeks of each other reached diametrically opposite conclusions about adult neurogenesis in humans.


## 2. What's Actually True

The original "no new neurons" doctrine was based on early 20th-century studies using basic histological techniques that could detect cell division but lacked the sophisticated markers needed to identify newly born neurons definitively. The absence of evidence was interpreted as evidence of absence, establishing a scientific consensus that persisted for decades despite limited methodological tools.

The first major challenge came from Joseph Altman's work in the 1960s, using radioactive thymidine labeling to track DNA synthesis in rat brains. Altman found clear evidence of new neuron formation in adult rodent hippocampus, but his findings were largely dismissed by the neuroscience establishment. The techniques were considered unreliable, and the implications were deemed too revolutionary to accept without overwhelming proof.

The field shifted dramatically with Peter Eriksson's landmark 1998 study in *Nature Medicine*. Eriksson and colleagues analyzed postmortem brain tissue from terminal cancer patients who had been injected with bromodeoxyuridine (BrdU)—a thymidine analog that incorporates into DNA during cell division—as part of their cancer treatment. Using advanced immunohistochemical techniques, they identified newly generated neurons in the adult human hippocampus, proving definitively that the adult human brain could produce new cells.

This breakthrough was confirmed and extended by Kirsty Spalding's team in 2013, using an ingenious approach based on carbon-14 dating. They exploited the fact that atmospheric nuclear testing in the 1950s-60s created a pulse of carbon-14 that was incorporated into the DNA of cells formed during that period. By analyzing the carbon-14 signature in hippocampal neurons from people who lived through this period, they could determine when the cells were born and confirmed ongoing neurogenesis throughout adult life.

However, the field was thrown into turmoil in March 2018 when Shawn Sorrells and colleagues published a *Nature* study analyzing 59 human brain samples and finding that hippocampal neurogenesis drops precipitously after birth and is virtually undetectable in adult tissue. Using state-of-the-art techniques to identify immature neurons, they found abundant neurogenesis in infant brains but essentially none in adult samples.

Within weeks, Maura Boldrini's team published a contradictory study in *Cell Stem Cell*, analyzing 28 human brains and finding robust neurogenesis throughout adult life, with thousands of new neurons generated in the hippocampus annually. Both groups used rigorous methodology but reached opposite conclusions, creating an unprecedented controversy in modern neuroscience.


The epistemological status of adult human neurogenesis as of 2026 reflects the complexity of studying rare cellular events in human tissue with inherent methodological limitations. The categorical claim that "adults can't create new brain cells" is demonstrably false in the broader sense—adult neurogenesis occurs in multiple brain regions including the olfactory bulb (though humans may be an exception) and potentially the striatum, and the phenomenon is well-established across many mammalian species.

However, the specific question of robust hippocampal neurogenesis in adult humans remains contested due to legitimate methodological disagreements rather than settled science. The controversy reveals how technical detection challenges can create persistent uncertainty even when sophisticated tools are available.

The functional implications of this uncertainty are significant. If adult hippocampal neurogenesis is robust, it could contribute to learning, memory formation, and recovery from depression or brain injury, supporting therapeutic approaches that aim to enhance neurogenesis. If it is minimal or absent, therapeutic focus should shift to other plasticity mechanisms like synaptic modification or neural network reorganization.

Current research increasingly focuses on functional rather than purely anatomical questions: regardless of whether new neurons are generated, what are the mechanisms of adult brain plasticity and how can they be therapeutically enhanced? This pragmatic approach acknowledges the methodological challenges while pursuing clinically relevant outcomes.

The controversy also illustrates how scientific progress sometimes involves moving from one consensus (no neurogenesis) through a period of apparent consensus (yes neurogenesis) to sustained disagreement based on improved methodology revealing the complexity of the original question. This trajectory reflects the sophistication of modern scientific methods that can detect increasingly subtle phenomena while also revealing the limitations of those same methods.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The adult neurogenesis controversy represents a fascinating case study in how methodological differences can generate radically different conclusions from ostensibly similar experiments. The disagreement centers not on data manipulation or experimental misconduct, but on technical approaches, tissue processing methods, and interpretive frameworks that genuine experts can reasonably dispute.

The Sorrells team used fresh-frozen tissue and focused on doublecortin (DCX) staining, a marker for immature neurons. Their approach emphasized the importance of tissue quality and argued that previous positive findings resulted from artifacts in fixed, processed tissue or misidentification of mature cells as immature ones. They maintained that true neurogenesis requires the presence of immature neurons in various developmental stages, which they could not find in adult human samples.

The Boldrini team used perfusion-fixed tissue and a broader panel of neurogenesis markers, including DCX but also other indicators of cell proliferation and neural maturation. They argued that their tissue processing better preserved cellular architecture and that neurogenesis markers can be reliably detected using their methodology. They interpreted the absence of findings in fresh-frozen tissue as a methodological limitation rather than evidence against neurogenesis.

These technical disagreements revealed fundamental epistemological questions about evidence standards in neuroscience. How should researchers interpret negative findings when detection methods are difficult and potentially artifact-prone? What constitutes sufficient evidence for extraordinary claims that challenge existing dogma? How should the field weigh methodological differences when experts disagree about optimal approaches?

The controversy also highlighted species differences that complicate extrapolation from animal models. While adult neurogenesis is well-established in rodents, primates show reduced levels, and humans may represent a further reduction or qualitative difference. This raises questions about whether robust adult neurogenesis is evolutionarily conserved or whether human brain development follows a different trajectory that emphasizes early plasticity over lifelong neuron production.

Subsequent research has attempted to resolve the controversy through improved methodology, larger sample sizes, and alternative approaches. Some studies support continued low-level neurogenesis, while others find minimal or absent adult neuron production. The field remains genuinely divided, with methodological improvements making the disagreements more sophisticated rather than resolving them.


## 4. Verdict

**Augmented Inquiry (A)**: The claim that "humans can't create new brain cells in adulthood" requires substantial qualification and represents an oversimplification of a genuinely complex scientific question with active methodological controversies among legitimate experts.

**Broad Claim Status**: MOSTLY FALSE. The categorical statement that adult brains cannot generate new neurons is contradicted by well-established evidence for adult neurogenesis in multiple brain regions across many species, including compelling evidence in humans from carefully controlled studies.

**Human Hippocampal Neurogenesis**: CONTESTED. The specific question of robust adult hippocampal neurogenesis in humans remains genuinely disputed among experts due to methodological disagreements rather than settled evidence, with high-quality studies reaching opposite conclusions.

**Clinical Implications**: UNRESOLVED. Whether adult neurogenesis, if present, contributes significantly to learning, memory, or therapeutic outcomes remains unclear and depends partly on resolving the basic scientific questions about occurrence and magnitude.

The gap between categorical claims and scientific reality illustrates how simple statements about complex biological phenomena can be misleading even when based on historically dominant scientific views. The neurogenesis controversy demonstrates that modern neuroscience sometimes reveals questions to be more complex rather than simply answering them definitively.

For clinical and educational purposes, the most accurate statement would be that adult brains show remarkable capacity for change through multiple plasticity mechanisms, of which neurogenesis may or may not be a significant component in humans. This uncertainty does not diminish the reality of adult brain plasticity but rather highlights the diverse mechanisms through which it might occur.

The controversy serves as an important reminder that scientific progress sometimes involves moving from false certainty through genuine uncertainty toward more nuanced understanding, and that methodological advances can complicate rather than simply resolve scientific questions. The adult neurogenesis debate remains a active area of investigation with significant implications for understanding brain function and developing therapeutic interventions.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Adult neurogenesis sits within the broader field of neuroplasticity—the brain's capacity for change throughout life. This field has revolutionized neuroscience over the past several decades, moving from static views of brain function to dynamic models emphasizing lifelong adaptability. The neurogenesis controversy represents one specific mechanism within this broader plasticity framework.

Related plasticity mechanisms include synaptic plasticity (changes in connection strength), structural plasticity (changes in dendritic spines and axonal connections), and functional plasticity (recruitment of new brain regions for specific tasks). These mechanisms are well-established and provide alternative explanations for adult brain adaptability even if neurogenesis is minimal.

The field connects to clinical applications in depression treatment, where some antidepressants may work partly by enhancing neurogenesis, and stroke recovery, where understanding plasticity mechanisms could guide rehabilitation strategies. The therapeutic implications create stakes beyond pure scientific curiosity, as treatment approaches might differ depending on which plasticity mechanisms are most important.

The controversy also relates to broader questions about human brain evolution and development. Humans have unusually large brains with extended developmental periods, and it remains unclear whether adult neurogenesis represents a conserved mammalian feature or something that varies substantially across species based on ecological and evolutionary pressures.

Methodological advances continue to refine the tools available for studying neurogenesis, including improved tissue processing, more specific cellular markers, and non-invasive imaging approaches that might eventually allow real-time study of neurogenesis in living humans. Single-cell RNA sequencing and other genomic approaches are providing new windows into cellular identity and developmental state.

The field has also grappled with publication bias and reproducibility issues, as positive findings are often more publishable than negative results, potentially skewing the literature toward overestimating neurogenesis rates. The 2018 controversy prompted discussions about pre-registration, standardized protocols, and collaborative approaches to resolve technical disagreements.

Current trends include focusing on functional neurogenesis (whether new neurons, if present, actually integrate into circuits and affect behavior) rather than purely anatomical detection, examining neurogenesis in disease states and aging, and developing therapeutic approaches that enhance plasticity through multiple mechanisms simultaneously.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not specifically addressed the adult neurogenesis controversy, despite its fundamental implications for neuroscience and public understanding of brain function. This represents a significant gap in science fact-checking, particularly given the field's high-stakes disagreements and the direct relevance to therapeutic claims about brain training, depression treatment, and neurological recovery.

Under Snopes' framework, this claim would likely receive a "Mixed" or "Mostly False" verdict, as the categorical statement that adults "can't create new brain cells" has been convincingly disproven in multiple brain regions across many species, including compelling evidence in humans. However, the specific question of robust adult hippocampal neurogenesis in humans remains genuinely contested, creating complexity that would require careful qualification in any fact-check verdict.

The absence of authoritative fact-checking on this topic highlights challenges in evaluating contested scientific claims where genuine experts disagree based on methodological and interpretive differences rather than clear-cut evidence manipulation or pseudoscience.
