## The Claim

On September 23, 2024, a photograph was shared on the Reddit community r/interesting showing a person dressed in a black hooded sweatshirt and wearing a transparent, pleated or lens-shaped clear mask. The post's caption described the item as "a mask made to block AI based facial recognition from all angles." Within days, the post had accumulated more than 99,000 upvotes on the platform, indicating broad traction among users interested in surveillance technology, privacy, and wearable technology.

The implicit claims embedded in the post's framing were multiple. First, that the mask was real — a manufactured product that exists in three-dimensional physical space, not a rendering, a design sketch, or a digital composite. Second, that it functioned as described — actually preventing AI-based facial recognition systems from identifying the wearer not just from the front but from multiple or all angles. Third, that it was available, or at least had been produced, as a commercially accessible item. The framing invited viewers to understand this as a gadget they might plausibly acquire, a piece of consumer privacy technology that had crossed from theoretical into practical.

None of these three implicit claims is straightforwardly true. And none is straightforwardly false. The photograph, Snopes found, was genuine — it had circulated as far back as 2019, in a Business Insider article on clothing designed to defeat facial recognition software. The mask was real as a design object. But the question of whether it could actually defeat modern AI facial recognition from all angles, and whether it was ever sold commercially, remained unresolved. That combination of confirmed reality and unresolved functionality is precisely what makes this claim a genuine epistemic puzzle.

## What's Actually True

The mask in the photograph was designed by Jip van Leeuwenstein, a product designer who created it while studying at Utrecht School of the Arts in the Netherlands. The project was called "Surveillance Exclusion," and van Leeuwenstein described its purpose on his personal website in explicitly critical terms. The work emerged from a concern about the expanding infrastructure of surveillance — not just government security cameras but the commercial surveillance apparatus, including advertising industry systems that use real-time facial recognition to identify individual shoppers, track their interests, and serve them personalised advertising as they move through physical space.

Van Leeuwenstein's project statement reads as a direct critique of this architecture: "Camera's and other technologies create a safer living environment than ever before. Mega databanks and high resolution cameras stock hundreds of exabytes a year. But who has access to this data? Not only the security department but also the advertisement industry is interested in this technology. They pay to use real time data to their advantage. They create advertisements that call your name, keep records of your personal interests and they follow you everywhere you go."

The mask itself was designed with a specific perceptual logic: by making the lens-shaped transparent structure visible as a mask — by not hiding its presence — it allowed the wearer to maintain human social legibility (facial expressions, eye contact, communication) while distorting the face's geometry in ways the designer believed would confuse facial recognition software. The transparency was intentional — the goal was not to conceal but to distort computationally while remaining humanly face-readable.

Van Leeuwenstein published an image of the mask (or a mock-up) to his Instagram account in July 2017. His work was subsequently featured at the Inter-Society for the Electronic Arts (ISEA) symposium in 2020, an annual gathering of practitioners working at the intersection of art, science, the humanities, and emerging technologies. He was described by ISEA as "a speculative designer questioning today's society" whose work "balances between design and science" and is "designed to make the spectator wonder; how did we arrive at this point?"

The critical phrase there is "speculative designer." Speculative design, as a discipline formally articulated by designers Anthony Dunne and Fiona Raby in their 2013 book *Speculative Everything: Design, Fiction, and Social Dreaming*, is a practice that uses designed objects and scenarios to pose questions about possible futures or critique present conditions — not necessarily to produce functional, market-ready products. Speculative design objects are more often props for thought experiments than items for purchase. When ISEA describes van Leeuwenstein as a speculative designer, it is situating his work within a tradition where the "product" is primarily a provocation.

A Civil Liberties Union for Europe article from 2021 also described the mask as an effort to distort a person's appearance, but did not specify whether the product was publicly available, commercially manufactured, or a one-of-a-kind prototype. Snopes reached out to van Leeuwenstein to ask directly whether the mask existed as a genuine product or was simply a design concept, but had not received a response prior to the article's publication.

The photograph's virality in September 2024 — five years after its original Business Insider placement and seven years after van Leeuwenstein first shared it — reflects the renewed urgency of facial recognition and AI surveillance discourse in the mid-2020s. The claim of "blocking facial recognition from all angles" resonates because the surveillance landscape against which such a mask would be deployed has expanded substantially since 2017.

## Why People Believe This

The 99,000-upvote traction of the Reddit post reflects several converging psychological and cultural currents that make this kind of claim highly shareable and emotionally resonant.

**Surveillance anxiety has gone mainstream.** In the post-Snowden era, public awareness of mass surveillance capabilities shifted from niche activist concern to something approaching mainstream anxiety. The revelation that intelligence agencies operated bulk data collection programs on a previously unimagined scale (Snowden, 2013, various documents published through *The Guardian* and *Der Spiegel*) accelerated a reappraisal of digital privacy. Academic research has since documented what scholars call "chilling effects" — documented behavioural changes in people who believe they are being watched, extending even to searches on culturally sensitive topics (Penney, 2016, *Columbia Law Review*). Awareness that commercial actors, not just government agencies, use facial recognition for tracking and targeting has added a consumer dimension to this anxiety.

**Consumer-facing AI makes the abstract tangible.** Until relatively recently, facial recognition was a technical capability associated with government contexts — border control, law enforcement, military intelligence. Its migration into consumer retail environments, stadium security, and urban advertising infrastructure has made it viscerally immediate. Accounts of retailers deploying systems that silently match shoppers against databases, of photographs scraped from social media being used to train identification models without consent (Hill, 2020, *New York Times*; Garvie et al., 2016, *Georgetown Law Center on Privacy and Technology* report "The Perpetual Line-Up"), have created a felt sense of exposure that earlier surveillance discourse lacked. A mask that promises protection against this specific threat offers something concrete against something newly concrete.

**Wearable counter-surveillance is aesthetically compelling.** Fashion, activism, and technology have a long history of collaboration on counter-surveillance aesthetics. Garments designed to fool thermal cameras, makeup patterns designed to confuse facial recognition algorithms (Adam Harvey's CV Dazzle project, 2010, extensively documented), and infrared-LED-equipped headwear all occupy a distinctive visual genre that is interesting both as functionality and as aesthetic statement. Van Leeuwenstein's mask fits this genre — it looks unusual, unsettling, and interesting. The visual form is the commentary.

**The "from all angles" claim does extra work.** The phrase "from all angles" in the Reddit caption is a claim that goes significantly beyond what the original designer appears to have asserted. Van Leeuwenstein's own description says only that wearing the mask makes it "possible to become unrecognizable for facial recognition software." The viral framing added the "from all angles" qualifier, which is both more impressive and more doubtful. Modern three-dimensional facial recognition systems — used at some airports and in some law enforcement contexts — use multiple camera angles and depth mapping to construct a full facial model. Defeating such systems from all angles simultaneously would require a fundamentally different kind of interference than a transparent lens-mask designed for forward-facing camera contexts.

**Uncertainty is uncomfortable, and the claim filled a gap.** People encountering the photograph wanted the mask to be a product — a real, functional, commercially available counter-surveillance tool. This desire shaped interpretation. The ambiguity in the original design was resolved in the direction of functionality and availability because that resolution served viewer preferences. Understanding that the object is a speculative design provocation — intentionally occupying the uncertain space between product and thought experiment — requires more technical and cultural context than a viral post naturally provides.

---

## Verdict

**The claim is a mixture, and the mixture is more interesting than either a simple true or false.**

The mask is a real material object designed by a real product designer at a real art school, for a real purpose he articulated clearly. It exists in photographs. It was displayed at an international symposium. It is not an AI-generated image, not a Photoshop composite, not a hoax. Van Leeuwenstein genuinely designed it to distort facial recognition.

What remains undetermined is: whether the mask was ever produced beyond the prototype stage; whether a commercially available version was ever manufactured; whether it actually achieves what the viral caption claims, especially the "from all angles" assertion; and whether the specific technology the mask was designed against in 2017 — the systems available then — resembles modern multi-angle deep learning facial recognition closely enough that a design from that era would remain effective against current systems.

Snopes appropriately rated this as "Mixture," with the knowing half being that the designer genuinely created this for the stated counter-surveillance purpose, and the undetermined half being its commercial existence and actual functional efficacy.

## The Wider Picture

Van Leeuwenstein's mask belongs to a broader genre of counter-surveillance design that has expanded substantially since the mid-2010s, driven by the growing ubiquity of facial recognition deployment and the development of accessible high-resolution camera infrastructure.

**The CV Dazzle lineage.** The most prominent prior work in this space is Adam Harvey's CV Dazzle project, begun in 2010, which used high-contrast makeup patterns derived from Dazzle camouflage — a World War I naval camouflage technique — to confuse the Viola-Jones face detection algorithm (Viola & Jones, 2001, *IEEE Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition Conference*). Harvey's research demonstrated that specific geometric disruptions to facial symmetry and contrast patterns could prevent face detection algorithms of that era from identifying the region as a face at all. CV Dazzle received extensive academic and popular coverage and influenced a generation of subsequent counter-surveillance design work.

The important caveat — one that applies equally to van Leeuwenstein's later mask — is that counter-surveillance design approaches are trained against specific algorithmic systems at a specific technological moment. As facial recognition technology evolved from Viola-Jones-style classical computer vision to deep learning convolutional neural networks (Taigman et al., 2014, "DeepFace," *IEEE CVPR*; Schroff et al., 2015, "FaceNet," *IEEE CVPR*), the vulnerabilities that earlier counter-surveillance approaches exploited became less relevant. A mask designed to defeat 2017-era facial recognition may not be effective against 2024-era systems trained on millions of images with robust adversarial augmentation.

**Privacy visors and IR countermeasures.** A parallel technical approach to wearable counter-surveillance involves near-infrared LED lighting, which overwhelms the optical sensors used in many camera systems while remaining invisible to the human eye. Isao Echizen, a professor at the National Institute of Informatics in Tokyo, designed and demonstrated such a "privacy visor" fitted with IR LEDs. This approach targets sensor physics rather than algorithmic pattern recognition and therefore does not become obsolete in the same way as makeup or mask approaches whose effectiveness depends on understanding specific algorithmic weaknesses.

**The speculative design tradition.** As noted above, the tradition of speculative design in which van Leeuwenstein trained treats designed objects as propositions, not solutions. Dunne and Raby (2013) describe speculative design as applying the imagination of design practice to "thinking about possible futures" — using fictional or near-fictional objects to make arguments, pose questions, and provoke critical reflection. The mask, understood in these terms, is an argument about surveillance rather than a product for resisting it. The argument: that surveillance has become pervasive enough that ordinary public existence requires counter-measures; that the human desire to maintain social legibility (to be readable by other humans, to communicate emotionally through facial expression) is in tension with the desire to defeat machine readability; that this tension demands designed resolution.

**Regulatory context.** The function that van Leeuwenstein's mask addresses — commercial and governmental facial recognition in public spaces — has become substantially more legally contested since the mask was designed in 2017. The European Union's AI Act, formally adopted in 2024, classifies real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces as a high-risk AI application subject to strict regulation and largely prohibited for private operators (EU AI Act, 2024, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689). Several US cities have enacted municipal bans on police use of facial recognition. The regulatory landscape is shifting, potentially reducing the functional urgency of anti-recognition masks while not eliminating the design argument they embody.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check on this claim, authored by Madison Dapcevich and published October 1, 2024, demonstrates good practice in handling viral claims where the provable components and the unknowable components require different epistemic treatment.

**Verifying the photograph's authenticity.** The primary verification technique was a Google keyword reverse image search, which surfaced the 2019 Business Insider article as the earliest traceable placement of the image. This approach is methodologically appropriate: origin-tracing for viral images uses search engines, reverse image search tools, and news database searches to establish the earliest provenance of an image and thereby assess whether a new presentation of it is fabricating context.

**Distinguishing the designer's claim from the viral caption.** Dapcevich carefully notes that van Leeuwenstein's own description says only that the mask makes it "possible to become unrecognizable for facial recognition software," whereas the viral Reddit caption claimed the mask blocks recognition "from all angles." This distinction is epistemically important — the more sweeping claim is not supported by the designer's own documentation — and the fact-check rightly flags it.

**Preserving ambiguity regarding commercial availability.** The fact-check explicitly acknowledges that it could not determine whether the mask was produced as a commercial product or was solely a design concept. This is admirable epistemic honesty. A weaker fact-check might have resolved this ambiguity by default in one direction; Dapcevich reports it accurately as undetermined and places it in the "Undetermined" framing within the Mixture rating.

**Contextualising the counter-surveillance design space.** The fact-check briefly surveys related counter-surveillance design work — Polish designer Ewa Nowak's 2019 face jewellery, Isao Echizen's privacy visor — providing readers with a sense that van Leeuwenstein's mask is part of a broader and legitimate design tradition rather than an isolated curio. This contextualisation helps readers understand the claim more completely than the viral post allowed.

The "Mixture" verdict is appropriate and precisely scoped. The mask is real. The designer's intent to defeat facial recognition is real. The "from all angles" claim is unverified and probably overstated. The commercial availability is unverified. Holding these components simultaneously — neither dismissing the object as fake nor accepting the viral framing's full scope — is exactly the kind of qualified, evidence-indexed conclusion that distinguishes useful fact-checking from either credulous reportage or reflexive debunking.
