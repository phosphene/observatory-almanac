# Image Shows Alcohol's Effect on the Brain?

## The Claim

A side-by-side image circulating widely across social media platforms purports to show the devastating neurological consequences of chronic alcohol consumption. On one side sits a darkened, shrunken, irregularly textured mass labelled "drinker's brain." On the other, a pristine, smoothly rendered organ labelled "nondrinker's brain." The visual contrast is stark — designed to be alarming, designed to be shared. And shared it was: a post by the X (formerly Twitter) account @TheBetterPath_ had accumulated approximately 4.2 million views as of November 2024 (Wrona, 2024, Snopes). The image had earlier appeared as a YouTube thumbnail in 2022 for a video titled "How Alcohol Affects The Human Brain," beginning its lifecycle as a piece of viral health content that would eventually reach millions of eyes across multiple platforms and languages.

The implicit argument embedded in the image is simple and visceral: *this is what drinking does to you.* It requires no explanation, no statistical reasoning, no engagement with dose-response curves or confounding variables. The shrunken, discoloured specimen speaks for itself — or so the viewer is meant to believe. The claim leverages a deep human instinct: the authority of the photographic image as documentary evidence. If a photograph shows a damaged brain, and a caption attributes that damage to alcohol, most viewers will accept the pairing without interrogation. The image circulates as a self-contained argument, its persuasive force residing not in the text that accompanies it but in the visceral horror of the visual comparison itself.

But the image is miscaptioned. The "drinker's brain" is not a brain damaged by alcohol. It is not a brain damaged by anything at all. It is, in fact, one of the most remarkably *preserved* biological specimens in the archaeological record — a 2,600-year-old Iron Age brain that survived millennia of burial through an extraordinary molecular process that scientists are still working to fully understand.

## What's Actually True

The fact-check is straightforward. As Snopes investigative journalist Aleksandra Wrona documented in her November 24, 2024, analysis, the image labelled as a "drinker's brain" is a photograph of an ancient preserved brain discovered in 2008 by archaeologists working with the York Archaeological Trust in the United Kingdom (Wrona, 2024, Snopes). The specimen was recovered from a waterlogged pit at Heslington, near York, dating to the Iron Age — roughly the sixth century BCE. The skull in which it was found belonged to an individual who had been decapitated, and the brain tissue had survived inside the cranial vault for approximately 2,600 years, preserved by conditions that remained, at the time of discovery, largely unexplained.

The photograph that went viral was taken from a 2020 peer-reviewed paper published in the *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* by Axel Petzold and colleagues at University College London (UCL) and other institutions. The paper, titled "Protein Aggregate Formation Permits Millennium-Old Brain Preservation," investigated the molecular mechanisms by which this particular brain — and others in the archaeological record — had resisted the normal processes of decomposition (Petzold et al., 2020, *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*). The original image caption in that paper reads: "Careful removal of the sediment uncovers a surface resembling the gyri of a human brain" (Petzold et al., 2020). The caption is descriptive and archaeological. It says nothing about alcohol. It says nothing about damage. It describes an act of careful excavation revealing a surface that, despite two and a half millennia of burial, still retained recognisable anatomical features.

The other half of the viral comparison — the "nondrinker's brain" — is a generic digital model or stock image of a human brain, available on commercial image platforms such as Adobe Stock and Alamy (Wrona, 2024, Snopes). It is not a photograph of any specific individual's brain. It is a rendering — a computer-generated idealisation of cerebral anatomy, the kind of image used in textbook illustrations and pharmaceutical marketing materials. To compare this polished digital artefact with an archaeological photograph and draw conclusions about alcohol neurotoxicity is not merely misleading; it is epistemically incoherent. The two images were never meant to exist in the same frame of reference. One is a real photograph of a real specimen taken for a real scientific investigation. The other is a digital construct produced for commercial licensing.

## What These Two Images Actually Show — and Why That Matters

There is something deeply ironic — one might say philosophically instructive — about the specific misattribution at work in this viral image. The photograph that millions of people interpreted as evidence of biological *destruction* is, in fact, evidence of extraordinary biological *preservation*. The Heslington brain is, by some measures, the best-preserved ancient brain ever discovered in the British Isles. Its survival through 2,600 years of burial represents a biological anomaly that challenged existing models of post-mortem tissue degradation. Where every other soft tissue in the skull had long since decomposed, the brain endured. Petzold and colleagues found that this preservation was made possible by the formation of tightly folded protein aggregates — molecular structures that effectively sealed the tissue against microbial attack and enzymatic breakdown (Petzold et al., 2020, *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*). The proteins had cross-linked and condensed into stable configurations that resisted decomposition across geological timescales.

This is not damage. This is the opposite of damage. This is a tissue that *refused to die* in the conventional biochemical sense — a specimen that retained its macroscopic morphology (the folds and grooves of the cerebral cortex, the gyri and sulci that define human brain anatomy) for longer than most civilisations persist. The dark coloration and irregular texture that viewers interpreted as signs of alcohol-induced deterioration are, in reality, the visual signatures of mineralisation and protein condensation — the very processes that made the specimen's survival possible. The brain looks different from a fresh specimen not because something destroyed it, but because something extraordinary *sustained* it.

The irony compounds when you consider the specific scientific question Petzold et al. were investigating. Their paper asks how biological tissue can persist across millennia in conditions that would ordinarily result in rapid autolysis and putrefaction. The answer they propose involves protein aggregate formation — a process that, in living brains, is associated with neurodegenerative disease. Amyloid plaques in Alzheimer's disease, for instance, are precisely such protein aggregates. Petzold et al. observed that similar aggregation mechanisms, operating post-mortem in specific environmental conditions (anaerobic, waterlogged, chemically stable sediment), could produce structures of remarkable durability (Petzold et al., 2020). The Heslington brain's preservation, in other words, is a window into protein chemistry at its most extreme — a natural experiment in molecular stability that lasted twenty-six centuries.

To take this image and caption it as evidence of alcohol damage is to perform a kind of epistemic alchemy in reverse: transforming a story about resilience into a story about ruin. It strips the image of its genuine scientific significance — which is considerable — and replaces it with a false narrative that, while emotionally effective, has no relationship to the photograph's actual provenance. The viewer who encounters this image learns nothing true about alcohol. But they also learn nothing true about the astonishing archaeology of the Heslington brain, about protein aggregation chemistry, about the conditions under which biological tissue can defeat entropy for millennia. The miscaptioning destroys information in both directions simultaneously.

The "nondrinker's brain," meanwhile, teaches us nothing about neuroscience at all. It is a digital rendering — a smooth, idealised representation that no living brain actually resembles. Real brains are wet, wrinkled, gelatinous, and asymmetrical. They are surrounded by cerebrospinal fluid and encased in meninges. They do not gleam under studio lighting. The stock image presents an aesthetic ideal of neurological health that is itself a kind of fiction — a "brain" that exists only as a collection of polygons and texture maps on a server at Adobe or Alamy. The comparison thus pairs an archaeological artefact misidentified as pathology with a digital fantasy misidentified as health. Neither image tells the truth about what it depicts, and the combination tells a lie about something that didn't happen.

## What Alcohol Actually Does to the Brain: The Real Science

The bitter irony of this entire episode is that the real science of alcohol-related brain damage is robust, well-documented, and genuinely alarming — rendering the use of a fake image entirely unnecessary. Chronic heavy alcohol consumption causes measurable, reproducible, and in many cases severe damage to the human brain through multiple converging pathways. The fabricated visual comparison undermines public understanding of these effects by associating legitimate health concerns with fraudulent evidence, making it easier for sceptics to dismiss the entire topic as exaggeration.

The neurotoxic effects of ethanol on the central nervous system have been extensively characterised in both clinical and preclinical research. Oscar-Berman and Marinković (2007) provided a comprehensive review of alcohol-related brain damage in *Alcohol Research & Health*, documenting the range of structural and functional impairments associated with chronic alcohol use disorder. Their analysis identified damage patterns affecting the frontal lobes, the limbic system, the cerebellum, and several subcortical structures, with consequences including executive function deficits, impaired emotional regulation, degraded motor coordination, and compromised memory consolidation (Oscar-Berman & Marinković, 2007, *Alcohol Research & Health*). These are not hypothetical effects or extrapolations from animal models; they are findings replicated across decades of human neuroimaging studies, neuropsychological assessments, and post-mortem examinations.

Zahr and colleagues (2011), writing in *Dialogues in Clinical Neuroscience*, elaborated on the specific mechanisms by which alcohol exerts its neurotoxic effects. Their review emphasised that alcohol-related brain damage is not a single pathology but a constellation of interacting insults: direct ethanol neurotoxicity, thiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency caused by alcohol's disruption of nutritional absorption, hepatic encephalopathy resulting from alcohol-induced liver failure, and neuroinflammatory cascades triggered by chronic ethanol exposure (Zahr et al., 2011, *Dialogues in Clinical Neuroscience*). Each of these pathways can independently produce significant neurological damage; in combination, they can be devastating. The clinical syndromes that result — Wernicke encephalopathy, Korsakoff syndrome, alcoholic cerebellar degeneration, alcohol-related dementia — represent some of the most severe acquired neurological conditions in medicine.

Structural neuroimaging studies have consistently demonstrated that chronic heavy drinking is associated with reduced total brain volume, enlarged ventricles (indicating loss of brain parenchyma), and disproportionate shrinkage of the prefrontal cortex and the cerebellum (Oscar-Berman & Marinković, 2007; Zahr et al., 2011). White matter integrity is particularly vulnerable: diffusion tensor imaging studies have shown widespread disruption of white matter microstructure in alcohol-dependent individuals, suggesting degradation of the myelinated axon tracts that connect distant brain regions and enable coordinated cognitive function (Pfefferbaum & Sullivan, 2005, *Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research*). These are measurable, quantifiable changes visible on brain scans performed on living individuals — not inferences drawn from centuries-old archaeological specimens.

The National Institute on Alcohol Abuse and Alcoholism (NIAAA), the primary US federal agency for research on alcohol use and its consequences, has synthesised decades of this research into public health guidance. The NIAAA's clinical resources document that even moderate alcohol consumption can have measurable effects on brain structure and function, and that heavy or prolonged consumption is associated with an elevated risk of cognitive decline, dementia, and specific neurological syndromes (NIAAA, 2024). The NIAAA also emphasises that some alcohol-related brain damage is partially reversible with sustained abstinence — a finding that has important implications for treatment and recovery. Neuroimaging studies of individuals in early sobriety have shown measurable recovery of brain volume and white matter integrity over periods of months to years, suggesting that the brain retains significant regenerative capacity even after sustained insult (Bartsch et al., 2007, *Brain*; Cardenas et al., 2007, *Alcoholism: Clinical and Experimental Research*).

More recent research has further refined the picture. A large-scale UK Biobank study by Topiwala and colleagues (2022) examined brain imaging data from over 25,000 participants and found that even moderate alcohol consumption — within levels many people consider safe — was associated with lower grey matter volume, reduced white matter integrity, and evidence of neuroinflammation (Topiwala et al., 2022, *Nature Communications*). The relationship between alcohol intake and brain structure appeared to follow a dose-response curve with no clearly safe threshold, a finding consistent with earlier work suggesting that the neurotoxic effects of ethanol may be continuous rather than exhibiting a sharp threshold between "safe" and "unsafe" levels of consumption.

The science, in short, is clear, convergent, and deeply concerning. Alcohol genuinely damages the brain. The evidence base spans thousands of studies across multiple disciplines, methodologies, and populations. None of this evidence requires — or benefits from — the circulation of a miscaptioned archaeological photograph. The real science is more than sufficient to sustain public health messaging about alcohol's neurological consequences. The fake image doesn't strengthen that message; it weakens it by providing ammunition to those who would dismiss legitimate alcohol research as just another instance of social media misinformation.

## How Miscaptioned Images Compound Into Health Mythology

The alcohol-brain image belongs to a specific genus of misinformation: the miscaptioned scientific photograph. In this pattern, a real image from a legitimate scientific context is extracted from its original setting, stripped of its actual caption and provenance, and paired with a new narrative that has no relationship to the image's true content. The image's apparent authority — its photographic realism, its suggestion of clinical or laboratory origin — is preserved, while its actual meaning is replaced. This is a particularly insidious form of misinformation because it exploits the very trust that scientific photography has earned. The viewer is not looking at a fabricated image; they are looking at a real photograph. The lie resides not in the image but in the caption, and most viewers never interrogate the caption because the image seems to validate it.

This pattern is not unique to the alcohol-brain case. Miscaptioned scientific and medical images circulate widely across social media platforms, covering topics from vaccine safety to cancer treatment to environmental pollution. The mechanism is always the same: a visually compelling image, divorced from its context, is conscripted into service for a narrative it was never meant to support. The resulting misinformation is resilient because the image itself is not fake — it passes reverse image searches, it has a legitimate provenance, and it was published in a reputable venue. The falsity lies in the invisible layer between image and caption, a layer that most social media platforms are poorly equipped to audit.

The compounding effect occurs when miscaptioned images enter the ecosystem of health mythology — the informal, socially transmitted body of beliefs about health, risk, and the body that exists alongside (and sometimes in opposition to) formal medical knowledge. Once a miscaptioned image becomes associated with a health claim, it becomes self-reinforcing: people who already believe the claim accept the image as confirmation, and people encountering the image for the first time accept the claim because the image seems to validate it. The image becomes a citation — a piece of "evidence" that can be invoked in conversations, arguments, and social media debates without any reference to its actual origins. The Petzold et al. (2020) paper, a genuine contribution to our understanding of protein chemistry and archaeological preservation, becomes an unwitting supplier of visual ammunition for a health claim it has nothing to do with.

The damage extends in multiple directions. For the specific scientific claim — that alcohol damages the brain — the miscaptioned image creates a credibility trap. When the misattribution is exposed, as Snopes and others have done, sceptics can point to the debunking as evidence that concerns about alcohol's neurological effects are overblown or fabricated. "If they had to use a fake image," the reasoning goes, "the real evidence must not be very strong." This is, of course, a logical fallacy — the weakness of one piece of evidence says nothing about the strength of the overall evidence base — but it is a psychologically compelling one. The miscaptioned image thus manages to simultaneously exaggerate and undermine the health claim it purports to support.

For the scientific work from which the image was stolen, the damage is different but real. Petzold et al.'s research on protein aggregate formation and archaeological brain preservation is a fascinating contribution to interdisciplinary science, with implications for our understanding of protein chemistry, taphonomy, and the limits of biological preservation. That work deserves public attention on its own merits. Instead, its most widely circulated visual output has been absorbed into a health meme that has nothing to do with its content. The Heslington brain — a specimen of genuine archaeological wonder, one that rewrites assumptions about what biological tissue can endure — is known to millions of people only as "that gross alcohol brain." The real story is both more interesting and more scientifically significant than the false one, but the false one spreads faster because it connects to existing anxieties and requires no background knowledge to process.

The lesson generalises beyond this single case. In an information ecosystem where images move faster than context, where captions are treated as metadata rather than claims requiring verification, and where emotional valence determines sharing behaviour more reliably than accuracy, the miscaptioned scientific image will continue to be a potent vector for misinformation. The defences against it are labour-intensive and culturally unglamorous: provenance checking, reverse image searches, consultation of original sources, and a cultivated habit of asking "where did this image actually come from?" before accepting what it purports to show. These are the epistemic equivalences of hand-washing — simple, effective, and chronically under-practised.

## Verdict

**Miscaptioned.** The viral image comparing a "drinker's brain" to a "nondrinker's brain" is a fabricated juxtaposition. The photograph labelled as showing alcohol damage is actually a 2,600-year-old Iron Age brain, preserved through extraordinary molecular mechanisms, photographed for a 2020 *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* paper on protein aggregate formation (Petzold et al., 2020). The "healthy brain" is a generic digital rendering available on commercial stock image platforms. Neither image has any relationship to alcohol consumption.

The underlying health concern — that chronic alcohol use causes significant, measurable brain damage — is well-supported by decades of rigorous neuroscience research (Oscar-Berman & Marinković, 2007; Zahr et al., 2011; Topiwala et al., 2022; NIAAA, 2024). The real evidence does not need, and is actively harmed by, the circulation of miscaptioned photographs. The image tells a false specific story about a true general phenomenon, and in doing so, weakens public trust in the legitimate science it claims to represent.
