# Some alcoholic beverages contain radioactive isotopes


## 1. The Claim

The claim that alcoholic beverages contain radioactive isotopes circulates in two distinct registers that share a surface form but carry very different implications. In its scientific register the claim is a straightforward empirical observation, taught in university radiochemistry courses and routinely applied in food authentication laboratories: all organic matter derived from living organisms contains measurable quantities of carbon-14, a naturally occurring radioactive isotope of carbon, and essentially all biological material also contains potassium-40, a radioactive isotope that constitutes approximately 0.012 percent of all naturally occurring potassium. Since wine, beer, and spirits are produced from fermented biological material — grapes, grain, sugar cane, agave, barley, and dozens of other agricultural products — they necessarily contain these isotopes.

In its popular register, however, the claim frequently migrates towards alarm. Social media posts, alternative health blogs, and anti-alcohol web content sometimes deploy the radioactivity framing to suggest that drinking is more dangerous than publicly acknowledged, that the nuclear industry has contaminated the global food supply in ways that corporate interests suppress, or that radiation in alcohol explains certain health effects allegedly ignored by mainstream medicine. The framing is occasionally also anti-vaccine or anti-pharmaceutical, positioning radioactive alcohol as an example of how dangerous things are hidden in plain sight.

A third register is purely curiosity-driven and benign: the "fun fact" that wine is radioactive is presented as an interesting piece of science journalism, typically mentioning that archaeologists and oenologists use carbon-14 and other radioisotopes to date ancient wines or detect fraudulent vintages. This version is accurate and carries no misleading implication, but it shares vocabulary with the alarming version and can be cited by the alarming version as a source.

The claim therefore exists on a spectrum from accurate-and-benign to accurate-and-misleading, with the misleading versions rarely lying outright but consistently failing to convey the magnitude comparison that gives the fact its full meaning: the radiation dose from a glass of wine is many orders of magnitude smaller than routine background radiation exposure, far smaller than a transatlantic flight, and not meaningfully different in health terms from eating a banana or breathing indoor air. The claim is true; the implied threat is false.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Carbon-14 in all organic matter.** Carbon-14 is a radioactive isotope of carbon produced continuously in the upper atmosphere when cosmic ray neutrons interact with nitrogen-14 atoms. It enters the global carbon cycle through atmospheric carbon dioxide, which is absorbed by plants during photosynthesis and thereby incorporated into all organic matter. Every living organism on Earth contains carbon-14 at a roughly constant ratio relative to stable carbon-12, maintained at equilibrium through continuous exchange with atmospheric CO₂. At biological death — or, in the case of fermented products, at grain harvest or grape picking — the organism ceases to incorporate new carbon-14, and the existing C-14 decays at the known rate characterised by its half-life of approximately 5,730 years (Libby, W.F., Anderson, E.C., and Arnold, J.R., 1949, *Science*). This is the basis of radiocarbon dating.

Because alcoholic beverages are produced from recently harvested biological material, they contain contemporary carbon-14 at the current atmospheric equilibrium level. Stuiver and Polach (1977, *Radiocarbon*) established the international reference standard for carbon-14 activity in modern samples (often expressed in units of percent modern carbon, or pMC), and contemporary wines typically register close to 100 pMC — essentially the natural background level dictated by cosmic ray production. There is no sense in which this represents contamination or an elevated risk; it is the baseline state of all recently living matter.

**Potassium-40 as a second isotope.** Potassium-40 is a primordial radioactive isotope that has existed since before the formation of the solar system. It constitutes a fixed fraction of all natural potassium (approximately 0.0117 percent by abundance, as reviewed in Chen et al., 2011, *Journal of Radioanalytical and Nuclear Chemistry*). Since potassium is an essential macronutrient and is present in virtually all living organisms, it is present in virtually all food and drink, including alcoholic beverages. Beer, wine, and especially potassium-rich fermented beverages contain potassium-40 in concentrations that vary with the potassium content of the source material.

The human body itself contains approximately 140 grams of potassium, of which approximately 0.017 grams is potassium-40, making the human body naturally radioactive at a specific activity of approximately 4,400 becquerels (Bq) from potassium-40 alone (National Council on Radiation Protection and Measurements, NCRP Report No. 93, 1987). A glass of wine containing perhaps 200 mg of potassium contributes a negligible incremental fraction of potassium-40 to this background.

**Caesium-137 and Strontium-90 as measurable but trace contaminants.** Following atmospheric nuclear weapons testing in the 1950s and 1960s, and following the 1986 Chernobyl disaster, wines produced during and after these events contained measurable traces of caesium-137, strontium-90, and tritium above pre-nuclear-era background levels. Multiple studies have documented this. Crecelius et al. (1975, *Analytical Chemistry*) documented weapon-test fallout isotopes in California wines. More recently, Hubert et al. (2009, *Journal of Radioanalytical and Nuclear Chemistry*) measured caesium-137 in French wines from different vintages, finding detectable but extremely low concentrations that correlate with the Chernobyl fallout period. Steinhauser et al. (2014, *Science of the Total Environment*) examined post-Chernobyl contamination in Austrian and Bavarian beers.

The critical quantitative point is that even these weapons-testing- and Chernobyl-era vintages, which showed elevated isotopic signatures above natural background, remained many orders of magnitude below any threshold associated with health risk. The European Union's regulatory limit for caesium-137 in food is 600 Bq/kg for general foodstuffs (European Commission Regulation 2016/52). Maximum levels detected in wines closest in time to the Chernobyl event were in the range of a few Bq/L — well below regulatory limits and far below natural potassium-40 activity in the same beverages.

**The radioisotope wine authentication application.** The most practically consequential implication of radioactive isotopes in wine is entirely beneficial: the detection of nuclear-era isotopes allows the authentication of wine vintages. A wine claimed to be from 1920 but containing caesium-137 — which did not exist in the environment before 1945 — is demonstrably fraudulent. Crespy et al. (2019, *Food Control*) and Parisod et al. (2018, *Food Chemistry*) both describe the use of isotopic fingerprinting, including C-14, Sr-87/86, and Pb isotopes, as forensic tools against wine fraud. This application represents an elegant scientific inversion: the same property that the alarming version of the claim presents as sinister is deployed in the service of consumer protection.


## 3. Why People Believe This (And What Epistemics It Exploits)

The claim that alcoholic beverages contain radioactive isotopes is epistemically unusual because it is entirely true as a factual proposition — far more so than many viral claims — and yet its popular circulation consistently misrepresents its significance. Understanding why requires attention to a specific cognitive architecture that the phrasing activates.

**The magnitude problem in lay radiological reasoning.** Radiation is a domain in which lay intuitions about magnitude are systematically miscalibrated. Sociotechnical studies of risk perception — particularly Slovic's canonical work on the psychometric paradigm of risk (Slovic, 1987, *Science*) and subsequent research — have consistently found that nuclear and radiological hazards are perceived as uniquely frightening relative to their actuarial risk, a finding explained by factors including dread, lack of personal control, involuntary exposure, and the invisibility of the hazard. When people hear that something is radioactive, they categorically assign it to the "extremely dangerous" bin, without performing any magnitude comparison. The word "radioactive" activates the schema of nuclear accidents and cancer, not the schema of cosmic-ray-generated carbon isotopes in every cell of every living organism.

This creates a systematic exploitability in claims of the form "X contains radioactive Y." The statement can be made with complete technical accuracy about virtually any food, any body of water, any soil sample, or any living organism — because natural radioactivity is universal — while the listener hears something that sounds like a revelation about hidden danger. The claim is not lying; it is exploiting a mapping failure between the scientific denotation of "radioactive" (emitting ionising radiation through nuclear decay, at any rate whatsoever) and the lay connotation (dangerously so).

**The asymmetric precision artifact.** A related mechanism is what might be termed the asymmetric precision artifact: claims of hidden danger are made with the impressive precision of science ("contains 4.2 Bq/L of carbon-14"), while the comparative context that would allow evaluation ("background radiation contributes approximately 2,400 μSv per year of which this represents 0.0003 μSv") is either absent or buried. Precision without a comparator creates the impression of a significant finding. Bad-faith actors in alternative health content specifically exploit this: they cite real measurements from peer-reviewed radiochemistry papers to justify claims of hidden risk, selectively omitting the dose-response context that the same papers always include.

**The contamination-by-association logic.** Posts framing alcohol's radioactivity as news of hidden corporate harm often invoke the Chernobyl fallout data, genuinely reported in peer-reviewed literature, to imply ongoing, suppressed contamination. The rhetorical move is to cite the real scientific literature on Chernobyl-era wine contamination (which did occur, was publicly researched, and was always far below health-relevant thresholds) as though it demonstrates ongoing suppression of evidence of harm. It does the opposite: the existence of published peer-reviewed literature on post-Chernobyl wine isotopes is evidence that this was measured, publicised, and found to be below concern thresholds. The claim that it was suppressed is directly falsified by the papers it cites.

**The epistemic novelty.** The genuinely novel observation about this claim is not that it is false (it is true) but that it exemplifies a category of misinformation that does not require a false premise: the harm comes entirely from omitting the quantitative context that gives the true fact its meaning. This is structurally different from the standard misinformation model of false versus true claims, and it is more resistant to standard fact-checking approaches that operate by identifying factual errors. The claim passes a fact-check on its literal proposition; the misleading inference is produced by the listener, not by any stated falsehood. Combating this category of claim requires communicating magnitude — specifically, the dose-response relationship and the comparison to other natural exposures — rather than simply verifying or denying the core fact.


## 4. Verdict

**True but systematically misleading in its popular framing.**

The factual core is unambiguous: alcoholic beverages do contain radioactive isotopes. This is not a hypothesis, not a contested finding, and not something the food industry or the nuclear industry have any interest in concealing. It is a direct consequence of the fundamental radiochemistry of all organic matter derived from living organisms. Carbon-14 and potassium-40 are present in wine, beer, and spirits at exactly the concentrations one would predict from first principles, and their presence has been measured, published, and applied in beneficial authentication science for decades.

The implicit threat carried by most popular treatments of this claim is not supported by the evidence. No study has demonstrated a detectable health effect attributable specifically to the radioactive isotope content of alcoholic beverages at typical consumption levels. The radiation exposure from alcohol, in so far as it can be estimated, is swamped by background radiation exposure from other sources, including the natural radioactivity of the human body itself.

The well-documented health risks of alcohol consumption — including the association between alcohol and multiple cancer types, liver disease, neurological effects, and cardiovascular events — are real and serious. They operate through chemical pathways involving ethanol and its metabolite acetaldehyde, not through radiation. Framing the radiation content of alcohol as a health concern is doubly misleading: it exaggerates a non-risk while directing attention away from the actual and well-established risks of alcohol consumption.

**EARC classification: A (Accurate with critical context required).** The central claim is factually accurate. The typical popular framing omits the quantitative context — specifically magnitude comparison — that gives the fact its actual significance, converting an accurate observation about natural radiochemistry into a misleading implication of hidden health risk.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The radioactivity of food and drink is a topic that sits at the intersection of two genuinely important public knowledge gaps: the poor calibration of lay radiation risk intuitions, and the pervasive misuse of accurate scientific data to generate misleading popular narratives. The same mechanism exploited in the alcohol-radioactivity claim appears in coverage of banana radioactivity (true and harmless), Brazil nut radioactivity (true and harmless, though Brazil nuts are the food with the highest natural radioactivity for reasons related to radium uptake by their root systems), and in more harmful contexts such as misrepresentations of low-dose radiation from diagnostic medical imaging.

The scientific literature on radioactivity in food and drink is genuinely extensive and respectable. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) publishes technical reports on naturally occurring radioactive materials (NORM) in food. National radiation protection bodies — including the United States Nuclear Regulatory Commission, the UK's Health Security Agency, and the European Food Safety Authority — have all assessed the contribution of food and drink to natural background radiation exposure and published population-level dose estimates that uniformly place the contribution of specific foods, including alcohol, within the range of minor contributors to unavoidable natural background exposure.

The Chernobyl fallout dimension of this topic intersects with an important area of genuine public interest: the documented spread of caesium-137 and other isotopes across European agricultural products in 1986 and in subsequent years. This is a legitimate historical and public health story that has been extensively and transparently documented. Peer-reviewed studies by European food safety agencies and academic radiochemistry groups have traced isotopic contamination patterns across wine, grain, milk, and other products, finding elevated (but well below-threshold) levels in products from the most heavily affected regions. This work is the opposite of suppression — it is a scientific community performing its routine function of measuring, publishing, and contextualising environmental contamination data.

The persistence of the claim in alternative media partly reflects a broader pattern of distrust in food safety institutions. Where that distrust is directed at real and documented harms of alcohol — its extensive catalogue of dose-dependent health effects through chemical toxicology is among the most thoroughly documented in medicine — the distrust has legitimate objects. Channelling it toward radiation is a misdirection that leaves the real risks underweighted.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not addressed this specific claim as of July 2026. The claim's structure — factually true but contextually misleading — falls into a category that standard professional fact-checking tools handle poorly. Binary verdict systems (true/false/mixture) are not well suited to claims whose problem is not falsity but omission of quantitative context; a verdict of "true" without elaboration leaves the misleading inference intact.

Science-focused fact-checkers and science communication institutions have addressed the general issue of food radioactivity in educational contexts. The American Nuclear Society has published public-facing material on naturally occurring radioactive materials in food. The UK's Centre for Radiation, Chemical and Environmental Hazards (UK-CRCE) has published dietary radiation dose estimates. These resources are typically framed as educational rather than as fact-checks of specific viral claims, because the underlying science is not controversial.

The most effective intervention against this family of claims is dose comparison communication — specifically, the technique of expressing radiation from specific food sources in comparative units against well-known reference exposures (background radiation per year, one dental X-ray, the radiation difference between living at sea level versus high altitude) and against the threshold doses at which epidemiologically measurable health effects begin to appear. This communication approach was developed partly in response to public misunderstanding of post-Fukushima food contamination data and has been evaluated in studies of science communication effectiveness (Fischhoff, B. and Davis, A.L., 2014, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*).

Where this specific claim intersects with anti-alcohol advocacy — which is itself based on substantially better-evidenced health concerns — the most constructive approach is to acknowledge the accurate kernel, provide the missing magnitude context, and redirect to the actual evidence base for alcohol's health effects, which does not require misrepresenting radiochemistry to be compelling.
