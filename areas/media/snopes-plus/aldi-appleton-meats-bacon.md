## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In March 2024, a copied-and-pasted Facebook rumor spread widely claiming that Aldi, the German-founded discount supermarket chain, was selling bacon produced not from conventionally raised pigs but from cells cultivated in laboratory conditions. The rumor specifically named "Appleton Meats" as the brand involved, claiming that company was developing cell-based meat products including ground beef, chicken, and mouse-meat cat treats, and had ambitions to enter the Canadian and then American market by 2022–2024.

Snopes Senior Reporter Jordan Liles (2024) rated the claim **False**. The rumor conflated two entirely separate entities: Aldi sells deli products—including bacon—under the **Appleton Farms** store brand, while **Appleton Meats** is (or was) a small, early-stage Canadian cell-based agriculture startup that had no commercial products available, no retail partnerships, and no association with Aldi. The photograph embedded in many versions of the rumor in fact showed a package clearly labeled "Appleton Farms"—visible evidence that directly contradicted the claim the posts were making.

This entry examines the precise nature of the brand-confusion mechanism, the status of Appleton Meats as a company, the regulatory and commercial landscape for cell-based meat in the United States and Canada, and the broader cultural context of supermarket-focused food misinformation in the early 2020s.

## §2 Appleton Farms vs. Appleton Meats: The Brand Confusion Examined

The critical analytical distinction in this claim is between two entities whose names differ by only one word:

**Appleton Farms** is Aldi's store brand for deli meats and related products in the United States. As listed on Aldi's website, the brand covers bacon, ham, prosciutto, salami, and other conventionally produced meat products. Appleton Farms is a private-label branding program operated under contract manufacturing arrangements common to the discount grocery model; as with most Aldi store brands, public disclosure of the specific manufacturers who produce Appleton Farms products is limited. The brand is conventional in all legal and regulatory senses: the products are derived from animals raised through standard livestock agriculture practices, processed at USDA-inspected facilities, and labeled according to federal food labeling requirements.

**Appleton Meats** was a different entity entirely: a small, privately funded Canadian startup that described itself as working on cell-based (cultivated) meat through cellular agriculture methods. According to contemporaneous documentation archived by cell-based technology tracking website cellbasedtech.com (accessed by Liles, 2024), Appleton Meats was in the "R&D phase" focused on developing ground beef for hamburger patties. The company's website—last successfully archived by the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine in October 2019—described its mission as producing "meat without animals" by "harnessing the power of cellular biology." The company was listed on both Crunchbase and other startup tracking services as apparently inactive or closed, with no funding rounds, commercial partnerships, or product launches documented after 2019.

The company profile cited in the viral Facebook posts included claims about Appleton Meats' product development roadmap (ground beef, chicken, mouse-meat cat treats) and its planned commercial timeline (launching viable products by 2022–2024 in Canada first, then expanding to the United States). These details appear to derive from early company communications or third-party profiles, not from any current commercial activity.

The identification of the original source of the rumor—specifically, the person identified as "Rebbeca Rogowski" credited in many viral copies of the post—is not addressed in the Snopes fact-check. This credit suggests a specific individual authored the original viral text, but whether that authorship was intentionally deceptive or the result of genuine confusion is unknown. The structural mechanism of the misinformation does not require intent to deceive: a user who genuinely confused the brand names could write the post in good faith, and the network amplification process would take over.

The photograph included in many versions of the viral post—showing a package of Appleton Farms bacon—is the most striking evidentiary detail. This photograph, which was likely added by an early sharer either to illustrate the (confused) claim or as a product image, showed clearly visible "Appleton Farms" branding. Rather than disconfirming the claim for most readers who viewed it, the photograph appears to have been taken by many as visual confirmation: the image showed bacon from a store, which was read as corroborating the claim. This represents a documented pattern in visual misinformation processing, wherein readers attend to the presence of an image rather than its specific content when that content would require active attention to contradict a pre-formed expectation (Ecker et al., 2010, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, Springer).

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: How Near-Identical Brand Names Become Misinformation Vectors

The unusual epistemic feature of this case is the mechanism by which brand similarity—two names differing by a single common noun—creates a misinformation vector that is structurally different from either straightforward fabrication or deliberate deception.

Brand confusion misinformation exploits what cognitive psychologists call *lexical similarity effects*: the tendency for near-phonological or near-orthographic similarity to create false memory associations (Gallo, 2006, *Associative Illusions of Memory: False Memory Research in DRM and Related Tasks*, Psychology Press). When a consumer sees "Appleton Farms" on a package in a store, the name is processed as a recognizable token associated with Aldi's store brand. When the same consumer subsequently encounters a social media post about "Appleton Meats," the lexical proximity activates the stored association, making the connection seem plausible without requiring any logical inference that the two entities are the same.

This mechanism is particularly effective in the context of cell-based meat, because cultivated meat products represent a domain of genuine uncertainty and public concern. The technology is real—companies including UPSIDE Foods, Good Meat, and others have received USDA regulatory approvals for cultivated chicken products in the United States (USDA, FSIS approval, June 2023)—but the regulatory pathway for commercial cultivated meat is complex, and consumer awareness of which products are actually available in retail markets at any given moment is limited. This uncertainty creates what Sunstein and Vermeule (2009, *Journal of Political Philosophy*, Wiley) describe as a "conspiracy-receptive" information environment: audiences lack firm factual anchors that would allow them to immediately reject implausible claims as impossible.

The temporal detail embedded in the rumor (Appleton Meats planned to launch products "by 2022 to 2024") exploited this uncertainty window: by March 2024, readers who knew nothing about the actual fate of Appleton Meats would have no particular reason to doubt that a cell-based meat startup had successfully launched commercial products within an announced 2022–2024 window. The rumor made a plausible operational claim about a hypothetical product at an implausible retail location (a discount grocery chain rather than a specialty health food or innovator retail context).

The deeper epistemic novelty is the relationship between cultivated meat's real-world regulatory and commercial uncertainty and the misinformation environment it generates. The technology is at the frontier of food science, with a growing peer-reviewed literature on bioreactor optimization (Post, 2012, *Meat Science*, Elsevier; Datar & Betti, 2010, *Renewable Agriculture and Food Systems*, Cambridge University Press), yet public understanding of its actual regulatory status, commercial availability, and production costs is limited. This creates an epistemic gap that misinformation readily fills, particularly when the false claims engage pre-existing anxieties about food adulteration, corporate secrecy, and covert changes to food supply without consumer consent.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Liles' (2024) fact-check correctly identifies the core brand confusion and appropriately rates the claim false. Several additional analytical threads merit development:

**The fate of Appleton Meats**: The Snopes article notes that the company appears to have closed, based on Crunchbase and Wayback Machine evidence. However, a more complete account would identify when and why the company ceased operations, whether it completed any funded research, and whether its intellectual property or personnel moved to other ventures. Early-stage cell-based meat startups have had widely varying fates: many closed between 2018 and 2023 due to funding difficulties, technical challenges, or strategic pivots (Good Food Institute market surveys, 2022).

**The Aldi brand structure**: Aldi's private-label strategy involves separating products into national "Aldi Finds" specials and ongoing ALDI-exclusive brands. The Appleton Farms brand history, contract manufacturing relationships, and supplier geography are not addressed in the fact-check; this context would be valuable for readers interested in how Aldi's procurement model differs from conventional supermarkets.

**The "Rebbeca Rogowski" credit**: The attribution line in viral posts credit a named individual. The Snopes article does not investigate whether this person exists, how the original post was written, or whether the name is a pseudonym or genuine. Identifying the origin point of viral misinformation claims is methodologically important for understanding diffusion patterns.

**US regulatory status for cultivated meat at time of publication**: As of March 2024, UPSIDE Foods and Good Meat had both received USDA-FSIS approval to sell cultivated chicken in the United States (June 2023), making the general category of "cell-based meat at supermarkets" no longer entirely implausible. However, no cell-based pork or bacon products were in commercial distribution, and neither approved company had entered discount grocery retail at that time. This regulatory context is not provided in the fact-check.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Cultivated Meat Anxieties, Discount Retail, and Supermarket Misinformation

The Aldi-specific targeting of this rumor is not arbitrary. Aldi has been the subject of repeated supermarket misinformation claims across multiple years, suggesting that the brand carries specific properties that make it a persistent target. As Liles (2024) notes, Aldi was previously falsely accused of selling horse meat (2013) and of removing crosses from hot cross buns (2019). These disparate claims share a structural signature: Aldi's private-label model, which does not prominently disclose manufacturers, creates a transparency gap that misinformation can exploit. When a supermarket does not disclose who makes its store-brand products, rumors about what those products contain are harder to immediately refute than they would be for branded products with publicly documented supply chains.

Discount grocery retail also occupies a specific cultural position in the United States and Europe: it is associated with budget-conscious consumers, large purchase volumes, and in some framings, lower quality or less vetting of products. This association creates a plausibility surface for claims that Aldi might be cutting corners—or more sinisterly, substituting unconventional ingredients—in ways that premium retailers would not. This is factually unjustified (Aldi products must meet the same regulatory standards as any other food retailer) but culturally available as an interpretive frame.

The cell-based meat dimension of the rumor intersects with a significant 2023–2024 political controversy in the United States. Multiple state legislatures introduced or passed bills restricting the labeling or sale of cultivated meat, with Florida Governor Ron DeSantis signing a bill in May 2024 that criminalized the sale or offering of cultivated meat in Florida (S.B. 1084, Florida Legislature, 2024). These legislative actions were accompanied by explicit messaging framing cultivated meat as a threat to traditional food systems, rural communities, and consumer autonomy. The political salience of "lab-grown meat" during this period means the Aldi rumor was released into an information environment highly primed to receive and amplify claims about covert cell-based meat distribution.

The broader cultivated meat literature provides essential context. Post (2012, *Meat Science*, Elsevier) published the first scientific description of in-vitro muscle tissue production for food consumption, establishing the technical basis for the industry. Tuomisto and Teixeira de Mattos (2011, *Environmental Science & Technology*, American Chemical Society) modelled the potential environmental footprint of cultured meat production, suggesting substantially reduced land use, water consumption, and greenhouse gas emissions compared to conventional livestock—a finding that has been partially contested by subsequent lifecycle analyses (Lynch & Pierrehumbert, 2019, *Frontiers in Sustainable Food Systems*, Frontiers). Good et al. (2021, *Nature Food*, Springer Nature) documented the investment landscape and commercialization challenges facing the cultivated meat sector, noting that production costs had fallen dramatically but remained substantially above conventional meat prices for most products.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: FALSE**

Aldi does not sell Appleton Meats bacon or any product from Appleton Meats. Aldi sells deli products—including bacon—under the **Appleton Farms** store brand, which is entirely conventional in origin, produced from standard livestock agriculture. Appleton Meats was a separate Canadian cell-based meat startup that had no commercial products, no association with Aldi, and appears to have ceased operations before March 2024. The photograph circulated with the rumor shows a clearly labeled "Appleton Farms" package, directly contradicting the text claim.

**EARC Classification: A (Anthropological — consumer brand association and cultivated meat anxiety)**

The analytical gap is anthropological: understanding why this claim propagated virally requires understanding both the cognitive mechanism of brand-name confusability and the cultural anxiety landscape surrounding cultivated meat technology, discount grocery retail transparency, and covert food system change. The brand confusion mechanism is the proximate cause; the cultural anxiety is the enabling condition that made the confusion viral rather than quickly corrected.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Complete corporate history and shutdown circumstances of Appleton Meats, Inc.
- Manufacturing relationships behind Aldi's Appleton Farms store brand
- Identity and intent of "Rebbeca Rogowski" credited in the original viral posts
- Current regulatory and commercial status of cell-based pork products globally
