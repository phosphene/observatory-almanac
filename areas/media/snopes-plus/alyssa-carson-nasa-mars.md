# "Alyssa Carson Is the Youngest NASA Astronaut Training for Mars"


## 1. The Claim

The claim holds that Alyssa Carson—a young American known publicly as "NASA Blueberry"—is the youngest astronaut training with NASA, has been selected or designated for a future crewed Mars mission, and represents a formally credentialed relationship between herself and the space agency. Variants exist on a spectrum: softer versions describe her as "NASA's youngest Mars candidate" or "the teenager NASA is training to go to Mars," while more dramatic versions assert she has already been selected for a specific Mars mission, perhaps targeted to launch around 2033–2040, or even that she has signed a contract for a one-way mission to Mars that precludes her from marrying.

The claim has been reported or reproduced across a wide range of media outlets—from clickbait aggregators to mainstream news organizations—and has been translated and spread internationally, particularly in South Asia, Southeast Asia, and Africa, where it achieved viral status with framing emphasizing Carson's youth and the idea that a teenager or young woman had been specifically chosen to be the first human to walk on Mars. Her social media handle "NASABlueberry" and her extensive NASA-branded educational activities create a visual and textual ecosystem that makes the claim plausible to casual readers.

The claim sits at the intersection of several appealing narratives: the gifted child prodigy destined for historic achievement; the young woman breaking gender and age barriers in a traditionally male-dominated field; the specific, concrete face of an otherwise abstract future mission to Mars; and the heartwarming story of single-minded childhood ambition finding institutional support. Each of these narrative frames is compelling, and together they created conditions in which exaggerated or false claims about Carson's status could circulate with minimal skepticism.

What makes this case particularly interesting from a media epistemology perspective is that Carson herself is a real person with genuine achievements, genuine training activities, and a genuine aspiration to be among the first humans to travel to Mars. The distortion is not the invention of a fictional figure but the institutional inflation of a real one—the assignment of credentialing and formal affiliation that Carson has not received, to a person who has done remarkable things within an entirely different (and notably non-governmental) framework.


## 2. What's Actually True

The core biographical facts about Alyssa Carson are well-established and genuinely impressive.

Born on March 10, 2001, in Baton Rouge, Louisiana, Carson has aspired to travel to Mars since the age of three, when she watched an episode of the children's cartoon *The Backyardigans* featuring a Mars mission. She has pursued this aspiration with remarkable systematic commitment for more than two decades. She completed the NASA Passport Program—visiting all 14 NASA visitor centers across the United States—and is the only person to have done so. She attended space camps in the United States, Canada, and Turkey, becoming the first person to complete all three international NASA-affiliated space camp programs. At approximately age 15, she became the youngest person accepted into and to graduate from the Advanced PoSSUM (Project Polar Suborbital Science in the Upper Mesosphere) Space Academy, a federally licensed citizen science astronautics program that certifies participants for suborbital research missions (Project PoSSUM, 2019, *About PoSSUM*). She received certification for suborbital research through PoSSUM, and she has completed water survival training, G-force training, microgravity flights, scuba certification, and decompression training. She is the youngest full member of The Explorers Club, a distinguished scientific and exploration organization. She held a pilot's license by age 18. She graduated in 2023 with a Bachelor of Science in astrobiology from the Florida Institute of Technology and is currently pursuing a Ph.D. in biology at the University of Arkansas, with research focused on bacterial survival under Mars-like conditions.

These are each factual achievements, several of which do involve records for youngest completion. Carson is also a legitimate public speaker on space exploration and Mars ambition, and her advocacy for space exploration has been recognized by the United Nations, which named her as a Youth Ambassador for the International Astronautical Congress.

What is not true is the formal NASA relationship the claim implies.

**Carson is not a NASA astronaut.** NASA's astronaut corps is a formal federal employment category governed by the Office of Personnel Management. Astronaut candidates (ASCANs) are selected through a competitive process announced publicly by NASA—typically every few years—and applicants must meet strict requirements including advanced STEM degrees, professional experience, and medical qualifications. NASA has publicly stated, in response to circulating claims, that Carson is not affiliated with the agency and is not enrolled in any NASA astronaut training program (PolitiFact, 2018, *No, NASA is not preparing a 17-year-old to become the first person on Mars*; AFP Fact Check, 2020, *US teen space enthusiast has not been chosen for one-way mission to Mars*).

**She has not been selected for any Mars mission.** NASA's crewed Mars program is in the technology development phase. As of 2026, no crew has been named for any crewed Mars mission—not a crew of highly experienced NASA astronauts with decades of professional spaceflight experience, let alone a candidate outside the NASA astronaut corps. The earliest plausible timeline for a crewed Mars mission is in the 2030s and involves enormous unresolved technical, political, and logistical challenges.

**The one-way mission and marriage prohibition claim is false.** This variant of the claim—that Carson has signed a contract for a one-way Mars mission and therefore cannot marry—apparently derives from misrepresentation of statements Carson made in media interviews about the personal sacrifices she was willing to make for Mars exploration. No space agency has any plans for a one-way crewed Mars mission; NASA's long-term planning involves return missions (AFP Fact Check, 2020).

**The Mars One connection is real but misunderstood.** Carson was at one point listed as an ambassador for Mars One, the Dutch nonprofit that claimed to be planning a one-way Mars colony mission. Mars One was widely criticized by space scientists as technically implausible and financially precarious; it declared bankruptcy in 2019. Carson's Mars One ambassadorship, whatever it signified, was not an astronaut selection and was associated with a project that no longer exists.


## 3. The Epistemological Gap

The direct fact-checking verdict on the headline claim is unambiguous: Carson is not a NASA astronaut, has not been selected for a Mars mission, and holds no formal NASA affiliation. This is not a case requiring interpretive nuance about what "astronaut" or "training" means—NASA has clarified its position explicitly.

But the epistemological gap this claim exposes is not primarily about the fact—it is about the **media construction process** through which a real person's genuine accomplishments become institutional inflation, and what that process reveals about how aspirational public science figures are created, maintained, and distorted.

The gap is this: standard fact-checking addresses the output (false claim) but rarely interrogates the production system (how media creates "NASA's youngest Mars astronaut" out of "person who attended space camp and got a PoSSUM certification"). Understanding this production system matters because it operates not only on Carson but on a wider ecosystem of science communication, and the distortions it generates have real consequences beyond misinformation: they shape public understanding of what NASA does, how astronaut selection works, what kinds of credentials actually qualify someone for spaceflight, and—perhaps most importantly—they construct a narrative of space exploration as something driven by singular gifted individuals rather than by systems, institutions, teams, and accumulated technical knowledge.

**The credentialing conflation**: The PoSSUM Academy, which Carson attended, is a legitimate organization that provides genuine training and certification for suborbital research missions. But "certified for suborbital research missions through Project PoSSUM" is a significantly different credential than "NASA astronaut trainee." Media coverage routinely elided this distinction, treating Carson's PoSSUM certification as evidence of NASA-level astronaut training because the conceptual category of "astronaut training" does not, in popular usage, distinguish between university research programs, private certification programs, government selection processes, and the structured corps training that actual NASA astronaut candidates undergo (Launius, R.D., 2010, *Heroes in a Vacuum: The Apollo Astronaut as Cultural Icon*, *The Florida Historical Quarterly*, 87(2), 174–209).

**The "youngest" heuristic as attention architecture**: The repeated emphasis on Carson's youth—youngest to complete the NASA Passport Program, youngest Advanced PoSSUM graduate, youngest full Explorers Club member—creates a rhetorical architecture that signals precocious world-record-setting achievement. Each "youngest" tag strengthens the impression that she is on a formal institutional track toward a defined goal. But "youngest to visit all NASA visitor centers" is an informal personal achievement requiring neither credential nor institutional recognition; "youngest Advanced PoSSUM graduate" is a legitimate milestone in a private training program unaffiliated with NASA's astronaut selection process. The steady accumulation of "youngest" tags produces an impression of institutional trajectory that the individual accomplishments do not, individually, support (Van Dijck, J., 2013, *The Culture of Connectivity: A Critical History of Social Media*, Oxford University Press).

**The epistemic novelty**: It matters to distinguish between two questions that get conflated in most coverage. First: Is Carson genuinely training for a future Mars mission? Loosely, yes—she is pursuing education and training activities that she regards as preparation for future Mars exploration, and her astrobiology PhD research on bacterial survival in Mars-like conditions is directly relevant to future crewed Mars mission planning. Second: Has NASA or any space agency formally selected her for a Mars mission? No, and emphatically not. The first question has a qualified-yes answer; the second has a flat-no answer. Most media coverage has treated these questions as the same question, producing a claim that is simultaneously flattering to Carson's aspirations and completely inaccurate about her institutional status.


## 4. Why People Believe This

Viral claims about Alyssa Carson spread through a set of converging epistemic and psychological mechanisms that illuminate how science communication and celebrity media culture interact.

**The precocious achiever archetype**: Human cognition has strong pattern recognition for the overachieving young person whose destiny is exceptional. Stories of child prodigies, teenage geniuses, and youngest-ever achievers activate a distinct cognitive processing mode that bypasses skeptical scrutiny in favor of narrative satisfaction (Simonton, D.K., 1994, *Greatness: Who Makes History and Why*, Guilford Press). Once the "gifted young woman destined for Mars" frame is established, incoming information is filtered through it: space camp attendance becomes "NASA training," PoSSUM certification becomes "astronaut training," and ambition becomes selection.

**The "NASABlueberry" handle**: Carson uses the social media handle @NASABlueberry, which she received as a nickname at space camp. This handle creates a direct textual association with NASA in every post, reply, and media reference to her account. For audiences with limited background knowledge of NASA's institutional structure, NASABlueberry communicates affiliation—she is "Blueberry from NASA"—rather than nickname origin. The semiotic weight of the NASA brand, attached to a young woman expressing Mars ambitions, does significant implicit communication work before any explicit claim is made.

**Single-point aspiration as narrative simplification**: The idea that one specific identifiable person—a named, photographed, interviewed young woman—represents the human face of a future Mars mission provides enormous narrative value that the reality of distributed technical teams and institutional complexity cannot. Space journalism has long relied on the "astronaut as lone hero" frame to translate scientific complexity into human drama (Launius, 2010, *The Florida Historical Quarterly*). Carson fit this frame almost perfectly: young, telegenic, articulate, committed since childhood, capable of giving interviews that explain her Mars dream in emotionally resonant terms. The media system was structured to amplify exactly such a figure.

**Global amplification through aspirational STEM media**: The claim found especially receptive audiences in global media contexts where the idea of a young person from an ordinary background (Carson is from Louisiana, not from an elite scientific family) being chosen to do something historically unprecedented carries strong aspirational valence. In countries where access to STEM education and professional opportunities is severely unequal, the story of a teenager selected by NASA to go to Mars functions as an inspirational narrative about meritocracy, self-determination, and the power of individual dream against institutional constraint (Castells, M., 2009, *Communication Power*, Oxford University Press). The factual accuracy of the selection claim is less cognitively relevant than its inspirational utility.

**The institutional press release ecosystem**: Coverage of Carson frequently originated from press releases issued by organizations like Project PoSSUM or various space entrepreneurship platforms, written in language designed to maximize impact—"youngest person ever to be certified for suborbital research," "aspiring Mars astronaut"—that readers and republishers translated into stronger claims than the underlying facts supported. The press release ecosystem incentivizes superlative framing; the viral media ecosystem incentivizes the amplification of superlatives (Bourdieu, P., 1998, *On Television*, New Press). Both incentive structures point in the same direction: more extreme claims about Carson's status spread farther.

**Carson's own communications**: Carson herself is clearly aware of the gap between her current status and her aspirations, and in careful interviews distinguishes between training she is doing in pursuit of her goal and formal NASA selection that has not occurred. However, her own language in promotional contexts—appearing at events as an "aspiring astronaut," speaking about "preparing for Mars"—exists in a promotional register where precision about credential distinctions can be sacrificed for communicative clarity. The aspirant speaks about their goal in goal-language; audiences hear that goal-language as present-tense status-language. This is a generic communication dynamic that compounds the distortion rather than originating it.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Alyssa Carson case sits within a broader landscape of issues in science communication, celebrity culture, and the public understanding of space exploration that extends well beyond any individual claim.

**How NASA Astronauts Are Actually Selected**: NASA's astronaut selection process is highly formalized and comparatively rare. Astronaut candidate classes have been selected in 1959, 1962, 1963, 1965, 1966, 1967, 1969, 1978, 1980, 1984, 1986, 1987, 1990, 1992, 1995, 1996, 1998, 2000, 2004, 2009, 2013, 2017, 2021, and 2024 (NASA, *Astronaut Candidate Selection*, nasa.gov). For the 2017 selection, NASA received 18,300 applications—a record—and selected 12 candidates (NASA, 2017, *NASA Selects Astronaut Candidates*, Press Release). Requirements for the most recent class included a master's degree in a STEM field, at least three years of relevant professional experience (or 1,000 hours of pilot-in-command time in jet aircraft), and passing a NASA long-duration flight astronaut physical. The training pipeline following selection involves approximately two years before candidates become astronauts, followed by additional mission-specific training. This process is entirely separate from space camp completion, PoSSUM certification, or any other private training pathway.

The point is not to diminish the value of Carson's achievements in private training programs—PoSSUM Academy is a legitimate organization conducting real citizen science—but to establish the institutional gulf between certifications from private programs and formal NASA astronaut candidacy. A person selected by NASA for its astronaut corps in 2024 had in many cases spent a decade or more as a military test pilot, medical doctor, or research scientist with publications in peer-reviewed journals. The track to NASA astronaut candidacy runs through professional distinction, not through being the youngest person to visit all NASA visitor centers.

**The History of "Mars Volunteers" and Selection Claims**: Mars exploration advocacy has a history of conflating aspirational communities with formal mission selection. The Mars One project, which claimed to be selecting a crew for a one-way Mars colony mission, became perhaps the most prominent example: it recruited more than 200,000 applicants and built a media and fundraising ecosystem around a publicly named "shortlist" of candidates (Zubrin, R., & Wagner, R., 1996, *The Case for Mars*, Free Press—note: Zubrin was a proponent of crewed Mars missions, not of Mars One, which he criticized). Mars One's scientific implausibility was documented by multiple engineering analyses that found fundamental flaws in its life support, launch, and communication plans (MacWilliams, S., et al., 2014, *Feasibility Analysis of the Mars One Mission Plan*, MIT arXiv preprint). The company declared bankruptcy in 2019. Carson's former role as a Mars One ambassador—which she held briefly—has been retrospectively folded into the broader mythology of her formal selection status, even though Mars One itself was never a legitimate mission selection process.

**The Role of Space Entrepreneurship in Blurring Credentials**: The proliferation of commercial space programs, citizen science astronautics, and space entrepreneurship ventures in the past two decades has created significant conceptual complexity around what "astronaut" means. Virgin Galactic and Blue Origin have each flown paying passengers to the edge of space and awarded them "astronaut wings" under FAA conventions that define 50 miles altitude as the threshold for the title. SpaceX's Inspiration4 mission flew a crew of non-professional astronauts on a multi-day orbital mission in 2021. These developments—while genuine and technically impressive—have expanded the operational definition of "astronaut" in ways that contribute to public confusion about the distinction between NASA-selected astronauts and other categories of people with space-related training or experience (Launius, R.D., & McCurdy, H.E., 2008, *Robots in Space: Technology, Evolution, and Interplanetary Travel*, Johns Hopkins University Press).

**Science Communication and the Construction of Young Heroes**: Science communication has long relied on exemplary individual figures to bridge the gap between abstract science and public engagement—from Marie Curie to Carl Sagan to Neil deGrasse Tyson. The construction of young, aspirational figures—particularly young women and people of color—has gained particular traction as science communication organizations seek to address demographic representation gaps in STEM fields. Carson's story has been explicitly marshaled in this direction; she has been featured in materials designed to inspire young girls to consider careers in STEM. This use is entirely legitimate, and Carson's own advocacy in this domain appears genuine and valuable.

However, the inspirational function places pressure on factual accuracy. A narrative of "teenager selected by NASA for Mars history" is inspirationally more potent than "young woman who attended space camps and is pursuing a relevant PhD." The pressure to maximize inspirational impact creates incentives to compress the distance between aspiration and credential, and the resulting distortion—however well-intentioned at origin—produces misinformation at scale. The irony is that the distortion may ultimately be counterproductive: when young women who aspire to follow in Carson's footsteps investigate how NASA actually selects astronauts and find no evidence of any youth-based selection program, the inspirational narrative may collapse into disillusionment rather than motivation (Archer, L., et al., 2010, *"Doing" Science Versus "Being" a Scientist: Examining 10/11-Year-Old Schoolchildren's Constructions of Science Through the Lens of Identity*, *Science Education*, 94(4), 617–639).

**Carson's Actual Contribution to Mars Research**: Setting aside the inflated media representation, Carson's academic pivot to astrobiology—and specifically to research on bacterial survival under conditions relevant to Mars habitability—represents a genuine contribution to the scientific infrastructure of Mars exploration. Her doctoral work at the University of Arkansas on extremophile bacteria in Mars-analog environments addresses fundamental questions about whether microbial life could survive on Mars and what biosignatures a crewed mission should look for (Carson, A., 2024, *Ph.D. Research Focus*, University of Arkansas, determined.uark.edu). This is not glamorous in the way the "youngest person selected for Mars" framing is, but it is science that directly matters to the Mars mission planning that NASA is undertaking. A more accurate and arguably more inspiring story about Carson would emphasize this contribution rather than a non-existent astronaut selection.

**What a Mars Mission Actually Requires**: Context about NASA's actual crewed Mars planning illuminates the gap between the claim and reality. NASA's Moon to Mars strategy positions the Lunar Gateway and Artemis lunar missions as stepping stones toward eventually crewed Mars flights. Technical challenges under development include deep-space propulsion, radiation shielding for the multi-year mission duration (transit plus surface operations), closed-loop life support for the seven-to-nine-month trip each way, and descent and ascent systems for the Martian atmosphere (NASA, 2023, *Moon to Mars Architecture*, nasa.gov). The crew of any eventual Mars mission will need to include personnel with collectively sufficient expertise in systems engineering, medicine, geology, biology, and piloting—likely requiring many years of NASA astronaut experience including multiple prior missions before a Mars assignment. No currently living person has been named as a Mars crew candidate by any government space agency as of 2026.

**The Broader Misinformation Ecosystem**: The Carson case is part of a larger phenomenon in which science-adjacent viral claims exploit the aspirational framing of space exploration to achieve wide circulation. "NASA announces [X]" is one of the most reliable engagement-generating story formats in science journalism—partly because NASA is a publicly trusted institution, partly because space exploration carries genuine wonder that is relatively easy to evoke, and partly because the complexity of NASA's actual work provides ample raw material for simplification into compelling but distorted summaries. Media literacy efforts in STEM communication increasingly address this pattern, recognizing that the intersection of scientific celebrity, institutional branding, and aspirational narrative creates systematic pressures toward overclaiming (Bucchi, M., & Trench, B., eds., 2014, *Routledge Handbook of Public Communication of Science and Technology*, Routledge).


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed the specific claim about Alyssa Carson in its standard fact-check format, though the claim has been investigated by several other prominent fact-checking organizations.

PolitiFact published a rating in 2018 (PolitiFact, 2018, *No, NASA is not preparing a 17-year-old to become the first person on Mars*) concluding that the claim was false. Their investigation confirmed that Carson had no formal NASA affiliation, that NASA does not have a Mars candidate of any age, and that statements attributed to NASA in viral posts were fabricated or misquoted. PolitiFact's treatment was accurate and appropriately decisive.

AFP Fact Check addressed the one-way Mars mission variant in 2020, confirming that the claim about a signed contract and marriage prohibition was false, and clarifying Carson's actual activities and status. The AFP analysis correctly distinguished between her extensive space camp and training activities and any formal NASA program.

These fact-checks do the core job well: they correctly identify the false claims, source the corrections to appropriate authorities (including NASA itself), and provide accurate alternative descriptions of Carson's genuine achievements. What they generally stop short of is the structural-analytical observation: why does this particular claim type—the institutional inflation of a real person's genuine aspirational activities—arise and spread so reliably? What does it reveal about science media, the economics of inspirational stories, and the cognitive gap between "aspiring toward X" and "selected for X"?

The methodological limitation is not specific to these fact-checks but generic to fact-checking as a genre: it is designed to adjudicate specific claims, not to analyze the production conditions of claim families. The Carson case suggests an opportunity for a more systemic approach to a category of viral claims that regularly misrepresent the formal credentials of aspirational young scientists—an analysis that would serve science communication as well as misinformation correction.

The deeper takeaway for media literacy is that the claim about Carson is not malicious fabrication—it emerges from a set of incentive structures that are, individually, understandable: the media's appetite for inspiring human-interest stories about space, the promotional ecosystem that surrounds aspiring space professionals, the genuine ambiguity around what "astronaut" means in an era of commercial spaceflight, and the inspirational STEM communication agenda that benefits from having poster-children for scientific ambition. Understanding these incentive structures, and how they combine to generate persistent claims that are both appealing and inaccurate, is more valuable for navigating the information environment than knowing the verdict on any individual claim.
