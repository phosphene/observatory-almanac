## The Claim

In the final days of December 2020, as billions of holiday packages moved through the global logistics network, a claim began circulating widely on social media: Amazon, the multinational e-commerce and shipping company, was said to spray all of its shipping boxes with toxic pesticides — specifically with rodenticides designed to kill warehouse rodents — and these chemicals were supposedly capable of causing severe chemical burns in household pets.

The origin of the claim was a Facebook post shared by the New York-based charity Uptown Cat Rescue on December 27, 2020, which warned pet owners that "ALL" Amazon boxes were sprayed with "toxic pesticides to kill wearouse [sic] rodents." The post spread rapidly, compounding Snopes reader enquiries. A related chain of claims emerged separately: a woman named Debora Berger had shared, within the Facebook group "Yorkie Lovers," photographs originally posted by one Alicia Plant, showing a cat that appeared to have suffered chemical burns allegedly caused by licking an Amazon delivery box.

The claim thus had two distinct components: a general claim (Amazon has a blanket policy of spraying all boxes with toxic pesticides) and an anecdotal claim (at least one pet had suffered chemical burns attributable to such a practice). By the time Snopes published its investigation on December 30, 2020, the posts had accumulated tens of thousands of shares across multiple platforms including Reddit, Twitter, and Facebook.

The timing was significant. The claim emerged during the peak of the 2020 holiday shipping season, a period uniquely primed for consumer anxiety about delivery safety. Amazon had expanded its shipping operations substantially during the 2020 coronavirus pandemic — increasing its shipping flight activity by at least 30% since April 2020, according to a September 2020 report by the Chaddick Institute for Metropolitan Development — meaning an unusually high proportion of households were receiving Amazon packages during this period.

## What's Actually True

The core claim is false. Amazon does not have a policy of spraying its shipping boxes with any pesticides, rodenticides, or insecticides. This was confirmed directly to Snopes journalist Madison Dapcevich by Amazon spokesperson Leah Seay in December 2020 with notable specificity: "Boxes aren't sprayed for any reason. If somewhere down the supply chain are they sprayed? The answer to that is no."

Seay elaborated on the material composition of Amazon's boxes: corrugated shipping boxes are made from wood pulp and wood pulp binders — the same materials used by every manufacturer in the industry. According to the United States Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), corrugated cardboard is one of the most recycled products in the United States. Waste Management, one of the country's largest garbage and recycling service providers, has documented that corrugated shipping boxes are made from recycled materials or lumber industry byproducts (such as sawdust), and the recycling process involves re-pulping, separation, bleaching, and screening and cleaning to eliminate contaminants. There is no standard industry practice of applying pesticides to corrugated shipping boxes.

One nuance emerged from Seay's statement that deserves acknowledgment: because Amazon contracts multiple airlines and warehouse facilities across a global supply chain, she could not rule out the possibility that boxes might be incidentally cross-contaminated by chemicals from other sources — such as disinfectants used to clean airplane holds, or rodenticides used at specific warehouse storage facilities. The U.S. Department of Transportation permits the disinsection of aircraft to protect public health and the environment, conducted either by spraying aerosolized insecticides in the aircraft cabin or treating interior surfaces with residual insecticide. Neither method is thought to present a risk to human or pet health at the concentrations involved.

As for the anecdotal claim about a cat suffering chemical burns: Snopes contacted Alicia Plant, who had originally shared the photographs. Plant confirmed she had posted the images but had since deleted the post. Her statement to the journalist was notably non-confirmatory of the original claim: "I don't want to bash Amazon. I love Amazon and I have deleted my post bc of all the hate. Amazon and I have been in contact and I do not wish to speak about it as it is being handled by them." Plant's unwillingness to confirm the causal chain — Amazon boxes causing chemical burns — and the deletion of her original post significantly weakens the evidentiary basis of the anecdotal component.

This pattern is typical of viral pet-safety claims: a genuine and emotionally resonant photograph of an injured animal, an asserted causal link to a well-known consumer product or brand, and a rapid spread driven by the combination of pet owner anxiety and distrust of large corporations. The emotional charge makes the claim narratively compelling even under conditions of thin or absent substantive evidence.

## Why People Believe This

The Amazon box chemical claim succeeded virally for reasons that are worth examining in detail, because they persist across a broad class of consumer product safety misinformation.

**Corporate scale and plausibility signalling.** Amazon is one of the world's largest companies, operating warehouses across dozens of countries, handling billions of packages annually, and coordinating a logistics chain of extraordinary complexity. It is not intrinsically implausible that a company of this scale might apply pesticide treatments to its packaging as a standard pest-control measure within storage facilities. The claim exploited the genuine opacity of logistics operations to non-expert consumers: most people have no direct knowledge of how corrugated cardboard is manufactured, how warehouse pest control is conducted, or what constraints govern the treatment of shipping materials. The unfamiliarity of the domain made the claim difficult to immediately contradict from personal knowledge.

**Emotional valence of pet safety.** Claims about threats to companion animals routinely spread farther and faster than equivalent claims about inanimate objects or abstract harms. Research on moral psychology — including work by Jonathan Haidt and colleagues (Haidt, 2001, *Psychological Review*) on the role of disgust and harm sensitivity in moral judgment — suggests that perceived threats to vulnerable and dependent creatures (children, pets) activate strong emotional and protective responses. A claim that reframes an ordinary household item (a delivery box) as a threat to a beloved pet combines the emotional registers of danger, betrayal, and domestic invasion in a particularly potent way. The epistemic novelty here is that the vector of claimed harm is a thoroughly mundane, routine object — the Amazon box — rather than an obviously dangerous substance or situation. This inversion of the ordinary-into-threatening is a characteristic feature of high-virality consumer safety misinformation.

**The pivot around the original poster's deletion.** Plant's deletion of her original post, and her subsequent vague reference to Amazon handling the matter, created a structural ambiguity that was itself interpretable as sinister. In the grammar of social media information dynamics, a prominent post being deleted can read as suppression — as though a company has pressured an individual into silence. The epistemic novelty specific to this case is how corporate scale itself becomes a mechanism for creating the appearance of evidentiary suppression: Plant's statement that "it is being handled by them" was amenable to multiple readings, and the most alarming reading spread with vigour despite being unsupported by any disclosed facts.

**Historical precursors.** Claims involving packaging materials and pet illnesses are not new to the 2020 holiday season. Snopes noted in its investigation that similar allegations had surfaced previously, including a 2020 UK case in which a dog's jaw was reportedly glued shut after chewing an Amazon parcel. The existence of prior incidents — even unverified ones — creates a background narrative template into which new incidents slot readily. Each new claim benefits from the credibility momentum of previous claims, even when those previous claims were themselves unsubstantiated.

## Verdict

**The claim is false.** Amazon does not spray its boxes with pesticides, rodenticides, or insecticides. The company confirmed this directly to Snopes through its spokesperson. Corrugated cardboard is manufactured from wood pulp and wood pulp binders, matching industry-standard materials, and is subject to no standard pesticide treatment in its production or distribution.

The anecdotal element — the photographs of a cat allegedly suffering chemical burns — cannot be independently verified. The original poster deleted her post and declined to discuss the matter in a way that substantiated the causal claim. The photographs, whatever their actual cause, do not constitute evidence that Amazon boxes caused the injuries depicted.

The limited nuance acknowledged by Amazon — that cross-contamination from other supply chain sources cannot be entirely excluded — does not rescue the claim. The claim was specifically that Amazon boxes are deliberately sprayed with toxic chemicals as a standard practice. This claim is false. The theoretical possibility of incidental cross-contamination at some point in a complex global supply chain, involving substances considered safe at relevant concentrations, is a materially different claim from the one that went viral.

Snopes rated this claim **False**.

## The Wider Picture

This claim has a structural shape that recurs across a well-documented category of consumer safety misinformation. Understanding its anatomy is useful beyond the specific case of Amazon boxes.

**The logistics opacity problem.** Modern supply chains are extraordinarily complex and largely invisible to end consumers. Most people have essentially no direct knowledge of what happens to consumer goods between manufacture and delivery — the warehouses, sorting facilities, transportation networks, and handling steps that intervene. This opacity creates an epistemological gap that is routinely exploited by consumer safety misinformation: the claim needs only to allege something plausible-sounding about what might happen in the invisible portion of the process, and the lack of direct consumer knowledge makes it difficult to immediately refute from first principles.

The corrugated cardboard industry is a useful case study here. The EPA's Container and Packaging product-specific data provides detailed public information about the composition and recycling of corrugated cardboard, which is one of the most-recycled materials in U.S. municipal solid waste streams. Industry standards for shipping box production do not include pesticide application — not for logistical reasons but for a much simpler reason: it is unnecessary for the function of a shipping box, adds cost, and would trigger substantial regulatory obligations around chemical treatment of consumer goods packaging. Understanding these industry parameters allows a more confident assessment of the claim's plausibility than simply trusting a corporate spokesperson.

**Corporate spokesperson statements.** The Amazon spokesperson's statement to Snopes is notable for being unusually candid about its own limitations. Seay confirmed that Amazon does not have a policy of spraying boxes, but acknowledged the complexity of the global supply chain and the inability to guarantee zero cross-contamination. This kind of qualified denial — "no, we don't do this, but I can't rule out everything that happens across thousands of facilities globally" — is actually more credible than a blanket denial with no caveats, because it reflects the genuine epistemic situation of a large corporation: its spokesperson can speak to policy and standard practice, but not to every possible contingency across a global operation.

**Pet safety claims as a genre.** The genre of "ordinary household item harms pets" misinformation is persistent and large. Claims about Febreze, essential oils, scented candles, and cleaning products causing pet illness have circulated widely and repeatedly on social media, with varying degrees of truth. Some involve genuine risks that are understated (tea tree oil is genuinely toxic to cats; certain cleaning products pose real hazards). Many involve highly exaggerated or fabricated risks. The Amazon box claim falls into the fabricated category, but it draws on the same emotional architecture as the genuine-risk claims: the vulnerability of pets, the domestic setting, the trusted household object revealed as threat. The cumulative effect of this genre on consumers is a kind of diffuse hypervigilance that does not reliably track actual risks.

**The 2020 holiday season context.** The timing of the claim — December 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic — added specific contextual fuel. Amazon had significantly expanded its operations during 2020 (the Chaddick Institute for Metropolitan Development estimated a 30% increase in shipping flight activity since April 2020). More households than ever were receiving Amazon deliveries, often as a primary rather than supplemental retail channel during pandemic lockdowns. Consumer anxieties about supplies, packaging, surface contamination, and corporate behaviour were elevated. The claim landed in unusually fertile ground.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes investigation by Madison Dapcevich is a model of the appropriate methodology for a claim of this type: direct contact with the named corporation, pursuit of the primary sources (Original Poster Alicia Plant), explanation of the material facts (cardboard composition, EPA recycling data, DOT disinsection policy), and clear disclosure of the limitations of the corporate spokesperson's knowledge.

The investigation demonstrates an important principle in fact-checking corporate product safety claims: corporate denial is a data point, not conclusive evidence. The Snopes piece does not simply say "Amazon says they don't do it, therefore they don't do it." It contextualises the denial by explaining *why* the practice would be unusual (corrugated cardboard composition, industry standards), *what* the theoretical exceptions to a blanket denial might involve (supply chain cross-contamination), and *how* the primary anecdotal evidence was undermined (Plant's deletion and non-corroborating statement).

The fact-check appropriately acknowledges the one genuine nuance — that cross-contamination from supply chain sources cannot be ruled out with certainty — without allowing that nuance to rescue the claim from its false rating. A claim that goes viral as "Amazon deliberately sprays all boxes with toxic chemicals" is not rescued by the theoretical possibility that a box *could* at some point be incidentally exposed to disinsection chemicals in an aircraft hold.

The methodology of pursuing the primary source — Alicia Plant, not just Debora Berger who shared the post — is particularly important. By the time Snopes contacted Plant, she had deleted her post and settled into a guarded communication style. This pattern of post-deletion and vague reference to corporate contact effectively closed off the evidentiary chain. Fact-checkers encountering this pattern (original poster deletes content, claims corporate handling) face a structural challenge: the absence of the original evidence can be portrayed as suppression rather than retraction. Documenting the sequence carefully, as Dapcevich did, is the appropriate response.

The broader lesson for consumers encountering claims of this type is to seek primary source confirmation — not of the corporation's denials, but of the claimed *mechanism*. The question is not "does Amazon say it doesn't spray boxes?" but rather "is spraying corrugated cardboard boxes with pesticides a real, documented practice anywhere in the shipping industry?" The answer to the second question is readily available through EPA and industry recycling standards, and it supports the corporate denial rather than undermining it.
