# American mayors pledged to uphold the Paris Climate Agreement after Trump withdrew the United States


## 1. The Claim

On June 1, 2017, President Donald Trump announced that the United States would withdraw from the Paris Climate Agreement, the landmark 2015 international accord in which 196 parties committed to nationally determined contributions toward limiting global average temperature rise to well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels, with efforts to limit the increase to 1.5°C (United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change, 2015, *Paris Agreement*, UNFCCC Treaty Collection). The announcement was made in the White House Rose Garden and framed as an act of economic nationalism: Trump argued that the agreement imposed unfair burdens on the American economy and workforce while allowing other major emitters to make less demanding commitments.

The claim that has entered widespread circulation is that in response to Trump's withdrawal announcement, American mayors — along with governors, business leaders, university presidents, and other institutional actors — pledged to uphold the Paris Agreement on behalf of their communities. This claim appears across news coverage from 2017 onward, in climate advocacy materials, in political speeches, and at international climate meetings, where representatives of American subnational governments have appeared at the tables typically occupied by national delegations.

The specific framing takes several forms. In its simplest version: mayors pledged to uphold Paris. In more elaborate versions: hundreds or thousands of mayors made this pledge; the coalition represents a majority of US GDP and population; Bloomberg pledged personal funds to compensate for the US withdrawal; and subnational US actors have continued to participate in global climate governance despite the national government's absence. The claim is regularly cited as evidence that climate action can persist across political transitions at the national level, and it has been deployed internationally as evidence that the US's commitment to the Paris framework was not simply erased by the Trump administration.

Understanding this claim requires examining the actual record of what happened in the days, months, and years following the June 1 announcement — and understanding the constitutional and governance complexity underlying what "upholding" a treaty actually means when done by actors who are not constitutional parties to it.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The "We Are Still In" coalition.** Within days of Trump's withdrawal announcement, a broad coalition of American subnational actors — initially organised around a statement co-authored by California Governor Jerry Brown and former New York City Mayor Michael Bloomberg — publicly committed to continue pursuing the goals of the Paris Agreement. The coalition was named "We Are Still In," and it published an open letter that was formally submitted to the United Nations and to the UNFCCC Secretariat. The letter stated directly: "We will intensify efforts to meet each of the goals of the Paris Agreement, and work together to deepen the ambition of our own commitments" (We Are Still In, 2017, *Open Letter to the International Community and Parties to the Paris Agreement*).

The initial signatories included mayors from major US cities — Los Angeles, New York, Houston, Chicago, Philadelphia, Seattle, Denver, and hundreds of smaller municipalities — as well as governors of California, New York, Washington, Oregon, Virginia, and other states. Business signatories included major US corporations across sectors. Within months, the coalition had grown to more than 2,500 entities; by 2019 it had grown to more than 3,900 signatories representing communities across all 50 US states (Hsu, A., Brandt, J., Widerberg, O., Chan, S., & Weinfurter, A., 2019, "Track and trace it: Let's log non-state climate action," *Nature Climate Change*, 9, 489–490). The coalition's collective economic weight was estimated to represent more than 68 percent of US GDP.

**What the coalition actually pledged.** The content of the pledges varied by signatory type. Mayors committed to city-level climate action plans: reductions in municipal energy use, transitioning city vehicle fleets to electric power, upgrading building codes, setting renewable energy mandates, and generally publishing and pursuing Paris-aligned emissions trajectories. Governors committed to state-level policies including renewable portfolio standards, carbon pricing mechanisms (California maintained its cap-and-trade programme; RGGI states continued their regional scheme), and clean energy investment. Businesses committed to supply chain emissions reductions, renewable energy procurement contracts, and internal carbon pricing. In aggregate, Hsu et al. (2019) found that the subnational commitments from We Are Still In signatories represented a quantifiable portion of the emissions reductions the US had originally pledged under its Nationally Determined Contribution to the Paris Agreement — not a symbolic gesture but a material policy commitment.

**Bloomberg's commitment.** Michael Bloomberg, serving simultaneously as a major We Are Still In organiser and as the UN Secretary-General's Special Envoy for Climate Action, personally pledged $15 million to the UNFCCC to partially compensate for the loss of US government funding that Trump's withdrawal threatened to curtail (Bloomberg Philanthropies, 2017, *Bloomberg Philanthropies Announces $15M Commitment to UNFCCC*, press release). This commitment was publicly stated, documented, and fulfilled. Bloomberg Philanthropies simultaneously expanded its "Climate Challenge" programmes targeting American cities and continued engagement with international climate governance infrastructure.

**US Conference of Mayors resolution.** The United States Conference of Mayors — the official nonpartisan organisation of US cities with populations of 30,000 or more — passed a resolution in June 2017 formally committing member cities to pursuing the goals and ambitions of the Paris Climate Agreement. This was not merely a collection of individual statements but a formal resolution of the membership organisation, endorsing the Paris goals as US municipal policy and calling on the federal government to rejoin. The resolution was passed at the organisation's annual meeting and carried full institutional standing.

**UNFCCC recognition.** American subnational climate actors — operating through We Are Still In, the C40 Cities network, ICLEI — Local Governments for Sustainability, and the America's Pledge initiative — were formally recognised within UNFCCC processes. At COP22 in Marrakech (2016) and all subsequent Conference of Parties meetings, non-state and subnational actors participated through the Non-State Actor Zone for Climate Action (NAZCA) platform managed by the UNFCCC. After the US federal withdrawal announcement, American mayors and governors received formal delegation observer space at COP23 in Bonn (2017) under a special "America's Pledge" banner organised by Bloomberg and Governor Brown. The UNFCCC accepted submission of the America's Pledge quantitative report — which Hsu and colleagues prepared — as part of the official COP23 proceedings.

**The constitutional limits of mayoral treaty adherence.** The claim is accurate but rests on a form of commitment that is categorically different from national treaty adherence. Under the United States Constitution, the treaty-making power resides exclusively with the federal government: treaties are negotiated by the executive and ratified by the Senate with a two-thirds supermajority (US Constitution, Article II, Section 2). Mayors and governors have no authority to enter into binding international agreements on behalf of the United States, nor can they assume the US government's legal obligations under international law. The federal government's treaty commitments cannot be substituted, superseded, or maintained by city or state action.

What mayors and governors can do — and what the We Are Still In coalition actually committed to — is to align their domestic policies with the same goals enshrined in the Paris framework. A city can adopt a 2°C-aligned emissions trajectory in its own climate action plan. A state can mandate renewable energy standards that reduce its statewide emissions consistent with Paris targets. These are real and potentially significant policy actions, but they operate entirely within the domestic legal framework. They are, in international law terms, voluntary domestic policy commitments made in parallel with the goals of a treaty — not the treaty itself and not a continuation of US federal adherence.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The epistemic novelty: subnational actors as genuine climate governance.** The most significant intellectual contribution of the 2017 episode was its demonstration — at dramatic scale — that subnational governments had acquired meaningful agency within international climate governance. This development exceeded what most international relations scholars had anticipated; the theoretical architecture of international relations, long dominated by state-centric Westphalian assumptions, provided limited conceptual vocabulary for understanding entities that could commit meaningfully to international norms without being formal parties to international treaties.

Bansard, J.B., Pattberg, P., & Widerberg, O. (2017, "Cities to the rescue? Assessing the performance of transnational municipal networks in global climate governance," *International Environmental Agreements: Politics, Law and Economics*, 17(2), 229–246) examined transnational municipal networks including C40 Cities and ICLEI and found that these networks had accumulated genuine governance functions — facilitating peer learning, enabling accountability mechanisms, and contributing verifiable emissions reductions — that positioned them as substantive actors rather than merely symbolic ones. The Paris Agreement's own architecture had already accommodated this: Article 6 and the Lima-Paris Action Agenda reflected a deliberate design choice to make subnational and non-state action legible and countable within the overall framework, distinguishing Paris from its predecessor Kyoto Protocol, which was designed exclusively around national state actors.

Hale, T. (2016, "All Hands on Deck: The Paris Agreement and Nonstate Climate Action," *Global Environmental Politics*, 16(3), 12–22) developed the theoretical framework for understanding how non-state and subnational actors in climate governance had moved from a supplementary supplementary role to a constitutive one. This shift was intentional in Paris's design: it reflected a realistic assessment that nationally determined contributions alone were insufficient to achieve the Agreement's temperature goals and that closing the ambition gap would require contributions from entities below and beside the state level.

**Political urgency and framing inflation.** The political urgency of the 2017 moment also contributed to rhetorical inflation in how the pledges were described. Climate advocates, international partners, and sympathetic journalists were motivated to emphasise the resilience of American climate commitment. This led to framing that sometimes collapsed the legally meaningful distinction between domestic policy alignment and formal treaty adherence. When news coverage described American mayors as "still in the Paris Agreement," the shorthand, though politically useful, obscured the precise legal reality. This conflation was widely understood by sophisticated actors as advocacy framing even when they employed it.

**Real quantifiable impact.** The belief that the pledges matter is also supported by quantitative evidence. Hsu et al. (2019) and the America's Pledge technical analyses showed that We Are Still In commitments, if fully implemented, would contribute materially toward the original US Nationally Determined Contribution targets. The state of California alone — as the world's fifth-largest economy by this point — ran a cap-and-trade programme and renewable energy mandates with genuine emissions impact. Vandenbergh, M.P., & Gilligan, J.M. (2017, *Beyond Politics: The Private Governance Response to Climate Change*, Cambridge University Press) documented how private and subnational actors could achieve approximately 25 percent of the emissions reductions needed to hold global warming below 2°C without any action from the US federal government. The pledge was thus not purely symbolic; it was accompanied by substantial policy infrastructure.


## 4. Verdict

**True — with constitutionally and legally important context.** American mayors, governors, business leaders, and other institutional actors did in fact pledge to uphold the goals of the Paris Climate Agreement following Trump's announcement. The pledges were real, publicly documented, formally submitted to the UNFCCC, and backed in many cases by concrete domestic policy commitments. The We Are Still In coalition grew to encompass a statistically significant share of US economic and demographic weight. Bloomberg's $15 million funding commitment to the UNFCCC was fulfilled. The US Conference of Mayors passed a formal institutional resolution. American subnational actors participated formally — under their own designation — in UNFCCC processes at subsequent COPs.

The claim is accurate as stated, at the level at which it circulates. The context that any thorough treatment should add is that "upholding" the Paris Agreement at city and state level means aligning domestic policies with its temperature goals — not assuming formal treaty status, which only the federal government can do under the US Constitution. The pledges were substantive domestic policy commitments, not formal treaty adherence in international law terms. This distinction is constitutionally significant and matters for understanding what international climate governance actually is — but it does not undermine the basic accuracy of the claim.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The 2017 episode accelerated a governance trend that had been developing for more than a decade: the emergence of cities and subnational regions as serious actors in global climate governance. The C40 Cities Climate Leadership Group, formed in 2005, and ICLEI — Local Governments for Sustainability, founded in 1990 — had long provided infrastructure for city-level climate collaboration across national boundaries. The We Are Still In coalition and its successor, America Is All In, added political urgency and dramatically expanded scale to this existing infrastructure in the US context.

Kim, J., & Vandenbergh, M.P. (2019, "Bridging the Gap: Informal International Lawmaking and the Paris Agreement's Nonstate Actors," *Duke Law Journal*, 69, 1741) examined how the informal normativity of subnational climate pledges had already begun influencing state-level behaviour and might, over time, acquire a kind of soft law standing even absent formal treaty incorporation. Their analysis suggested that the governance innovation represented by Paris was not merely a design choice by diplomats but an acknowledgment of structural changes in where effective environmental governance actually resided in the twenty-first century.

Subsequent political developments tested the durability of this model. When the Biden administration rejoined the Paris Agreement in February 2021 — just hours after Biden's inauguration under Executive Order 13990 — the subnational commitments did not simply dissolve into federal policy. Many cities and states maintained their independent climate frameworks alongside renewed federal engagement. The America Is All In coalition continued operating, reflecting a widely shared judgment that diversified climate governance was structurally more resilient than a purely federal approach. Whether this instinct is correct is an empirical question that the post-2025 political cycle will test again.

The political economy of subnational climate action also involves interests beyond symbolic commitment. Renewable energy investment, green building standards, and electric vehicle infrastructure create local economic benefit streams that generate constituencies for continued policy regardless of federal direction (Meckling, J., & Nahm, J., 2018, "The politics of technology bans: Industrial policy competition and green goals in the auto industry," *Energy Policy*, 126, 470–479). This embeds the Paris-aligned policy commitments into local economic structures in ways that make reversal costly even for politically opposed local administrations.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Mainstream fact-checking organisations including Snopes and PolitiFact have generally rated the core claim that American mayors pledged to uphold the Paris Agreement as true. The factual record is not contested; the pledges were public, documented, and reported extensively in real time. The challenge for fact-checkers has been less whether the pledges occurred and more how to contextualise the constitutional limitation: fact-checks that simply verify "yes, mayors pledged" without noting that cities are not and cannot become formal parties to the treaty miss what is arguably the key instructive element.

The more sophisticated fact-checking treatments added the constitutional context while affirming the basic accuracy. This represents the appropriate approach: a verdict of true with contextual education rather than a simple binary judgment. The claim is not an error; it is a simplified accurate statement that benefits from elaboration.

For media literacy purposes, this episode is useful for teaching the difference between domestic policy alignment and formal treaty adherence — a distinction that matters in many international governance contexts beyond climate. It also illustrates how politically motivated framing can attach to accurate factual claims. The same set of events was framed by climate advocates as demonstrating the resilience of US climate commitment and framed by critics as irrelevant symbolism that did not constitute treaty compliance. Both framings read selectively from the accurate factual record. The media literacy task is to report the record accurately and provide conceptual tools for evaluating competing framings without adjudicating their political implications. The factual baseline — pledges were made, coalition was real, commitments were documented, policy action followed in many cases — is not in dispute.
