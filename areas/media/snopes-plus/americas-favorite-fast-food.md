# Are These America's Favorite Fast Food Restaurants?

## 1. The Claim

On June 5, 2020, the Barstool Sports Twitter account shared an image of a map of the United States labeled to show "America's Favorite Fast Food restaurants by state." The map assigned a different chain to each state, and the results were immediately confusing. Expected favorites like McDonald's, Chick-fil-A, and Chipotle were largely absent or in unexpected states. In their place: Long John Silver's was assigned to multiple states; Rainforest Cafe appeared in Colorado; Sbarro was represented; and a range of regional, minority-presence, and otherwise implausible chains populated the map in ways that seemed to bear no relationship to any observable consumer preference data.

The map spread rapidly on social media, generating confusion and genuine debate about whether it could be accurate. Restaurant chains that appeared as unlikely state favorites began trending on Twitter as users tried to process what seemed like inexplicable data. Comments ranged from sincere puzzlement ("How is Rainforest Cafe Colorado's favorite? There isn't even one there") to defensive to dismissive. The virality of the map was driven partly by genuine shared curiosity about regional fast-food preferences—a legitimate and frequently-studied question—and partly by the cognitive dissonance of results that seemed simultaneously official-looking and empirically wrong.

Snopes writer Dan Evon investigated and published findings on June 5, 2020, with a subsequent correction on November 15, 2020 (which identified the publisher's name as Barstool Sports, having previously and erroneously rendered it "Bar Stool Sports"). The Snopes rating is **False**: the map is not based on any real data, was created as a joke by Barstool Sports, and its attribution to the "Public Information and Statistics Society"—a fictional organization—signals the satirical intent through an acronym joke.

## 2. What's Actually True

The map is fake. Barstool Sports created it as a joke. The evidence is multi-layered and unambiguous.

**The PISS tell:** The first and most deliberate indicator of satire is the attributed data source. The map credits its data to the "Public Information and Statistics Society," an organization that does not exist. More pointedly: PISS is the acronym. This is not a subtle wordplay; it is a standard Barstool Sports brand-consistent vulgar humor signature, directly embedded in the map's framing. Snopes notes: "the acronym for this group is PISS, which, to say the least, is a bit suspicious." Any credulous reader is presented with a data source name that, read carefully, announces its own fabricated nature.

**The geographic impossibility:** The map lists the Rainforest Cafe as Colorado's favorite restaurant. As Evon documents, there are no Rainforest Cafe locations in the state of Colorado. A chain with zero presence in a state cannot logically be that state's "favorite" restaurant by any survey methodology. This geographic error is not a minor discrepancy or a matter of interpretation; it is a structural impossibility in the claim, and it is visible to anyone familiar with basic geography and the Rainforest Cafe's limited-location footprint (Rainforest Cafe operates in a small number of tourist-destination and mall-based locations, not as a mainstream fast-food chain with broad geographic presence).

**The punny follow-up tweet:** After the map went viral, Barstool Sports posted a follow-up tweet whose text Snopes characterizes as pointing to "the humorous intentions of this map." Evon's summary: "In short, this viral map supposedly showing the most popular restaurant chains by state isn't real and was a joke shared by Barstool Sports that isn't based on any data from a reputable source. In other words, Barstool Sports and the Public Information for Statistics Society are just 'taking the piss.'" The "taking the piss" idiom—sourced to the English phrase for mocking or having a laugh at someone's expense—is the intended joke made explicit.

**The implausibility profile of the results:** Beyond Colorado/Rainforest Cafe, the distribution of chains across the map does not correspond to any known consumer survey data. Restaurant industry surveys and publicly available data from Nielsen, Statista, and the American Customer Satisfaction Index consistently show that McDonald's, Chick-fil-A, Subway, Starbucks, and similar national chains dominate consumer preference polling in most states. A map in which these expected leaders are absent or displaced by Long John Silver's, Sbarro, and other lower-presence chains cannot reflect genuine consumer preference data without extraordinary methodological explanation that no such survey would omit.

## 3. Epistemic Novelty: The Official-Format Satire Problem and Credulous Data-Reading

The Barstool Sports fast-food map belongs to a specific and increasingly prevalent category of content that might be called **official-format satire**: fictional information presented in the visual grammar of authoritative data visualization. Unlike traditional satire, which typically signals its fictional status through exaggeration, absurdism, or explicit framing ("This is satire"), official-format satire mimics the visual conventions of genuine data products—maps, infographics, charts, statistical tables—to the degree that undiscriminating viewers treat it as real.

The fake map contained all the visual markers that encode data authority in contemporary information culture: state-boundary mapping, color-coding by state, attribution to a named organization, and a confident declarative title. These signals activate what might be called **format-trust credentialing**—the tendency to extend belief to content that visually resembles authoritative information products, independent of whether the specific content has been verified (Sundar, 2008, in *The SAGE Handbook of Online Information Behavior*). In the digital media environment, where authoritative data visualization and meme-grade fabrication can be rendered with equivalent visual polish, format-trust credentialing is a particularly dangerous cognitive shortcut.

The Barstool Sports map exploited this credulous data-reading tendency deliberately. The satirical intent was encoded, but the encoding was not prominent: the PISS acronym requires careful attention to the attribution line (which many viewers skip), and the Rainforest Cafe/Colorado error requires geographic knowledge that may not be widely held. Viewers who lacked either piece of contextual knowledge had limited tools to detect the satire through content inspection alone.

This creates what epistemologists of testimony might call an **evidential asymmetry problem**: the evidence that the map is fake (non-existent data organization, geographic impossibility, confirming follow-up tweet) is distributed across sources and requires active verification to assemble, while the evidence that it might be real (it looks like a data visualization, it was shared by a major media brand, fast-food preference surveys do exist) is passively available in the viewing experience itself. Passive evidence favors credulity; active fact-checking corrects it. Research on the relationship between active versus passive information processing and misinformation susceptibility has consistently found that cognitive engagement is protective—but demanding active engagement of every viewer of every viral map is not realistic (Pennycook & Rand, 2019, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 23(5), 388–392).

The Barstool Sports case also illustrates the **brand-context collapse problem**. Barstool Sports is a digital media brand known primarily for sports commentary, irreverent humor, and a brand voice that ranges from earnest fandom to deliberate provocation. Its regular audience recognizes the brand voice and is likely to read a PISS-attributed map as a joke without further investigation. But Twitter's resharing mechanics distribute the content well beyond the original audience, into communities with no established relationship to Barstool Sports' brand norms and no background against which to contextualize the joke. Content travels further than context, creating a systematic gap between intent and reception.

## 4. Context: Viral Maps, Fast-Food Geography, and Real Consumer Preference Data

The claim the Barstool Sports map was pastiching—America's favorite fast food restaurants by state—is a genuinely interesting empirical question with real data behind it. Multiple organizations have produced legitimate, methodology-disclosing surveys of regional fast-food preferences in the United States, providing a substantive context against which the fake map can be contrasted.

The American Customer Satisfaction Index (ACSI), a long-running academic-industry partnership based at the University of Michigan's Ross School of Business (Fornell, Johnson, Anderson, Cha, & Bryant, 1996, *Journal of Marketing*, 60(4), 7–18), publishes annual customer satisfaction scores for restaurant chains including fast-food operators. These surveys systematically measure customer satisfaction through a standardized methodology across chains and over time, providing reliable data on which chains their customers rate most satisfactorily—though "highest satisfaction among customers" is not the same as "most widely beloved by all residents of each state."

YouGov and The Harris Poll have also conducted public opinion surveys on fast-food preferences, and the results consistently position McDonald's, Chick-fil-A, Subway, and a small set of national-scale brands as most widely recognized high-preference chains across the U.S. population. State-by-state variation exists—California is associated with In-N-Out Burger preference, Chick-fil-A is particularly dominant in southeastern states, Whataburger has intense loyalty in Texas—but these geographic variations produce a pattern quite different from the Barstool map's implausible results.

Fast-food geography is also a genuine academic subfield interest. Geographers and public health researchers have studied the spatial distribution of fast-food access in relation to income, race, and urban/rural demographics (Galvez, Morland, & Raines, 2008, *Preventive Medicine*, 46(4), 358–363; Budzynski & Kim, 2020, *Applied Geography*, 125). These studies typically focus on access and health outcomes rather than preference, and the distinction is important: a region may be saturated with particular chains not because consumers prefer them but because those chains have pursued geographic expansion strategies that maximize their presence in that region.

The fake map's inclusion of Rainforest Cafe and Sbarro as state favorites is particularly noteworthy in this context. Rainforest Cafe is an experiential dining concept operated in tourist destinations and major malls—its primary identity is as a themed entertainment experience, not a quick-service convenience meal—and its overall U.S. location count is small. Sbarro, primarily a mall food-court pizza chain, has operated with a substantially reduced footprint compared to even its 1990s peak. Presenting either of these as a state's "favorite" fast food is not merely improbable—it is essentially impossible under any reasonable survey methodology, since the stated favoritism would require either very high spontaneous preference or very high access, neither of which is plausible for either chain in most states.

## 5. Barstool Sports, Virality, and the Economics of Engagement Through Confusion

Barstool Sports is a sports media company that has built a large audience through a particular digital media strategy: content that generates strong engagement responses, including confusion, mock outrage, heated debate, and the kind of "wait, is this real?" processing that drives shares and comments as users seek to resolve their uncertainty. Multiple Barstool Sports viral moments have followed similar arcs: content that looks either real or at least ambiguous, initial viral spread, clarification or confirmation of the joke, and post-clarification viral spread as the meta-story ("people thought this was real!") generates a second engagement wave.

The economics of this strategy are well understood in the attention economy literature. Conflict-generating and uncertainty-generating content systematically outperforms informational content in social sharing metrics (Berger & Milkman, 2012, *Journal of Marketing Research*, 49(2), 192–205). A deliberately ambiguous fake map of America's fast-food preferences produces exactly the engagement profile this model predicts: immediate sharing (the map is interesting), followed by confusion-debate (wait, is this real?), followed by resolution (it's a joke, look at the acronym), followed by resharing of the resolved version ("You won't believe people thought this was real").

The June 5, 2020 timing is also worth noting: this was approximately ten days after the death of George Floyd on May 25, 2020, a period of intense social and political turbulence in the United States when social media was heavily oriented toward protest, policing, and racial justice content. The deployment of a deliberately silly food-map joke on this date reads either as a deliberate tonal choice (offering viewers a break from serious content) or as an example of the kind of content that algorithms promote during periods when conventional engagement with serious topics may be psychologically exhausted. Either reading is consistent with the map's unusual virality relative to its minimal informational content.

## 6. Verdict, Gaps, and Ongoing Questions

Snopes rates the claim **False**. The map purporting to show America's favorite fast food restaurants by state is not based on real data. It was created by Barstool Sports as a joke. The data attribution is a fake organization with a vulgar acronym; the geographic assignments include an impossible result (no-locations chain as a state's favorite); the follow-up content from Barstool Sports confirmed the satirical intent. None of the chain assignments should be understood as reflecting any actual consumer preference data.

**EARC classification: R (Rhetorical).** The claim is false and was always false. The fake map is a rhetorical artifact—a parody of data visualization deployed for comedic and engagement-generating purposes. Its evaluation requires understanding the satirical intent, the detection heuristics that help identify official-format satire, and the social media dynamics that distributed it beyond its intended audience.

**Gap analysis:** The primary gap is the absence of any systematic study of how official-format satire from sports and entertainment media brands spreads and is received across different audience segments. Barstool Sports' audience presumably recognized the PISS acronym joke; secondary and tertiary audiences who encountered the map through resharing may not have had the same cultural context. A media literacy study examining how recognition of the joke varied across demographic groups—particularly age, familiarity with Barstool Sports, and media literacy level—would provide useful empirical grounding for claims about the differential impact of satirical content on different audiences.

A secondary gap: what do we actually know about fast food preferences by state? The viral map, though fake, activated genuine consumer curiosity about regional variation in fast-food preference. A systematically gathered, methodologically transparent, peer-reviewed survey of fast-food preferences by state would be genuinely valuable, but no widely accessible such product appears to exist in the academic literature. Most academic fast-food geography focuses on access and health outcomes; most consumer research on preference is conducted by brands or market research firms and not published with full methodology disclosure. The gap between public curiosity (real) and publicly available rigorous data (limited) is part of what makes a fake map like this one temporarily credible—it fills a recognized information need.

A tertiary gap involves the correction article's limited scope: Evon's Snopes treatment addresses the map's fakeness concisely and correctly but does not engage the consumer psychology of how people process implausible "data" visualizations, the epistemological challenge of official-format satire, or the platform mechanics that allowed the map to reach audiences for whom the context was absent. A more complete fact-check would address not only whether the claim is false (it is) but why it was believed by a significant enough share of viewers to generate trending discussions of Rainforest Cafe and Sbarro—which is the more interesting epistemic question.
