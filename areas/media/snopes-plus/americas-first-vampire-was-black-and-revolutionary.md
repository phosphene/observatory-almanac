# America's First Vampire Was a Black Man in the Revolutionary Era


## 1. The Claim

The claim appears with particular emphasis around each Halloween season: that the first vampire in American literature was not a pale, aristocratic European but a Black enslaved man—and that the story featuring him was not only the first American vampire narrative but also potentially the first American anti-slavery fiction, published decades before the works now conventionally cited as the origins of that genre.

When the claim circulates in popular media—as it did, notably, in an article by academic Marc Leverette republished by Snopes in 2020 for its "The Conversation" series—it typically centers on *The Black Vampyre: A Legend of St. Domingo*, an 1819 novella attributed to "Uriah Derick D'Arcy." The story was published anonymously in pamphlet form in New York, parodied a recently published British vampire tale, and featured a Black protagonist who transforms into a vampire seeking revenge against his enslaver and the brutal plantation system in what is now Haiti. The text arrived before John William Polidori had even been identified as the true author of the story it parodied, and it appeared more than a decade before Lydia Maria Child published the work typically credited as the first American anti-slavery novel.

The claim makes several nested assertions: that *The Black Vampyre* is the first American vampire story; that its vampire is Black; that the story has anti-slavery intent; and that this text is remarkably unknown compared to its actual historical significance. Each of these claims can be examined, and each proves to be substantially accurate.

The "Revolutionary era" framing in some versions of the claim is slightly loose—*The Black Vampyre* was published in 1819, thirty-six years after the formal end of the American Revolutionary War—but the text is itself saturated with revolutionary themes, specifically those of the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), the only successful large-scale slave revolt in history, which resulted in the independence of the first Black republic in the Western Hemisphere. The spirit invoked is revolutionary, even if the calendar does not align with the American founding.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim is accurate in its core assertions and illuminating in its implications.

*The Black Vampyre: A Legend of St. Domingo* was published in New York in 1819, shortly after John Polidori's "The Vampyre: A Tale" appeared in London's *New Monthly Magazine*—erroneously attributed, at first, to Lord Byron. Byron was enormously popular in America, and the vampire tale created an immediate sensation when it was reprinted in Boston and Baltimore. The American response, *The Black Vampyre*, appeared within months, explicitly engaging with Polidori's text while transposing its setting to the Caribbean and inverting its racial and social dynamics.

The authorship remains disputed. The text was published under the name "Uriah Derick D'Arcy"—a near-phonetic anagram of "Richard Varick Dey," pointing toward Richard Varick Dey (1801–1837), a young New York writer from a notable family. A later 1845 reprinting attributed the work to Robert C. Sands, adding further confusion. The attribution question has not been definitively resolved, but modern scholarship tends toward Dey as the most probable author.

The plot centers on a ten-year-old enslaved child—referred to as "the Prince"—on a plantation in St. Domingo (colonial Haiti), owned by a cruel enslaver named Personne. No matter how many times Personne attempts to kill the child, the body keeps returning to life. When Personne orders the boy to be burned, the child moves with supernatural speed, causing Personne himself to fall into the fire. As Personne dies, his wife discovers that their own infant son has been replaced in the cradle by only skin, bones, and nails.

Some years later, Personne's widow Euphemia is visited by two strangers: "an extremely handsome Black man, dressed as a Moorish prince," accompanied by a pale European boy. The Black man—the vampiric Prince, grown—charms Euphemia with his elegance and wins her hand in marriage, which takes place that same evening. That night, he reveals to her that he is a vampire and that she has been transformed as well. The European boy is, ultimately, the Prince's supernatural heir—the original Personne infant, claimed years ago and raised as the Prince's own. The narrative reaches its conclusion when Zembo, the white foster son, becomes complicit in the Prince's final destruction—a troubling turn that scholars have debated as either a conservative concession to white readership anxieties or a darker irony about the fragility of cross-racial solidarity.

The text is simultaneously a parody of British Gothic conventions, an anti-slavery argument, an exploration of interracial intimacy at a time when it was legally and socially forbidden, and a coded commentary on the Haitian Revolution's aftershocks reverberating through American political culture. The Haitian Revolution had concluded only fifteen years before the novella's publication, and its implications—that enslaved Black people had successfully overthrown a European colonial power by force—remained an incendiary presence in American political discourse. White slaveholders feared slave insurrection explicitly in Haitian terms; abolitionists drew strength from the example of Haitian independence.

By giving the vampire's supernatural power to a Black enslaved man—and by making that power specifically a force of justice and vengeance rather than pure predatory evil—D'Arcy was doing something that had never been done in American literature before. The vampire figure reversed the moral valence that had accrued to the European Gothic vampire: here, it was the enslaver who was the parasite, and the enslaved man's vampirism represented not damnation but a kind of supernatural liberation.

Scholars have also noted the text's engagement with Obeah, the Afro-Caribbean spiritual system that colonial powers systematically demonized and criminalized throughout the Caribbean. The novella's imagery of resurrection, transformation, and supernatural vengeance draws on a colonial European understanding—or misunderstanding—of Obeah, embedding the vampire narrative within a specific cultural and political geography rather than the generic European Gothic countryside.

The claim that *The Black Vampyre* may be the first American anti-slavery short story is advanced with appropriate caution by scholars. It predates Lydia Maria Child's *An Appeal in Favor of That Class of Americans Called Africans* (1833) by fourteen years, and it precedes the wave of 1830s–1850s abolitionist fiction by a significant margin. Whether it is the "first" depends on definitional questions about what constitutes anti-slavery intent and what counts as a distinct literary form, but its credentials are genuine and its chronological priority over the canonical anti-slavery canon is clear.

The claim that the text is essentially unknown is also accurate. It does not appear in the standard anthologies of vampire fiction. It is absent from the major academic histories of vampire literature. It is not taught in most American literature survey courses. Its rediscovery in recent decades owes primarily to the work of scholars like Ed White and Duncan Faherty, who prepared an accessible online edition specifically to enable its classroom use—and to subsequent academic work that has illuminated its multiple layers of cultural and historical significance.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The claim circulates annually with the rhythms of Halloween content cycles, which gives it a seasonal predictability. But the reasons people find it compelling—and believable—are more substantive than seasonal novelty.

The story has the structure of a recovery narrative, which carries particular emotional and intellectual weight in contemporary discourse about historical erasure. The pattern—"this significant thing existed, and it was suppressed or ignored, and here is what was lost"—resonates strongly in a cultural moment when recovery of marginalized historical voices is a recognized scholarly and political project. The *Black Vampyre* story fits this template nearly perfectly: it is a pioneering Black literary contribution, it is genuinely unknown, and its obscurity is not accidental but reflects systematic patterns in how the literary canon was constructed and who was included.

The story's specificity also helps. This is not a vague claim that "Black stories were erased from history" but a precise claim about a specific text from a specific year with recoverable content. Readers can find the novella, read it, and evaluate the claim directly. That empirical accessibility increases both the claim's plausibility and its staying power.

The Halloween context arguably undersells the text's significance while guaranteeing its annual recirculation. Framed as a seasonal curiosity, *The Black Vampyre* reaches a broader audience than it would through purely academic channels—but that framing also risks reducing complex anti-slavery political fiction to Gothic entertainment trivia. This tension between popular accessibility and analytical depth is a recurring feature of how recovered historical texts get introduced to contemporary audiences.

The claim also benefits from the accumulated credibility of the scholars who champion it. Academic Marc Leverette's work on the subject, amplified by outlets like *The Conversation* and reprinted by Snopes, lends the claim institutional legitimacy that distinguishes it from unverifiable folk history.


## 4. Verdict

**AFFIRMED**: The claim is accurate. *The Black Vampyre: A Legend of St. Domingo* (1819), attributed to Uriah Derick D'Arcy (most likely Richard Varick Dey), is America's first vampire story and features a Black enslaved man as its central vampire figure. The text is a pioneering anti-slavery work, predates the canonical American abolitionist literary tradition by more than a decade, and engages directly with the revolutionary themes of the Haitian Revolution. Its obscurity in both popular culture and academic scholarship is genuine and documented.

The "Revolutionary era" framing requires minor contextual qualification: the text is a product of 1819, postdating the American Revolution but written in the shadow of the Haitian Revolution, which ended in 1804. The revolutionary spirit is present but Caribbean rather than colonial American in its specific reference point. This discrepancy is worth flagging in careful readings, though it does not undermine the core claim's accuracy.

Minor uncertainties about authorship (Dey vs. Sands) and about the precise scope of the text's anti-slavery intent (some scholars read the ending as ambivalent or conservative) exist but do not alter the fundamental accuracy of the claim. The text is real, its priority is documented, its content is recoverable, and its historical significance is established.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The story of *The Black Vampyre* opens onto larger questions about how literary canons are formed, what gets preserved and what disappears, and how the boundaries of cultural memory are drawn and redrawn.

The conventional history of vampire fiction moves from Eastern European folklore through Polidori's "The Vampyre" (1819) to Sheridan Le Fanu's *Carmilla* (1872) and Bram Stoker's *Dracula* (1897), with American contributions typically appearing after Stoker's definitive formulation. *The Black Vampyre* disrupts this genealogy by inserting American literature at the very beginning of the vampire's literary career—and by doing so with a work that explicitly reverses the social politics that have attached to the vampire ever since. Stoker's Dracula is the dangerous foreign Other arriving in civilized England; D'Arcy's Prince is the enslaved man rising against the civilization that enslaved him. These are fundamentally different political imaginaries, and the suppression of the D'Arcy text from the canon leaves the vampire's literary history systematically distorted.

The relationship between vampire mythology and the Haitian Revolution is a rich scholarly subject in its own right. Following Haitian independence in 1804, the American slave states worked systematically to isolate the new republic diplomatically and commercially, fearing that its example would inspire revolts closer to home. The Haitian zombie—distinct from the vampire—has its own complex relationship to plantation slavery, representing in some scholarly interpretations not the predatory undead but the enslaved person denied even the freedom of their own death. The vampire and the zombie, in this Caribbean context, are figures shaped by the same historical conditions but with different valences: where the zombie is the body owned and controlled after death, the vampire in D'Arcy's telling is the body that escapes that ownership entirely and turns it back on the enslaver.

The recovery of *The Black Vampyre* also raises questions about the mechanisms of literary obscurity. The text was published in a limited pamphlet edition, attributed pseudonymously to an author whose identity was not certain even at the time of publication, and reprinted only once (in 1845) before disappearing from literary discussion. Its recovery required dedicated archival work by scholars who were specifically looking for what had been overlooked. This pattern—of significant work becoming invisible not through deliberate censorship but through the compound effect of ephemeral publication, uncertain authorship, and a canon-building apparatus that was not looking for it—is common in the histories of marginalized literary traditions.

The racial politics of the Gothic genre extend well beyond this single text. The Gothic tradition has throughout its history been preoccupied with fears of contamination, invasion, and transgressed boundaries—fears that in the American context have frequently been proxies for anxieties about race, miscegenation, and the social order of slavery and its aftermath. Reading *The Black Vampyre* against this tradition reveals the genre's racial unconscious more starkly than almost any other text, precisely because D'Arcy was consciously inverting the conventions rather than implicitly enacting them.

Contemporary horror as a genre has increasingly engaged with this history explicitly. Films like Jordan Peele's *Get Out* (2017) and *Us* (2019), and novels like Victor LaValle's *The Ballad of Black Tom* (2016), participate in a project of recuperating and transforming a literary tradition that was built in part on the racialized fears and anxieties of white Western cultures. The existence and priority of *The Black Vampyre* provides an older precedent for this contemporary work—evidence that the project of using horror conventions to speak about racial violence and liberation has American literary roots reaching back to the earliest decades of the republic.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's handling of this topic is unusual: the platform published the claim not as a fact-check but as original journalism, republishing a piece from *The Conversation* in which academic Marc Leverett presented the claim. The article was published in October 2020 under its "News" rather than "Fact Check" category, with a clear disclosure that it was an academic contribution rather than a Snopes editorial.

This is an interesting edge case in the fact-checking world. Snopes was not debunking or confirming a viral rumor but amplifying a historical recovery argument made by a credentialed scholar. The platform used its reach to circulate a claim that was accurate and underreported rather than false and viral. That represents a different mode of the fact-checking mission—using a credibility-focused platform to surface what is true and neglected rather than only to correct what is false and popular.

The piece generated significant engagement and was widely shared, particularly in the days surrounding Halloween 2020. This kind of engagement—where historical recovery narratives spread virally through social media on topically appropriate occasions—represents an increasingly common pattern in how suppressed or neglected historical knowledge re-enters popular consciousness.

A dedicated, rigorous Snopes-style fact-check of the specific claim ("America's first vampire was Black and revolutionary") would need to engage with the authorship disputes, the precise dating of the text relative to other anti-slavery works, and the definitional questions around "first American vampire story." The claim survives this level of scrutiny well, with only minor qualifications required. The absence of that dedicated treatment reflects Snopes's decision to present the material as journalism rather than fact-checking—a reasonable editorial choice, though one that leaves the evidentiary architecture less explicit than the Truth Vault format aims to provide.

The broader gap in fact-checking infrastructure here is notable: claims about literary history and historical priority are not conventional Snopes territory, regardless of their accuracy or their public importance. The Truth Vault format can accommodate these claims in a way that standard fact-checking platforms, organized around viral misinformation, cannot.
