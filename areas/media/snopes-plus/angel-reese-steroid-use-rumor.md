# Angel Reese Fired by Chicago Sky for Steroid Use?

## 1. The Claim

In October 2024, a wave of videos circulated across social media platforms claiming that Angel Reese, the standout rookie forward for the Chicago Sky of the Women's National Basketball Association (WNBA), had been fired from the team after being caught using performance-enhancing steroids. The videos, which appeared on Facebook, YouTube, and other platforms, presented the assertion as a breaking news story, complete with dramatic narration, sensational thumbnails, and the visual language of sports journalism. Many of these videos bore hallmarks of artificial intelligence-assisted production, including AI-generated voiceovers, scripted narratives, and synthetic visual sequencing.

A representative example was a video posted on October 14, 2024, by the YouTube channel BasketBuzz. The video's narration declared: "Angel Reese finally got what she deserved — a termination letter from the Chicago Sky with her name on it, all wrapped up with a nice bow of: 'you're fired.' You might ask the reason for firing. Well, she got caught juicing up with steroids, and honestly, no one's really shocked. From the start of the season, she's been serving us more drama than basketball games." The video accumulated over 150,000 views within days of its upload and was shared widely across multiple platforms.

The claim tapped into a preexisting narrative around Reese, who had emerged as one of the most prominent and polarizing figures in women's basketball following her collegiate career at Louisiana State University (LSU) and her much-publicized rivalry with fellow rookie Caitlin Clark. The framing of Reese as a "villain" in the WNBA's emerging star system provided fertile ground for a story that depicted her downfall, regardless of its factual basis.

Snopes investigated the claim in October 2024 and rated it "Unfounded," determining that the assertion was fabricated without any supporting evidence from credible sources. The investigation found no reports from reputable sports media outlets such as ESPN, The Athletic, or the Chicago Tribune supporting the claim, no statements from the Chicago Sky organization, and no evidence of any league disciplinary action related to performance-enhancing drugs.

## 2. What's Actually True

The factual foundation beneath this claim is essentially nonexistent. However, several contextual truths are worth noting. Angel Reese was indeed a rookie with the Chicago Sky during the 2024 WNBA season after being drafted seventh overall. She had a highly visible debut season, setting multiple rookie records and drawing significant media attention. The WNBA, like all professional sports leagues, maintains a collectively bargained anti-doping policy administered in conjunction with the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) standards, and players are subject to testing.

What is also true is that Reese had been the subject of intense public scrutiny throughout her college and professional career. The "villain" persona that followed her from LSU was largely constructed through media framing that emphasized confrontational moments, competitive trash talk, and her high-profile on-court interactions with Clark. This framing was documented by multiple sports media outlets, including a September 2024 piece by *Today* that examined the manufactured nature of the rivalry narrative. The *Sports Illustrated* women's basketball section also ran analysis suggesting that Reese's treatment reflected a double standard in how female athletes—particularly Black female athletes—are portrayed when they display confidence and competitive intensity.

It is also true that AI-generated disinformation has become an increasingly significant problem in sports media. As AI video creation and voice synthesis tools have become more accessible, bad actors have been able to produce convincingly realistic content at low cost and high volume. The BasketBuzz video that was central to the viral spread of this claim included a disclaimer stating: "This video presents opinions and information sourced from various platforms and does not claim absolute truth." This disclaimer, buried in the video description, did nothing to prevent the content from being presented and shared as factual news.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's investigation, conducted by journalist Jack Izzo, correctly identified the claim as unfounded and provided several key evidentiary points to support this conclusion. The article noted the absence of any credible reporting from mainstream sports outlets, the use of AI-generated production techniques in the videos, and the disclaimer in the BasketBuzz video. It also connected the rumor to the broader narrative of Reese's manufactured rivalry with Clark, recognizing that the claim's audience was likely composed of those who had embraced a negative perception of Reese.

The rating of "Unfounded" was appropriate given the complete lack of evidence for the claim and the demonstrable fabrication of the story. Snopes's inclusion of context about the Reese-Clark dynamic and the earlier debunked claim about Reese losing $50 million in endorsements positioned this specific rumor within a pattern of attacks against the player.

However, the Snopes article could have gone further in several important directions. First, the article did not thoroughly explore the role of AI-generated content in sports disinformation as a systemic issue. While it noted that AI tools were used, it did not examine the broader infrastructure—including monetization incentives, platform amplification algorithms, and the growing ecosystem of sports rumor channels—that makes such content economically viable and algorithmically promoted. This is a critical omission because the claim about Reese is not an isolated incident but rather a case study in a rapidly growing disinformation category.

Second, the article's analysis of the gendered and racial dimensions of the claim was underdeveloped. Snopes noted Reese's "villain" persona but did not engage meaningfully with the extensive scholarship and journalism on how Black female athletes are disproportionately subjected to character attacks, steroid accusations, and professionalism critiques compared to their white counterparts. The accusation of steroid use specifically invokes a long history of racialized suspicions about Black athletes' bodies and achievements—a pattern that runs from Jesse Owens through Jackie Robinson to Serena Williams. The Snopes article would have benefited from at least acknowledging this context.

Third, the article focused entirely on the specific false claim without examining the broader information ecosystem that sustains it. The same YouTube channels and Facebook pages that propagated the Reese steroid rumor likely produce similar content about other athletes. A more systemic analysis would have helped readers understand that they were not encountering a single false story but rather a content-production model built on fabricated controversy.

Finally, the article's sourcing was thin. Aside from links to the viral videos themselves and two articles about the Reese-Clark dynamic, the article cited no sources on performance-enhancing drug testing in the WNBA, no interviews with league officials, and no independent verification beyond the absence of reporting from major outlets. While "absence of evidence" is a legitimate investigative approach, a more robust article would have actively sought statements from the Chicago Sky, the WNBA, or Reese's representatives.

Another notable omission is that the article did not examine the financial incentives driving AI-generated sports disinformation. YouTube channels like BasketBuzz operate within a monetization ecosystem where sensationalism directly correlates with revenue. The Reese steroid video's 150,000 views likely generated meaningful ad revenue for its anonymous creators, creating an economic model that incentivizes future fabrications. The article could have educated readers about this dynamic, equipping them to recognize that viral sports rumors are often the product of cold economic calculation rather than genuine journalistic or fan-driven enterprise. When readers understand that someone is making money from their outrage or engagement, they are better positioned to evaluate the content critically. This omission is consequential because the same channels that fabricated the Reese story are likely still producing similar content about other athletes, and the financial incentives have not changed.

## 4. Why This Matters

The Angel Reese steroid use rumor matters for several interconnected reasons that extend well beyond a single falsehood about a single athlete.

First, this case exemplifies the growing problem of AI-generated disinformation targeting individual public figures, particularly women and people of color. The technology required to produce convincing video narratives with synthetic voiceovers has become cheap and accessible enough that virtually anyone can create content that looks and sounds like professional journalism. When such content is amplified by algorithm-driven platforms that prioritize engagement over accuracy, a fabricated story can reach hundreds of thousands of viewers within days. The Reese case demonstrates that this is not a future threat but a present reality.

Second, the targeting of Reese specifically reflects deeply embedded patterns of misogynoir—the intersection of misogyny and anti-Black racism that shapes how Black women are perceived and treated in public life. The accusation of steroid use carries particular weight in the context of women's sports, where Black female athletes have long been subjected to suspicion about their bodies, their femininity, and the legitimacy of their achievements. The Williams sisters, Caster Semenya, and countless others have faced similar accusations, often tied to racist and sexist assumptions about what female athletes should look like and how they should behave. Reese, who built her brand around confidence, competitiveness, and unapologetic self-expression, was a predictable target.

Third, the willingness of audiences to believe and share this claim without evidence points to a troubling dynamic in sports fandom and media consumption. The narrative that Reese was a "villain" who deserved her comeuppance was so widely accepted by a segment of the audience that they did not require evidence to accept a story about her downfall. This predisposition to believe negative information about disliked figures is a well-documented cognitive bias and one that disinformation producers actively exploit. When combined with the algorithmic amplification of such content, the result is an information ecosystem in which falsehoods that confirm existing prejudices spread faster than corrections.

Fourth, the case illustrates the limitations of the standard fact-checking model. By the time Snopes published its debunking, the videos had already accumulated hundreds of thousands of views. The correction likely reached a fraction of that audience. This "asymmetry of reach" between false claims and their corrections is a persistent challenge in disinformation research and one that the Reese case vividly illustrates. The story was designed to go viral before it could be debunked, and it succeeded.

## 5. Research Notes

The investigation into the Angel Reese steroid rumor reveals several patterns consistent with organized disinformation operations, though the available evidence points more toward opportunistic content creators than coordinated state actors.

The channel that produced the most widely circulated video, BasketBuzz, appears to be part of a network of sports rumor channels that generate high volumes of content using AI tools. These channels typically repurpose publicly available footage, combine it with AI-generated scripts and voiceovers, and publish multiple times daily. Their business model relies on YouTube's monetization system, in which higher view counts generate advertising revenue. The Reese video's 150,000 views would have generated modest but real income for its creators, creating a financial incentive for continued fabrication.

The use of AI-generated content poses particular challenges for fact-checkers. Traditional verification methods that rely on tracing claims to identifiable human sources become less effective when the content itself is generated by language models and voice synthesis tools. It is also worth noting that the disclaimer in the BasketBuzz video—stating that the content represented opinions rather than facts—technically shields the creator from platform content policies while doing nothing to prevent the video from being shared as news.

The claim's rapid spread was facilitated by the existing Reese-Clark rivalry narrative, which had been heavily promoted by the WNBA itself and by sports media outlets. While sports leagues routinely market rivalries to increase fan engagement, the Reese-Clark dynamic was notable for the degree to which racial undertones shaped the coverage. Clark, who is white, was typically framed as the wholesome, technically skilled hero, while Reese, who is Black, was framed as the brash, confrontational villain. This dichotomy, established during the 2023 NCAA women's basketball championship game, persisted into the professional ranks and preconditioned audiences to accept negative stories about Reese.

The absence of any formal denial from the Chicago Sky, the WNBA, or Reese herself is notable. In most cases when a viral false claim targets a public figure, the subject or their representatives issue a statement within days. The lack of response suggests either that the parties involved judged the claim too absurd to warrant a response—a risky strategy in a disinformation environment—or that they were unaware of the extent of its spread.

Platform accountability is another dimension worth noting. YouTube's content moderation policies at the time of the Reese video's spread were theoretically designed to catch content that violates community guidelines, including misinformation presented as factual news. However, the BasketBuzz video's disclaimer—stating that the content represented opinions rather than facts—appears to have been sufficient to evade automated detection systems. This tactic of embedding disclaimers that are visible in the video description but absent from the script and thumbnail is increasingly common among AI-generated disinformation channels. It represents a cat-and-mouse game between content moderators and bad actors, one that platforms have yet to develop effective defenses against. The Reese case provides a clear example of how platform policies designed to protect free expression can also shield deliberately misleading content from removal.

## 6. Verdict

**Snopes Rating: Unfounded — Upheld.**

The claim that Angel Reese was fired by the Chicago Sky for steroid use is entirely fabricated. There is no evidence that Reese was fired, that she tested positive for performance-enhancing drugs, or that the Chicago Sky took any disciplinary action against her. The videos promoting this claim were generated with AI tools and contained no credible sources, no corroborating reporting, and no statements from any relevant parties.

Snopes correctly identified the claim as unfounded and provided adequate evidence for this conclusion. However, the article underplayed the systemic dimensions of the problem—particularly the AI-generated disinformation infrastructure, the racialized and gendered nature of the targeting, and the asymmetry between the spread of the false claim and the reach of its correction.

The Truth Vault affirms the Snopes rating while recommending that future coverage of similar incidents engage more deeply with the social dynamics that make such claims believable and the economic incentives that drive their production. The Angel Reese case is not an anomaly; it is a template for a growing category of disinformation that will continue to target public figures, especially women and people of color, for as long as the economic and algorithmic incentives remain in place.

Readers should treat any claim about an athlete's firing, suspension, or disciplinary action that originates from unknown channels or lacks confirmation from established sports media with extreme skepticism. When the only sources are AI-generated videos posted by anonymous channels, the claim should be presumed false until independently verified.
