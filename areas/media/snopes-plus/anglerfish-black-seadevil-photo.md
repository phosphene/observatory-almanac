## The Claim

In late September 2025, a Reddit thread on r/interesting went viral after a user posted a graphic consisting of two stacked photographs under the title "A photo of an anglerfish's actual size." The top image showed a dark, unsettling fish underwater, its characteristic bioluminescent lure extending from a stalk above its enormous open mouth — a precise match for the cultural image of the anglerfish that has lodged in popular imagination since the creature was made famous by the 2003 Pixar film *Finding Nemo*. The bottom image showed the same type of fish — small, dark, spheroid — being held in the palm of a gloved human hand, its entire body fitting within the span of the fingers.

The graphic gathered tens of thousands of upvotes within days. A similar side-by-side had appeared on Instagram months earlier, in July 2025, with a comparable number of likes. A Facebook post from February 2025 had also circulated extensively. The claim implicit in the posts was simple but striking: the terrifying deep-sea predator, the creature of nightmares and Pixar aesthetics, is in fact a small animal that fits in a human hand.

A second-order claim was also embedded in the framing: that both images showed the same species, and together accurately depicted the typical size of that species. This is the claim that requires verification, because photographs can mislead about scale, and because "the same species" requires confirmation that the images were not mismatched.

---

## What's Actually True

Both photographs are authentic, unmanipulated images. They were not produced by artificial intelligence. They were not digitally altered to change the apparent size of the fish relative to the hand. They show the same species — or, more precisely, they show what expert sources consistently identify as the same species — and together they accurately convey what a fish of that species looks like in a human hand.

The photographs originated with a real expedition. The Spanish marine conservation NGO Condrik Tenerife, whose mission focuses on the preservation of sharks and rays in Atlantic waters, posted a video of the anglerfish to Instagram on 5 February 2025. The video showed the fish being handled and released during what appeared to be an expedition in shallow or nearshore waters off Tenerife, one of Spain's Canary Islands in the northeastern Atlantic. Other expedition participants subsequently posted their own photographs and videos. One participant, photographer Marc Martín (Instagram: @vidamarina.tenerife), posted a carousel of photographs that included the underwater image used as the top panel of the viral graphic. The bottom panel — showing the fish in the gloved hand — appeared first in an 11 February 2025 post by Dr. Carlos Cuadrado Gómez-Serranillos on X (Twitter), and a TikTok video from 13 February 2025 by user @jara.natura, who was on the expedition, confirmed the same gloved-hand scene from a different angle.

The species identity of the fish was confirmed in a March 2025 article by marine science journalist Molly Tsongas for *Oceanus*, the ocean science magazine published by the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution. The article, "A Rare Black Seadevil Anglerfish Sees the Light" (Tsongas, Molly, *Oceanus*, March 20, 2025, Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution), identified the fish as a black seadevil anglerfish. The black seadevil — *Melanocetus johnsonii* — is one of more than 200 recognised species of deep-sea anglerfish, placed in the order Lophiiformes. Females of the species typically grow to approximately 15–20 centimetres in length, or about 6 inches, according to the *Oceanus* article and a Monterey Bay Aquarium species profile. The Monterey Bay Aquarium lists a maximum female length of approximately 20 centimetres (Monterey Bay Aquarium, "Black Seadevil Anglerfish," accessed October 2025). Males of the species are dramatically smaller: they remain under 3 centimetres, typically under an inch, throughout their lives.

The fish in the viral photographs was almost certainly a female, not a male. The evidence for this is direct: the fish possessed a visible bioluminescent lure — the distinctive appendage extending from the fish's forehead on a modified dorsal spine, tipped with a light-producing organ called the esca. According to the Smithsonian Ocean portal (Magbilang, Lila, "Anglerfish Lure Prey throughout the Ocean," Smithsonian Institution, September 2024), the bioluminescent lure is found only in females of deep-sea anglerfish species. Males lack the lure entirely. An anglerfish with a visible lure is, by anatomy, a female. At 6 inches or so, she fits comfortably in a palm. This is not a trick of scale — it is the actual size.

The title "palm-sized" is therefore accurate — indeed, it slightly understates the typical size in a way that might be surprising from the other direction. The fish shown is not miniaturised by comparison with a child's hand or abnormally large gloves. The animal genuinely is small enough to cup in an adult human hand.

## Why People Believe This

The widespread surprise generated by the images — and the question of whether they might be deceptive — arises from a specific and well-defined mismatch between cultural representation and biological fact.

**The Pixar fixation and what it cost marine biology.** The anglerfish's popular image was substantially shaped by *Finding Nemo* (Stanton, Andrew, Pixar/Disney, 2003), in which a large anglerfish — drawn roughly to match visual impressions of a female *Melanocetus* or similar species — serves as a predatory nighttime threat to Marlin and Dory. The sequence is brief, visually striking, and effective at coding the anglerfish as massive and terrifying. What the film does not convey — because it was not relevant to the narrative function of the scene — is that a real black seadevil is approximately the size of a tennis ball. The cultural image of the anglerfish is calibrated for cinematic threat; the biological reality is calibrated for hunting small crustaceans and fish in a pressure environment 200–1,000 metres below the surface.

This is a common pattern in popular representations of deep-sea animals broadly. The deep ocean is narratively coded as dangerous, alien, and vast, and its animals are frequently depicted at scales that emphasise threat. The giant squid — *Architeuthis dux* — grows to genuinely large sizes (confirmed individuals up to approximately 13 metres, with arms; Roper & Boss, 1982, *Scientific American*, 247: 96–105), but is more commonly depicted in cultural imagery at scales that overwhelm ships. The colossal squid (*Mesonychoteuthis hamiltoni*) reaches substantial mass (the largest confirmed specimen weighed approximately 494 kilograms; Guerra et al., 2011, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, 278: 2006–2011) but is still not the ship-sinking behemoth of mythology. The deep ocean generates narrative inflation because it is genuinely inaccessible and genuinely strange — conditions that make it difficult for popular imagination to be corrected by ordinary experience.

**The "too terrifying to be small" heuristic.** There is a cognitive asymmetry in how we process threatening versus non-threatening animals. Animals that occupy high menace positions in human aesthetic and emotional responses tend to be imagined at larger-than-actual sizes. Spiders, for example, are consistently estimated at larger sizes than their actual dimensions by arachnophobic individuals (Riskind et al., 2012, various psychology of phobia literature). The anglerfish's design — huge gaping jaw, needle teeth, bioluminescent lure in the void, nightmarish face — places it very high on the perceived-menace spectrum. Our intuition resists assigning it the size of a golf ball. The palm-sized viral images therefore create genuine cognitive dissonance: the face in the image matches the menace template; the scale in the image does not match what that template predicts.

**The epistemics of size representation in photography.** One reason the images prompted a fact-check question rather than simple acceptance is that photographs are genuinely unreliable instruments for conveying scale. A fish held in a small hand close to the camera can look smaller than it is; a fish photographed alone in open water provides no scale reference at all. The viral graphic's juxtaposition — underwater fish, then fish in palm — is explicitly designed to make the scale legible, but design to make scale legible and actual scale correspondence are not the same thing. Viewers familiar with photographic manipulation, scale tricks, and misleading composition were right to ask whether the two images were genuinely the same species, genuinely at the same scale, and genuinely from the same expedition. These are proportionate questions given the prevalence of misleading scale-comparison imagery on social media.

**Epistemic novelty: the extreme sexual dimorphism as depth-ocean adaptation.** The genuinely surprising and underappreciated aspect of anglerfish biology is not the fish's size per se, but the extraordinary degree of sexual dimorphism it represents. The male *Melanocetus johnsonii* — under an inch long, lacking a lure, swimming free in deep water until it can find a female — is not a smaller version of the female in the way that size differences typically work in sexually dimorphic species. It is a fundamentally different type of organism, organised entirely around one biological function. Some anglerfish species take this further into what is called "sexual parasitism": males attach themselves to females and eventually fuse with their circulatory systems, losing all independent existence and becoming permanently dependent on the female's bloodstream for nutrition. They are, in the most literal possible biological sense, part of the female — reduced to reproductive organs on her body.

This phenomenon — described in detail across the anglerfish literature, including Pietsch, Theodore W., "Dimorphism, Parasitism, and Sex Revisited: Modes of Reproduction Among Deep-Sea Ceratioid Anglerfishes (Teleostei: Lophiiformes)" (Pietsch, 2005, *Ichthyological Research*, 52: 207–236) — is one of the most extreme examples of sexual selection and sex-differentiated life history in vertebrate biology. The males' minute size is not incidentally amusing; it is the product of intense selection pressure for finding and attaching to females in a nearly lightless, three-dimensional, food-sparse environment where encounters are rare. Small males are metabolically cheaper to produce and can maximise their search radius before exhausting energy reserves. The extreme dimorphism is the solution to a specific environmental problem, and understanding it reframes the viral photograph: what looks like a "small fish" is specifically a female anglerfish — a predator adapted over millions of years to hunt in the dark ocean — and she is exactly the size she is supposed to be.

## Verdict

The photographs are authentic, unmanipulated, and show what they claim to show: a female black seadevil anglerfish (*Melanocetus johnsonii*) from a February 2025 expedition by Condrik Tenerife off the Canary Islands. The species is real, the expedition is documented, and the fish's size — approximately 15–20 centimetres for an adult female — is accurately conveyed by the palm-held image. Snopes rated the claim "True."

The larger truth the images convey — that a creature the popular imagination has rendered monstrous is, in biological reality, small enough to cup in a hand — is not an embarrassment or a deflation. It is a fact about extraordinary adaptation, extreme sexual dimorphism, and the genuine strangeness of deep-sea life, none of which requires inflation to be interesting.

## The Wider Picture

**Anglerfish biology and the deep ocean's illumination problem.** The black seadevil's lure solves a problem that is, in the biology of the deep ocean, nearly universal: how do you find food and mates in an environment where light does not penetrate and where the volume of habitat is enormous relative to population density? The esca — the light organ at the tip of the lure — contains bioluminescent bacteria of the genus *Aliivibrio* (formerly *Photobacterium*), which produce light through a luciferin-luciferase reaction requiring oxygen. The fish positions the esca in front of its open mouth, and small fish or crustaceans attracted to the light in the otherwise lightless environment swim toward it and are captured by a rapid strike. This is a complete system: the fish produces no metabolic energy for movement in hunting (it waits for prey to come to it), and the bioluminescent lure does not require the fish to be large. A tennis-ball-sized predator with a light on its head is, in the deep ocean, a completely sufficient hunting apparatus.

**Bioluminescence as the deep ocean's dominant communication channel.** The anglerfish lure is one of hundreds of independently evolved bioluminescent systems in the ocean. Research published by Haddock, Moline, and Case in *Annual Review of Marine Science* (Haddock et al., 2010, vol. 2, pp. 443–493, "Bioluminescence in the Sea") documented the extraordinary diversity of marine bioluminescence and estimated that the majority of deep-sea organisms produce light in some form. Bioluminescence evolved independently many times in marine lineages — a striking example of convergent evolution driven by the same selective pressure in different phylogenetic starting points. The anglerfish lure is among the most specialised of these systems because it is not merely defensive (startling a predator) or communicative (signalling to conspecifics) but specifically predatory (luring prey into a terminal encounter). It represents one of the deep ocean's most clearly legible evolutionary solutions.

**The Condrik Tenerife expedition and citizen science documentation.** The fact that the viral images originated with a marine conservation NGO working primarily on shark and ray preservation, and spread through social media posts from expedition participants, illustrates a relatively recent phenomenon in marine biology: citizen-level documentation of rare or unusual deep-sea specimens brought to the surface through commercial or recreational fishing operations. The anglerfish found off Tenerife was apparently retrieved from the water by expedition members — whether it was brought up alive, found dead on the surface, or captured in some other way is not fully documented in the public record. The encounter was documented photographically and videographically, shared on social media, and ultimately analysed by a Woods Hole journalist who confirmed the species. This is not peer-reviewed science, but it is a form of distributed observational documentation that has become increasingly important for tracking species encounters, range shifts, and unusual specimens in the age of camera-equipped smartphones and real-time social media sharing.

**Deep-sea anglerfish rarity and the problem of sampling.** The Tenerife encounter was explicitly described by the *Oceanus* article as remarkable precisely because black seadevil anglerfish are rarely seen in surface conditions or nearshore waters. They are bathypelagic animals — inhabitants of the dark ocean at depth — and encounters with live or freshly dead specimens at the surface are infrequent enough to be newsworthy within marine biology. This rarity is not because the fish are rare in the sense of low global population; it is because their habitat is remote, they are not targeted by commercial fishing, and their small size means they are not reliably captured even in fine-mesh research trawls. The encounter off Tenerife is the kind of observational windfall that occasionally provides direct specimens of animals ordinarily known only from trawl captures and deep-water vehicle video footage.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by Emery Winter, published 13 October 2025, is a well-structured example of image provenance verification combined with biological species confirmation.

**Provenance tracing to social media source.** Winter traced both images to their specific points of first posting: Marc Martín's Instagram carousel for the underwater image, and Dr. Carlos Cuadrado Gómez-Serranillos's X post for the palm-held image. The additional TikTok video by @jara.natura — showing the gloved-hand scene from a different angle — provided corroborating documentation that the palm-held image was not a deceptive scale manipulation. This multi-source provenance check is the methodologically correct approach for viral image verification: a single source is vulnerable to isolated fabrication; converging documentation from multiple independent sources at the same event is much harder to fake.

**Species identification via marine science journalism.** Winter used the *Oceanus* article by Tsongas as the species identification source rather than attempting direct taxonomic determination from the image. This is the right methodological move for a generalist fact-checker: the appropriate question ("What species is this?") requires expert biological input, and citing a credentialed source (Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution's science magazine) is more defensible than the fact-checker attempting to make a species identification from a photograph. The Monterey Bay Aquarium's species profile provided additional corroboration of the size characteristics.

**The significance of the lure.** The fact-checker noted, correctly, that the bioluminescent lure is present on the fish in the photographs and that the lure is exclusive to female deep-sea anglerfish. This is both a critical species-verification detail and an important biological insight that the viral posts did not typically include. The lure's presence proves this is a female; the female's typical size range (up to 6–8 inches) confirms the palm-sized claim; the biological explanation for why females are large and males are tiny (sexual selection, parasitism in some species) provides the explanatory framework within which the size observation makes sense. A fact-check that includes these links between evidence and mechanism is more educationally valuable than one that merely confirms or denies.

**What the fact-check cannot fully address.** The fact-check appropriately notes that Winter reached out to Marc Martín for confirmation and had not received a reply at time of publication. This is standard practice and appropriate transparency. What the fact-check does not fully address is the exact circumstances under which the fish was encountered — was it brought up alive, found dead, temporarily captured for photography? The TikTok video appears to show a live fish being handled and released, which would indicate the encounter was a surface-level find rather than a deep-trawl capture, which would itself be remarkable for a bathypelagic species. The full natural history of this specific encounter — which may ultimately be more interesting than the photographs alone — remains to be fully documented.

The black seadevil in the gloved hand is a more honest ambassador for the deep ocean than any cultural fabrication. It is small enough to cup, alien enough to disturb, and armed with more biological sophistication than a dozen feature films have managed to convey. The surprise provoked by the photographs is genuine and appropriate — but what should surprise us is not that the fish is smaller than expected, but that something this intricate and this specifically adapted exists at all.
