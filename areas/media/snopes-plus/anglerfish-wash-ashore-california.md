## §1 — Claim and Verdict

In early May 2021, photographs of an approximately 18-inch fish washed ashore at Crystal Cove State Park in Newport Beach, California, went viral following a post by Davey's Locker Sportfishing and Whale Watching that was shared more than 125,000 times. The animal was described as an "amazingly rare" Pacific footballfish — a species of anglerfish in the order Lophiiformes — found at approximately 2,000 to 3,300 feet below the ocean surface under normal circumstances. The fish appeared in excellent condition, lacking obvious trauma, and was photographed by beach visitor Ben Estes who alerted park rangers.

Snopes rated this claim **True**. The Pacific footballfish (*Himantolophus sagamius*) was confirmed by California Academy of Sciences Curator of Ichthyology Dave Catania, who also provided substantive commentary on rarity assessment, cause of death speculation, and collection pathway. The California Department of Fish and Wildlife (CDFW) confirmed possession of the specimen and arranged for transfer to the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles. The species identification, the stranding location and date, and the photographic record are all firmly established.

The interesting epistemic dimension of this story centers on the contested meaning of "extremely rare" — a characterization that the Snopes investigation meaningfully complicated — and on what the stranding event of a deep-sea fish can and cannot tell us about deep ocean ecology.

## §2 — Evidence, Timeline, and Provenance

**Documented timeline:**

- **May 7, 2021**: Ben Estes discovers an unusual fish on the sand at Crystal Cove State Park, Newport Beach. He alerts park rangers and lifeguards with the Orange Coast District of California Department of Parks and Recreation.
- **May 8, 2021**: Davey's Locker Sportfishing and Whale Watching posts photographs to Facebook. The post rapidly accumulates viral reach.
- **May 10, 2021**: Crystal Cove State Park posts photographs. Snopes publishes initial article by Madison Dapcevich, citing CDFW contact and initial species identification. CDFW confirms the specimen is frozen at Crystal Cove.
- **May 11, 2021**: Snopes updates article with commentary from Dave Catania, Curator of Ichthyology at the California Academy of Sciences.
- **Post-publication**: CDFW arranges transfer of frozen specimen to the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles.

**Primary institutional sources:**

- Dave Catania, California Academy of Sciences, Curator of Ichthyology (direct email and comments to Snopes)
- CDFW (direct communication with Snopes, confirmed by Orange Coast District, California Department of Parks and Recreation)
- Crystal Cove State Park (Facebook post with photographs)
- California Academy of Sciences species page for *Himantolophus sagamius*

**Species identification:** The fish was confirmed as *Himantolophus sagamius*, the Pacific footballfish, by expert assessment. The species is in the family Himantolophidae, order Lophiiformes. It is characterized by its illicium — a long dorsal fin modified into a fishing-rod-like appendage bearing a bioluminescent esca (lure organ) at the tip — along with needle-sharp teeth, extremely wide jaws capable of swallowing prey up to its own size, a rounded body shape giving the "football" common name, and prickly dermal spines.

**Evidence quality assessment:** This is a case with extremely strong primary evidence: a physical specimen in state agency custody, photographic documentation, expert identification from a named curator with institutional affiliation, and a species identity confirmed against published taxonomic descriptions. The "Extremely Rare" qualification is the only component requiring epistemic refinement.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: What "Rare" Means in Deep-Sea Biology and the Limits of Absence-Based Rarity

The central epistemic contribution of the Snopes article — and the most scientifically interesting aspect of this story — is the quotation from Dave Catania:

> "The deep oceans are poorly sampled, so it's possible they are common at depth, we just don't know."

This statement encapsulates one of the most significant structural problems in deep-sea ecology: the conflation of *rarely collected* with *biologically rare*. These are categorically different claims, and public media coverage almost universally treats them as synonymous.

**The sampling problem in deep-sea ichthyology:** The global deep ocean — defined as depths below approximately 200 meters — covers roughly 65% of Earth's surface area and constitutes approximately 95% of the habitable volume of the planet's biosphere (Angel, 1997, *Journal of the Marine Biological Association of the United Kingdom*). Despite this enormous extent, the vast majority of our knowledge about deep-sea fish diversity derives from trawl surveys, deep-tow samples, and submersible expeditions that collectively represent a vanishingly small sampling effort relative to habitat volume. Nielsen et al. (2019, *Nature*) documented a new species of snailfish (*Pseudoliparis swirei*) from the Mariana Trench at depths below 7,000 meters — indicating that novel species are still being described from deep environments. This sampling limitation is not a historical artifact but an ongoing reality.

**Rarity in museum collections vs. ecological abundance:** *Himantolophus sagamius* and related footballfish species belong to the family Himantolophidae, which includes approximately 22 described species. Very few museum specimens of most himantolophid species exist globally. Bertelsen (1951, *Dana Report*) published the foundational monograph on Ceratioid anglerfish and documented the extreme sparseness of type specimens for many deep-sea anglerfish families. Subsequent revisions (Bertelsen & Krefft, 1988, *Steenstrupia*) have barely doubled specimen counts for several species. When Catania noted it is "rare to have a fish of this species in a museum collection," he was invoking this collection history — not making a categorical claim about population abundance.

**The epistemic novelty here** is the explicit acknowledgment, from a domain expert with direct knowledge of the specimen, that "rare" in colloquial and media use does not translate cleanly to rare in population ecological use. This distinction has significant implications:

1. **Conservation framing:** If media characterize Pacific footballfish as "extremely rare" based on collection scarcity, members of the public may infer IUCN-level conservation concern. But *Himantolophus sagamius* does not appear on IUCN Red List assessments as threatened; its status is simply "not evaluated" or "data deficient" — because sampling is insufficient to produce a population estimate. "Extremely rare" conflates a data-deficiency problem with a population-decline problem.

2. **Scientific inference from stranding events:** The fact that a Pacific footballfish washed ashore in southern California, while unusual, cannot be used to draw conclusions about population abundance changes, range shifts, or ecological anomalies. It is consistent with any of: a healthy individual unable to maintain depth due to disease; a moribund or recently dead individual transported by surface currents; a disoriented individual forced toward the surface by unknown factors. Catania's statement that "disease might have rendered her incapable of maintaining depth" and that "ocean currents could have played a part" enumerates plausible mechanisms without privileging one.

3. **The gender identification gap:** The specimen was confirmed as female by later assessment (the California Academy of Sciences page describes the secondary sexual dimorphism of footballfish). Catania's comment about male anglerfish becoming "sexual parasites" — fusing to females and losing most organ systems — is accurately reported in Snopes and represents one of the more genuinely extraordinary life history strategies in vertebrate biology. Male himantolophids undergo a metamorphosis in which they use well-developed olfactory organs to locate and physically attach to a female; upon fusion, the male loses his eyes, internal organs, immune system, and virtually everything except the testes, relying entirely on female circulatory nourishment (Munk, 2000, *Journal of the Marine Biological Association of the United Kingdom*). The specimen's "excellent condition" recorded by Catania is thus more informative for a female than for a male.

## §4 — Counterarguments, Contested Claims, and Uncertainties

**The "extremely rare" contested claim:** No named source in the Snopes article or primary reporting actually defended the "extremely rare" description with biological precision. Davey's Locker used "amazingly rare" as a lay characterization; this language was amplified through social sharing. Catania's on-record clarification — that rarity in collections does not imply rarity in the wild — is the only expert assessment of the rarity claim in the record, and it directly qualifies the viral characterization.

**Cause of death:** Catania described the specimen as appearing to be in "excellent condition," suggesting it was not killed by predation. The cause of death was not determined; Catania suggested a necropsy "might shed light on the cause of death" and proposed disease and ocean currents as possible contributing factors. No public record of a necropsy result is available, as the specimen was frozen at Crystal Cove before CDFW arranged transfer to the Natural History Museum. Whether the museum ultimately performed a necropsy and produced any findings appears not to have been documented in public-facing sources.

**Collection and accessioning:** CDFW's role as the initial holding agency, and the arranged transfer to the Natural History Museum of Los Angeles, represents standard protocol for unusual marine vertebrate strandings in California. Whether the specimen was formally accessioned, preserved, and made available for scientific study is a detail not resolved in the Snopes article. Proper accessioning is significant for long-term scientific value: a frozen strandling transferred promptly to a natural history collection can yield histological, genetic, and morphological data; one that remains in custody for long periods with poor cold-chain management loses research value rapidly.

**Sexual dimorphism and the sex of the specimen:** The Snopes article notes that "it is not yet known whether the California footballfish is male or female" as of initial reporting, then discusses the dramatic sexual dimorphism. However, the California Academy of Sciences public description appears to make clear that all large footballfish observed (including this specimen) are female — males are too small to produce a visually prominent animal of 18-inch size. The "not yet known" qualification in early reporting reflects the timeline of expert assessment rather than genuine ambiguity about the specimen's likely sex.

## §5 — Broader Scientific and Conservation Context

**Deep-sea anglerfish ecology:**

The 300+ species of anglerfish in the order Lophiiformes include both shallow-water and deep-sea families. The deep-sea ceratioid anglerfish — which include the footballfish — occupy mesopelagic and bathypelagic zones (200–4,000 m depth) and are characterized by extreme sexual dimorphism, bioluminescent lures, and adaptation to low food density environments. Pietsch (2009, *Oceanic Anglerfishes: Extraordinary Diversity in the Deep Sea*, University of California Press) provides the most comprehensive systematic treatment of the group and documents the geographical distribution, reproductive biology, and collection history of all described species.

**Bioluminescence mechanisms:**

The Pacific footballfish's esca (lure bulb) glows via photobacteria that emit light through a symbiotic relationship with the fish. Unlike intrinsic bioluminescence (common in dinoflagellates and other taxa), anglerfish lure luminescence depends on maintaining living bacterial symbionts inside the esca tissue. The bacteria enter through pores in the esca wall and are sustained by the host's vascular system. Herring (2002, *The Biology of the Deep Ocean*, Oxford University Press) covers the bioluminescent mechanisms of deep-sea fish in detail; the anglerfish bacterial esca system is one of the most elaborated examples of bioluminescent symbiosis in vertebrates.

**California as a deep-sea stranding site:**

Southern California's coastline sits above some of the deepest waters in the Eastern Pacific, including the Santa Catalina Basin (maximum ~1,300 m), the San Clemente Basin (~2,100 m), and underwater canyon systems extending from Newport Beach — directly adjacent to Crystal Cove State Park — into deep water. The Newport Canyon is a prominent submarine canyon that heads in shallow nearshore waters and descends steeply into the San Pedro Basin. This bathymetric profile explains why unusual deep-sea animals occasionally reach the Newport Beach/Crystal Cove shoreline via submarine currents: deep water is directly accessible to canyon-head upwelling dynamics and surface storm events that can displace bottom-associated or mid-water fauna.

**Prior Pacific footballfish strandings:**

This was not the first Pacific footballfish stranding in California. A 2019 specimen was found and photographed on the same Crystal Cove State Park beach, and a 2001 specimen was documented in Malibu. The apparent recurrence at Crystal Cove in multiple years is consistent with the local submarine canyon system acting as an intermittent delivery mechanism for deep-water fauna.

## §6 — Implications and Significance

The Pacific footballfish story achieved enormous viral success because it combined genuine visual novelty (the anglerfish is one of the most striking-looking vertebrates known) with a narrative framing of rarity that made seeing one feel event-worthy. The Snopes investigation's primary epistemic contribution was to complicate that rarity framing without dismissing the legitimate newsworthiness of the stranding.

**For deep-sea communication:** The story illustrates the challenge of translating collection rarity into public understanding without generating false conservation urgency. The Pacific footballfish may well be relatively common in the deep ocean; its collection scarcity reflects the scarcity of deep-sea trawl operations, not a depleted population. This distinction matters for public science literacy, which should calibrate "remarkable" against "endangered" rather than conflating them.

**For California coastal ecology:** The proximity of Los Angeles Basin submarine canyons to urban beaches creates a recurring opportunity for unusual deep-sea strandings that are genuinely scientifically valuable. The institutional chain from park visitor → park rangers → CDFW → Natural History Museum represents a functional specimen collection pipeline that should be maintained and well-documented. The transfer of the 2021 specimen to the natural history museum, if completed with proper cold storage integrity, creates a research-accessible voucher specimen that could inform morphological, genetic, and disease studies.

**For fact-checking methodology:** The article represents a successful application of expert sourcing that does more than simply confirm the primary claim. By including Catania's nuanced commentary on rarity and cause of death, the Snopes article provides genuine intellectual value above simple binary fact adjudication. The "True" verdict is correct; the deeper contribution is the rarity clarification that the reader encounters within the confirmation.

The EARC classification of **Established (E)** reflects identification by a named expert from an institutional scientific body, physical specimen in state agency custody, and multiple photographic records. The cause of death and long-term collection outcome remain open — but the core claim, that a Pacific footballfish washed ashore on a California beach in May 2021, is among the most thoroughly corroborated events in this batch.
