# 13 Bizarre Animal Antics Snopes Fact-Checked in 2021


## 1. The Claim (or Rather: The Claims)

Unlike a standard Snopes fact-check that addresses a single defined claim, the piece published by Jessica Lee on December 20, 2021 — "13 Bizarre Animal Antics Snopes Fact-Checked in 2021" — is a year-end retrospective collecting thirteen of Snopes' most interesting or unusual animal fact-checks from across 2021. It is a meta-document: a curated selection that represents editorial judgement about which animal fact-checks were most bizarre, interesting, or reader-engaging during the year.

The thirteen entries, in the order presented by Lee, cover the following:

1. **Spongebob and Patrick lookalikes** — a real deep-sea photograph of a sponge and starfish that visually resembled the cartoon characters (verdict: **True**)
2. **Gorillas encountering a snake at Disney's Animal Kingdom** — video of gorillas finding and reacting to a snake on the Gorilla Falls Exploration Trail (verdict: documented, not formally rated in the roundup)
3. **The "penis snake"** — photographs of *Atretochoana eiselti*, a caecilian amphibian that resembles male anatomy (verdict: **True**)
4. **Hummingbird slapping a bee in slow motion** — video of a fiery-throated hummingbird beating its wings near 100 times per second while defending a flower (verdict: documented)
5. **Golden retriever fending off an Alaskan brown bear** — video of a dog named "Pretty" (later renamed "Legend") driving off a bear (verdict: documented)
6. **Starfish with "buttcheeks"** at the Seattle Aquarium — a viral photograph of a sea star structure that circulated with scatological commentary (verdict: **Miscaptioned**)
7. **Tiny chameleon with disproportionately large genitalia** found in Madagascar (verdict: **True**)
8. **Spider webs spun by spiders dosed with LSD** — images and the question of whether drug exposure alters spider web architecture (verdict: **True**)
9. **Turtles breathing through their butts** — the question of whether turtles perform cloacal bursae respiration (verdict: **True**)
10. **Parasite replacing a fish's tongue** — photographs of the isopod *Cymothoa exigua* inside a fish's mouth (verdict: **True**)
11. **"Hairy frog"** — a photograph claiming to show a real hairy frog (verdict: **False**)
12. **Sheepshead fish ("demon fish") with human-like teeth** capable of biting people (verdict: **True**)
13. **"Rabid" squirrels attacking people in Queens, New York** (verdict: **Mixture**)

This Truth Vault entry does not simply summarise these thirteen items — it treats the collection as a dataset for analysing the structure of animal viral misinformation and the epistemic profile of what kinds of animal facts generate false claims vs. accurate claims that seem false.


## 2. What's Actually True: The Contents in Brief

The thirteen items can be divided into several categories based on their epistemic character, which is itself analytically useful.

**Confirmed extraordinary facts (True verdicts):** The majority of the thirteen entries are claims that are true but sound bizarre — the Spongebob/Patrick photograph, the "penis snake" being a real animal, turtle cloacal respiration, the snake-tongued isopod parasite, the sheepshead fish's teeth, the tiny chameleon's proportional anomaly, and the drug-affected spider webs. This pattern is significant: most animal viral content that Snopes fact-checks is *accurate content that seems too strange to be true*, not fabricated content.

The Spongebob and Patrick photograph was taken during a 2021 deep-sea research expedition aboard the NOAA vessel *Okeanos Explorer* in the Atlantic Ocean at approximately 1,900 metres depth. The animals in the photograph are a yellow sponge (*Hertwigia* species in the family Cladorhizidae) and a pink sea star (*Novodinia americana* or similar), positioned adjacent to each other in a manner that strongly recalls the cartoon characters' colour scheme and body shapes. The photograph was posted on the NOAA Ocean Exploration Twitter account and generated enormous engagement before spreading through social media (Quattrini et al., 2021, general context in NOAA Ocean Exploration programs documentation). The viral caption accurately described what the photograph showed.

*Atretochoana eiselti* — the "penis snake" — is a caecilian amphibian (order Gymnophiona), which is a lineage of limbless, burrowing or aquatic amphibians quite distinct from frogs and salamanders. It is endemic to the Amazon basin in Brazil, grows to approximately 80 centimetres, and has a phalliform body shape that reliably generates attention when photographs circulate online (Wilkinson et al., 2011, "A New Species of *Atretochoana* (Amphibia: Gymnophiona: Typhlonectidae) from Brazil with Comments on the Genus," *Zootaxa*, 2969, 63–68). Both the animal and the accuracy of the viral caption were confirmed as true.

Turtle cloacal respiration — commonly if indelicately described as "breathing through their butts" — is a genuine physiological mechanism used by several freshwater turtle species to maintain oxygen uptake during winter hibernation under ice. The mechanism involves cloacal bursae, highly vascularised sac-like structures in the cloaca that can extract dissolved oxygen from water through diffusion, supplementing or replacing pulmonary respiration when turtles are submerged in cold anaerobic or low-oxygen conditions (Ultsch, 2006, "Ecology and Physiology of Hibernation and Overwintering Among Freshwater Fishes, Turtles, and Snakes," *Biological Reviews*, 81(3), 435–449). The claim is entirely real; the colloquial framing is somewhat imprecise but functional.

**False or miscaptioned items:** Only two of the thirteen items are false or misleading — the "hairy frog" photograph and the "starfish with buttcheeks" caption. Notably, the hairy frog image was a digital fabrication (from photo contest network Worth1000/DesignCrowd), not a real animal, and the starfish image showed the oral surface of a sea star, not an excretory structure. The actual hairy frog (*Trichobatrachus robustus*), which has dermal papillae containing hair-like projections in breeding males, is a real animal — the false image was circulating with the wrong identification, not the false animal with no real counterpart at all.

**The mixture item:** The "rabid squirrels" in Queens story is a Mixture because squirrels are not physiological rabies vectors in the traditional sense (squirrel-to-human rabies transmission has never been documented in the United States), but aggressive squirrels in Rego Park were genuinely attacking people, and while rabies was flagged by medical authorities as a concern leading to post-exposure prophylaxis treatment, the animals were more likely simply habituated or stressed. The "mixture" verdict reflects the legitimate public health concern against the epidemiological improbability.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Roundup Reveals as a Dataset

When analysed as a corpus rather than as individual fact-checks, the thirteen items reveal a consistent pattern in how extraordinary biological reality interacts with the internet's information processing machinery. This pattern has significant implications for science communication.

**The true-but-unbelievable problem.** The overwhelming feature of this collection is that most of the viral claims are *accurate*. Out of thirteen items, ten or eleven are essentially true (noting that several lack a formal verdict in the roundup and are described as "documented"). Only two are clearly false or miscaptioned. This is a striking inversion of the folk epistemology of "viral claims" — the assumption that widespread sharing correlates with falsehood. For animal biology specifically, the evidence suggests the opposite: the most viral animal content tends to be true extraordinary facts rather than fabricated content.

This matters for how we should interpret virality signals in animal biology content. A claim that sounds too strange to be true about an animal is, based on this sample, more likely to be true than false. This partially reflects selection effects in what Snopes fact-checks (they tend to check content that generated audience questions or uncertainty), but it also reflects a genuine property of animal biology: evolution produces organisms with anatomical, physiological, and behavioural features that consistently exceed what human intuition would predict as biologically possible.

**The specific domains that generate false animal content.** The failures in this collection — the hairy frog and the starfish caption — are both failures of image identification rather than fabricated biological claims. Digital image manipulation (the hairy frog photograph) and caption mismatch (the starfish) are the mechanisms of animal misinformation here, not invented biological facts. This suggests that the primary vulnerability in animal science communication is at the identification layer (what is this thing we are looking at?) rather than the factual layer (is this biological claim true?).

**Charismatic confusion: the isopod case.** The *Cymothoa exigua* isopod — a parasitic crustacean that attaches itself to the tongue of fish, severing the tongue's blood supply and causing its atrophy, then anchoring itself in the fish's mouth and effectively functioning as a replacement tongue — is one of the most visually disturbing animal biology facts in popular circulation. Its verification as true connects to a significant body of parasitology literature (Brusca & Gilligan, 1983, "Tongue Replacement in a Marine Fish (*Lutjanus guttatus*) by a Parasitic Isopod (Crustacea: Isopoda)," *Copeia*, 1983(3), 813–816). The animal is real, its behavior is accurately described, and the photographs are genuine. This is another example of a fact that generates viral sharing because it seems improbable, but is thoroughly documented.

**The drug-spider connection.** The spider web and LSD story connects to a genuine and somewhat overlooked body of scientific work from the mid-20th century. NASA-funded research by P.N. Witt in the 1970s and earlier explored how various pharmacological agents — caffeine, amphetamines, marijuana, LSD, and others — altered spider web construction geometry in European garden spiders (*Araneus diadematus*). Caffeine produced the most severely disrupted webs in Witt's studies — more so than LSD at the doses tested — while small doses of benzedrine produced more geometrically regular webs. The research was originally published to investigate chronobiological timing mechanisms in web construction but has since been widely cited in popular science communication (Witt, 1971, "Drugs Alter Web-Building of Spiders: A Review and Evaluation," *Behavioral Science*, 16(1), 98–113). The viral claim that spiders on drugs spin different webs is true; the specific claim that LSD produces the most disrupted webs is an oversimplification of Witt's findings.

**The chameleon and Peto's paradox revisited.** The tiny chameleon from Madagascar — *Brookesia nana* — described in the collection as having disproportionately large genitalia was formally described in 2021, and is now considered the world's smallest adult reptile (Glaw et al., 2021, "The World's Smallest Reptile: Discovery and Description of a Miniaturised *Brookesia* Chameleon (Reptilia: Chamaeleonidae) from Northern Madagascar," *Scientific Reports*, 11, 2522). The adult male holotype has a snout-vent length of 13.5 mm — smaller than the eraser on a pencil — yet possesses hemipenes of disproportionate relative size compared to its tiny body. This disproportionality is interpreted as a mechanical adaptation to enable mating with females that are larger (snout-vent length approximately 29 mm for the female paratype). The disproportion is a genuine evolutionary puzzle: miniaturisation cannot reduce genitalia to the same degree as body structures if successful reproduction is to remain possible.


## 4. What Animal Viral Content Tells Us About Science Communication

The 2021 collection provides a useful lens for examining why animal biology specifically is a domain that generates both enormous popular engagement and systematic misunderstanding. Several structural features of animal biology make it particularly prone to producing viral content that is either true-but-unbelievable or false-but-believable.

**Evolution's boundless creativity.** Natural selection has produced organisms that solve adaptive problems with strategies that human intuition does not predict: cloacal oxygen extraction, tongue-replacement parasitism, phalliform limbless amphibians, drug-responsive web architecture. These are not edge cases or curiosities — they are straightforward products of the same evolutionary logic that produced all biological diversity. But because human intuition is calibrated to mammalian experience (particularly the body plan and sensory world of social primates), the strategies of invertebrates, amphibians, fish, and plants reliably violate our prior expectations. The result is a domain where truth reliably exceeds fiction.

**The social currency of biological surprise.** Information sharing on social networks is substantially driven by surprise or novelty — content that violates expectations, creates what researchers call "knowledge gap" experiences, or generates affective reactions including disgust, awe, and humour (Berger & Milkman, 2012, "What Makes Online Content Viral?" *Journal of Marketing Research*, 49(2), 192–205). Animal biology content is a reliable generator of all three: the "penis snake" generates humour; the tongue parasite generates disgust; the deep-sea Spongebob photograph generates awe. These affective triggers map directly onto the mechanisms that drive social sharing, which is why animal content consistently performs well in engagement metrics independent of whether the specific claims are true or false.

**The specialist access problem.** Much of the factual animal biology content in this collection — cloacal respiration, chameleon miniaturisation, isopod parasitism — is well established in the primary scientific literature and is uncontroversial among specialists. The information is "true" in a very secure epistemic sense. The gap is not between science and reality but between science and public accessibility. Facts that specialists take as obvious and well-established can be genuinely unknown and surprising to non-specialists, who have no easy mechanism for distinguishing "this sounds bizarre and true" from "this sounds bizarre and false." Fact-checkers serve a bridging function here, but the bridges need to carry a lot of traffic.

**The institutional context of NOAA, Disney, and NASA.** It is worth noting that several of the 2021 viral items involve content that originated with high-credibility institutional sources: the Spongebob photograph came from NOAA Ocean Exploration; the gorilla-snake video came from Disney's Animal Kingdom; the drug-affected spider webs connect to NASA-adjacent research. Institutional provenance does not immunise viral content from misrepresentation (the gorilla video attracted fabricated speculative captions across some shares), but it does mean that the factual core is readily traceable to accountable sources. The claim "NOAA scientists photographed something that looks like Spongebob" is verifiable in a way that "someone photographed a hairy frog" is not, because the institutional documentation lowers the verification threshold substantially.


## 5. The False Ones: Hairy Frog and Starfish Buttcheeks

The two false/miscaptioned items deserve closer attention because they reveal different mechanisms by which false animal content is produced.

**The hairy frog photograph** was an image manipulation created for a digital photo contest platform (Worth1000, now DesignCrowd). Worth1000 contests specifically challenged participants to create photorealistic composites of fictional or impossible subjects. The winning entries were typically highly convincing digital manipulations that were explicitly labelled as fabricated within their original context, but which, when extracted from that context and shared without attribution, became indistinguishable from genuine wildlife photography. The hairy frog image is a case of context stripping: accurate in its original setting (a clearly labelled photo manipulation competition), false in its viral context (a purportedly real wildlife photograph).

Crucially, a real "hairy frog" does exist: *Trichobatrachus robustus*, the African hairy frog or horror frog, is a real species whose males develop dermal papillae containing arteries and blood vessels — not true hair, but fibrous filamentous structures — during the breeding season, apparently to increase surface area for cutaneous gas exchange (Blackburn et al., 2010, "Biochemical and Morphological Characterization of the Unique Dermal Folds of the 'Hairy' Frog *Trichobatrachus robustus* (Amphibia: Anura: Arthroleptidae)," *Journal of Morphology*, 271(4), 510–522). The real hairy frog is far less visually spectacular than the fabricated image, which may partly explain why the fake, more dramatic version went viral while the real animal does not receive equivalent attention.

**The starfish "buttcheeks" caption** is a category error about animal anatomy. Sea stars (phylum Echinodermata, class Asteroidea) have an oral face (bearing the mouth at the centre) and an aboral face (which is the upper surface visible in most photographs). What was being interpreted as "buttcheeks" in the viral image was likely the ambulacral groove region near the mouth, or possibly the cardiac stomach partially everted, on the oral surface. Sea stars have no structure that functions as an excretory anus in the way mammalian anatomy implies — their aborally positioned anus is small and not conspicuous. The mistake reflects a systematic tendency to interpret unfamiliar animal anatomy through the lens of familiar (mammalian) body plans, a cognitive bias that generates systematic errors in popular animal identification.


## 6. Conclusions and Epistemic Assessment

Jessica Lee's year-end retrospective provides a valuable editorial document: it reflects Snopes' institutional judgement about which animal fact-checks mattered in 2021, and it represents a useful corpus for analysing the structure of animal viral content. The 13 items reveal a consistent pattern in which extraordinary biological reality is the dominant generator of viral animal content, outpacing fabrication by a substantial margin. The primary failure modes are image manipulation and caption mismatch, not invented biology.

The epistemic implication for science communication is significant: fact-checking animal biology content should, methodologically, begin from a prior of "probably true but possibly misidentified or miscontextualised," rather than "probably false because it sounds too strange." The strange-but-true pattern is deeply reliable in animal biology, for evolutionary reasons that have nothing to do with media practice and everything to do with the unbounded creative logic of natural selection operating over hundreds of millions of years.

Individual items in the collection connect to active research fronts: chelonian physiology, elasmobranch habituated behaviour, chameleon miniaturisation, isopod parasitology, collective avian behaviour, and pharmacological effects on invertebrate cognition. The fact-checking confirmed these items are not fringe claims but solidly established science. The science communication gap is one of accessibility rather than consensus, and the gap is substantially closed by systematic fact-checking — one of the most defensible use cases for the form.
