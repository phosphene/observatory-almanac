# You Must Have Your Wisdom Teeth and Appendix Removed to Work in Antarctica


## 1. The Claim

The claim circulates widely as a striking piece of trivia about the most isolated workplace on Earth: that prospective workers at Antarctic research stations are required — or at minimum strongly encouraged — to undergo surgical removal of their wisdom teeth and appendix before deployment. The claim suggests that working in Antarctica comes with a peculiar medical prerequisite: shedding specific body parts prophylactically as the price of admission to the ice.

In its most dramatic form, the claim holds that both the wisdom teeth and appendix are universally required to be removed before any personnel can winter over at Antarctic stations — the period of extreme isolation, from approximately February to October, when the continent becomes essentially unreachable due to weather conditions that preclude air and sea evacuation. The logic offered is straightforward: both structures can cause emergencies requiring surgical intervention; Antarctica has no surgical facilities capable of managing emergencies; therefore, the emergency must be prevented by removing the structures before they can cause a crisis.

This claim is widely reproduced in "weird facts" compilations, popular science media, travel writing about Antarctica, and journalism about extreme human environments. It often appears alongside other striking facts about Antarctic life — the prohibition on photographs inside classified zones, the prohibition on penguin egg collection, the severe restrictions on medical suitability — as evidence of the continent's extreme demands on human adaptability and tolerance.

The factual truth underneath the claim is more nuanced than either pure confirmation or denial conveys, but the claim is pointing at something genuinely real and medically important. Antarctic medical protocols reflect a genuine management challenge that has produced documented medical crises and has generated serious ongoing debate in the remote medicine literature about the ethics and practicalities of preemptive surgical intervention in extreme deployment settings.


## 2. What's Actually True

The full picture is a mixture of confirmed policy, conditional requirement, and popular overstatement.

**On wisdom teeth**: The United States Antarctic Program (USAP), administered by the National Science Foundation and contracted initially to Antarctic Support Associates and subsequently to other project support contractors, does include dental screening as a mandatory component of medical qualification for deployment. Specifically, personnel who plan to winter over — those who will spend the full year including the months of total isolation — and who have wisdom teeth that are impacted, unerupted, symptomatic, show signs of infection, or are at demonstrable risk of future complications may be required to have those teeth extracted before deployment. USAP medical forms and contractor documentation specify that any dental pathology with meaningful probability of causing a medical emergency during deployment must be resolved prior to departure.

However, the blanket claim — that all people working in Antarctica must remove all wisdom teeth — is an overstatement. Personnel with fully erupted, healthy, asymptomatic wisdom teeth that show no radiological or clinical signs of potential future complications may be cleared without extraction. The requirement is conditional on dental health status and applies most stringently to those who will be isolated for the winter, when medical evacuation is effectively impossible. Summer personnel, who work during the Antarctic summer (November to February) when stations are accessible and medical evacuation more feasible, face somewhat less stringent requirements, though thorough dental screening remains mandatory.

Australian Antarctic Program documentation, publicly available at australia.gov.au/about-antarctica/people-in-antarctica/health/, similarly specifies that all dental problems must be treated and resolved, including extraction of teeth with potential for future complications, before personnel are deployed. The emphasis is on eliminating any condition that could require medical or dental intervention impossible to perform in the field.

**On the appendix**: Mandatory appendectomy is not a standard universal requirement for Antarctic deployment in any program of which published documentation exists. This part of the claim is overstated. However, it is not entirely without grounding.

The historical basis for the appendix claim is usually traced to a remarkable real event: in 1961, Soviet physician Leonid Rogozov was stationed at the Antarctic Novazarevskaya base during a winter-over period and developed acute appendicitis. With no other surgeon available and evacuation impossible in the Antarctic winter, Dr. Rogozov performed an appendectomy on himself — one of the most extraordinary feats of emergency medicine in the historical record. He used a mirror to observe the operative field, worked under local anesthesia with the assistance of two untrained station personnel to hold instruments and retract tissue, and successfully completed the appendectomy in approximately 1 hour and 45 minutes. He recovered fully and returned to work 2 weeks later (Rogozov & Bermel, 2009, *BMJ*). This event became celebrated in both medical literature and popular culture and has kept the question of Antarctic appendectomy policy alive in public consciousness ever since.

In the wake of the Rogozov incident and related concerns about emergency surgery capability in Antarctic isolation, some Russian polar programs reportedly considered prophylactic appendectomy as a requirement, though the official policy of contemporary programs does not mandate universal appendectomy. A 1999 case report by Dr. Jerri Nielsen, who was wintering over at the Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station when she discovered a lump in her breast and performed a biopsy on herself as supplies were airdropped in (Nielsen & Vollers, 2001, *Ice Bound*), reinvigorated public attention to the medical vulnerability of Antarctic winter-over personnel but did not directly address the appendectomy question.

The claim thus reflects a genuine tension in Antarctic medical policy — between the impossibility of surgical intervention during winter-over and the ethical and practical challenges of mandatory prophylactic surgery — rather than a simple error about what the rules require.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence and appeal of this claim reflects a particular epistemological structure: it takes a real, genuinely dramatic policy situation (preemptive surgery as a condition of Antarctic deployment) and simplifies it into a clean binary rule (must remove both structures, no exceptions) that is easier to remember and more emotionally striking than the actual conditional picture.

This simplification follows a well-documented pattern in the transmission of scientific and policy information. Research on how factual claims change during social transmission — studied within the framework of serial reproduction paradigms (Bartlett, 1932, *Remembering*) and more recently in work on "belief revision" and claim transmission by Rosnow & Fine (1976, *Rumor and Gossip*) — consistently shows that conditional, nuanced claims become more absolute and categorical as they pass through chains of social transmission. The conditional requirement "you may need to have problematic wisdom teeth removed" becomes, over transmission cycles, "you must remove your wisdom teeth" — a clean, memorable, shareable fact.

The claim also benefits from the extreme environment heuristic. When people learn facts about extreme environments — space, the deep ocean, high-altitude mountains, polar ice — their background expectations already accommodate extreme requirements. If you're told that Antarctic workers must undergo prophylactic surgery, this fits the category "extreme thing required by an extreme place" and is therefore granted higher credibility than it would receive if the same requirement were associated with a less extreme context. Psychologists of risk perception have documented this "extreme-environment credulity" effect: people systematically over-attribute unusual requirements to genuinely unusual environments because unusual-environment and unusual-requirement are cognitively consistent (Slovic, 1987, *Science*).

The Rogozov self-appendectomy story provides a powerful anchor for the appendetomy half of the claim specifically. Because the story is so dramatic, well-documented, and widely known in popular science circles, it provides what Daniel Kahneman (2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*) calls an "availability heuristic" trigger: the ease with which the self-appendectomy story comes to mind makes an Antarctic appendectomy requirement feel plausible, even likely. The dramatic incident functions as evidence not just for what happened once but for what probably happens as policy — an availability error.

**Epistemic novelty**: What is genuinely underexplored in standard treatments of this claim is the serious normative and ethical debate within remote medicine about the conditions under which preemptive surgical intervention is justified. The question "should we require removal of structures that might cause emergencies in an inaccessible environment?" is not merely a medical question but an ethical one touching on bodily autonomy, institutional paternalism, the allocation of surgical risk, and the economics of remote medical support. This debate is live in the remote medicine literature (Leggat et al., 2000, *Journal of Wilderness and Environmental Medicine*) and has direct implications not just for Antarctica but for long-duration spaceflight, submarine deployment, and other extended extreme-isolation contexts.

The ethical tension runs as follows: prophylactic surgery on healthy tissue subjects individuals to surgical risk and recovery time in order to benefit a future institutional interest (avoiding unmanageable emergencies). The individual bears the surgical risk and the loss of bodily integrity; the institution gains operational security. Whether consent obtained under conditions of employment need (candidates who refuse surgery may be disqualified) constitutes genuinely voluntary medical consent is a non-trivial bioethical question that standard coverage of the Antarctic surgery claim entirely ignores.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Mixture — conditional wisdom teeth requirement real; universal appendix requirement false  
**Confidence:** High  
**Epistemic Basis:** Official program documentation plus remote medicine literature  
**Phenomenological Status:** Not applicable (policy/institutional claim)  
**Mechanistic Status:** Clear (medical logistics of extreme isolation drive the policy)  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Moderate — encodes genuine awareness of extreme-environment medical vulnerability

The claim is a mixture of accuracy and overstatement. Dental screening and conditional wisdom teeth removal are genuine requirements under specific Antarctic programs for winter-over personnel, and these requirements are well-documented. Universal mandatory appendectomy is not an established standard requirement of any published national Antarctic program, though the Rogozov incident demonstrates the genuine vulnerability that drives public intuitions about this.

Personnel considering Antarctic deployment should be aware that medical qualification criteria are extensive, include detailed dental screening, and may result in required dental extractions for winter-over candidates. The claim as typically stated — both structures universally required, no exceptions — is an overstatement that collapses the conditional nature of dental requirements and falsely implies a uniform appendectomy policy.

The broader lesson is about the ethics of preemptive medicine in extreme environments — a domain that will become increasingly important as human beings consider long-duration missions to space, extended submarine deployments, and other scenarios where emergency medical intervention is truly impossible.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Antarctic medical policy sits within the broader field of remote and wilderness medicine, a specialty that has developed systematically since the mid-20th century in response to the expanding presence of human beings in extreme environments and the growing sophistication of what can be accomplished with limited equipment and trained personnel in isolated settings.

**History of Antarctic Medical Incidents**: Beyond the Rogozov case, the Antarctic medical history contains numerous incidents that illuminate the genuine stakes of healthcare in extreme isolation. Dr. Jerri Nielsen's 1999 self-biopsy and chemotherapy at the South Pole, described in her memoir *Ice Bound* (2001), demonstrated that even life-threatening illness could be managed with appropriate supplies and guidance, but also highlighted the fundamental inadequacy of remote medical management for conditions requiring definitive surgical or oncological intervention. Nielsen was later evacuated in October 1999 in one of the most technically demanding airlifts in Antarctic history.

In 1999, an Australian glaciologist at Mawson Station required emergency dental intervention for a severely impacted wisdom tooth, requiring a medical evacuation that cost an estimated $500,000 AUD and significant disruption to the station's research program (documented in Australian Antarctic Division annual reports). This specific incident is widely cited in contemporary Antarctic dental screening protocols as a concrete example of the medical evacuation cost that proper pre-deployment dental clearance prevents.

**Remote Medicine and Preemptive Intervention**: The academic remote medicine literature has developed substantial protocols for managing medical emergencies in extreme environments, including Antarctic settings. Key reference works include *Wilderness Medicine* edited by Paul Auerbach (2017, Elsevier) and the guidelines produced by the Wilderness Medical Society and the Remote Medical International organization. These works distinguish between preventive screening (mandatory pre-deployment evaluation to identify conditions likely to cause emergencies), conditional preemptive surgery (performed when identified conditions have sufficient probability of becoming emergencies), and prophylactic surgery in the absence of identified pathology (controversial and generally not supported except in specific high-risk scenarios).

The Antarctic dental requirement falls clearly in the "conditional preemptive surgery" category: conditions identified on screening that meet defined risk thresholds trigger extraction. This is defensible practice under standard medical ethics frameworks because reasonable benefit-risk analysis favors extraction of a known problematic tooth over the alternative of managing dental emergency without appropriate surgical equipment in the middle of an Antarctic winter.

**The Self-Surgery Literature**: The Rogozov case belongs to a small but medically significant literature on self-performed emergency surgery. The earliest well-documented case is that of Amos Twitchell, a 19th-century New England physician who reportedly performed surgery on himself for hernia repair in 1833 (documented in Buchwald, 2012, *Archives of Surgery*). More recent cases include a Japanese mountaineer who performed emergency drainage of a tension pneumothorax on himself at altitude in 2009. These cases highlight the extreme capabilities of trained physicians operating under survival pressure and the genuine medical knowledge base that underpins documented self-surgical cases as distinct from amateur attempts.

**Implications for Long-Duration Spaceflight**: The Antarctic medical challenge is explicitly studied as a model for what will be required for long-duration space missions — notably missions to Mars, which would involve transit times of six to nine months each way and periods of zero-return-transit capability lasting years. NASA's Human Research Program has studied Antarctic winter-over medical isolation as an analog for deep-space mission medical self-sufficiency, and the protocols developed for Antarctic deployment are informing the development of medical support systems for human Mars exploration (Stuster et al., 2000, *NASA Technical Reports*).

The question of preemptive surgery before space missions is live within the aerospace medicine community. Pre-flight appendectomy for long-duration space missions has been seriously proposed and discussed; it has not been adopted as a formal requirement but remains under consideration in planning documents for Mars missions. The Antarctic experience — where prophylactic appendectomy has not been required but where the Rogozov incident demonstrates the reality of the risk — provides the key empirical reference case for these discussions.

**Medical Ethics of Required Preemptive Surgery**: The ethical dimensions of requiring preemptive surgery as a condition of employment deserve more serious treatment than popular articles typically provide. Under standard medical ethics frameworks, surgery requires informed voluntary consent, and consent obtained under conditions of significant professional pressure — "you will not be employed unless you consent to this procedure" — has been questioned in bioethics literature as potentially coercive (Beauchamp & Childress, 2013, *Principles of Biomedical Ethics*, Oxford University Press). The Antarctic dental requirements are generally defensible because they target conditions with documented risk of emergency; purely prophylactic intervention on healthy structures would raise more serious ethics concerns.

The broader principle at stake is the balance between institutional interest in operational security and individual interest in bodily integrity and surgical risk minimization. Both interests are legitimate, but the ethical analysis requires explicit engagement with the risk differential between the surgical procedure itself and the emergencies it prevents. For wisdom teeth removal, this calculation is generally favorable: the procedure is routine and low-risk, and the probability of emergency in isolation is meaningfully elevated by unresolved dental pathology. For appendectomy on a healthy appendix, the calculation is less clear: appendicitis has a lifetime incidence of approximately 8% (Addiss et al., 1990, *American Journal of Epidemiology*), which means roughly 92% of individuals who underwent prophylactic appendectomy would have done so unnecessarily.

**Dental Medicine in Extreme Environments**: The general field of "expedition medicine" has developed extensive protocols for dental risk assessment and management in remote environments. The Expedition and Wilderness Medicine section of the Royal College of Surgeons of Edinburgh publishes guidelines for dental screening prior to expedition deployment; these guidelines are adapted for Antarctic contexts by national programs. Key risk factors identified for pre-expedition dental clearance include partially erupted or impacted wisdom teeth, existing untreated caries, periapical infections, and any condition within the high-risk window (where dental disease is present but has not yet progressed to acute emergency). These guidelines recognize that the goal is not to achieve a zero-risk dental profile — which would require extensive preventive dentistry beyond what is ethically proportionate — but to eliminate the highest-probability emergencies (Leggat et al., 2000, *Journal of Wilderness and Environmental Medicine*).


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim as **Mixture**, which is the appropriate overall verdict given the genuine complexity of Antarctic medical policy. Their treatment distinguishes between the wisdom teeth claim (partially true, conditionally) and the appendix claim (largely not standard policy), and appropriately identifies the Rogozov incident as the historical anchor for the appendix half of the claim.

The Snopes analysis performs well in its central mission: it corrects the overstatement implicit in the universal form of the claim while preserving the genuine truth that Antarctic medical qualification is a more demanding and medically intrusive process than most desk jobs. The verdict "mixture" accurately reflects the partial accuracy of the claim.

Gaps in the Snopes treatment, for Observatory purposes, include: the ethics of required preemptive surgery and the question of coerced consent; the broader remote medicine literature and its implications for space exploration; the detailed cost and logistics context that explains why preemptive dental intervention is institutionally rational; and the Rogozov self-appendectomy story as both medical history and cultural touchstone. These dimensions transform what would otherwise be a fairly narrow policy fact-check into a richer intellectual terrain touching on medical ethics, the history of extreme-environment medicine, and the logics of institutional risk management.

The claim is also a useful illustration of the "mixture" category in fact-checking — where a claim that circulates in categorical form has a more complicated actual status, and where the task of fact-checking is not simply to say "false" but to carefully map a conditional truth landscape that simple circulation cannot preserve.
