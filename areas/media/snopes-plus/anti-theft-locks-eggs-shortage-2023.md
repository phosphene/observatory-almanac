## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

On or around January 12, 2023, a photograph posted on Reddit purported to show a person holding a carton of eggs that had been secured with an anti-theft device—the type of plastic, magnetic, or spider-coil locking mechanism typically used on high-value consumer electronics, cosmetics, or alcohol. The image was shared widely on Reddit and subsequently across other social media platforms, with various captions implying or stating directly that grocery stores had begun locking egg cartons to prevent theft during a period of extreme shortages and high prices.

Snopes investigator Bethania Palma (2023) assigned this claim the rating **Unfounded**. The rating reflects a specific epistemic condition: no credible evidence exists that the locking of egg cartons was a genuine, widespread or even isolated retail security practice during the January 2023 shortage, but the impossibility of comprehensively verifying what occurred at every grocery store in the United States means the practice cannot be ruled out as having occurred at any single location. The image's satirical origin—it appears to have been staged—is strongly implied by the practical absurdities of securing eggs against theft with anti-theft locks, and Palma (2023) noted that such locks would conflict with the common practice of consumers opening cartons to check for broken eggs before purchase.

This entry examines the documented facts of the 2022–2023 avian influenza outbreak and its effect on egg prices and availability, the practical contradictions in the anti-theft lock claim, the epistemology of the "Unfounded" verdict classification, historical context for retail security measures during supply-constrained periods, and what this meme reveals about how economic anxiety translates into satirical memetic signaling.

## §2 The Egg Shortage: Documented Facts and Scale

The economic and biological context underlying this viral image is fully real. A highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) outbreak—specifically the H5N1 strain—had been spreading through U.S. commercial poultry operations since February 2022, representing the largest HPAI outbreak in U.S. history as of that date. Palma (2023) cited The New York Times December 2022 reporting (Jiménez, 2023) and USDA data: as of January 2023, approximately 57 million chickens had been affected by the virus since February 2022, with more than 44 million laying hens culled to prevent spread.

The culling of 44 million laying hens—representing roughly 12–14% of the total U.S. laying hen flock at the outbreak's peak—produced a significant and sustained reduction in egg supply. The USDA's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS) maintained a running tally of confirmed HPAI cases in commercial and backyard flocks; the scale of the outbreak was unprecedented in terms of flock losses compared to previous U.S. HPAI events.

The supply reduction translated directly into price increases that were dramatic by consumer standards. Retail egg prices averaged approximately $1.79 per dozen in January 2022; by December 2022, the USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service reported average retail prices had risen to approximately $4.25 per dozen, an increase of approximately 137% year-over-year. In some markets and store formats, prices exceeded $7–8 per dozen for large eggs. The price spike made eggs one of the most visible and socially discussed consumer price increases during the broader 2022–2023 U.S. food inflation episode, adding to what was already elevated public sensitivity about grocery costs.

Several major retailers imposed purchase limits on eggs in December 2022 and January 2023—restrictions similar to those imposed on toilet paper and hand sanitizer during the early COVID-19 pandemic. These limits, while documented, were temporary and varied by retailer. Bon Appétit (January 2023), cited by Palma (2023), noted that consumers were genuinely asking which food substitutes could replace eggs in cooking and baking, indicating a level of supply concern that extended beyond price anxiety into actual availability fears at the retail level.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Satirical Meme as Social Reality Probe and the Unfounded Classification

The epistemic novelty in this case is the viral image's function as what might be called a **satirical reality probe**—a piece of content that occupies the ambiguous territory between pure parody (demonstrably false claim asserted for comedic effect) and documentary evidence (photograph of a real phenomenon documenting a genuine real-world condition). This ambiguity is epistemically significant because it shapes how the image is processed by audiences and evaluated by fact-checkers.

Palma's (2023) use of the "Unfounded" verdict—rather than "False"—reflects an important distinction in Snopes' rating taxonomy. "False" implies that the claim has been affirmatively disproven; "Unfounded" implies that credible evidence supporting the claim could not be found, but that the claim has not been demonstrated to be false. In this case, the distinction is non-trivial: the Unfounded rating acknowledges that in a country with approximately 35,000 grocery stores plus tens of thousands of convenience stores and other food retailers, a single instance of an employee improvising an anti-theft device on egg cartons cannot be ruled out. What can be said is that no systematic evidence of the practice was found and that logistical considerations make widespread adoption essentially implausible.

The satirical function of the original Reddit image is suggested by several features. First, the practical absurdity is significant: anti-theft devices designed for consumer electronics require specialized magnetic keys to open, would add cost and handling time at checkout proportionate to the devices' value (which is several dollars per unit—more than the carton of eggs), and would make it impossible for consumers or staff to open the carton to check for breakage, a common and often encouraged pre-purchase behavior. Eggs that cannot be inspected before purchase present a distinct consumer quality problem. Second, the image appears to have been staged—the lock placement on a commercial egg carton is visually legible as a photo composition designed to communicate a specific political-economic joke rather than document an observed reality.

The meme's communicative function is indexical rather than referential: it gestures toward the excess of the egg shortage situation and satirizes both consumer anxiety (eggs as luxury goods requiring security measures) and potential corporate or retail overreach (treating a basic food as a high-value asset to be protected from theft). Shifman (2014, *Memes in Digital Culture*, MIT Press) identified this class of meme as "reaction memes that encode social commentary through absurd visual juxtaposition." The locked-egg image functions similarly to "depression meal" memes and "we're so back / it's so over" meme formats: it communicates shared economic anxiety through the visual language of hyperbolic social commentary.

Barthes (1977, *Image, Music, Text*, Hill and Wang) introduced the concept of the photographic message as bearing both denotative and connotative layers; the locked egg carton's denotative content is simply a man holding an egg carton with a security device attached, while its connotative content is a political-economic commentary about the commodification of basic food under inflationary pressure. Fact-checking the denotative claim with an "Unfounded" verdict is epistemically appropriate, but fails to engage the connotative level at which the image was primarily operating for most of its audience.

Pennycook and Rand (2019, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, Elsevier) documented that analytical thinking style predicts resistance to misinformation spread, but also that images with clearly satirical intent—even when shared by users who understand the satire—can generate non-trivial levels of sincere belief in the depicted claim among low-media-literacy audiences who encounter them without the original satirical framing. The locked egg meme is a textbook case: most Reddit users sharing the image understood it as satirical commentary; some subset of consumers encountering a screenshot devoid of context believed it documented a real retail practice.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Palma's (2023) investigation identified the right verdict category (Unfounded), provided accurate context about the egg shortage's scale and causes, and noted the practical absurdities of the anti-theft lock claim. Additional contextual dimensions merit attention.

**Actual retail security measures in practice**: The investigation examined whether anti-theft locks had been placed on egg cartons but did not explore what retail security measures were actually deployed during the shortage. Several documented practices included: purchase quantity limits (documented by multiple retailers), locking cases for high-value eggs (particularly organic and specialty eggs), placement of eggs behind service counters at some smaller stores, and increased store security presence more generally. These actual measures provide a factual baseline against which the locked-carton meme can be compared.

**HPAI outbreak causes and timeline**: Palma (2023) accurately cited the 57 million birds affected and the 44 million laying hens culled, but did not describe the H5N1 strain's specific epidemiology, why this outbreak was disproportionately large relative to prior U.S. HPAI events, or the structural features of industrial egg production (concentrated flock sizes, high-density housing, biosecurity challenges) that contributed to rapid spread. These factors are relevant to understanding both the shortage's severity and its duration.

**Egg substitute context**: The broader context of egg-replacement behavior during the shortage—consumers turning to flax eggs, chia eggs, applesauce, and commercial egg replacers for baking; restaurants modifying menus; food banks reporting egg shortages—is mentioned by Palma (2023) only in passing through the Bon Appétit citation. This consumer behavioral context would enrich understanding of the shortage's real social impact.

**Reddit post identification**: Palma (2023) cited a Reddit post at r/funny (reddit.com/r/funny/comments/10djydx/) as the photograph's primary source but did not identify whether the photograph originated on Reddit or had been posted elsewhere first, whether the poster indicated satirical intent in the post's comments, or what the post's engagement metrics were (upvotes, cross-posts) to characterize its reach.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Retail Security Theater, Food Price Memory, and Prior Shortage Crises

The locked-egg carton image belongs to a tradition of retail security theater satire that extends back to the covid-19 toilet paper shortage of March 2020, when similarly circulated photographs (some real, some staged) showed security measures applied to paper goods. The structural parallel between the 2020 toilet paper shortage and the 2023 egg shortage is significant: in both cases, a commonplace, inexpensive staple good became temporarily scarce and price-elevated, triggering both genuine consumer anxiety and a wave of social commentary memes that encoded political-economic critique through absurdist juxtaposition.

Grocery retail security systems in the United States have in fact expanded substantially in the 2020s in response to organized retail crime (ORC) and shrink reduction pressures. The Retail Industry Leaders Association (2023 report) noted that ORC-related losses increased significantly in the 2021–2023 period across multiple product categories; items at elevated theft risk typically have high value-to-weight ratios and easy resaleability. Eggs fail both of these criteria—they are fragile, perishable, bulky, and essentially impossible to resell as stolen goods in any commercially meaningful way. The locked-carton meme satirizes a retail logic that would not, in practice, apply to the egg category regardless of price.

Historical comparison with actual food-security measures during wartime rationing is also instructive. U.S. Office of Price Administration rationing programs during World War II (1942–1945) included rationing books for eggs, beef, sugar, and other goods; consumers were required to surrender ration stamps to purchase rationed items. This was a government-imposed allocation mechanism, not a retail theft-control mechanism, and it did not involve physical locks on products. The societal memory of rationing is distant enough that the locked-carton image could activate that memory associatively in some audiences without triggering the logical distinction.

Kahneman (2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Farrar, Straus and Giroux) identified what he termed "cognitive ease" as a driver of belief acceptance: claims that fit into existing mental frameworks—such as "companies are doing extreme things in response to extreme shortages"—are processed more fluidly and accepted with less critical scrutiny than claims that require framework violation. The authenticity of the egg price crisis made the anti-theft lock meme *cognitively easy* to accept despite its practical implausibility, because the broader framework (shortage-scarcity-extreme-measures) was fully accurate.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Research Gaps

Palma's (2023) **Unfounded** verdict is the epistemically appropriate response: no evidence confirmed the practice, but no global negative could be established given the size and heterogeneity of the U.S. retail grocery sector. The humor of the meme served as both the primary driver of its spread and the primary reason most of its audience did not take it as a factual claim—but a meaningful minority audience segment processed it as documentary rather than satirical, warranting clarification.

**Epistemic classification**: This is a case of *satirical-meme misread*—content primarily functioning as social commentary that a subset of receivers decoded as factual documentary evidence. The same image can function simultaneously as satire for high-media-literacy audiences and as misinformation for lower-media-literacy audiences, a duality that presents structural challenges for fact-checking that focuses on single-point verdict issuance.

**EARC gap E (Empirical Retail)**: The primary research gap is the absence of documentation about what retail security measures were actually deployed during the January 2023 shortage—both to provide a factual baseline and to characterize the realistic upper bound of commercially plausible security extensions to the egg category. Secondary gaps include the meme's specific origin provenance (was the image staged or captured of an actual situation) and longitudinal data on consumer belief uptake from satirical food shortage content.

**Research vectors**: Retail industry security trade publications, RILA/NRF retail loss prevention reports for 2022–2023, documentation of actual egg retailer security protocols from grocery retail trade associations, and media studies research on satirical-to-sincere belief conversion in food-scarcity meme contexts would all strengthen future analysis of this category.
