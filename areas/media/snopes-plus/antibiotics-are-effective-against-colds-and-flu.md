# Antibiotics are effective against colds and flu

## 1. The Claim

The assertion that antibiotics effectively treat the common cold or influenza is one of the most medically consequential misconceptions circulating in popular culture. The claim appears in multiple forms: as a direct expectation that doctors should prescribe antibiotics for respiratory illness; as a "preventive" rationale ("I take them so I don't get a secondary infection"); as a folk explanation for recovery ("I took antibiotics and felt better in a week"); and as bureaucratic pressure on healthcare systems where patient satisfaction scores depend partly on whether a prescription was dispensed. Together these variants constitute a coherent but thoroughly incorrect popular pharmacology of respiratory illness.

The social infrastructure reinforcing the claim is substantial. Primary care consultations for cold and flu frequently end with antibiotic prescriptions, even in countries with strong antibiotic stewardship programmes. Pharmacies in many low- and middle-income nations dispense antibiotics without prescription, and patients self-medicating with leftover antibiotics report improvement — improvement that would have occurred in the same timeframe without any treatment at all. Online and social media spaces amplify individual anecdotes of apparent recovery, while the medical counter-narrative — "you would have recovered anyway" — offers little psychological satisfaction to anyone who feels sick now.

This entry examines the biological impossibility of the claim, the epidemiological evidence against it, and the mechanism by which the misconception persists across educational levels, economic strata, and healthcare systems.

## 2. What's Actually True

The common cold is caused overwhelmingly by viruses: rhinoviruses account for approximately 30–50% of cases (Heikkinen & Järvinen, 2003, *The Lancet*), with additional major contributions from coronaviruses, parainfluenza viruses, respiratory syncytial virus, and adenoviruses. Influenza is caused exclusively by influenza A and B viruses, with seasonal epidemics driven by antigenic drift and occasional pandemics by antigenic shift. None of these pathogens are bacteria. None respond to antibiotics.

The inability of antibiotics to affect viruses is not a matter of degree — it is a fundamental mechanistic incompatibility. Antibiotics target structures and metabolic processes that bacteria possess and viruses do not. Beta-lactam antibiotics (penicillins, cephalosporins) disrupt peptidoglycan synthesis in bacterial cell walls; viruses have no cell walls. Aminoglycosides and macrolides bind to bacterial ribosomes; viruses use hijacked host ribosomes to translate their proteins, which no antibiotic is designed to block. Fluoroquinolones inhibit bacterial DNA gyrase; viral replication uses cellular polymerases that fluoroquinolones do not target. The mismatch is total, not partial.

This mechanistic analysis is confirmed by clinical evidence. Jefferson et al. (2013), in a Cochrane systematic review of antibiotic prescribing for uncomplicated upper respiratory tract infections, concluded that antibiotics provided no clinically meaningful benefit for symptom duration, severity, or prevention of serious complications, while increasing the rates of adverse drug effects including diarrhoea, vomiting, and rash. The review synthesised data from over 2,000 patients across multiple controlled trials. Fahey et al. (1998, *BMJ*) reached compatible conclusions in a separate Cochrane analysis of acute cough and lower respiratory tract infections, finding that antibiotic treatment did not reduce duration of illness, proportion of patients improved at follow-up, or days of impaired activity.

For influenza specifically, antiviral medications (neuraminidase inhibitors such as oseltamivir) do have modest evidence of benefit when administered early, but these are categorically different compounds that work by blocking viral neuraminidase — a target antibiotics do not touch. The existence of antivirals underlines the principle: effective treatment requires a drug matched to the pathogen's biology.

Natural history is itself a powerful rebuttal. The common cold resolves in 7–10 days without treatment in the overwhelming majority of cases (Tyrrell & Fielder, 2002, *Cold Wars: The Fight Against the Common Cold*, Oxford University Press). Influenza typically produces acute symptoms for 3–5 days with gradual recovery over 1–2 weeks in otherwise healthy individuals (Nichol et al., 1995, *NEJM*). The frequent observation — "I took antibiotics and recovered" — exploits a timing coincidence that would have unfolded identically with no medication whatsoever.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemics of this belief system are layered and instructive. At the first layer sits simple confusion about categories: a large proportion of the general public does not reliably distinguish between viruses and bacteria. In the United Kingdom, a 2015 survey commissioned by Public Health England found that 44% of respondents believed antibiotics were effective against viruses — a finding consistent with comparable surveys in the United States, Europe, and Australia (McNulty et al., 2007, *Journal of Antimicrobial Chemotherapy*). This is not merely ignorance: it is stable, confident ignorance, resistant to brief educational interventions.

The second layer is social and clinical. Mangione-Smith et al. (1999, *Archives of Pediatrics & Adolescent Medicine*) — studying over 300 primary care visits for paediatric respiratory illness — found that physicians prescribed antibiotics 62% of the time when they perceived parents expected the prescription, compared to 7% when they did not. The patient's expectation was the dominant predictor of prescribing behaviour, not clinical presentation. This dynamic creates a feedback loop: patients who expect antibiotics receive them; their expectation is reinforced; the belief transmits to the next generation of patients, friends, and family members.

The third and epistemically most interesting layer is **post-hoc temporal conditioning**. Humans are exceptionally susceptible to assuming that C caused D if C preceded D. When a patient takes an antibiotic on day two of a cold and feels better by day seven, the antibiotic is credited. The absence of a control condition — one's own counterfactual self who did not take the antibiotic — is invisible. This post-hoc ergo propter hoc reasoning is not unique to medically unsophisticated patients; it appears in clinicians who "notice" that patients prescribed antibiotics "do well," because patients prescribed nothing are less likely to be the memorable clinical stories. The cognitive architecture that builds narrative from temporal sequence systematically outperforms the statistical reasoning needed to recognise spurious correlation in self-resolving illness. This represents a genuine epistemic asymmetry: the mechanisms by which false medical beliefs persist are better calibrated to human cognition than the mechanisms by which correct but probabilistic information propagates.

An additional layer is the "just in case" logic, often applied by healthcare providers uncertain whether an infection is viral or bacterial. Williams et al. (2016, *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*) analysed randomised trials of delayed versus immediate antibiotic prescribing for upper respiratory infections and found that delayed prescribing substantially reduced antibiotic use without worsening outcomes, but required active patient communication — a resource that is structurally scarce in many healthcare systems. The practice of prescribing "just in case" therefore reflects a rational response to a constrained environment, but one whose downstream harms — resistance selection — are substantially greater than its individual-level benefits.

## 4. Verdict

**False.** Antibiotics cannot treat the common cold or influenza. The claim fails at the mechanistic level (antibiotics target bacterial processes that viruses lack), the clinical trial level (randomised controlled trials and Cochrane reviews consistently show no benefit), and the epidemiological level (populations with high antibiotic use for viral illness show higher antibiotic resistance without lower cold or flu rates).

The appropriate treatment for uncomplicated cold and flu in otherwise healthy individuals is supportive: rest, hydration, analgesia for fever and muscle pain (paracetamol or ibuprofen), and decongestants for nasal congestion if symptomatic relief is desired. In high-risk individuals — the elderly, immunocompromised patients, those with chronic cardiorespiratory disease — antiviral treatment for confirmed influenza (oseltamivir or zanamivir) is warranted within 48 hours of symptom onset, but this is a distinct drug class acting on viral biology.

There is one partial caveat that is often misused to justify the claim: secondary bacterial infections can occasionally follow viral respiratory illness. Community-acquired pneumonia, bacterial sinusitis, and acute otitis media may develop as complications in a minority of patients, particularly children and older adults. When secondary bacterial infection is diagnosed, antibiotic treatment is appropriate. But this exception does not support treating all or most viral respiratory illness with antibiotics prophylactically. Petersen et al. (1997, *Family Practice*) found that approximately 2% of cold episodes led to bacterial complications requiring antibiotic treatment — too low to justify prophylactic prescribing in 100% of patients, particularly given the population-level harms of antibiotic overuse.

The verdict is therefore: **False**, with the narrow caveat that secondary bacterial complications sometimes occur and warrant targeted treatment. The original claim — that antibiotics are effective against colds and flu as such — remains wrong.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The mechanism by which individual antibiotic misuse creates systemic harm is selection pressure. Every dose of antibiotic eliminates susceptible bacterial strains while creating survival advantage for resistant variants. This holds even when antibiotics are taken for a viral illness: the antibiotic still acts on the patient's commensal bacterial flora, selecting for resistance. Conjugative plasmids carrying resistance genes spread laterally between bacterial species, meaning resistance arising in gut commensals can transfer to future pathogens.

The scale of antibiotic resistance as a public health emergency is quantifiable. The CDC estimates that antibiotic-resistant infections cause at least 2.8 million illnesses and 35,000 deaths annually in the United States (CDC, 2019, *Antibiotic Resistance Threats in the United States*). A 2016 review commissioned by the UK government estimated that without intervention, antimicrobial resistance could kill 10 million people annually by 2050, surpassing cancer as a mortality cause (O'Neill, 2016, *Review on Antimicrobial Resistance*). These projections are driven substantially by the overuse of antibiotics in settings — including viral respiratory illness — where they provide no benefit.

The burden falls disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries, where over-the-counter antibiotic availability is higher, laboratory diagnostic capacity to distinguish viral from bacterial infections is lower, and healthcare system infrastructure to support antibiotic stewardship is weaker. Van Boeckel et al. (2014, *The Lancet Infectious Diseases*) modelled global antibiotic consumption and found that LMIC consumption was growing fastest, with self-medication for respiratory illness a major driver.

The agricultural context amplifies the resistance crisis. Approximately 63–73% of all antibiotics consumed globally are used in food animal production, often sub-therapeutically for growth promotion (Van Boeckel et al., 2015, *Science*). Although the WHO has classified certain antibiotics as critically important for human medicine and recommended their phase-out in agriculture, compliance is uneven. The ecological reservoir of resistance in agricultural settings creates transfer pathways to human pathogens through food chains, environmental contamination of water and soil, and occupational exposure.

New antibiotic development has not kept pace with resistance emergence. Between 1980 and 2000, few novel antibiotic classes were approved. Regulatory and financial barriers to antibiotic development are high, partly because truly effective antibiotics — once approved — are used sparingly to preserve efficacy, reducing commercial return. The result is a narrowing therapeutic pipeline at precisely the moment when resistance is widest.

Antibiotic stewardship programmes — systematic efforts to optimise antibiotic use in clinical settings — have demonstrated success in reducing inappropriate prescribing without worsening patient outcomes. Llewelyn et al. (2017, *BMJ*) reviewed evidence for stewardship interventions and found robust evidence for reduced antibiotic prescribing in primary care and hospital settings through audit and feedback, clinical decision support tools, and point-of-care testing that rapidly distinguishes viral from bacterial infection. These programmes demonstrate that the problem is tractable — but only if the underlying misbelief in antibiotic effectiveness against viral illness is simultaneously corrected.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not published a dedicated entry on the specific claim that antibiotics are effective against colds and flu, though the platform has addressed related claims about antibiotic use and resistance in individual fact-checks. The gap likely reflects the claim's status as uncontested medical consensus — most fact-checking organisations concentrate on claims that are actively contested in public discourse, whereas this one is contested mainly between medical understanding and lay belief rather than between competing expert communities.

Major health authorities have been unequivocal. The CDC's "Be Antibiotics Aware" campaign (launched in 2003 as Get Smart, retitled 2016) explicitly states: "Antibiotics do NOT work for colds, flu, and most sore throats and bronchitis." The WHO's Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance (2015) identifies inappropriate antibiotic use for viral infections as a primary driver of resistance. The UK's National Health Service patient guidance similarly states that antibiotics "are not routinely used to treat chest infections, ear infections in adults, sore throats, [or] coughs and colds."

Journalism on antibiotic resistance frequently revisits this misconception, but popular science communication faces the same challenge identified in the clinical research: brief educational interventions show modest and often short-lived effects on beliefs and behaviour. The misconception is sustained by social norms, patient–provider power dynamics, and cognitive biases that resist informational correction alone. This signals a need for structural interventions — policy, prescribing incentives, diagnostic access — alongside continued public communication.

The claim's medical verdict is unambiguous. Its persistence is a public health problem of the first order, one whose consequences are already visible in hospital ward mortality statistics and whose worst outcomes now arriving on the horizon make correction ever more urgent.
