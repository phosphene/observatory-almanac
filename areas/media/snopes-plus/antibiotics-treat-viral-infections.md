## The Claim

When a cold or flu strikes and people feel genuinely miserable — congested, feverish, exhausted, unable to eat, barely able to get out of bed — many turn to an old mental shortcut: *if I feel this sick, I probably need antibiotics*. The reasoning goes something like: "Antibiotics kill infection. I have an infection. Therefore, antibiotics should help me." In some family circles, the belief is explicit — parents request antibiotics for their children at the first sign of a respiratory infection. In others, it is more implicit: the patient mentions they have been sick for a week, the doctor hedges against a possible bacterial turn, and a prescription emerges almost as a precaution.

The claim takes several forms. At its most direct: *antibiotics treat the cold and flu*. At its softer edges: *antibiotics help you recover faster from viral illnesses*, or *even if they do not cure the virus, they prevent it from getting worse*. The CDC has repeatedly documented this phenomenon. Studies consistently show that patients report feeling substantially better within a few days of starting antibiotics during an upper respiratory illness — and many attribute that improvement to the drugs themselves.

This is a phenomenologically real belief. It is not based on complete ignorance of medicine. Rather, it is grounded in a genuine experience of recovery — an experience that is real but misattributed. The belief survives particularly well because it comes packaged with an apparent mechanism (fighting infection), an apparent outcome (recovery), and cultural reinforcement from pharmacies, historic prescribing norms, and a widespread conflation of "infection" with "bacterial infection."

## What Is Actually True

The science here is unambiguous and has been for decades. Antibiotics work by targeting bacterial cellular machinery — specifically structures such as the bacterial cell wall, bacterial ribosomes, or bacterial DNA replication enzymes. They attack features that exist in bacteria but not in the cells of the human host, which is how they kill pathogens without (ideally) killing the person taking them.

Viruses are not bacteria. They are radically different entities. A virus is not a cell at all; it is genetic material (DNA or RNA) wrapped in a protein coat called a capsid, sometimes with a lipid envelope around that. Viruses do not have cell walls. They do not have ribosomes in the bacterial sense. They reproduce not by dividing independently but by hijacking host cell machinery — they inject their genetic material into a human cell and co-opt that cell's own molecular tools to replicate. There is no bacterial cell wall to disrupt, no bacterial ribosome to jam, no bacterial DNA replication complex to impair.

This means antibiotics have zero mechanism for attacking viruses. They are completely pharmacologically inert against the pathogens responsible for the common cold (predominantly rhinoviruses, though also coronaviruses, adenoviruses, and others) and influenza (influenza A and B viruses). Taking amoxicillin for an influenza infection is, from a pharmacological standpoint, indistinguishable from taking a sugar pill — at least in terms of fighting the virus.

The human immune system, however, is not inert. For most healthy adults, uncomplicated viral upper respiratory tract infections — the common cold, typical influenza — resolve on their own. The immune system mounts a defence, generates antibodies, deploys T-cells, clears the infected cells and viral particles, and the patient recovers. This takes time: most colds resolve within 7 to 10 days. Influenza typically peaks within 24 to 48 hours and begins to resolve within a week, though fatigue can persist longer.

The timing of this natural resolution is important. Patients who start antibiotics on day 2 or 3 of a cold often feel better by day 7 or 8. Their immune system did the work. The antibiotics were pharmacological spectators.

The CDC's research into antibiotic prescribing in the United States has found that approximately 30 percent of outpatient antibiotic prescriptions — tens of millions of prescriptions annually — are either unnecessary or inappropriate. Respiratory infections are the leading driver of this overprescription. A landmark study published in JAMA found that upper respiratory infections, acute bronchitis, and sinusitis together accounted for the overwhelming majority of antibiotic prescriptions written in error. The CDC's "Be Antibiotics Aware" national campaign was launched specifically because this pattern was so entrenched in clinical practice.

There is one nuance that complicates the clean picture: secondary bacterial infections. During or after a viral illness, the immune system is sometimes suppressed or the mucosal barriers are disrupted enough that bacteria can establish a foothold. Bacterial sinusitis, bacterial pneumonia, and streptococcal pharyngitis can develop as secondary complications. In these cases, antibiotics are genuinely appropriate. But these are complications of the underlying viral illness — they are not treating the virus itself. And diagnosing a true secondary bacterial infection requires clinical assessment; the mere presence of a cold or flu does not mandate antibiotics.

## Why People Believe This

The psychology of this belief is sophisticated and worth unpacking carefully, because it does not arise from simple ignorance.

**The temporal coincidence trap.** Human cognition is extraordinarily sensitive to temporal correlations. When two events occur close together in time — especially when one precedes the other — the mind infers causation almost automatically. This is not a cognitive defect; in the evolutionary environment in which we developed, temporal correlation was often a reliable guide to causal structure. But it fails spectacularly in medical contexts. A patient starts antibiotics on day 3 of a cold. The cold naturally resolves by day 8. The patient experiences recovery and attributes it to the antibiotics. Each repetition of this experience reinforces the belief. The antibiotics get the credit that belongs to the immune system.

**The mechanism feels plausible.** Patients are not thinking in terms of "bacterial cell wall synthesis inhibition." They are thinking: I have an infection, antibiotics fight infection, therefore antibiotics should help. This reasoning is logically coherent given a lay understanding of what antibiotics are — it is only wrong at the level of biological detail. The distinction between bacterial and viral infection is not intuitive. Both make you feel terrible. Both have the word "infection" attached to them. The fact that the molecular targets of antibiotics are entirely absent from viruses is not information that surfaces in everyday experience.

**The placebo and expectation architecture.** Beyond the coincidence trap, there is genuine evidence that taking medication — any medication, even an inert one — can reduce subjective symptom burden through expectation effects. A patient who believes antibiotics will help them feel better by day 3 may genuinely feel somewhat better by day 3, both because the natural illness trajectory produces improvement around that time and because expectation-driven neurobiological changes modulate symptom perception. This makes it extraordinarily difficult for personal experience to disconfirm the belief.

**Historical prescribing normalisation.** For decades — well into the late 20th century — it was standard medical practice to prescribe antibiotics for upper respiratory infections far more liberally than current evidence suggests is appropriate. Patients who grew up in this prescribing culture were repeatedly reinforced in the belief that antibiotics were the correct treatment for getting sick. Many doctors prescribe antibiotics even today partly because they have learned that patients expect them and will seek care elsewhere if denied; this creates a feedback loop that perpetuates the belief through institutional practice.

**The precautionary logic.** Some patients reason: even if the cold is viral, maybe a bacterial infection is developing, so antibiotics are a precaution. This is not entirely irrational — bacterial superinfection is a real risk. But evidence shows that prophylactic antibiotic use in uncomplicated viral infections does not meaningfully reduce the rate of secondary complications, while it does meaningfully increase bacterial resistance, harm the gut microbiome, and create side effects. The precautionary calculation, when properly accounted for, does not favour antibiotics for most uncomplicated viral illnesses.

**Confirmation from apparent outcomes.** People who feel better after taking antibiotics for a cold do not typically think "I would have recovered without them." They think "the antibiotics worked." People who take no antibiotics and also recover within a week often simply do not think about this situation at all, or they attribute the recovery to something else they did — resting, drinking fluids, taking paracetamol. The counterfactual — recovery without antibiotics — is invisible to personal-narrative formation in a way that recovery with antibiotics is not.

## Verdict

**False, with a clinically significant nuance.**

Antibiotics do not treat colds or influenza. They have no pharmacological action against any virus. The experience of recovery after taking antibiotics during a viral illness is a classic case of post hoc ergo propter hoc reasoning: the recovery would have happened anyway. The antibiotics took the credit.

The nuance is genuine: bacterial secondary infections can and do develop during or after viral respiratory illness, and in those cases antibiotics are appropriate. But this is an argument for attentive clinical assessment — not for reflexive antibiotic prescribing at the first sign of a cold or flu.

The EARC mode for this claim is **A** (Ambiguous at the surface, resolved by evidence): the claim feels plausible because recovery really does follow antibiotic use, but careful examination reveals the recovery is coincidental rather than caused.

Snopes and the scientific consensus align clearly: this claim is false.

## The Wider Picture

The overprescription of antibiotics for viral respiratory infections is not merely a minor medical error. It is one of the most consequential ongoing public health problems in the world.

**Antibiotic resistance.** Every unnecessary course of antibiotics creates selective pressure for resistant bacteria. In any population of bacteria, random mutations produce varieties that are slightly resistant to a given antibiotic. When the antibiotic is present, resistance confers survival advantage, and resistant strains proliferate. When antibiotics are prescribed needlessly — to a patient whose infection is entirely viral — the drugs enter the body, traverse the gut, and exert selection pressure on the commensal bacterial community. The patient's own gut microbiome is exposed to the drug, and any bacteria there that carry resistance genes may be enriched. The WHO has identified antibiotic resistance as one of the greatest threats to global health, food security, and development. The CDC estimates that antibiotic-resistant bacteria cause at least 2.8 million infections in the United States annually, resulting in over 35,000 deaths. Unnecessary prescriptions for viral infections are a significant driver of this crisis.

**Disruption of the microbiome.** Antibiotics are not surgically precise. They do not target only the pathogen the clinician has in mind. They affect the entire bacterial community they encounter. A course of broad-spectrum antibiotics for a cold can substantially alter gut microbiome diversity and composition, with effects that can persist for months. The gut microbiome has increasingly been implicated in immune function, mental health, metabolic regulation, and disease susceptibility. Treating a viral cold with antibiotics is, from a microbiome standpoint, using a sledgehammer to miss a nail while inadvertently demolishing a wall you did not know was structural.

**Direct patient harm.** Antibiotics cause side effects. Gastrointestinal disruption — diarrhoea, nausea, bloating, cramping — is common. Yeast overgrowth, particularly vaginal candidiasis in women, is a frequent secondary consequence of broad-spectrum antibiotics wiping out competing bacteria. Allergic reactions, including severe anaphylaxis in susceptible individuals, occur at meaningful rates. Patients who take antibiotics for colds face these risks for zero therapeutic benefit against the viral illness.

**Healthcare system costs.** Unnecessary antibiotic prescriptions waste healthcare resources, drive up the cost of care, and consume clinician time in ways that could otherwise be directed to patients with genuine need. There is also a time cost: the consultation in which a patient receives an unnecessary antibiotic prescription is a consultation in which they did not receive education about symptomatic management, hand hygiene, viral transmission reduction, or the actual timeline of natural recovery.

**What actually helps.** For uncomplicated viral upper respiratory infections, evidence-based management is symptomatic: adequate hydration, rest, paracetamol or ibuprofen for fever and aches, intranasal decongestants for stuffiness, throat lozenges or saline gargles for sore throats. The illness will resolve within the normal timeline. For influenza specifically, antiviral agents such as oseltamivir (Tamiflu) have genuinely modest efficacy when started within 48 hours of symptom onset — but these are antivirals, not antibiotics, and they work by targeting viral neuraminidase rather than any bacterial structure.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes and other leading fact-checking organisations classify antibiotic-for-viral-infection claims as **False** across the board. The evidence base here is so robust, and the scientific consensus so complete, that there is no credible medical authority arguing the contrary case. The challenge for fact-checkers is not in establishing the verdict — which is straightforward — but in explaining *why the belief is so persistent despite being false*, which is a subtler and more interesting problem.

Fact-checkers typically highlight three elements. First, the distinction between bacteria and viruses: these are fundamentally different types of biological entity, and antibiotics are mechanistically non-functional against viruses. Second, the natural resolution timeline: most colds resolve in 7 to 10 days regardless of treatment, which means any treatment started during a cold will coincide with recovery. Third, the costs of overprescription: antibiotic resistance, microbiome disruption, and direct side effects are real and substantial.

The CDC's "Be Antibiotics Aware" campaign has been referenced extensively by fact-checkers as the authoritative US public health voice on this issue. The WHO's Global Action Plan on Antimicrobial Resistance provides the international framing. The Cochrane Collaboration has published systematic reviews finding no meaningful clinical benefit for antibiotics in uncomplicated viral respiratory infections and documenting the risk of side effects.

What makes this claim epistemically interesting — and what sophisticated fact-checkers try to capture — is that it is phenomenologically real. People genuinely recover after taking antibiotics. Their experience is not fabricated. The error is in the causal inference. This is a category of health myth that is harder to displace than outright fabrication, because disconfirming it requires convincing someone that their own felt experience of recovery is not what it appears to be. That is a much more challenging epistemic task than showing that a conspiracy theory lacks evidence.

The most durable corrective, according to health communication researchers, is not to dismiss the patient's experience of recovery but to accurately attribute it: "Yes, you recovered — your immune system did an excellent job. The cold ran its natural course. The antibiotics, unfortunately, were not able to help against the virus, though I understand it can feel that way." Medical education programmes that have adopted this framing report greater patient satisfaction than those that take a simple "you're wrong" approach.

The lesson for media literacy: when evaluating medical claims, the question is not just "did recovery follow treatment?" but "would recovery have occurred without treatment?" That counterfactual thinking is the foundation of rigorous clinical research and the tool that most reliably separates genuine treatment effects from temporal coincidence. Antibiotics for viral infections fail that test entirely.
