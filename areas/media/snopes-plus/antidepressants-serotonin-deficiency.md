# Antidepressants work by correcting a serotonin deficiency in the brain

## 1. The Claim

One of the most widely held beliefs about psychiatric medication holds that clinical depression is caused by a deficiency of serotonin — a neurotransmitter — in the brain, and that antidepressant medications work by correcting this deficiency, restoring the brain to its natural chemical equilibrium. This "chemical imbalance" model of depression has achieved the status of common knowledge: it appears in pharmaceutical advertisements, in patient information leaflets, in popular self-help literature, in magazine summaries of mental health research, and in the explanations that millions of clinicians have given millions of patients when initiating antidepressant treatment.

The dominant version describes it as follows: depression is a brain disease characterized by insufficient levels of serotonin in certain neural circuits; selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), the most commonly prescribed class of antidepressants, work by blocking the reuptake of serotonin at the synaptic cleft, thereby increasing available serotonin and alleviating depression. Just as diabetics need insulin because their bodies do not produce enough of it, depressed people need SSRIs because their brains do not produce or retain enough serotonin. The medication restores what is missing.

This framing has enormous practical consequences. It shapes how patients understand their diagnoses, how they evaluate the decision to take medication, whether they experience stigma (a brain disease model tends to reduce self-blame while sometimes increasing social stigma), how long they continue medication, and what they expect from treatment. It also has policy and commercial consequences: the chemical imbalance model provided the intellectual scaffold for the mass-market antidepressant advertising campaigns of the 1990s and 2000s, most famously Pfizer's long-running campaign for Zoloft (sertraline), which depicted an animated figure beneath a cloud until a serotonin molecule brightened its life. That campaign helped transform SSRIs from psychiatric medications into consumer products, and drove the annual antidepressant prescription total in the United States to over 250 million by the early 2020s.

## 2. What's Actually True

**The serotonin hypothesis was never established science.** The serotonin hypothesis of depression — the proposition that depression is caused by low serotonin — was a speculative hypothesis proposed in the 1960s, primarily associated with Joseph Schildkraut (1965), "The Catecholamine Hypothesis of Affective Disorders," published in the *American Journal of Psychiatry*, and with work by Alec Coppen in the same decade. The hypothesis was always tentative: Schildkraut's original paper described it as "a useful oversimplification" and explicitly flagged that the evidence was insufficient to draw firm conclusions. It was proposed as a research direction, not as an established mechanism.

Critically, SSRIs do not provide direct evidence for the serotonin deficiency model. The fact that SSRIs block serotonin reuptake and that some patients improve on SSRIs does not confirm that those patients had low serotonin before treatment. This is the fundamental logical error in the popular account: inferring the cause of a disease from the mechanism of a drug that treats its symptoms. Moncrieff and colleagues (2022), "The Serotonin Theory of Depression: A Systematic Umbrella Review of the Evidence," published in *Molecular Psychiatry*, conducted a comprehensive review of the evidence for the serotonin hypothesis across six research areas: serotonin metabolite studies, serotonin receptor studies, serotonin transporter (SERT) studies, depletion studies, genetic studies, and antidepressant drug studies. They found no consistent evidence that people with depression have lower serotonin activity or levels than people without depression. Their conclusion: "The main areas of serotonin research provide no consistent evidence of there being an association between serotonin and depression, and no support for the hypothesis that depression is caused by lowered serotonin activity or concentrations."

**Tryptophan depletion studies do not support the hypothesis.** One of the stronger potential tests of the serotonin theory involves tryptophan depletion: since tryptophan is the dietary precursor to serotonin, temporarily reducing tryptophan through dietary manipulation should produce depression in healthy or recovered individuals if low serotonin causes depression. A meta-analysis by Ruhe, Mason, and Schene (2007), "Mood Is Indirectly Related to Serotonin, Norepinephrine and Dopamine Levels in Humans: A Meta-Analysis of Monoamine Depletion Studies," published in *Molecular Psychiatry*, found that tryptophan depletion did not produce depression in healthy volunteers without a personal or family history of depression. Some vulnerability was observed in individuals with a prior episode, but the effect was inconsistent and small — not the dramatic crash one would predict if serotonin were the primary determinant of depressive state.

**The chemical imbalance framing was pharmaceutical marketing, not science.** Lacasse and Leo (2005), "Serotonin and Depression: A Disconnect Between the Advertisements and the Scientific Literature," published in *PLoS Medicine*, systematically compared the claims made in pharmaceutical advertisements for SSRIs with the scientific literature and found a profound disconnect. Drug advertisements consistently claimed that SSRIs worked by correcting a specific chemical imbalance, that this imbalance was associated with depression, and that SSRIs restored brain chemistry to its natural state. None of these claims were supported by the scientific literature at the time they were made. The authors documented that the serotonin deficiency model had been largely abandoned in research psychiatry by the time it was being used in direct-to-consumer advertising as an established fact.

**Do antidepressants work?** This is the key adjacent question, and the answer is importantly nuanced. Kirsch and colleagues (2008), "Initial Severity and Antidepressant Benefits: A Meta-Analysis of Data Submitted to the Food and Drug Administration," published in *PLoS Medicine*, analyzed FDA trial data for the six most widely prescribed antidepressants and found that, for mild to moderate depression, the clinical benefit over placebo was below the threshold considered clinically meaningful. Effect sizes were larger for severe depression. This analysis has been contested — Cipriani and colleagues (2018), "Comparative Efficacy and Acceptability of 21 Antidepressant Drugs for the Acute Treatment of Adults with Major Depressive Disorder: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis," published in *The Lancet*, found in a meta-analysis of 522 trials that all 21 antidepressants tested outperformed placebo to a statistically and clinically meaningful degree, with effect sizes varying by drug and severity. The current scientific consensus supports antidepressants as effective treatments for moderate-to-severe depression, but this consensus coexists with acknowledgment that their mechanism of action is unknown and that the serotonin deficiency theory does not explain their benefits.

The gap between "these drugs work for some people" and "these drugs work by correcting serotonin deficiency" is where the popular claim fails. The efficacy of antidepressants does not vindicate any particular mechanistic theory.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The chemical imbalance theory's grip on popular understanding is exceptionally strong, and its persistence despite scientific disavowal reflects several compounding mechanisms.

**The biomedical model's rhetorical appeal and the stigma-management function.** The serotonin deficiency framing offered something that its predecessors — psychoanalytic accounts of depression rooted in unresolved conflict, early childhood experience, or character pathology — did not: it was exculpatory. If depression is a biological deficit, like a vitamin deficiency, then the person experiencing it is not weak, self-indulgent, or inadequately coping. They are sick in a physiological sense that commands the same moral neutrality as diabetes or hypothyroidism. Kvaale, Haslam, and Gottdiener (2013), "The 'Side Effects' of Medicalization: A Meta-Analytic Review of How Biogenetic Causal Attributions Affect Stigma," published in *Clinical Psychology Review*, found that biological causal attributions for mental illness reduced self-stigma (blame of self) while increasing social stigma (others' perceptions). The chemical imbalance framing thus had genuine psychosocial utility: it relieved patients of a particularly painful form of self-attribution while providing a coherent, dignified narrative for their experience. This **therapeutic function of false belief** is a specific mechanism that distinguishes the chemical imbalance myth from other neuromyths — people are not merely credulous, they are motivated to believe something that serves an important psychological need.

**The iatro-epistemic authority loop.** When a psychiatrist or general practitioner hands a patient a prescription for sertraline and explains that "many people have a chemical imbalance — not enough serotonin — and this medication helps correct that," the explanation carries authoritative weight. The patient's subsequent experience — some improvement in symptoms, or at least the activation of meaningful treatment-seeking — reinforces the framing. Read, Cartwright, and Gibson (2014), "Adverse Emotional and Interpersonal Effects Reported by 1829 New Zealanders While Taking Antidepressants," published in *Psychiatry Research*, found that a substantial majority of patients surveyed had been told by their prescribers that their depression was caused by a chemical imbalance. The medical encounter is itself a mechanism of belief propagation: the doctor-patient relationship transmits the serotonin hypothesis not as a hypothesis but as a diagnosis. The patient's belief is not formed from reading pharmaceutical advertisements but from a clinical explanation given in a context of therapeutic vulnerability and professional authority. This constitutes **physician-mediated misinformation** — not in the sense of deliberate deception, but as the downstream effect of a marketing framework that successfully colonized clinical communication.

**The commensurability with visible pharmaceutical action.** SSRIs produce noticeable physiological effects: many patients notice changes in emotional tone, appetite, sexual function, and sleep patterns within days to weeks. These effects are real, and they make the serotonin-correction story feel mechanistically confirmed. The inference structure is: "I was depressed; I took a drug that affects serotonin; I felt better; therefore my depression was caused by insufficient serotonin." The middle term is real (SSRIs do affect serotonin systems) and the outcome is real (some patients do improve), but the conclusion does not follow. This is a **pharmacological post hoc fallacy** — a special case of the broader pattern of inferring disease etiology from therapeutic response. It works in this case because serotonin-affecting drugs produce subjectively noticeable effects that feel like restoration of a prior state, which is exactly the experiential prediction the serotonin deficiency model makes. The phenomenology of SSRI response is compatible with the deficiency model, even though that compatibility is logically equivocal.

**Asymmetric temporal dynamics of correction.** The chemical imbalance theory became entrenched in public consciousness largely through pharmaceutical marketing in the late 1980s and 1990s. The systematic scientific critique — particularly the Moncrieff et al. (2022) umbrella review in *Molecular Psychiatry* — arrived decades later, after hundreds of millions of patients had been prescribed SSRIs under this explanatory framework and after the theory had been embedded in medical education, patient information resources, and clinical communication norms. Correcting a culturally embedded belief requires overcoming the **temporal advantage of entrenchment**: the false claim had decades to become normalized before rigorous critique achieved similar prominence. By 2022, the serotonin theory was so deeply embedded in clinical practice culture that many treating physicians continued using the language of chemical imbalance even while acknowledging, if pressed, that the underlying science was more uncertain.

## 4. Verdict

**False as stated; more complex question requires careful calibration**

The claim that antidepressants work by correcting a serotonin deficiency in the brain is false. There is no established evidence that depression is caused by low serotonin. The systematic review by Moncrieff and colleagues (2022) in *Molecular Psychiatry* surveyed the available evidence across all major research strands and found no consistent relationship between serotonin levels or activity and depression. The serotonin hypothesis was a speculative research framework, explicitly labeled as incomplete by its originators, that was transformed into a confident public claim by pharmaceutical marketing without corresponding scientific validation.

This verdict does not imply that antidepressants do not work — the efficacy question is separate from the mechanism question. The evidence for antidepressant efficacy in moderate-to-severe depression is legitimate; the evidence for the serotonin deficiency mechanism is not. Patients who benefit from SSRIs are experiencing real therapeutic effects that are not explained by the serotonin deficiency model.

The correct statement of the evidence is approximately: "We do not know precisely how antidepressants work. They are associated with benefit for some patients with moderate-to-severe depression. The serotonin correction theory was a hypothesis that was adopted into public discourse before it had been established, and subsequent evidence has not supported it."

## 5. The Wider Picture

The chemical imbalance story is one of the most consequential pharmaceutical marketing successes in the history of medicine. The decision by Pfizer, Eli Lilly, GlaxoSmithKline, and other manufacturers to frame SSRIs through the chemical imbalance model was a deliberate rhetorical strategy. The SSRIs themselves were effective products; what the chemical imbalance framing provided was a mechanism story that (a) destigmatized use, (b) implied an ongoing physiological need analogous to insulin for diabetes, and (c) supported indefinite prescription continuation rather than time-limited courses. Each of these characteristics served commercial interests directly. A drug that corrects an ongoing biological deficiency is chronically consumed; a drug that addresses episodic psychological distress is used only during episodes.

Healy (1997), "The Antidepressant Era," Harvard University Press, documented the commercial strategy in historical detail, showing that the serotonin framing was adopted in consumer communication precisely because it was comprehensible, comforting, and conducive to long-term use — not because it was scientifically established. The pharmaceutical companies succeeded in having a marketing hypothesis treated as scientific fact through a combination of direct-to-consumer advertising (legal in the US from 1997), physician detail visits, continuing medical education programs, and the funding of patient advocacy organizations that incorporated the chemical imbalance framing into their own communications. The American Psychiatric Association's educational materials referenced the chemical imbalance theory as established science; patient advocacy organizations used exactly the Pfizer advertising language in their explanatory pamphlets. An epistemically closed loop formed: marketing created a claim; medical organizations legitimated it; patients received it as clinical fact; clinicians rehearsed it because patients found it helpful; and the loop became self-sustaining.

The consequences of the chemical imbalance myth are not only epistemological. Deacon and Baird (2009), "The Chemical Imbalance Explanation as a Defense of Antidepressants: Exonerating Helplessness," published in the *Journal of Affective Disorders*, found that patients who endorsed the chemical imbalance model were more likely to attribute their improvement exclusively to medication and less likely to credit their own coping efforts, therapy participation, lifestyle changes, or social support. This **learned therapeutic passivity** has clinical implications: patients who believe their recovery is chemical may underinvest in non-pharmacological interventions that have independent evidence bases, including cognitive behavioral therapy, exercise, social connection, and behavioral activation — all of which have documented efficacy in depression treatment. Cognitive behavioral therapy has been shown to have durable effects that persist after treatment ends; pharmacological treatment generally requires ongoing use to maintain benefit. A patient who understands only the chemical mechanism may reasonably conclude that CBT is less necessary than the medication, which shapes treatment decisions in ways that may not serve their long-term interests.

The Moncrieff et al. (2022) paper in *Molecular Psychiatry* produced a significant public response that illustrated the cultural stakes of the chemical imbalance question. The paper was covered extensively in mainstream media — *The Guardian* ran it as a front-page story — and prompted defensive responses from psychiatric professional organizations. The UK's Royal College of Psychiatrists acknowledged the serotonin deficit claim was not supported but defended antidepressant prescribing on the grounds that efficacy was independent of mechanism. This response was scientifically defensible but obscured the cultural harm done by decades of false mechanistic claims: patients who had been told their brains were chemically deficient, who had organized their self-understanding around that claim, who had justified medication use on that basis, were now being told the underlying explanation was false while being reassured that the medication was still worth taking. Managing that cognitive discontinuity is not a trivial clinical or social challenge.

The broader question the chemical imbalance story raises is about the appropriate relationship between scientific uncertainty and clinical communication. Psychiatry is a field in which mechanisms of most interventions are not well understood. Antidepressants work better than placebo for many people; how they work is not clear. Given this uncertainty, clinicians face a genuine communication dilemma: patients find the "we don't know how this works" explanation unsatisfying and anxiety-inducing; the chemical imbalance story is comforting, reduces stigma, and appears to improve adherence. Was it wrong for clinicians to use it? Morally, the answer probably hinges on whether the false belief could be produced without consent and without awareness of its evidence status — and the systematic review evidence suggests that at the relevant time (1990s–2000s), many clinicians using the language were themselves believers rather than knowing communicators of a myth. The pharmaceutical marketing succeeded not only with patients but with the medical community.

The story has important lessons for the relationship between pharmacoepidemiology, pharmaceutical marketing, and public scientific literacy. When commercial interests are aligned with a mechanistic narrative — as they were for SSRI manufacturers and the chemical imbalance story — the resources available for propagating that narrative through medical education, advertising, patient advocacy, and clinical guideline development create asymmetries that scientific peer review cannot easily counteract. Peer review operates on a timescale and with a reach that are both considerably smaller than those of direct-to-consumer pharmaceutical campaigns. The Moncrieff et al. (2022) umbrella review summarized a literature that had been skeptical of the serotonin hypothesis for decades; what 2022 added was a visible synthesis that achieved media salience. The underlying skepticism was available in the scientific literature from the 1990s onward, but it existed in a register where it could not compete with Pfizer's $100 million annual Zoloft advertising budget.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The serotonin-depression claim is at the harder end of the fact-checking spectrum, for reasons that illuminate structural limits of the fact-checking model generally. It is not false in the simple sense of being fabricated or demonstrably contradicted by an existing document. It is false in the sense of being a hypothesis that was publicly communicated as an established fact without adequate evidentiary basis, in a context where the primary propagators were pharmaceutical corporations and the primary conduits were physicians and medical organizations. Fact-checking organizations optimized for adjudicating specific viral claims — "did Politician X say Y" or "is Statistic Z accurate" — are not well-positioned to evaluate the more diffuse claim "is this mechanistic theory that has been communicated as established fact for decades actually established."

Snopes has not addressed this claim directly. Several other prominent fact-checking outlets have covered aspects of it, typically in the aftermath of the Moncrieff et al. (2022) umbrella review, with mixed success. Some covered the story in ways that were accurate (the serotonin theory is not established) but counterproductively alarming (emphasizing the "antidepressants DON'T work" framing that conflates mechanism with efficacy, prompting concerns about patients discontinuing medication unsafely). This reflects a genuine tension in responsible fact-checking of medical claims: correcting a false mechanistic theory must be implemented carefully in a context where the treatment associated with that theory has genuine clinical value. The responsible correction is neither "the theory is wrong so the treatment doesn't work" nor "the treatment works so the theory must be right" but a more precise statement that the mechanism remains unclear while the treatment has evidence-based applications.

The best fact-checking response would clearly separate three things: (1) the claim about mechanism (serotonin deficiency — not established, probably false); (2) the claim about efficacy (antidepressants help with moderate-to-severe depression — supported by evidence with appropriate caveats); and (3) the claim about clinical communication (patients were told the mechanism as established fact — true, and a harm worth acknowledging). No major fact-checking outlet has yet produced a treatment of this kind.

There is also a public health consideration specific to this claim: because the serotonin narrative is associated with medication taking, aggressive fact-checking without clinical contextualisation risks contributing to unsafe discontinuation of antidepressants. Antidepressant discontinuation without medical supervision can produce significant withdrawal effects and relapse. Any fact-checking of this claim must incorporate safety messaging around this specific risk — a requirement that extends the scope of fact-checking beyond the simple verdict function.
