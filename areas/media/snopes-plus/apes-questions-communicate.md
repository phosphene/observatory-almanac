# The Truth Vault: Apes Have Never Asked Questions Despite Scientists Communicating with Them Since '60s?

## 1. The Claim

A recurring assertion on social media, particularly on Reddit and X (formerly Twitter), holds that despite decades of effort by scientists to communicate with apes using sign language and symbol-based systems, no ape has ever asked a question. The claim often appears in threads about animal intelligence, human uniqueness, or the limits of interspecies communication, and it carries an implicit argument: if apes can learn hundreds of signs but never ask a question, then there is something qualitatively different about human language that apes cannot bridge.

The claim circulated widely in Reddit communities such as r/Damnthatsinteresting, where it was framed as a fascinating fact about the limits of ape cognition. On X, the account @historyinmemes posted the claim to a broad audience, generating thousands of shares and comments. The underlying implication is that the ability to ask questions represents a uniquely human cognitive capacity—a threshold that separates us from our closest evolutionary relatives. The claim is provocative because it suggests that despite appearances, ape communication is fundamentally different from human language in its structure and purpose.

The factual assertions embedded in the claim are: that scientists have been communicating with apes since the 1960s (or earlier); that apes have learned hundreds of signs or symbols; and that no ape has ever asked a question in any of these communication systems. Each of these assertions requires careful examination.

## 2. What's Actually True

The Snopes article, authored by Madison Dapcevich and published in September 2024, provides a thorough examination of the claim and reaches a nuanced conclusion. Let us examine what is actually true.

First, the claim's chronological framing is inaccurate. Scientists have been conducting systematic research into ape communication since at least the 1930s, not the 1960s as claimed. The pioneering work of Luella and Winthrop Kellog with a chimpanzee named Gua in the 1930s established the foundation for later research. Gua could understand approximately 100 human words, although she did not produce speech herself. This predates the 1960s by three decades.

Second, a substantial body of research documents apes engaging in what researchers variously describe as questioning behavior. The Snopes article cites multiple experts who affirm this. Heidi Lyn, a professor at the University of South Alabama's Comparative Cognition and Communication Lab, states flatly: "There is plenty of evidence of apes asking questions, although the structure may not look exactly like humans asking questions." Cat Hobaiter, a professor at the University of St Andrews who specializes in ape cognition and communication, similarly reports "plenty of descriptions across multiple enculturated ape studies that include the apes 'asking questions.'"

The most frequently cited example comes from the 1970s, when a chimpanzee named Sarah—trained by researchers Ann and David Premack—used a symbol-based system to apparently ask for the names of things. A 1974 New York Times article described the phenomenon with the headline: "'Talking' Chimpanzee Asks For Names of Things Now." Mike Beran, professor of psychology at the University of Georgia's Language Research Center, confirmed that researchers documented these questioning utterances in the 1970s, though he noted the ambiguity about whether the chimpanzee understood the interrogative nature of her own communication.

Third, the claim that apes have never asked questions depends heavily on how one defines "asking a question." If we require question-asking to take the form of a grammatically marked interrogative sentence—complete with subject-auxiliary inversion, rising intonation, or a question word such as "what" or "where"—then the evidence is indeed thin to nonexistent. But if we define question-asking functionally—as an act of communication that seeks information from another—then the evidence is substantial. A chimpanzee that looks its handler in the eyes and points to a banana may be understood as asking for the banana. A bonobo that uses a symbol to request an activity may be understood as asking to participate. Whether these behaviors count as questions depends on one's theory of language.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes' article on this topic is one of the more thoughtful and carefully researched entries in their catalog. The article is commendable for several reasons.

First, it goes beyond the typical binary framework of "True" or "False" to present a range of expert opinions, including views that complicate the simple picture. The article includes the skeptical perspective of Herbert Terrace, the psychologist known for his work with Nim Chimpsky, who has argued that perceived ape communication is often prompted or cued by human handlers. This inclusion of dissenting views demonstrates intellectual honesty and a willingness to engage with the scientific complexity of the question.

Second, the article provides useful historical context, tracing the history of ape communication research from Gua in the 1930s through Washoe, Sarah, Koko, and contemporary research. This contextualization helps readers understand that the claim's 1960s framing is itself inaccurate and that the research tradition is richer and longer than the viral post suggests.

Third, the article engages seriously with definitional questions, acknowledging that what counts as a "question" is itself a matter of scholarly debate. The article allows multiple experts to offer their definitions and interpretations, rather than imposing a single authoritative framework.

However, the Snopes article has significant limitations. The most important is that Snopes avoids issuing a definitive rating—the article is categorized as an "Article" rather than a "Fact Check" with a rating label. This abstention from judgment, while perhaps understandable given the complexity of the issue, leaves the reader without guidance on how to evaluate the claim. The article appears to conclude that the claim is "both true and false depending on definitions," but this is a conclusion of limited practical utility.

More substantively, the article does not adequately address the philosophical dimensions of the question. The claim that apes have never asked questions is not merely a factual assertion about ape behavior; it is a claim about the nature of language, intentionality, and the relationship between communication and cognition. These are not questions that can be resolved by consulting primatologists alone; they require engagement with the philosophy of language, the philosophy of mind, and the epistemology of animal cognition.

The article also does not grapple with the possibility that the claim's persistence in popular culture reflects something about human self-understanding rather than about ape cognition. The claim appeals to a desire for human exceptionalism—the need to maintain a clear boundary between us and them. By framing the question in purely empirical terms, Snopes misses the opportunity to examine why this particular claim resonates so strongly.

Finally, the article underplays the significance of the skeptical position. Herbert Terrace's critique is not merely a minor dissenting view; it raises fundamental questions about the interpretation of ape behavior that have shaped the field for decades. The article mentions Terrace briefly but does not explore the depth of his challenge or the responses it has generated.

## 4. Why This Matters

The question of whether apes can ask questions matters far beyond the narrow factual dispute. It touches on fundamental questions about human uniqueness, the nature of language, and our ethical relationship to other species.

The claim that apes have never asked questions is often deployed to support a sharp discontinuity between human and animal cognition. If apes cannot ask questions, the argument goes, then human language is not merely different in degree but different in kind from animal communication. This has implications for how we think about animal consciousness, animal rights, and the moral status of non-human primates. If apes are capable of complex communication that includes information-seeking, then the boundaries between human and animal cognition become fuzzier, and the ethical implications become more urgent.

The claim also matters for how we think about the history of science. The ape language research of the 1960s, 1970s, and 1980s was a major scientific and cultural phenomenon. Washoe, Koko, and Nim Chimpsky became celebrities, their achievements reported breathlessly in the popular press and their limitations debated in academic journals. The claim that all of this research failed to produce a single question undermines the significance of an entire research tradition. If the claim is false or misleading, then our understanding of that history needs revision.

Furthermore, the question illuminates the relationship between definition and discovery in science. What counts as a question is not a natural kind that science can simply identify; it is a theoretical category that carries assumptions about the nature of language and communication. The Snopes article's hesitation to assign a rating reflects this uncertainty, but it also highlights the limits of the fact-checking genre when confronted with questions that require theoretical as well as empirical resolution.

Finally, the claim matters because it exemplifies a pattern of argument that appears across many domains of science communication: the use of a striking, counterintuitive "fact" to support a philosophical position about human uniqueness. Similar claims have been made about tool use, culture, morality, and theory of mind—each time, the claim serves to police the boundary between human and animal. Examining these claims carefully reveals that the boundary is more porous than the claims suggest.

## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article, written by Madison Dapcevich and published September 2, 2024, provides the primary source for this entry. The article cites experts Heidi Lyn (University of South Alabama), Cat Hobaiter (University of St Andrews), Mike Beran (University of Georgia), and Herbert Terrace (Columbia University). These four perspectives represent a range of views within the field, from those who affirm ape question-asking to those who are skeptical.

The article references several key primary sources in the ape language literature. The 1933 book *The Ape and the Child* by the Kellogs documents the earliest systematic research. The Premacks' 1972 *Scientific American* article describes Sarah's symbol system. The 1974 New York Times article documents Sarah's apparent requests for names. The Gorilla Foundation provides documentation of Koko's sign vocabulary. The Friends of Washoe organization maintains historical records of Washoe's ASL acquisition.

The article also references more recent research on multimodal communication in chimpanzees, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (2007) and the American Journal of Primatology (2015). These studies suggest that ape communication integrates vocalizations, gestures, facial expressions, and body language in ways that may have supported the emergence of language in human ancestors.

Notably absent from the article is any discussion of the bonobo Kanzi, whose apparent linguistic abilities have been documented by Sue Savage-Rumbaugh and colleagues. Kanzi's use of lexigrams—geometric symbols representing words—has been cited as evidence of ape comprehension of syntax and reference. The omission of Kanzi from the article is a significant gap, as Kanzi's communication has been among the most widely discussed cases in the ape language literature.

Kanzi's achievements are particularly relevant to the question of ape question-asking because his communication system included symbols that could function interrogatively. Savage-Rumbaugh documented instances in which Kanzi used lexigrams in contexts that strongly suggested he was seeking information—pointing at symbols and then at objects or locations in ways that invited response. Whether these behaviors constitute "asking questions" depends, again, on definition, but they provide some of the most compelling evidence for interrogative communication in non-human primates.

The article also does not address the critical question of motivation. Even if apes can ask questions, do they want to? The philosophical literature on curiosity in animals suggests that information-seeking behavior is present across many species, from rats to corvids to primates. Apes in captivity, however, may have fewer reasons to seek information from their human caretakers, since their basic needs are met and their environment is structured. The absence of frequent question-asking may reflect environmental and motivational factors rather than cognitive limitations.

Another dimension worth exploring is the difference between spontaneous question-asking and trained question-asking. In the ape language studies, much of the communication that looks like questioning occurred in the context of training sessions where apes were explicitly rewarded for using symbols. Skeptics like Herbert Terrace argue that this constitutes conditioned behavior rather than genuine interrogative intent. Proponents counter that even human children's question-asking develops within a supportive social context of adult prompting and reinforcement, and that the distinction between trained and spontaneous behavior is not as clear-cut as the skeptics suggest. This debate mirrors similar discussions in developmental psychology about the origins of human language acquisition and whether it can be explained by simple conditioning or requires innate linguistic capacities.

The broader implication of the ape question-asking debate is that it forces us to examine our assumptions about what language is and who can have it. If we define language narrowly—as requiring grammar, syntax, and the ability to form fully structured interrogative sentences—then apes do not have language, and they cannot ask questions. But if we define language more broadly—as any system of symbolic communication that allows for the exchange of information, requests, and questions—then the evidence for ape question-asking is strong. The choice between these definitions is not merely semantic; it reflects deeper assumptions about the uniqueness of human cognition and the nature of animal minds.

The deeper literature on ape question-asking is sparse but suggestive. Researchers have documented instances of apes using signs or symbols in contexts that appear interrogative, but few studies have systematically investigated the phenomenon. The difficulty lies partly in the need to infer the communicative intent of the ape, which is inherently uncertain, and partly in the tendency of researchers to focus on declarative and imperative communication rather than interrogation.

## 6. Verdict

The claim that apes have never asked a question despite decades of human-ape communication is **an oversimplification that depends on an unreasonably narrow definition of "question."** If we define questioning functionally—as any communicative act that seeks information—there is substantial evidence from multiple ape species, including chimpanzees, gorillas, and bonobos, of behavior that researchers interpret as question-asking. If we define questioning narrowly—as requiring grammatical interrogative marking in a human language—then the evidence is thin, but this definition may be question-begging, as it assumes human language structure as the standard for all possible communication.

Snopes' article provides a nuanced and well-researched treatment of the question, but its refusal to assign a definitive rating leaves the reader without guidance. The article correctly identifies the complexity of the issue but does not adequately address the philosophical dimensions or the skeptical tradition represented by Terrace.

The Truth Vault therefore categorizes this entry as **requiring reframing** (earc_mode R). The viral claim is not straightforwardly false; it points to a genuine ambiguity in the interpretation of ape behavior. But the claim is misleading when presented as a settled fact, because it depends on contested premises about what counts as language and what counts as a question. The more accurate statement would be: "Whether apes have asked questions depends on how you define questioning; under functional definitions, there is evidence that they have, while under narrow grammatical definitions, the evidence is uncertain."

**Truth Vault Verdict: Requires Reframing. The claim is scientifically and philosophically ambiguous, and its presentation as a settled fact about ape cognition is misleading.**
