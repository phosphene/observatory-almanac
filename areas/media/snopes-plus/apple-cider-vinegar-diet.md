## §1 — Claim Anatomy

In April 2017, advertisements for a dietary supplement product — marketed variously as "Refresh Garcinia Cambogia" or "Garicinia Slim" — circulated on websites including Running Evolutions.com and The Platinum Beard, disguised as authentic journalistic content. The articles described a fictitious Cornell University nutrition science student named Amanda Haughman (in some versions, the purported weight loss was 31 pounds; in others, 37 pounds) who had allegedly used university research funds to discover a "diet hack" combining apple cider vinegar with Garcinia Cambogia supplements, achieving rapid weight loss without gym attendance at minimal personal cost.

The content was formatted to resemble a legitimate news article. It contained quote marks around attributed statements, identified an institutional affiliation (Cornell University), specified an academic department (nutrition sciences), provided a purported personal backstory (single mother, working toward a degree, no time for the gym), and included a fabricated interview formatted as a CNN news segment. The fake CNN interview lent the content a major media imprimatur, transforming what was essentially a supplement advertisement into purported investigative journalism by a credible national news organisation.

The claims operated at several levels. The narrative claim was that a real person named Amanda Haughman had conducted real research at Cornell and achieved real weight loss. The media claim was that CNN had covered this story. The product efficacy claim was that the combination of apple cider vinegar and Garcinia Cambogia produced meaningful weight loss. All three claim categories were false.

The product being marketed — Garcinia Cambogia combined with apple cider vinegar — is a category of dietary supplement that has been the subject of repeated false advertising enforcement actions and regulatory scrutiny. The Garcinia Cambogia market expanded rapidly in the United States following a television segment by Dr. Mehmet Oz in 2012, which the U.S. Senate subsequently scrutinised in 2014 as an example of television health promotion that outpaced evidence. The apple cider vinegar weight loss claim has existed in various forms since at least the 19th century and has been repeatedly evaluated without finding meaningful evidence of clinically significant fat reduction at typical dietary doses.

## §2 — Verification Record

Snopes investigator Dan Evon conducted a multi-pronged verification effort that identified and documented each of the major false components of the claim.

**The photograph.** The lead image purportedly showing Amanda Haughman's before-and-after weight loss was found to actually depict Rachel Graham, a woman profiled by the Today show in 2016 (Pawlowski, 2016, *Today*). Graham had lost approximately 90 pounds in one year — not the 31 or 37 pounds attributed to the fictional Amanda — and had done so through exercise and dietary change, with no involvement of apple cider vinegar or Garcinia Cambogia. Graham's own attributed quote in the Today profile explicitly rejected "secrets" or "gimmicks": "I want people to know that it is 110 percent possible [...] If you want to make changes, it is completely possible with healthy food and exercise." The use of Graham's photograph without consent to advertise a supplement she had not used and did not endorse constituted both a factual misrepresentation and an apparent violation of her image rights.

**The CNN interview.** Evon determined that no such CNN story existed. The fake interview was entirely fabricated, formatted to resemble a CNN news article with CNN's branding conventions. This represents impersonation of a major news organisation and is among the more legally significant elements of the content.

**The Cornell University affiliation.** No verification was possible or provided for the existence of Amanda Haughman at Cornell or in the nutrition science department. The name, identity, and university affiliation were consistent with a stock fictional persona generated for marketing purposes: a relatable, sympathetic demographic (single mother, student, financially constrained) with a plausible institutional connection.

**The supplement links.** The Platinum Beard article linked to a website selling Garcinia Cambogia. The Running Evolutions article linked to borrowed content from Barry's Boot Camp, a personal training service, rather than directly to a supplement seller — a detail that suggests either a content aggregation network or an evolving marketing arrangement. Evon reached out to Barry's Boot Camp for comment but received no reply.

Evon's conclusion was unambiguous: the content was an advertisement disguised as journalism, deploying fabricated identity, stolen photography, falsified media attribution, and manufactured institutional credentials to sell a dietary supplement.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis

The epistemic novelty of this case lies in its sophisticated deployment of layered authority signals. The content accumulated multiple distinct categories of credibility marker — academic institution, degree program, peer-reviewed methodology framing ("research project"), national news media, first-person authenticity (the "interview" format), and visual evidence (photographs) — and stacked them to produce a cumulative authority effect that would require systematic verification to penetrate.

Each individual authority signal is, in isolation, checkable. CNN has a verifiable archive. Cornell has a verifiable student directory (within FERPA constraints). Academic research projects produce verifiable outputs. Rachel Graham is a verifiable real person whose actual story is documented. But the content's design works against sequential verification: the very richness of the narrative — the detail of the research project, the specific department, the personal backstory, the fake interview transcript — consumes the cognitive resources that would otherwise be directed toward verification. A story with more detail feels more true, not less, despite the fact that a fabricated story can be made arbitrarily detailed.

This is a documented feature of motivated false narrative construction, sometimes analysed through the framework of narrative transportation theory. Green and Brock (2000, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) described narrative transportation as a mental state in which the reader is absorbed into a story's world in ways that reduce counterarguing, lower skepticism, and increase acceptance of the narrative's embedded claims. Highly detailed, emotionally resonant narratives produce stronger transportation effects. The Amanda Haughman story was designed to produce narrative transportation: the relatable single-mother persona, the clever use of university resources to solve a personal problem, the triumphant weight loss, the proud daughter — each element contributed to a reading experience that discouraged the cognitive distance required for verification.

The fake CNN interview deserves specific attention. Major news organisations function as trust intermediaries: their editorial standards, institutional accountability, and professional reputation are supposed to provide a quality filter that individual claims lack. Impersonating CNN appropriates this trust infrastructure without paying the reputational cost associated with maintaining it. The fake interview format — "CNN: Tell us, how did you know where to start? Amanda: To be honest, I really didn't..." — reproduces the surface conventions of television journalism (the on-camera question-and-answer format) in a text version that is simultaneously familiar and unverifiable from the reader's position.

The case also raises important questions about the information ecosystem in which such content circulates. It appeared on websites whose names (Running Evolutions, The Platinum Beard) did not signal diet supplement commerce. These sites function as neutral-seeming containers for what is functionally advertorial content, creating an additional layer of institutional misrepresentation: not only is CNN falsely claimed as a source, but the actual publishing venue — a commercial website for supplement marketing — is disguised as a lifestyle or wellness content platform.

## §4 — Scientific and Empirical Background

The claimed efficacy of apple cider vinegar combined with Garcinia Cambogia for weight loss is not supported by the scientific literature at clinically meaningful effect sizes and is appropriately characterised as unproven at the doses and durations described in promotional content.

**Apple cider vinegar.** The use of vinegar as a weight loss aid has a long folk medicine history, and some laboratory research has explored acetic acid's metabolic effects. Kondo et al. (2009, *Bioscience, Biotechnology, and Biochemistry*) conducted a randomised, double-blind trial in Japanese subjects with obesity, finding modest reductions in body weight (approximately 1–2 kg) in subjects consuming 15–30 mL of apple cider vinegar daily over 12 weeks compared to placebo. The effects were statistically significant but clinically modest, and the study population was Japanese adults; generalisability across populations with different baseline diets and metabolic profiles is uncertain. No study has documented 31–37 lbs of weight loss attributable to apple cider vinegar in a period comparable to that implied in the advertisement.

Additionally, concentrated acetic acid consumption carries documented risks including dental enamel erosion (Ehlen et al., 2008, *Journal of the American Dental Association*), pharyngeal and esophageal irritation, and potential interactions with potassium-lowering medications. These risks were not mentioned in the promotional content, as is typical of supplement marketing that emphasises purported benefit while omitting documented harm.

**Garcinia Cambogia.** The active compound in Garcinia Cambogia supplements is hydroxycitric acid (HCA), which has been proposed to inhibit the enzyme ATP citrate lyase, potentially reducing fatty acid synthesis and suppressing appetite. Onakpoya et al. (2011, *Journal of Obesity*) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of Garcinia Cambogia trials, finding small but statistically significant short-term weight reductions compared to placebo (approximately 1.3 kg). The clinical significance of this effect was assessed as uncertain, and the quality of available trials was noted as limited. A later review by Fassina et al. (2015, *Natural Product Research*) found similarly modest effects.

More seriously, the FDA in 2009 warned consumers to stop using certain Hydroxycut products containing Garcinia Cambogia after reports of serious liver injury, including at least one case requiring liver transplantation. The FDA warning noted that the precise mechanism of hepatotoxicity was unclear but that the association was sufficiently concerning to warrant a public safety advisory. This safety signal was not part of the promotional content's claims.

**The combination claim.** The advertisement specifically claims that the combination of apple cider vinegar with Garcinia Cambogia produces synergistic weight loss effects beyond either alone. No peer-reviewed clinical trial had examined or validated this specific combination as of 2017. The combination claim is a classic marketing construction in supplement advertising: by combining two individually tested (albeit weakly supported) compounds into a new combination product, the manufacturer generates a novel claim that is simultaneously beyond the scope of existing evidence and superficially reasonable to a consumer aware of each component's individual evidence base.

The institutional setting claimed in the advertisement — a Cornell University nutrition research project — adds a layer of perceived epistemic legitimacy that actual university-based nutrition research does not validate. Cornell's Division of Nutritional Sciences is a legitimate research institution; its implicit endorsement, fabricated in this advertisement, represents a deliberate appropriation of academic credibility.

## §5 — Misinformation Mechanics

The mechanics of this case represent an advanced iteration of a well-documented genre: the fake news article as commercial advertisement, commonly referred to in advertising regulation discourse as "native advertising" or, when deceptive, as "advertorial fraud." The advertising industry's shift toward content marketing — where promotional material is designed to resemble editorial journalism — has created a broad legitimisation cover for deceptive practices that deploy the same surface conventions while eliminating the editorial accountability structures.

Federal Trade Commission guidelines require that sponsored content be clearly identified as such, in a manner that ordinary consumers can recognise. The Amanda Haughman advertisement violated this requirement by formatting itself as a news article while providing no disclosure of its commercial nature. The FTC's enforcement history on this issue (and its 2015 guidance on native advertising) provides the regulatory context within which such content exists and has some constraint, but enforcement remains resource-limited and many violations go unchallenged.

The identity fabrication in this case — the creation of Amanda Haughman as a fictional persona with coherent biographical details — represents an escalation beyond simple false claims about product efficacy. It involves the construction of a fictitious social actor whose fabricated experience is designed to proxy for the testimonial evidence that genuine users of a product would provide if the product actually worked. The testimonial mechanism is psychologically powerful because it converts abstract product claims into concrete human experience, activating both narrative transportation and social proof. The fabricated testimonial captures both effects while bypassing the inconvenient requirement that the product actually perform as claimed.

The image theft from Rachel Graham is among the most documentably harmful elements of the case. Graham's genuine weight loss story — achieved through exercise and diet, documented transparently in a *Today* show profile — was appropriated to service a diametrically opposite narrative: that passive supplement consumption produces comparable results. This appropriation caused Graham potential reputational harm (association with a fraudulent product), social harm (her likeness circulating without consent in a commercial context), and informational harm (her documented message about sustainable dietary change being inverted into an advertisement for a quick fix).

The persistence of the "Cornell student" template across variation — some versions naming the weight loss as 31 lbs, others 37 lbs; some linking to different suppliers — suggests a modular content production approach in which multiple versions of the same fabricated narrative are deployed across multiple affiliated sites. This production model is consistent with the economics of supplement affiliate marketing, in which a network of content sites drives traffic to product pages and earns commission per conversion.

## §6 — Verdict and Takeaways

**Verdict: False.** The story of Cornell nutrition student Amanda Haughman losing 31–37 pounds using apple cider vinegar and Garcinia Cambogia was entirely fabricated. No such student exists; no such research project occurred; CNN aired no such segment. The photographs associated with the story belonged to Rachel Graham, whose actual weight loss was documented by *Today* in 2016 and involved no dietary supplements. The content was a commercial advertisement for Refresh Garcinia Cambogia and related products, designed to impersonate journalistic content by stacking false authority signals derived from Cornell University's academic prestige, CNN's media credibility, and a fictitious persona's sympathetic life narrative.

The gap is classified as type **A** (Accuracy): every material element of the claim was false. There is no factual kernel to contextualise or qualify; the claim is entirely fabricated.

The scientific record on the constituent products is clear: apple cider vinegar produces modest, clinically uncertain weight effects at daily doses in the range of 15–30 mL (Kondo et al., 2009, *Bioscience, Biotechnology, and Biochemistry*); Garcinia Cambogia produces similar modest effects (Onakpoya et al., 2011, *Journal of Obesity*) and has been associated with rare but serious hepatotoxicity events sufficient to prompt FDA safety warnings. Neither product has been demonstrated to produce the dramatic weight loss described in the advertisement. The specific combination of the two products has not been studied in peer-reviewed trials. Weight loss of 30+ pounds attributed to supplement use without dietary or exercise change is inconsistent with the known physiological mechanisms through which these compounds are proposed to act.

For consumers, the case exemplifies several hallmarks of dietary supplement misinformation: fabricated celebrity or institutional endorsement, before-and-after photograph misappropriation, fake media attribution (CNN in this case, often also NBC, ABC, or Dr. Oz), implausibly large, rapid weight loss claims, and first-person "testimonial" narratives with suspiciously perfect circumstances (busy single mom, no time for gym, university budget). Encountering any of these elements should trigger verification rather than sharing.

For regulators and researchers, the case illustrates the inadequacy of disclosure requirements as a complete solution to advertorial fraud: when the advertorial is designed to impersonate mainstream journalism, even a prominent "paid advertisement" disclosure would be insufficient to counteract the narrative authority constructed by fabricated journalistic conventions. Structural interventions — source authentication, photograph reverse-image search accessibility, and platform accountability for advertorial hosting — are increasingly important complements to disclosure-based approaches.
