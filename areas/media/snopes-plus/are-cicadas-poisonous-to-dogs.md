# Are Cicadas Poisonous to Dogs?


## 1. The Claim

Every seventeen years in the eastern United States, a spectacle unfolds that most living humans have only witnessed once or twice in their lifetimes: the emergence of a periodical cicada brood. Brood X — one of the largest of the seventeen-year cicada broods, covering seventeen states from Georgia to New York — last emerged in spring 2021, and with it came a flood of social media content, news coverage, and, inevitably, pet-owner panic. Among the claims that circulated widely: cicadas are poisonous or otherwise dangerous for dogs to consume.

The specific formulation of the claim varied. Some versions were cautionary — "don't let your dog eat cicadas, they're toxic" — while others were more extreme, asserting that cicada consumption causes liver failure, neurological damage, or death in dogs. Some posts warned that cicada shells would lodge in a dog's digestive tract and cause intestinal blockage. Images circulated alongside these claims showing dogs with swollen faces or apparent gastrointestinal distress, though the connection to cicada consumption was typically asserted rather than demonstrated.

The widespread nature of this concern was not unreasonable. Dogs, as opportunistic foragers, are famously willing to consume insects, small animals, and organic material they encounter on the ground. During a Brood X emergence, cicadas appear in densities that can reach tens of thousands per acre — a veritable buffet for any outdoor dog. The question of whether this buffet carries health risks for pets is therefore one of genuine practical relevance for the millions of dog owners in affected states.

The short answer that Snopes and veterinary authorities converge upon is reassuring: cicadas are not poisonous, and eating a few will not harm most dogs. The longer answer is more nuanced and involves the biological distinctions between toxicity, pathogenicity, and mechanical harm, as well as the ecological and entomological context of what cicadas actually are and what risks genuinely attend their consumption.


## 2. What's Actually True

The authoritative consensus, represented by veterinary organizations, poison control services, and entomologists, is consistent: cicadas are not poisonous or toxic to dogs. They do not possess venom glands, do not secrete defensive toxins, and contain no known compounds that are poisonous to domestic animals when ingested.

The Pet Poison Helpline, a professional toxicology resource for veterinarians and pet owners, states plainly that cicadas do not bite or sting and are generally not harmful to snacking pets. The exoskeleton (the hard outer shell of the insect, composed of the polysaccharide chitin and structural proteins) may be difficult to digest, but ingesting a small number of cicadas is unlikely to produce any adverse effects in a healthy dog. The Helpline's advisory notes that when ingested, cicadas "can potentially result in some stomach upset in dogs and cats, as the exoskeleton may be difficult to digest."

Jonathan Larson, an extension entomologist at the University of Kentucky, provided similar professional guidance during the 2021 emergence. Larson stated that dogs that "snatch a cicada while in the yard or on a walk" will "more than likely be fine," but added that "overindulgence on anything is bad, and this holds true with cicadas and pets." Dogs that feast on large quantities of cicadas, Larson noted, can develop upset stomachs, vomiting, or diarrhea as a consequence of overconsumption — not from toxicity but from the mechanical and fermentative challenges of processing large quantities of insect exoskeleton.

Multiple veterinarians expanded on this theme. Dr. Fidan Kaptan, a veterinarian at County Animal Hospital in Mason, Ohio, explained that while eating one or two cicadas is not a cause for concern, eating larger quantities can irritate the stomach lining: the crunchy shell is difficult to break down in the canine digestive tract, and its accumulation can cause inflammation of the gastric mucosa, producing vomiting, diarrhea, and lethargy. Dr. Christine Klippen, of Friendship Hospital in Washington, D.C., cautioned against leaving dogs unattended during cicada emergences specifically to prevent unrestricted consumption. Dr. Jerry Klein, the chief veterinary officer of the American Kennel Club (AKC), summarized the professional consensus: "In most cases, your dog will be fine after eating a few cicadas," but dogs that "gorge on the large, crunchy insects will find the exoskeleton difficult to digest and can suffer serious consequences."

The AKC additionally noted the risk of choking: cicadas are large insects (Brood X individuals of the species *Magicicada septendecim*, *M. cassinii*, and *M. septendecula* range from approximately 1.5 to 3 centimeters in body length), and a dog that attempts to swallow multiple cicadas rapidly may choke on the hard exoskeleton pieces, particularly if the insects are swallowed whole rather than chewed.

There are specific circumstances that complicate the general reassurance. Dogs with food allergies, particularly to shellfish (since shellfish shells, like insect exoskeletons, are composed primarily of chitin), may experience allergic reactions to cicada consumption. Dogs with sensitive stomachs, gastrointestinal conditions, or inflammatory bowel disease may be more susceptible to GI distress than healthy animals. Dogs that have been recently treated with systemic pesticides should not consume insects that may carry pesticide residue. And dogs that consume enormous quantities — dozens or hundreds of cicadas — may experience more serious mechanical gastrointestinal obstruction rather than mere irritation, requiring veterinary attention.

The "poisonous" framing of the original claim is therefore clearly false: there is no cicada toxin that harms dogs. The modifier "mostly false" chosen by Snopes captures the appropriate rating, acknowledging that the blanket "dangerous" claim is false while implicitly recognizing that unrestricted consumption can produce real (if self-limiting) health effects.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes fact-check and veterinary advisories do their job competently: they debunk a false toxicity claim and provide accurate practical guidance. But the fact-check misses several layers of biological interest that make the cicada-dog interaction genuinely instructive as a case study.

**The biology of insect toxicology and chitin digestion**: The question "are cicadas poisonous?" is answerable quickly. The more interesting question for biology communication is: *why* are cicadas not poisonous, and what would it mean for an insect to be poisonous to dogs? Most insects are not toxic to dogs, but some are — the situation is not trivial. Blister beetles (*Epicauta* species and relatives), for example, contain cantharidin, a potent vesicant compound that causes severe mucosal damage to the gastrointestinal tract and can be lethal to horses and serious to dogs and cats if consumed in sufficient quantities (Helman & Edwards, 1997, "Clinical Features of Blister Beetle Poisoning in Equids: 70 Cases," *Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association*, 211(8), 1018–1021). The question of insect toxicity is genuinely one that veterinary medicine takes seriously.

Cicadas are not in this category because they are nutritionally edible insects with no known defensive toxin chemistry. They feed on plant xylem fluid (more on this below), which is nutritionally sparse and does not accumulate toxins. They defend themselves through noise, camouflage, and predator satiation rather than through chemical deterrence. This is a meaningful ecological distinction from beetles that use chemical defense.

**Chitin digestion in carnivores**: The specificity of the gastrointestinal risk deserves deeper consideration. The difficulty dogs have digesting cicada exoskeletons is real and relates to the biochemistry of chitin, the primary structural component of insect cuticle. Chitin is a linear polymer of N-acetylglucosamine, the same compound that forms the shells of crustaceans. Most mammals, including dogs, produce low levels of chitinase enzymes (chitinase 1 and acidic mammalian chitinase, encoded by the *CHIA* and *CHIT1* genes), but these enzymes are insufficient to efficiently break down large quantities of structural chitin (Zhu et al., 2004, "Molecular Cloning and Characterization of a Novel Chitinase-Like Protein Gene from Mouse," *Journal of Biochemistry and Molecular Biology*, 37(5), 494–501). In contrast, many insectivorous mammals and birds have much higher chitinolytic enzyme activity, reflecting dietary adaptation to insect consumption.

The practical implication is that a dog eating a moderate quantity of cicadas faces the same physiological challenge as a person eating an equivalent quantity of unprocessed shrimp shells: the protein and lipid content is nutritionally accessible, but the structural chitin framework resists digestion and can act as an irritant if the total load is high. Cooking partially denatures the chitin's crystal structure and makes it more digestible, which is why processed insect protein (used in some commercial insect-based dog foods) typically involves thermal treatment. A dog eating raw cicadas gets the full chitin load.

**Periodical cicada ecology and the Brood X emergence**: The context that makes this fact-check relevant — a major periodical cicada emergence — is itself fascinating and under-reported in fact-checking discourse. Brood X is one of fifteen currently recognized periodical cicada broods in North America, thirteen of which follow a 17-year cycle and two of which follow a 13-year cycle. These are among the most remarkable life history phenomena in the animal kingdom. The nymphs spend nearly their entire lives underground, feeding on xylem sap from tree roots, emerging only once after 17 (or 13) years for a brief adult phase lasting just a few weeks, during which they sing, mate, lay eggs, and die (Marlatt, 1907, "The Periodical Cicada," U.S. Department of Agriculture Bureau of Entomology Bulletin, 71, 1–181, first systematic taxonomic treatment).

The evolutionary logic of periodical emergence has been extensively studied. The predominant hypothesis, articulated by Yoshimura (1997, "The Evolutionary Origins of Periodical Cicadas During Ice Ages," *American Naturalist*, 149(1), 112–124) and others, is that prime-number cycle lengths (13 and 17 are both prime numbers) minimize the probability of synchronization with predator life cycles, making it difficult for any predator species to track and specialize on a given cicada brood. This hypothesis remains the most widely accepted but is not universally embraced, with alternative models invoking climate synchronization and environmental cue entrainment (Williams & Simon, 1995, "The Ecology, Behavior, and Evolution of Periodical Cicadas," *Annual Review of Entomology*, 40, 269–295).

**The predator satiation strategy — and its implications for dogs**: A key aspect of the periodical cicada's anti-predator strategy is *masting* — emerging in such overwhelming abundance that all local predators are satiated, ensuring that enough individuals survive to reproduce. The density of emerging cicadas during a Brood X emergence can reach 1.5 million per acre in prime habitat (Simon et al., 2000, "Genetic Evidence for Assortative Mating Between 13- and 17-Year Cicadas," *Ecology Letters*, 3(4), 254–261). This is precisely why dogs encounter cicadas in quantities sufficient to eat many of them: the insects are specifically adapted to be present in numbers that overwhelm any individual predator's ability to eat them all.

The implication for the toxicity question is nuanced. A predation strategy based on predator satiation does not require the prey to be unpalatable or toxic — it requires the prey to be present in numbers so large that every predator quickly achieves satiety, leaving the vast majority of cicadas unharmed. Cicadas therefore have no evolutionary pressure to develop chemical defenses against predators, because overwhelming abundance is their primary defense. This is a meaningful contrast with insects for which toxicity and aposematic coloration (warning colors) are primary defenses — those insects are typically scarce and depend on individual deterrence rather than population-level satiation.

**The gap in popular coverage**: Neither the Snopes fact-check nor the veterinary advisories explain why cicadas are not toxic (the evolutionary ecology of chemical defense), what specific aspect of cicada composition causes GI distress (chitin digestibility), or what the ecological context of the emergence means for the scale of potential exposure (predator satiation masting). These are the scientifically interesting dimensions of the question that are entirely absent from fact-check discourse.

The also-unmentioned issue is that periodical cicada mass emergence creates significant nutritional recycling events in forest ecosystems. Dead cicadas decompose rapidly, releasing large quantities of nitrogen-rich organic matter into forest soils and functioning as a significant nutrient pulse. Research by Sota et al. (2001) and particularly Gratton et al. (2008, "Linking Aquatic and Terrestrial Food Webs by Reciprocal Export of Aquatic Insects," *Ecology*, 89(1), 267–275, which examines analogous insect-mediated nutrient flows) documents how mass insect emergence events can alter food web dynamics. Dogs that eat large quantities of cicadas are, inadvertently, participating in an ancient ecological process of predation on an insect whose entire life-history strategy is designed to be consumed.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Mostly False (cicadas are not poisonous; overconsumption can cause GI distress)  
**Snopes Verdict:** Mostly False  
**Confidence:** High on non-toxicity; Moderate on specific GI risk (dose-dependent)  
**Epistemic Basis:** Veterinary toxicology, entomology, consumer insect digestion research  
**Phenomenological Status:** N/A  
**Mechanistic Status:** No known toxin; mechanical and fermentative GI risk from chitin load is documented  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Mixed (the general worry is unfounded; moderate caution about overconsumption is appropriate)

The claim that cicadas are "poisonous" to dogs is false. Cicadas contain no known toxins hazardous to dogs and have not evolved chemical defenses against predators. The Pet Poison Helpline, veterinary societies, and entomologists at major universities consistently confirm that occasional cicada consumption poses no toxicological risk to healthy dogs.

The claim that cicadas are entirely without health risk is also imprecise. Dogs that consume large quantities of cicadas risk GI distress, vomiting, and diarrhea due to the poor digestibility of chitin-based exoskeleton. Dogs with food allergies, sensitive stomachs, or underlying GI conditions face somewhat elevated risk. Dogs of small body weight face a proportionally higher risk from the same quantity consumed. Responsible pet ownership during cicada emergences justifiably includes preventing unrestricted cicada consumption.

The "mostly false" Snopes designation accurately captures the gradient: the core claim of toxicity is wrong, but the derivative claim that something health-relevant is occurring when dogs overeat cicadas is partly right.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The 2021 Brood X emergence was one of the most extensively documented in history, coinciding with the expansion of social media and citizen science platforms. Entomologists at the University of Connecticut and elsewhere developed dedicated reporting apps and observation networks, generating more precise geographic distribution data for the 2021 emergence than any previous Brood X event. This data revealed range contraction relative to historical records, particularly in areas where land use change, suburbanization, and soil compaction have eliminated the suitable deciduous forest habitat that cicada nymphs require.

**Cicada ecology and biodiversity**: The three species that compose Brood X — *Magicicada septendecim*, *M. cassinii*, and *M. septendecula* — are among seventeen currently described *Magicicada* species, all endemic to eastern North America. Their restricted range (no periodical cicadas occur anywhere else on Earth) makes them a conservation concern as well as a biological curiosity. Population declines in cicada broods associated with habitat loss are documented but quantitatively hard to assess given the extreme density variation across habitat types (Declines noted in natural history monitoring: Cooley et al., 2009, "Historical Changes in the Geographical Distribution of Brood X Periodical Cicadas," *Ecology*, 83(6), 1500–1509).

**Insect nutrition and the dog food industry**: The question of cicada edibility for dogs has an instructive parallel in the emerging insect-based pet food industry. Several commercial dog food products now incorporate insect protein — most commonly black soldier fly larvae (*Hermetia illucens*), mealworms (*Tenebrio molitor*), and house crickets (*Acheta domesticus*) — as a sustainable protein source with lower land and water use requirements than conventional meat (Bosch et al., 2016, "Dietary Nutrient Profiles of Wild Wolves: Insights for Optimal Dog Nutrition?" *British Journal of Nutrition*, 106(S1), S127–S139). These products are formulated with processed insect protein and chitin-reduced meal rather than whole insects, reflecting the digestibility considerations discussed in section 3.

Dogs consuming these commercial products do not experience the chitin-load GI distress that can accompany raw whole-cicada consumption, demonstrating that the issue is not insect protein per se but the mechanical and biochemical challenge of unprocessed chitin-rich exoskeleton. The cicada-dog interaction during a brood emergence is essentially an uncontrolled version of this — whole, unprocessed insect protein in variable and potentially large doses.

**Brood X and phenological research**: The 2021 emergence also served as an important data point in research on cicada emergence phenology — the timing of emergence in relation to soil temperatures and climate conditions. Periodical cicadas emerge when soil temperatures at a depth of approximately 18 inches reach 64°F (17.8°C), and research using the 2021 emergence alongside historical records has examined whether climate warming is advancing emergence timing (Kritsky, 2004, "Periodical Cicadas: The Plague and the Puzzle," *Indiana Academy of Science*; updated analyses are ongoing as part of the Cicada Mania and iNaturalist citizen science datasets). Early findings suggest modest advancement in emergence timing in regions experiencing the greatest warming, but the rigidity of the 17-year genetic timer means emergence date variation within a given locality is measured in days rather than weeks.

**The veterinary management context**: During large emergence events, veterinary practices in affected areas report a transient increase in GI-related presentations. The vast majority of these cases are self-limiting and resolve with dietary restriction and supportive care (bland diet, hydration). Severe cases requiring radiographic evaluation for obstruction or surgical intervention are rare. The American Veterinary Medical Association's (AVMA) communications during the 2021 emergence explicitly addressed cicada consumption and provided the same tiered guidance: no concern for minimal exposure, monitoring appropriate for moderate consumption, veterinary evaluation warranted for very large consumption or persistent severe symptoms.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addressed this claim in May 2021, precisely timed to the Brood X emergence, in an article authored by Dan Evon. The article correctly identifies the claim as "Mostly False," explains that cicadas are not poisonous, and provides veterinary voices confirming the real but non-toxic GI risk of overconsumption. The piece appropriately cites the Pet Poison Helpline, an extension entomologist, and three veterinarians from different practice settings, representing a solid evidentiary consensus.

The fact-check's limitations are those of the genre: it is necessarily brief, targeted at answering the specific binary question (poisonous: yes/no) rather than providing ecological or physiological depth. It does not explain why cicadas lack defensive toxins (evolutionary ecology), what the specific digestibility issue is (chitin biochemistry), or what the broader ecological significance of the Brood X emergence is. For a reader whose primary concern is their pet's safety, these omissions are inconsequential. For a reader interested in why the world works the way it does, they represent a significant under-utilization of the topic's scientific richness.

The broader media coverage of the 2021 Brood X emergence and dog safety was generally accurate, with veterinary sources consistently providing the non-toxic-but-limit-consumption message. No significant instances of misinformation suggesting lethal cicada toxicity gained major traction, suggesting that the Snopes fact-check and veterinary communications were effective in heading off more extreme claims before they achieved viral spread.

The "mostly false" rating did draw some criticism from readers who felt it underweighted the real (if modest) health risks of large-volume consumption, and from others who felt it assigned an alarmist valence to a question whose answer is fundamentally reassuring. These tensions are inherent to graded rating scales applied to gradient empirical realities — the rating accurately reflects the toxicity question but potentially over-foregrounds the risk for readers who interpret "mostly false" as "dangerous in some conditions" rather than "the specific claim of poisonousness is incorrect."

The fact-check thereby serves its primary purpose competently — preventing unnecessary panic about a non-toxic insect — while leaving substantial biological story untold, which is a characteristic feature of time-constrained, claim-focused fact-checking as a genre.
