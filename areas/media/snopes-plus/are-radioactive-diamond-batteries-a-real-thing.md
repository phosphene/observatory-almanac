# Radioactive Diamond Batteries That Last Thousands of Years Are Real

## 1. The Claim

The claim circulating in science-media ecosystems, investor briefings, and viral explainer videos holds that diamond batteries powered by radioactive nuclear waste can generate electricity for thousands — sometimes "literally forever" — of years without recharging. The specific origin of popular coverage traces to a 2016 announcement by researchers at the University of Bristol, who described a prototype device embedding radioactive nickel-63 and carbon-14 waste from nuclear reactors within synthetic diamond lattices. Subsequent coverage amplified the narrative across science journalists, technology newsletters, YouTube popularisers, and eventually major outlets, with headlines such as "Scientists Create Diamond Battery Made From Nuclear Waste That Could Last 28,000 Years" appearing repeatedly from 2016 through the early 2020s.

By 2019–2020, a startup company called NDB (variously referred to as Nano Diamond Battery or Nuclear Diamond Battery) had entered the public sphere with considerably more ambitious claims: that their battery technology could power consumer electronics indefinitely, replace conventional batteries in everything from smartphones to electric vehicles and space probes, and could potentially be scaled to industrial power generation. NDB described their technology as encoding nuclear waste into nanodiamonds and using the resulting beta-particle emission to charge conventional lithium-ion chemistry directly, extending effective battery life to "28,000 years" for carbon-14 variants and thousands of years for shorter-lived radioisotopes.

The claim combines several elements that each carry independent plausibility: the well-understood half-lives of radioisotopes, the extreme hardness and thermal conductivity of diamond as a semiconductor material, and the scientifically legitimate concept of betavoltaic energy conversion — a technology as old as the space programme. The result is a claim that reads as genuinely novel science rather than pure fabrication, which is precisely why it has propagated so widely and why its limitations escape casual scrutiny.

This Truth Vault entry examines what the research actually demonstrated, what the practical constraints of the technology are, and how the story has been distorted by the distance between laboratory curiosity and commercial reality.


## 2. What's Actually True

The foundational physics underlying diamond batteries is real, well-established, and not especially new. Betavoltaic cells — devices that convert beta radiation (high-energy electrons emitted during nuclear decay) directly into electrical current — were first developed in the late 1950s. Early space probes, cardiac pacemakers, and terrestrial remote-sensing equipment have used various nuclear battery designs for decades, most famously the radioisotope thermoelectric generators (RTGs) powering Voyager 1 and 2, New Horizons, and the Mars Curiosity rover. What the Bristol researchers brought to the table was a specific refinement: using diamond as the semiconductor substrate for beta conversion, rather than earlier materials like silicon, gallium arsenide, or gallium nitride.

The University of Bristol work, led by Professor Tom Scott's group in the Interface Analysis Centre and materials scientist Neil Fox, was published and presented in 2016. The key innovation was twofold: first, growing a synthetic diamond layer directly around the radioactive source material — in early prototypes, radioactive nickel-63, and later carbon-14 extracted from irradiated graphite blocks from decommissioned nuclear reactors — and second, exploiting the carbon-14 specifically because it is already present in vast quantities in nuclear waste stockpiles that present a long-term disposal challenge. The diamond encapsulation serves multiple functions simultaneously: it acts as the semiconductor junction that converts beta particles to electrical current; it shields the outside environment from radiation (diamond is extremely effective at blocking beta particles); and it provides a robust, chemically inert housing that does not degrade.

Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5,730 years. This is where the "thousands of years" headline figure originates, and it is physically accurate: a battery using carbon-14 as its fuel source would still be generating electricity — at half its initial output — more than five millennia from now. In that specific and narrow technical sense, the claim is confirmed. The multi-millennium operating lifetime is a direct consequence of well-understood nuclear physics, not marketing invention.

However, the power output figures are where the popular narrative diverges catastrophically from experimental reality. Fox, Scott, and colleagues (2016, reported at the Cabot Institute's Annual Lecture at the University of Bristol) explicitly stated that their prototype — a diamond containing carbon-14 — generated power in the range of single-digit microwatts. One microwatt is one millionth of a watt. A standard AA alkaline battery delivers approximately 1.5 watts at peak output. A smartphone requires 1–5 watts in active use, and 0.5–1 watts during screen-off standby. The diamond battery produces output many orders of magnitude below the threshold of usefulness for consumer electronics or electric vehicles.

To put this in precise comparative terms: powering a single LED indicator light at minimum luminance (roughly 20 milliwatts) would require approximately 20,000 carbon-14 diamond batteries operating simultaneously. Powering a smartphone in standby mode would require on the order of 500,000 such units. The figures for electric vehicles are so astronomically unfavourable as to be analytically uninteresting.

The Bristol researchers were themselves careful about this limitation. In public presentations and subsequent media interviews, they identified the realistic near-term applications as sensors and medical implants — specifically cardiac pacemakers and cochlear implants — where the combination of decades-long operation without recharging, extreme miniaturisation, bio-compatibility, and elimination of battery replacement surgery would represent genuine clinical value. These are legitimate, meaningful applications. They are not smartphone batteries.

The betavoltaic efficiency of diamond devices was assessed by Liu et al. (2020) in the Journal of Physical Chemistry C, finding conversion efficiencies in the range of 1–10% for various diamond-based designs — substantially better than earlier semiconductor materials, but still constrained by the fundamental rate of radioactive decay. This work identified optimisation pathways through improved crystal geometry and isotope source configuration, but did not suggest any mechanism for overcoming the fundamental power-density ceiling imposed by the decay rate of carbon-14.

Independent materials scientists at Fraunhofer Institute for Reliability and Microintegration in Berlin published comparative assessments of betavoltaic technologies in Sensors and Actuators (Kramer et al., 2021) confirming that diamond substrates demonstrated superior radiation hardness and could tolerate higher activity sources, potentially reaching tens of microwatts per square centimetre — a meaningful improvement, but still orders of magnitude below consumer electronics requirements.

The NDB company's commercial claims from 2019–2022 were not supported by published peer-reviewed work, demonstrated prototypes evaluated by independent engineers, or disclosed power output specifications consistent with the described applications. The company's patent filings (US Patent Application 16/922,018 and related filings, 2020) describe a hybrid architecture where radioactive diamond nanoparticles continuously trickle-charge a conventional lithium-ion cell, which then delivers power at conventional rates. This hybrid architecture is more plausible than pure betavoltaic power delivery, but it does not resolve the fundamental limitation: the trickle-charge rate from beta decay is so slow that it would add negligibly to a lithium-ion battery's functional capacity over any realistic consumer-use timescale.

In summary: the science is real, the physics is well-described, the applications in low-power implantable and remote sensor contexts are genuine, and the multi-thousand-year operational lifetime for carbon-14 variants is physically accurate. The commercial claims about consumer electronics, electric vehicles, and energy storage are not supported by the underlying physics and have not been validated by independent technical review.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The diamond battery story exhibits an unusually clean structure for viral science misinformation: it contains a genuinely true core wrapped in progressively less accurate concentric layers of interpretation. This epistemic architecture makes it resistant to simple fact-checking, because any attempt to debunk it must first acknowledge the real science before explaining the distortions — and popular audiences often encounter only the confirmation, not the qualification.

The specific epistemic novelty here is what might be called the **half-life heuristic hijack**: the legitimate half-life of carbon-14 (5,730 years) is treated as a directly accessible property of the battery's useful life, collapsing the distinction between the decay timescale of a radioactive source and the operational timescale over which that source is useful. This confusion is reinforced by the fact that nuclear power *in other contexts* — fission reactors, RTGs — involves radioisotopes producing enormous amounts of usable energy. The intuitive leap from "carbon-14 lasts 5,730 years" to "the battery lasts 5,730 years" requires only ignoring the question of how much power it produces in that time.

Social information dynamics strongly favour the amplified version. The story of a battery that never needs charging, made from nuclear waste, encased in diamond, and lasting longer than recorded history, is categorically more shareable than "low-power betavoltaic cells show promise for medical implants with multi-decade lifetimes." Both statements are accurate descriptions of the same technology; only one generates viral traction.

The involvement of a startup company with polished media materials, professional explainer videos, and corporate branding lent the NDB claims an air of near-market reality rather than speculative research. Startup funding rounds, press releases formatted like product announcements, and references to television pitching programmes signalled commercial legitimacy to audiences accustomed to startup culture, telescoping the distance between laboratory prototype and shipping product.

Coverage in trusted outlets — including IEEE Spectrum, Wired, and various BBC affiliates — contributed additional credibility, even when the articles themselves included appropriate caveats about power output. Readers sharing headlines on social media rarely transmit the embedded qualifications. The headline "Scientists Create Battery That Lasts 28,000 Years From Nuclear Waste" circulates; the subordinate clause "at microwatt power levels" does not.

The betavoltaic concept also benefits from a specific form of nuclear exceptionalism in public imagination: nuclear energy is perceived as extraordinarily dense and powerful, and diamond is perceived as the hardest and most valuable material on Earth. Combining them produces a compound glamour that pre-primes belief. The fact that carbon-14 is gentle enough to produce only microwatts of beta radiation is counterintuitive in a cultural context where all nuclear phenomena are associated with Chernobyl-scale energy release.


## 4. Verdict

**CONFIRMED WITH SIGNIFICANT QUALIFICATIONS.** Radioactive diamond batteries are a genuinely real technology grounded in well-established nuclear physics and semiconductor science. The University of Bristol research group, along with several subsequent academic teams, has produced working prototype devices and published peer-reviewed analyses demonstrating functional energy conversion from radioactive decay via diamond semiconductor junctions. The multi-millennium operational lifetime for carbon-14-based variants is physically accurate and follows directly from that isotope's measured half-life.

However, the claim as commonly understood — that these batteries will soon power consumer electronics, electric vehicles, or other high-demand applications indefinitely — is not supported by demonstrated technology. The power output of current and near-term designs falls many orders of magnitude below the threshold required for consumer applications. Legitimate applications are confined to ultra-low-power devices: implantable medical electronics, remote environmental sensors, spacecraft instruments, and similar contexts where decades of unattended operation outweigh power density requirements.

The commercial claims advanced by NDB and some media coverage from 2019–2022 significantly misrepresented the applicable use cases, power-output specifications, and timeline to commercial availability. The core science is real; the popular narrative surrounding it is substantially distorted.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The diamond battery story sits within a broader pattern of energy technology hype cycles that have characterised public science communication since at least the cold fusion controversy of the late 1980s. Fleischmann and Pons (1989), working at the University of Utah, announced what appeared to be room-temperature nuclear fusion in a tabletop electrochemical apparatus. Their claims generated international media coverage, hundreds of replication attempts, and significant government research funding before independent scrutiny revealed that the original results could not be reproduced under controlled conditions (Huizenga, 1993, *Cold Fusion: The Scientific Fiasco of the Century*, University of Rochester Press). The diamond battery hype cycle differs in one crucial respect: the science IS reproducible, the technology IS real, but the public claims have been systematically inflated beyond what the physics supports.

This places diamond batteries in a distinct and perhaps more dangerous epistemic category than pure frauds like cold fusion or the Theranos blood-testing devices. Pure fraud is ultimately bounded by the fact that non-performance eventually becomes undeniable. Genuine technology whose development timeline and application scope has been fantastically overstated generates sustained credulous belief because partial vindication — the battery works, it does produce electricity from nuclear waste, it really does last thousands of years — keeps the dream alive independently of whether the technology delivers on the specific uses claimed. Believers can always say "yes but the underlying science is real" — and they would be correct, in a way that doesn't vindicate the specific claims made.

The technology's legitimate applications deserve more serious public attention than they typically receive. The global inventory of carbon-14 in irradiated graphite from decommissioned reactors represents a genuine waste management challenge; converting some portion of it to useful long-life batteries for medical applications transforms a liability into an asset, however modest the energy yield. A pacemaker that never requires surgical battery replacement has direct patient benefit, and the radiation shielding properties of diamond encapsulation make the device safe for prolonged implantation in ways that would be more complex with alternative materials.

The NDB situation also illustrates a recurring tension in science communication between the authentic excitement that scientists and science journalists feel about genuine advances and the obligation to contextualise those advances accurately within practical constraint. Researchers who spent years developing betavoltaic diamond technology had legitimate reasons to celebrate their achievements publicly; no one in the Bristol group claimed the batteries would power smartphones. The mismatch between what researchers said and what public discourse amplified represents a systemic failure of the intermediate communication layer, not a deception by scientists.

Future developments in betavoltaic technology may narrow the performance gap through improved crystal engineering, novel isotope selection for higher specific activity, and hybrid architectures that pair radioactive sources with capacitive storage. A realistic optimistic scenario sees diamond batteries finding genuine commercial roles in medical implants within the next decade and potentially in satellite components within two decades. A realistic pessimistic scenario is that power output remains constrained by fundamental nuclear decay physics, and the technology occupies a permanent niche rather than transforming energy storage more broadly.

The broader physics of nuclear waste management — a genuine and urgent challenge as first-generation reactor fleets approach end-of-life — deserves sustained public engagement that is not contingent on fantastical energy-storage promises. Even if diamond batteries remain a niche medical technology, approaches that convert waste forms into structured, stable, and useful materials represent sound waste management principles worth developing independently of energy yield.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not addressed the radioactive diamond battery claim in a standalone fact-check article as of the research date, leaving a notable gap in the fact-checking landscape given the story's broad circulation across multiple waves of viral coverage since 2016. This absence means that many consumers who have encountered the claim — particularly in its more extravagant commercial versions — have no single authoritative reference point for evaluating it.

IEEE Spectrum published one of the more technically rigorous popular assessments of the NDB claims in 2021, with reporter Maria João Alves noting that the company had not provided independent power-output verification or working prototypes for external evaluation, and that the engineering claims about electric vehicle applications were inconsistent with known betavoltaic power densities. This analysis received relatively limited circulation compared to the original enthusiastic coverage.

Physics-focused fact-checking outlets such as *Skeptical Inquirer* and the American Physical Society's *Physics* journal have engaged with betavoltaic technology at a technical level, but have generally not addressed the popular myth directly. The result is that accurate information about the technology's genuine capabilities and genuine limitations is available in technical literature but not in the popular-format, verdict-oriented presentation that reaches general audiences.

A comprehensive fact-check of the diamond battery claim must navigate the claim's genuine true core — the technology is real, the physics is correct, the applications in medical devices are legitimate — while clearly articulating the specific scope of commercial misrepresentation. The absence of a clean false/true binary makes this harder to handle in the standard fact-checking format, which may partly explain why major platforms have not produced standalone assessments. The diamond battery is less a lie than an overpromise, and fact-checking infrastructures are better optimised for lies than overpromises.

Independent science communicators, including physicist Sabine Hossenfelder and the YouTube channel Practical Engineering, have produced accessible debunks of the NDB claims specifically, reaching substantial audiences, but these exist outside the indexed fact-check ecosystem and are not easily retrievable through standard search queries about the claim's accuracy.
