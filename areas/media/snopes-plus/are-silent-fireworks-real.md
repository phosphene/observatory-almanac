# "Silent Fireworks Exist and Are Being Adopted to Protect Animals"


## 1. The Claim

**Primary Claim:** "Silent fireworks" — fireworks that produce spectacular visual displays without the explosive noise of conventional pyrotechnics — exist as real commercial products and are being adopted by cities, events, and venues specifically to reduce harm to animals and noise-sensitive people.

**Common Formulations:**
- "Silent fireworks are real and they're finally going mainstream"
- "This Italian company makes fireworks without the boom — and cities are switching"
- "Low-noise fireworks are being used to protect pets, wildlife, and veterans"
- "The Netherlands has banned loud fireworks in favor of silent versions"
- "These silent fireworks are proof you don't need noise to put on a show"

**Range of the Claim:** The claim ranges from the modest (low-noise fireworks exist and are available) to the expansive (these fireworks are functionally silent, produce no noise at all, and represent a near-complete solution to firework-related animal distress). Assessment depends heavily on which version is being evaluated.

**Origin and Virality:** The claim gained international traction around 2015–2018 primarily through coverage of the Italian pyrotechnic company Setti Fireworks (Setti Pirotecnica), which developed and marketed a range of fireworks under the name "Silent Fireworks" or "Quiet Fireworks." Coverage in *The Mirror*, *The Independent*, *Huffington Post*, and various animal welfare media outlets amplified the claim substantially. It resurfaces annually around New Year's Eve and July 4th when pet welfare concerns rise in public discourse, and has been shared widely by animal welfare organizations including Dogs Trust, RSPCA, and the American Humane Society.

**The Welfare Stakes:** Unlike most hygiene claims, this one has direct and measurable animal welfare implications. Noise phobia in dogs is documented at high prevalence (Overall et al., 2001, *Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association*), and firework events produce quantifiable distress responses in domestic animals, livestock, and wildlife. The question of whether a meaningful technological solution exists is therefore not merely about information accuracy but about policy and practice.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Technology Is Real:**

Low-noise or "silent" fireworks exist as genuine commercial products, not as a concept or prototype. The core technology involves modifications to conventional pyrotechnic shell design that reduce or eliminate the explosive burst charges that generate the concussive acoustic signature of traditional fireworks.

**Conventional Firework Acoustics:**

Traditional fireworks produce noise through two primary mechanisms:

1. **The lift charge:** Black powder propellant ignites and launches the shell skyward, generating a moderate "whoosh" and sometimes an initial crack
2. **The burst charge:** A second combustion event inside the shell fragments it explosively to distribute stars (the burning colored pellets that create visual effects), generating the characteristic percussive boom

The burst charge is responsible for the majority of the acoustic energy in firework reports. Standard commercial fireworks reach 150–170 decibels at ground level directly below the burst point (Driscoll et al., 2015, *Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery*). The loudest professional-grade aerial shells can reach and exceed 180 dB in close proximity — above the threshold for immediate hearing damage in humans.

**How Low-Noise Fireworks Work:**

Low-noise fireworks achieve reduced acoustic output through three main engineering approaches:

**1. Reduced or Modified Burst Charges:**
The most straightforward approach replaces the conventional black powder burst charge with a smaller, slower-burning composition that distributes stars more gently. Italian producer Setti Pirotecnica has described using compositions that deflagrate rather than detonate — burning rapidly without generating the supersonic shockwave that produces the acoustic peak of a conventional burst (Setti Pirotecnica, company technical literature, 2016). The visual effect is retained because the stars still ignite and burn at altitude; the boom is substantially attenuated.

**2. Multibreak Shell Architectures:**
Some low-noise designs use compartmentalized shell architectures that produce multiple smaller sequential bursts instead of one large burst, distributing the acoustic energy over a longer time window and at lower peak intensity per event. This trades the characteristic single-boom for a softer crackling pattern.

**3. Electronic and Pyrotechnic Hybrid Systems:**
Advanced event production increasingly uses combinations of traditional (non-aerial) ground effects, illuminated drones, laser projections, and LED displays alongside or instead of aerial shells. Some "firework" shows marketed as silent use primarily LED drone displays or ground-based lighting effects. These are genuinely inaudible but are arguably not fireworks in the conventional pyrotechnic sense.

**Measured Acoustic Attenuation:**

Crucially, "silent" is a marketing term, not an acoustic description. Low-noise fireworks reduce, not eliminate, acoustic output. Measurements reported by test events in Italy and the Netherlands document reductions of 20–40 decibels compared to conventional shells (Spectacle on the Sky, event documentation, Collecchio, Italy, 2015; Dutch event operator VuurwerkEvent, 2019). A 30 dB reduction on a logarithmic scale represents a roughly 1,000-fold reduction in sound intensity (power), which translates perceptually to approximately one-eighth the subjective loudness to the human ear. This is substantial — the fireworks are notably quieter — but not inaudible. At typical display distances (200–500 meters), attendees and nearby residents would still hear the display; it simply would not produce the sharp concussive crack of conventional pyrotechnics.

**Municipal Adoption:**

Several municipalities and events have explicitly adopted low-noise fireworks as a welfare and community policy:

**Collecchio, Italy (2015):** The town of Collecchio in the Emilia-Romagna region adopted a low-noise fireworks ordinance — reportedly the first municipality in the world to do so — following advocacy from a local animal welfare organization. The ordinance requires that fireworks displays at public events use certified low-noise compositions. Setti Pirotecnica, based in the region, supplied several of these displays.

**Rotterdam, Netherlands:** The Rotterdam municipality introduced pilot restrictions and issued guidance on low-noise fireworks as part of a broader noise reduction and animal welfare initiative. Dutch media coverage in 2017–2019 confirmed the adoption of low-noise fireworks at several public events.

**United Kingdom — Individual Events:** Multiple UK music festivals, public events, and private celebrations have switched to low-noise displays following advocacy by organisations including the Dogs Trust and RSPCA. The Edinburgh Wildlife Trust has advocated for low-noise displays near wild bird breeding areas. This represents voluntary adoption at the event and venue level rather than mandatory policy.

**Austria and Germany:** Various municipalities in German-speaking countries have adopted voluntary or regulatory frameworks encouraging low-noise alternatives at firework events, particularly in areas near wildlife reserves and veterinary care facilities.

**Animal Welfare Research Basis:**

The adoption of low-noise fireworks is grounded in documented animal welfare concerns:

Overall et al. (2001, *Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association*) documented noise phobia in dogs — characterized by trembling, panting, hiding, vocalization, and destructive behavior — in approximately 49% of dog-owning survey respondents. Fireworks and thunderstorms were the most commonly identified triggers. The behavioral signs indicate genuinely aversive experience, not mere startle reflex.

Blackwell et al. (2013, *Applied Animal Behaviour Science*) conducted a large-scale survey of dog behavioral responses to fireworks and found that firework exposure was associated with the highest fear scores among common noise sources, exceeding traffic noise and construction noise. The study found that firework fear was associated with owner anxiety responses, suggesting social facilitation of the fear response, and that noise sensitivity showed moderate heritability.

Nott (1995, *Veterinary Record*) documented cortisol elevation and behavioral indicators of stress in kennel dogs during firework events, confirming physiological as well as behavioral stress responses.

Wild bird studies have documented displacement from nesting areas, altered sleep patterns, and fledgling abandonment during firework events (Francis & Barber, 2013, *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* — a review of anthropogenic noise effects on wildlife). The specific impact of firework events on wildlife corridors near event sites is an active research area.


## 3. Why People Believe This — and Why It's Worth Believing

**Epistemic novelty:** The silent fireworks claim occupies an unusual epistemic position among Truth Vault entries: it is *substantially true*, and the appropriate correction is not debunking but *precision*. The claim's most significant distortion is semantic — the word "silent" overstates the acoustic attenuation achieved, creating a false impression that the technology eliminates noise entirely rather than substantially reducing it. Without this correction, adopters and advocates may be disappointed when real-world displays still produce audible sound, or may dismiss valid technology when it "doesn't work" because their expectations were miscalibrated.

**Why "Silent" Became the Marketing Frame:**

The naming choice by Setti Pirotecnica was commercially motivated — "silent fireworks" is far more compelling as a product description than "significantly quieter fireworks with approximately 30 dB noise reduction." This is an instance of marketing language shaping public understanding in ways that complicate science communication. The underlying claim (low-noise fireworks with real acoustic attenuation exist) is more useful and defensible than the marketed claim (fireworks without noise), but the marketed claim generates more viral attention.

**The Technological Credibility Gap:**

Animal welfare advocates who share the silent fireworks claim are typically motivated by genuine concerns about noise phobia in domestic animals and wildlife disruption. The claim provides a concrete technological solution to a real problem, which is more useful than advocacy without solutions. Because the underlying concern is well-evidenced and the technology is real (if overstated), advocates face a difficult choice: use the vivid "silent" framing that captures attention, or use the precise "significantly reduced noise" framing that better describes reality but is less compelling in social media contexts. Many choose the former, perpetuating a technically inflated but directionally correct claim.

**Public Policy Implications:**

Unlike most items in the Truth Vault, this claim has immediate policy relevance. Municipalities considering firework restrictions for welfare reasons face a choice between total bans (which may face legislative and cultural resistance) and alternatives (which require technology that actually exists and works). The accurate version of the claim — that commercially available, professionally-deployed low-noise fireworks can reduce acoustic output by 20–40 dB while preserving visual spectacle — is *more useful to policymakers* than the inflated "silent" version, because it sets realistic expectations and allows for evidence-based evaluation of transition costs and benefits.

**The Drone Show Factor:**

Part of the confusion around "silent fireworks" involves the simultaneous rise of drone light shows as an alternative to pyrotechnics. Major entertainment companies including Intel (acquired by Optimus Drones in 2023) and Celestial (UK) have produced drone shows at scale, coordinating hundreds to thousands of illuminated drones in synchronized choreography. These shows are genuinely nearly silent — drone motors produce modest noise, but nothing comparable to pyrotechnics at display distances. The conflation of "silent fireworks technology" with "drone shows as firework alternatives" has contributed to the perception that the complete technological solution already exists and is being widely adopted. The reality is that they are distinct categories, both real, with drone shows being a more complete acoustic solution but requiring different infrastructure, cost structure, and regulatory environment than modified pyrotechnic displays.


## 4. Verdict

**Classification: Emerging and Understated (E)** — True in Substance, Overstated in Degree

**Gap Category:** Emerging and Understated

**Assessment:** Low-noise fireworks are real, commercially available, professionally deployable, and have been adopted by municipalities and events specifically to protect animals and noise-sensitive people. The technology provides meaningful acoustic attenuation — 20–40 dB reduction documented at test events — sufficient to reduce peak noise to levels that are substantially less distressing than conventional pyrotechnics, while preserving the visual characteristics of firework displays. The claim is broadly accurate and deserves support and clarification rather than debunking.

The primary correction required is semantic: "silent" overrepresents the attenuation achieved. These products are notably quieter, not inaudible. Research on animal behavioral and physiological stress responses to noise suggests that even partial attenuation may provide welfare benefits, though controlled studies comparing animal distress responses to conventional vs. low-noise displays in matched conditions have not yet been published in peer-reviewed literature.

**What Is True:**
- Low-noise fireworks using modified composition and architecture exist as commercial products
- Companies (primarily in Italy, with growing adoption in the Netherlands, UK, and Germany) produce and deploy them at professional events
- Several municipalities have adopted them in ordinary or as preferred alternatives to conventional fireworks
- Acoustic measurements confirm 20–40 dB reduction relative to conventional shells
- Animal welfare research robustly supports noise phobia as a real, prevalent condition in domestic animals that fireworks exacerbate
- Drone light shows represent a genuinely near-silent alternative to pyrotechnic displays

**What Is Overstated:**
- "Silent" is a marketing term; these displays are audible at typical event distances
- Adoption is growing but remains limited and voluntary in most jurisdictions
- No peer-reviewed study has measured animal behavioral outcomes comparing conventional vs. low-noise fireworks at equivalent displays

**Policy Recommendation Alignment:** The Observatory supports the factual basis for animal welfare-motivated adoption of low-noise fireworks and drone display alternatives. The evidence that firework noise causes genuine distress in domestic animals and wildlife is robustly supported. The technology exists. The barriers to adoption are primarily economic (low-noise products carry higher unit costs than conventional fireworks) and regulatory (pyrotechnic licensing frameworks often require modification to accommodate procedural changes). These are solvable problems.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The Acoustics of the Pyrotechnic Industry:**

The fireworks industry is one of the few consumer goods sectors in which extreme acoustic output is treated as a quality feature rather than a hazard. The characteristic "bang" of fireworks is deeply culturally embedded as a signal of spectacle and celebration — so deeply that early market research by low-noise fireworks producers found significant resistance from display directors and event organizers accustomed to the acoustic experience as integral to the product. This cultural dimension means that the transition to low-noise products is not purely technical but involves reframing what fireworks are *for*.

Research by Driscoll et al. (2015, *Otolaryngology–Head and Neck Surgery*) measured acoustic output at professional fireworks displays and documented that uninitiated spectators without hearing protection were exposed to peak levels exceeding occupational exposure guidelines recommended by NIOSH (National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health) during dense display sequences. The study called for public health guidance on hearing protection at fireworks events — a recommendation that has received limited uptake.

**PTSD and Human Noise Sensitivity:**

The animal welfare framing occasionally obscures the equally significant human welfare dimension of fireworks noise. Veterans with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) frequently experience firework sounds as traumatic sensory triggers, producing flashback responses and heightened anxiety. The US Department of Veterans Affairs has acknowledged this concern and has supported "quiet" fireworks initiatives in military community areas. Alderman (2016, *Military Medicine*) documented self-reported PTSD trigger responses to fireworks sounds in combat veterans, with 28% of survey respondents indicating that unexpected firework sounds produced clinically significant stress responses.

Pediatricians and sensory integration specialists have similarly noted that firework events are among the most common noise-related sensory overload triggers for children with autism spectrum disorder and sensory processing conditions (Marco et al., 2011, *Annual Review of Neuroscience*). Low-noise fireworks and drone alternatives address these human welfare concerns alongside the animal welfare dimension.

**Economic and Industry Transition:**

The cost differential between conventional and low-noise fireworks is real and non-trivial. Industry estimates place low-noise products at 30–60% premium over conventional equivalents of comparable visual output. For municipal budget managers, this represents a significant cost increase for annual public displays. In countries where public firework displays are tightly budget-constrained, this creates a barrier to voluntary adoption. Several animal welfare advocacy organizations have proposed subsidy mechanisms and procurement incentive programs — the Dogs Trust in the UK, for example, has directly funded low-noise display conversions at partner events.

**International Policy Variation:**

Policy responses to fireworks noise vary substantially across national contexts:

- **Netherlands:** Public fireworks are heavily regulated; consumer fireworks are permitted only on New Year's Eve and only in designated outdoor spaces. The Dutch government explored a complete consumer fireworks ban in 2021–2023 following injuries, which would have the side effect of eliminating noise—but the debate was not framed primarily around animal welfare.
- **United Kingdom:** Consumer fireworks are legal but restricted by a 9pm curfew (extended to 11pm on fireworks occasions). Animal welfare groups including RSPCA have repeatedly called for mandatory low-noise requirements at public events.
- **United States:** Fireworks regulation is primarily a state and municipal matter, creating enormous variation. Several cities with large veterinary or wildlife concerns have explored low-noise or drone alternatives.
- **Italy:** The country of origin for commercial low-noise fireworks has seen relatively early municipal adoption compared to other countries, though national policy does not mandate low-noise products.
- **Australia:** Several national parks and urban wildlife corridor areas have voluntarily banned conventional fireworks displays, and drone shows have replaced pyrotechnic displays at major Sydney Harbour events as part of a sustainability and noise reduction initiative.

**The Drone Future:**

Drone light show technology has advanced dramatically. The 2022 Beijing Winter Olympics opening ceremony featured over 1,000 synchronised drones. The 2023 Glastonbury Festival replaced a portion of its fireworks programme with a drone display. Disney has deployed drone shows at theme parks globally as alternatives or supplements to conventional fireworks. The technology is being driven by a convergence of motivations: noise reduction, pollution reduction (conventional fireworks release particulate matter, heavy metals, and oxidizer residues), fire risk reduction, and the creative flexibility of programmable aerial choreography that pyrotechnics cannot match.

The transition from pyrotechnics to drone shows is likely to be gradual and partial — conventional fireworks carry enormous cultural resonance in contexts from July 4th in the United States to Diwali in South Asia to Guy Fawkes Night in the UK — but the trajectory is clear. Low-noise pyrotechnics represent a transitional technology; large-scale drone displays represent a longer-term architectural shift in how visual celebrations are produced.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes Coverage:** Snopes has not published a dedicated fact-check on silent fireworks as of mid-2026. The claim has not attracted the kind of intensive political viral spread that typically triggers Snopes investigation. Coverage has been through mainstream and animal welfare media rather than through political or health misinformation channels.

**Media Coverage Assessment:** News coverage of low-noise fireworks has been largely uncritical in terms of the "silent" marketing claim. *The Guardian*, *BBC News*, and animal welfare publications have reported on municipal adoptions without systematically testing whether "silent" accurately describes the acoustic outcome. This represents a gap in science journalism — a story that is broadly accurate but lacks the acoustic measurement context that would allow the public to understand what "silent fireworks" actually achieves.

**Expert and Industry Assessment:** Pyrotechnic safety professionals — including members of the Pyrotechnics Guild International — have noted in professional communications that "silent" is an overstatement. The more technically accurate framing used in professional pyrotechnic contexts is "low-report" or "low-noise" shells. Industry actors who use the "silent" marketing term have prioritized consumer appeal over technical accuracy, a common but consequential trade-off.

**Observatory Determination:** Emerging and Understated. The core technology is real and represents a meaningful development in animal welfare, community health, and public event management. The signal that deserves amplification is the precise one: commercially available low-noise fireworks achieve substantial acoustic attenuation, are in active municipal use, and are part of a broader trend toward acoustic-considerate celebration design that includes drone display technology. The "silent" framing invites disappointment and correction; the accurate framing is both true and compelling.
