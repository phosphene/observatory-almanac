## §1 — Claim and Viral Context

In December 2021, a video began spreading across social media platforms carrying a claim that would strike many viewers as simultaneously horrifying and plausible: that it showed "human sleeves," artificial replacement bodies capable of hosting a transferred human consciousness. The claim traveled through multiple online channels, with the conspiratorial website "Before Its News" presenting the footage as a "whistleblowers'" video documenting the existence of clones in Hollywood.

A related version of the video had already achieved viral penetration on TikTok in October 2021, where it accumulated significant engagement before the December wave added further amplification. The video purported to show two artificial humanoid bodies on display, attended by presenters in white uniforms who described the technology in clinical, matter-of-fact terms, speaking as if the capability to "plant chips into a person's head and download every experience and memory they've had" was imminent commercial reality.

The claim embedded in this circulation is formally a "miscaptioned" image or video — a category in which the footage itself is genuine and undoctored, but the description attached to it is materially false. The video is real; the event shown is real; the bodies on display are real physical objects. What is false is the identification of those objects as actual human cloning or consciousness-transfer technology, and the claim that the video documents an extant biotechnological capability.

The emotional architecture of this viral claim is worth examining. It draws simultaneously on two powerful and contradictory informational strategies: the conspiracy theory (secret technologies being hidden from the public) and the science-fiction horizon narrative (the future is arriving faster than we know). These two framings reinforce each other in this context: the conspiratorial framing licenses disbelief in official denials, while the science-fictional framing provides a ready-made conceptual vocabulary for understanding what is supposedly being shown. Viewers familiar with Netflix's "Altered Carbon" would, ironically, be better positioned to recognize the video for what it is — but viewers outside that cultural reference frame had no anchoring context that would allow them to identify the footage as a marketing exercise.

## §2 — Source Verification and Evidentiary Record

The footage originates from the 2018 Consumer Electronics Show (CES) in Las Vegas, held January 9–12, 2018. CES is the world's largest annual technology trade show, attracting hundreds of thousands of attendees and exhibitors ranging from established electronics giants to startup companies in all stages of development. The scale and density of the show — occupying approximately 2.9 million square feet of exhibit space — means that unusual booths can establish themselves in the middle of an authentic technology context without immediately appearing out of place.

Netflix arranged for the staging of a booth presenting a fictional company called "PsychaSec," drawn from the narrative of "Altered Carbon," the science-fiction series that was set to premiere on Netflix on February 2, 2018 — approximately three weeks after CES. The booth featured two artificial humanoid bodies — highly realistic props used in the television production — displayed in transparent housing units, attended by presenters trained to stay in character and present PsychaSec as a real biotech company.

The Wrap, a trade publication covering the entertainment industry, published a contemporaneous report on the booth (Burch, 2018) documenting the confusion the installation caused. The Wrap's reporter described the scene: "Hosts draped in all-white were showing off two artificial bodies that, 'in a few years,' humans could pay to trade their own crappy body for. At the same time, the host touted the company's ability to plant chips into a person's head and download every experience and memory they've had." The reporter further documented attempting to get a presenter to break character and acknowledge the Netflix connection, but finding that the hosts maintained the fiction with considerable discipline — requiring a persistent, pointed inquiry before a presenter would even acknowledge the stunt with a wink.

Engadget (2018) also covered the event at the time, describing it as Netflix's "Trojan horse" PR move: "establishing a fictional vendor booth deep within the Las Vegas Convention Center, worked a little too well." The Engadget piece noted that the journalist had been pre-briefed via press release, which gave them context that ordinary CES attendees — and later, social media viewers of the footage — did not have.

The Adweek trade publication (2018) added the marketing industry perspective, headlining the story as "The Most Disturbing Booth at CES Is Actually an Ad for Netflix's New Sci-Fi Show." This coverage establishes beyond doubt that the event was well-documented at the time of occurrence as a marketing stunt, with multiple credentialed trade press witnesses confirming the Netflix connection.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Performative Deception and the Consent Architecture of Marketing

The human sleeves case offers a striking example of what might be termed *performative deception* in the service of entertainment marketing — an organized, deliberate effort to create a false impression for a finite period in order to generate press coverage and consumer interest. This category of deceptive practice occupies an unusual ethical and epistemological space that fact-checking frameworks rarely explicitly address.

The PsychaSec booth was not created with the intent of permanently deceiving anyone about the state of human cloning technology. Netflix issued press releases simultaneously marketing the stunt; trade press were briefed in advance; the performance had a defined horizon (the series premiering in February 2018). The deception was, in the corporate marketing sense, "opt-in" for informed participants and "temporarily convincing" by design. The intent was to generate earned media coverage through novelty and shock — and it succeeded beyond any reasonable expectation, generating coverage in 2018 and then again in 2021–2022 when the de-contextualized footage went viral.

The epistemic novelty here is that this type of bounded, temporary, consent-framed marketing deception can, with temporal displacement, mutate into a much more harmful form of misinformation. The footage was not originally intended to deceive anyone about human biology; it was intended to entertain and intrigue attendees about a fictional TV show. Three to four years later, stripped of its context by the internet's indifference to temporal provenance, it served as "evidence" for conspiracy theories about human cloning and celebrity body doubles.

Research on the provenance problem in visual misinformation (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017, *Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policymaking*) identifies this pattern as one of the most challenging aspects of the contemporary information environment: footage that carries its own deceptive framing from its original production context can be repurposed into harmful misinformation with no additional fabrication required. The original producer (Netflix) cannot be held responsible for the 2021–2022 viral misuse; the footage carries no intrinsic marker that would enable a naive viewer three years later to identify it as a marketing prop.

The fiction of "Altered Carbon" itself adds another layer of epistemic complexity. The show's premise — that human consciousness can be digitized and transferred into artificial bodies ("sleeves") — is a well-developed science-fiction scenario with extensive literary and philosophical analysis behind it. The idea is not physically impossible in the way perpetual motion is impossible; rather, it sits in the domain of the "not currently achievable but hypothetically conceivable" — which is the space where the most durable conspiracy theories operate. Claims about technologies that do not yet exist but are conceivably possible are structurally harder to debunk than claims about impossible things, because the correct answer to "could we eventually have this?" is genuinely "we don't know."

## §4 — The "Altered Carbon" Premise: Distinguishing Fiction from Technology Capability Claims

"Altered Carbon" is a novel by Richard K. Morgan, first published in 2002, which Netflix adapted for television. The novel's central conceit is that in a far future, human consciousness can be stored in a "cortical stack" implanted at the base of the skull, and that these stacks can be transferred between biological or artificial bodies (sleeves). Death becomes technologically reversible for those with the resources to access compatible bodies; the wealthy can purchase high-quality "metsleeves" (designer bodies) while the poor are allocated whatever biological housing is available.

The technological premise is a sophisticated extrapolation of current trends in neuroscience, computer science, and materials science — none of which, as of 2024, have produced anything approaching the capabilities described. The cortical stack concept requires a complete functional map of the human connectome at synaptic resolution — a project that neuroscientists have described as achievable in principle over extremely long timescales but far beyond current capability. The Human Connectome Project (Van Essen et al., 2013, *NeuroImage*) mapped structural connectivity in the human brain at macroscale resolution; synaptic-level mapping of the full human brain remains beyond current technical reach by several orders of magnitude.

The props used by Netflix at CES 2018 were designed for cinematic realism, not functional capability. They are the product of skilled special effects and prop manufacturing, not bioengineering. The material qualities that made them visually convincing — smooth skin-textured surfaces, naturalistic proportions, inert display positioning — are all features of physical production artistry, not evidence of any biological or computational capability.

For audiences primed by conspiracy theories to see hidden truths in leaked footage, the visual realism of well-made cinematic props presents exactly the kind of "evidence" that confirmation bias processes: the things look sufficiently real that dismissing them feels intellectually dishonest. This is the implicit epistemology of the conspiracy-theorist interpretation — that visual verisimilitude is evidence of grounding in hidden reality.

## §5 — CES as a Misinformation Propagation Environment

The Consumer Electronics Show represents a unique epistemological environment: it is a space explicitly designed to present the future as imminent, to showcase technologies that range from fully deployable products to speculative prototypes. Attendee cynicism about the developmental stage of exhibitor claims is well-documented but variable; the show culture rewards ambitious forward-looking framing, which means that the distance between "this is a product you can buy today" and "this is a concept we are working toward" is frequently elided in exhibit hall presentations.

Netflix's exploitation of this ambiguity was clever precisely because it was legible within the existing norms of CES exhibiting. Attendees were already conditioned to encounter booths presenting things that did not yet exist as if they were imminent or real; requiring them to distinguish between a legitimate (if premature) startup and a fictional company required a level of meta-cognitive decoding that the show environment actively discourages.

Research on trade show communication (Gopalakrishna & Lilien, 2019, *Journal of Marketing Research*) documents that the primary function of trade show exhibits is impression formation, with factual information transfer as a secondary goal. Exhibits are, in economic terms, trust-signaling devices: the investment of resources in exhibit design, staffing, and presentation creates an implicit credibility warrant that audiences extend to exhibitors. Netflix's PsychaSec booth leveraged this credibility infrastructure to create a viral marketing event — and created, years later, a viral misinformation event as a side effect.

This case illustrates a tension in the contemporary information economy: marketing practices that are creative, effective, and temporally bounded can produce lasting epistemic damage when archived footage is decontextualized by years of platform circulation. There is no institutional mechanism that causes a piece of staged marketing footage to carry a permanent "this was a marketing stunt" label when it migrates across platforms and years.

## §6 — Summary Assessment and Research Gaps

**Verdict confirmation:** Miscaptioned. The video shows real footage from a real event (CES 2018), but the identification of the objects shown as actual "human sleeves" used for cloning is false. The footage documents a marketing display for Netflix's "Altered Carbon," using cinematic props. No human cloning or consciousness-transfer technology exists, and the footage provides no evidence of any such capability.

**EARC rating — C (Confirmable):** The documentary record establishing the marketing origin of the footage is extensive and credentialed: The Wrap, Engadget, and Adweek all covered the event contemporaneously. The Netflix connection is fully established. No scientific expertise is required to verify the verdict; the factual dispute is entirely about the origin and context of the footage, not about theoretical scientific capability.

**Epistemic gap — Low:** The footage's provenance is completely documented. The only genuine uncertainty is the degree to which the conspiracy framing has persisted despite debunking — a factor that is empirically documented as a persistent challenge for corrections (Lewandowsky et al., 2012) but does not affect the factual verdict.

**Research gaps:** The long-term persistence of this specific claim in conspiracy-theory ecosystems is not systematically documented. The extent to which the 2021–2022 viral wave was seeded deliberately by specific actors versus arising organically from platform recommendation algorithms is unknown. The relationship between the "Altered Carbon" intellectual property and the production of misleading footage — whether Netflix bears any residual communicative responsibility for downstream misuse — is an unresolved question in the communications ethics literature. Documentation of similar marketing-stunt-to-misinformation pathways at other major technology exhibitions would constitute a valuable research contribution.
