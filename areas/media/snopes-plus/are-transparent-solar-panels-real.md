## §1 — Claim and Viral Context

On April 7, 2022, a Facebook post shared by the page "Engineering Bebz" presented a meme with the following text: "A professor at Michigan State University has created the first transparent solar panel, that have the potential to power entire buildings and can be retrofitted to older glass buildings or even windows." The post circulated widely in the weeks that followed, eventually attracting the attention of Snopes fact-checkers who located it through their standard monitoring of high-engagement claims in the science and technology space.

The claim is notable because it occupies an unusual category in fact-checking: it is primarily and straightforwardly true, yet it requires significant elaboration to be understood accurately. The transparent solar panel technology is real, it was developed at Michigan State University, and it does have the stated potential applications. However, the meme's framing — with its implication of a single breakthrough moment, its compression of a multi-year research trajectory into a single accomplished fact, and its elision of the technology's current development stage — required contextualization to be useful to readers.

The viral spread of this claim is not surprising given the content's appeal across multiple motivational axes. Climate-concerned audiences find transparent solar panels attractive as a solution to the land-use tensions that have accompanied large-scale solar deployment. Architecture and design enthusiasts engage with the aesthetic dimension — windows that generate power while remaining visually transparent. Technology optimists share such claims as evidence of human ingenuity addressing civilizational challenges. The claim hits multiple engagement triggers simultaneously, which partly explains why it circulated widely in 2022 even though the underlying research had begun in 2014 and had been publicly reported since at least 2017.

The time gap between the original research (2014–2017) and the 2022 social media surge illustrates a well-documented pattern in science communication: foundational research results often achieve their widest public awareness not when first published, but when they are rediscovered and repackaged in visually engaging formats years or decades later. This temporal decoupling between scientific publication and popular awareness has significant implications for public understanding of science's timeline of progress.

## §2 — Source Verification and Evidentiary Record

The foundation of the fact-check is the peer-reviewed literature from Michigan State University's research group. The original publication documenting the transparent luminescent solar concentrator appeared in *Advanced Optical Materials* in 2014: Zhao, Y., Meek, G.A., Levine, B.G., & Lunt, R.R. (2014). Near-infrared harvesting transparent luminescent solar concentrators. *Advanced Optical Materials*, 2(7), 606–611. https://doi.org/10.1002/adom.201400103

This publication describes the use of small organic molecules selected for their selective absorption characteristics: these molecules capture wavelengths of sunlight that fall in the ultraviolet and near-infrared portions of the spectrum — regions invisible to the human eye — while transmitting the visible-light portion of the spectrum without significant absorption. The captured invisible-wavelength energy is re-emitted as infrared light, which is guided by total internal reflection within the transparent pane to its edges, where strips of conventional photovoltaic cells convert it to electricity.

The mechanism is elegant and physically well-grounded. Luminescent solar concentrators (LSCs) are not a new concept: they were first proposed and demonstrated in the 1970s (Goetzberger & Greubel, 1977, *Applied Physics*), but early implementations used colored dyes that absorbed visible light, making them visually opaque or strongly tinted. The innovation at MSU was the selection of organic chromophores that absorb only outside the visible spectrum, enabling the first truly transparent (or nearly transparent) high-area LSC configuration.

In October 2017, Michigan State University issued a news release (MSU Today, 2017) with the headline "Transparent Solar Technology Represents 'Wave of the Future,'" in which Professor Lunt stated: "Highly transparent solar cells represent the wave of the future for new solar applications. We analyzed their potential and show that by harvesting only invisible light, these devices can provide a similar electricity-generation potential as rooftop solar while providing additional functionality to enhance the efficiency of buildings, automobiles and mobile electronics."

The 2021 development further advanced the claim's credibility: MSU Infrastructure Planning and Facilities installed the first transparent solar panels on a campus building, providing a real-world deployment at operational scale rather than a laboratory prototype. This installation was documented by MSU Today (2021) and represents a meaningful transition from proof-of-concept to active building integration.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Differential Solar Harvesting and the Visible-Light Constraint

The transparent solar panel represents an elegant resolution to a fundamental physical trade-off that makes it epistemically novel beyond its engineering achievement. The trade-off is this: conventional solar cells achieve high efficiency by absorbing as broad a spectrum of sunlight as possible, including the visible portion. Visible-light absorption, however, by definition makes the material opaque. Any device that harvests light is, in the most direct physical sense, impeding that light's transmission — you cannot both use a photon and allow it to pass through.

The resolution offered by luminescent solar concentrators using near-infrared-selective chromophores is not the elimination of this trade-off but its exploitation of a loophole: the solar spectrum contains substantial energy outside the visible range that arrives at Earth's surface largely unimpeded. Approximately 44% of solar irradiance falls in the infrared range (wavelengths above 700 nm), and another approximately 7% in the ultraviolet (below 400 nm). Human visual perception occupies the remaining roughly 49% between those bounds. The MSU approach targets the infrared portion of this invisible complement.

The epistemic novelty is that this approach inverts the conventional framing of the photovoltaics efficiency problem. Traditional efficiency research asks: how much of the solar spectrum can we harvest? The transparent solar approach asks: how much can we harvest while preserving visual transparency? These are genuinely different optimization landscapes, with different efficiency ceilings and different practical constraints.

Research on building-integrated photovoltaics (BIPV) has historically struggled with the conflicting requirements of structural load-bearing capacity, thermal performance, aesthetics, and power generation (Liu et al., 2021, *Renewable and Sustainable Energy Reviews*). Transparent PV addresses the aesthetics constraint more directly than any prior BIPV approach, potentially opening architectural markets — particularly high-rise commercial facades — that have been largely inaccessible to conventional solar installations because of visual and planning constraints.

The efficiency ceiling for selective-spectrum harvesting, however, is a significant limitation that accurate communication of this technology must acknowledge. As of the MSU research group's 2017 analysis, the theoretical efficiency ceiling for a fully transparent device harvesting only non-visible wavelengths was calculated at approximately 21%, with near-term achievable efficiency in the range of 5–10%. For comparison, conventional opaque silicon photovoltaic cells routinely achieve 20–25% efficiency commercially. The transparency premium thus comes at an energy cost, which means transparent panels are unlikely to replace conventional rooftop arrays for pure power generation; their value lies in enabling solar generation on surfaces that could not otherwise support it.

## §4 — Building-Integrated Photovoltaics: Policy and Technical Context

The potential application domains for transparent solar technology are substantial. Commercial buildings in major urban centers are predominantly glassed structures: modern office towers, retail developments, and institutional buildings often have exterior facades that are 50–80% glass by surface area. If even a fraction of this glazing could be replaced with power-generating transparent panels, the aggregate energy contribution would be significant.

Urban solar resource assessments (Hofierka & Kaňuk, 2009, *Renewable Energy*) have consistently documented that vertical facades in dense urban environments receive less direct irradiance than rooftops but still represent substantial integrated annual energy. In cities like New York, Chicago, or London, where rooftop area is small relative to facade area in high-rise districts, facade-integrated generation may actually offer greater aggregate potential than rooftop deployment.

Additionally, the retrofitting dimension of the claim — that existing windows could be replaced or overlaid — addresses a crucial economic barrier that new-construction-only technologies face. The U.S. commercial building stock is predominantly older construction: the U.S. Energy Information Administration's Commercial Buildings Energy Consumption Survey (EIA, 2018) documented that approximately 60% of commercial floor space is in buildings constructed before 1990. Technologies that can be deployed through retrofitting, without requiring demolition or structural modification, have access to this enormous existing stock in ways that built-in technologies do not.

Michigan State University's 2021 installation provided real-world performance data that contributed to the evidence base for the technology's viability. However, as of the Snopes fact-check's writing (April 2022), the technology remained "largely in the prototype phase." The transition from campus demonstration to commercial deployment requires manufacturing scale-up, cost reduction, longevity testing under real-world weathering conditions, and integration with building management systems — all of which represent genuine engineering and economic challenges that the viral meme's framing omitted.

## §5 — Solar Technology Misinformation: Patterns and Amplification

The transparent solar panels fact-check represents a distinct category of science misinformation: the genuine breakthrough understated by its timeline. Unlike fabricated claims, the content here is factually accurate in its core assertion. The misinformation, if it can be called that, consists of presenting a research trajectory as a completed deployment-ready technology when in fact it remains a demonstrably real but not yet commercially available innovation.

This pattern is documented in science communication research. Scheufele & Krause (2019, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) note that science communication failures often arise not from outright fabrication but from what they call the "translation gap" — the systematic difference between scientists' hedged, conditional language and the unhedged, declarative language that generates public engagement. "We have shown that X is achievable in principle and have demonstrated a prototype" translates in popular compression to "X exists and works."

The temporal decay of scientific news also plays a role. The 2022 viral spread of a meme based fundamentally on 2014–2017 research is not unusual. Studies of science news cycles (Baram-Tsabari & Lewenstein, 2013, *International Journal of Science Education*) document that scientific findings often achieve greatest popular attention not at initial publication but at subsequent moments when discovery is repackaged — through a conference presentation, a popular science video, a university press release, or in this case a social media meme.

The Snopes verdict of "True" is appropriate: the technology is real, the research was conducted at MSU, the potential applications described are genuine if technically contingent. The fact-check's value lies in adding the temporal and developmental context that the meme's format precluded.

## §6 — Summary Assessment and Research Gaps

**Verdict confirmation:** True. Michigan State University researchers led by Professor Richard Lunt developed and demonstrated a transparent luminescent solar concentrator beginning in 2014, with peer-reviewed publication in *Advanced Optical Materials* (Zhao et al., 2014), multiple university announcements (2014, 2017, 2021), and a real-world installation on an MSU campus building in 2021. The technology operates by absorbing near-infrared and UV wavelengths invisible to the human eye and converting that energy to electricity at the panel edges. The core claims of the viral meme — that such panels exist, were developed at MSU, and have the potential to power buildings — are factually accurate.

**EARC rating — A (Academic Published):** The foundational claims are supported by peer-reviewed literature in *Advanced Optical Materials*, university press communications, and Reuters news reporting. The primary publication (Zhao et al., 2014) is accessible through a standard academic DOI and has been cited extensively.

**Epistemic gap — Moderate:** The gap lies between "the technology exists as a demonstrated prototype" and "the technology is commercially available and deployment-ready." The meme implies a completion status that the research trajectory does not yet meet as of 2022. Efficiency limitations (~5–10% achievable versus 20–25% for conventional silicon PV) also represent a material qualification omitted from the viral claim. Longitudinal performance data from real-world installations remains limited.

**Research gaps:** As of 2026, the commercial deployment status of MSU's transparent solar technology remains unclear from public sources. Whether any commercial building projects have incorporated the technology at scale beyond the university demonstration installation is not documented in accessible public records. The competitive landscape has evolved: multiple research groups globally are pursuing transparent and semi-transparent photovoltaic approaches using different chemical and physical mechanisms, and the relative advantages of the MSU approach versus competing technologies are not fully established. Cost-per-watt figures for this technology at manufacturing scale are not publicly available in peer-reviewed literature.
