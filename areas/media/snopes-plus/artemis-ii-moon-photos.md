# Artemis II astronauts took the first new photos of Earth from the Moon since Apollo


## 1. The Claim

When NASA's Artemis II mission launched on April 1, 2026 — the first crewed flight beyond low Earth orbit in more than half a century — one of the most widely circulated claims in media coverage and public discussion was that its astronauts would capture, and ultimately did capture, the first new photographs of Earth from the Moon since the Apollo programme ended in December 1972. The claim appeared across news sites, social media, and official NASA communications in various formulations: "the first human photos of Earth from the Moon in 54 years," "the first Earthrise photographs since Apollo," and, more simply, "the first new photos of Earth from the Moon since Apollo."

The emotional resonance of the claim is considerable. The Earthrise photograph taken by astronaut William Anders during Apollo 8 on 24 December 1968 is one of the most reproduced and influential images in the history of photography — described by nature photographer Galen Rowell as "the most influential environmental photograph ever taken," and widely credited with catalysing the modern environmental movement (NASA, 1968). The Blue Marble image taken by the Apollo 17 crew in December 1972, showing a fully illuminated Earth against the blackness of space, is similarly iconic. The suggestion that, for the first time in living memory, human eyes and human cameras were again positioned in the lunar vicinity to take such photographs carries genuine historical weight.

The specific images most widely shared from the Artemis II mission were those NASA termed "Earthset" photographs — images of Earth sinking below the lunar horizon, captured by Commander Reid Wiseman and Mission Specialist Christina Koch during the spacecraft's closest approach to the Moon on April 6, 2026. Koch's image, taken at 22:41 UTC using a Nikon D5 camera, showed a crescent Earth with cloud systems visible over the Australia and Oceania regions, slipping behind the cratered lunar surface as the Orion spacecraft — named "Integrity" by the crew — passed through the far side of the Moon and briefly lost contact with Earth-based stations. The image evoked immediate comparisons to the Apollo 8 Earthrise, and was presented by many outlets as a direct historical successor.

The claim, then, is both factually well-grounded and slightly imprecise. Its truth depends on a word that frequently goes unexamined: *human*.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Artemis II mission is real and the photographs are genuine.** Following the successful uncrewed Artemis I test flight in November 2022, Artemis II launched April 1, 2026, with a crew of four: Commander Reid Wiseman (NASA), Pilot Victor Glover (NASA), Mission Specialist Christina Koch (NASA), and Mission Specialist Jeremy Hansen (Canadian Space Agency). They travelled aboard the Orion spacecraft on a free-return trajectory around the Moon, reaching a maximum distance of approximately 252,756 miles (406,850 kilometres) from Earth — farther than any human had travelled since Apollo 17's crew in 1972 (NASA, 2026). The mission lasted approximately ten days and returned to Earth on April 10, 2026.

The photographs taken during the mission are genuine NASA-documented imagery. The Earthset sequence captured by Koch and Wiseman as Orion curved around the far side of the Moon on April 6 represents a first in the modern era of human spaceflight: no astronaut had been positioned in the lunar vicinity since Apollo 17's final departure on December 14, 1972, and none had been in a position to photograph Earth from that vantage point (NASA, 2026). In that specific and important sense, the claim is accurate.

**The "since Apollo" framing requires one significant qualification.** The claim is most naturally read as asserting that no photographs of Earth from the Moon have existed since Apollo. This is not correct. Since 2009, NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (LRO) has orbited the Moon on a continuous science mission, and its imaging systems — primarily the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera (LROC) — have periodically captured Earth from lunar orbit (Robinson et al., 2010, *Space Science Reviews*). Notable Earth images from LRO include a composite image taken on February 1, 2014, showing Earth rising above the Rozhdestvenskiy crater on the lunar far side, and a striking image from October 12, 2015, showing Earth with the Sahara Desert and South America prominently visible above the lunar horizon. Both were released by NASA and received substantial public attention. They are unambiguously photographs of Earth from the Moon; they are simply photographs taken by robotic optics rather than human observers (Petro et al., 2016, *Icarus*).

Beyond LRO, the China National Space Administration's Chang'e robotic lunar programme has produced multiple images of Earth from the lunar surface and lunar orbit, including photographs taken from the Chang'e 5 mission in 2020. The Japanese Aerospace Exploration Agency's (JAXA) Kaguya lunar orbiter captured high-definition video of Earthrise over the Moon in 2007 and 2008, more than a decade before Artemis II (Haruyama et al., 2012, *Earth, Planets and Space*).

**What is uniquely true about Artemis II** is that it produced the first photographs of Earth from near the Moon taken by human astronauts — with human hands, human eyes, and human presence — since the Apollo programme. That is a genuinely significant distinction. For most of human history and certainly for the entire span of the space age, human observation has carried a different cultural and emotional weight than robotic observation. The astronauts aboard Orion did not merely capture data; they looked at Earth with their own eyes from 252,000 miles away, and they reported their experience verbally. Koch described seeing the curvature of the Moon, the Earth as a crescent slipping behind it, and the transition into a communications blackout in terms that LRO's telemetry cannot approximate. The human presence is real and it matters.

**The technical and aesthetic distinction.** The Artemis II Earthset images also differ from LRO photography in compositional and aesthetic terms. LRO images are taken during orbital manoeuvres, are frequently composite products stitched from multiple narrow-angle camera frames, and are captured during calibration or special pointing operations rather than as the primary mission objective (Robinson et al., 2010, *Space Science Reviews*). The Artemis II photographs, by contrast, were deliberately composed by astronauts exercising the same craft practices as any working photographer — framing, timing, exposure selection — with Earth visible through the windows of a crewed spacecraft in a relationship to the lunar surface that human observers experience directly. The images in that sense carry a specific kind of authenticity that is distinct from robotic imagery, even when the robotic image is technically superior.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The human-focused frame of space coverage.** Popular space journalism, and NASA's own communications strategy, centres human experience and human firsts above most other framings. When Artemis II launched, coverage was saturated with "first crewed flight beyond low Earth orbit since Apollo 17" and similar formulations. In this context the further step to "first human photos of Earth from the Moon since Apollo" was made in virtually every article, and the further refinement — "first human photos, though robotic photos have existed since 2009" — is precisely the kind of nuance that get stripped out under editorial pressure for concision. The claim circulated in its simplified form because the simplified form is more powerful and, in the most important dimension, accurate.

**Visual memory and the cultural dominance of Apollo imagery.** The Apollo Earthrise and Blue Marble photographs are among the most reproduced images in the history of media. They occupy a category of singular cultural memory that LRO images — however technically impressive — do not. When most audiences encountered the Artemis II Earthset photographs and responded to them as the first new human view of Earth from the Moon in fifty-four years, they were responding to a genuine restoration of something that had been absent: the experience of human beings looking back at Earth from that particular distance. The emotional claim embedded in "first photos since Apollo" is largely correct even where the literal claim requires qualification.

**The mechanism: human-proxy extension of personal significance.** A specific psychological mechanism — what we might call *human-proxy extension* — amplifies the resonance of this claim. Audiences experience images taken by astronauts as personally implicated in a way they do not experience satellite or rover images. When Koch photographed Earthset, audiences could imagine themselves looking out through her eyes; the photograph becomes a proxy for a human experience of cosmic overview. LRO's photographs are remarkable documents, but they do not activate this mechanism. The claim "first since Apollo" is, in part, a sincere expression of this phenomenological difference — one that is legitimately meaningful even though technically imprecise.

**Confirmation bias via visual drama.** The Artemis II Earthset images are visually striking: Earth as a crescent sinking behind the lunar horizon, the rugged grey-brown surface of the far side in the foreground, the blackness of space above. They look like the kind of photograph that should be a historical first. They look Apollo-era in their drama and their vastness. An image that looks like a first feels like a first, and that affective response creates resistance to the information that robotic spacecraft had captured analogous views in the intervening decades, since that information diminishes the images' felt significance.


## 4. Verdict

**Mostly true, with an important qualification.** The Artemis II astronauts did produce the first photographs of Earth taken from the lunar vicinity by human beings since the final Apollo mission in December 1972. That is a genuine historical milestone and the photographs — particularly the Earthset sequence captured by Christina Koch on April 6, 2026 — are authentic, significant, and visually remarkable documents of humanity's return to the space beyond Earth orbit.

The qualification is that the claim as typically stated — "first photos of Earth from the Moon since Apollo" — implicitly erases the record of robotic spacecraft photography from the lunar vicinity, most significantly NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter (Robinson et al., 2010, *Space Science Reviews*), which has captured Earth from lunar orbit repeatedly since 2009. The claim is true if "photos" is read as "human-captured photos"; it is false if read as "photos of any kind."

The more precise and fully accurate version of the claim is: "The first photographs of Earth taken by human astronauts from the vicinity of the Moon since the Apollo programme." That version is warranted; the abbreviated popular version contains a real omission.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Artemis II Earthset photographs are part of a long and scientifically important tradition of imaging Earth from space. The Apollo Earthrise photograph has been studied extensively by earth scientists for what it revealed about terrestrial cloud dynamics, atmospheric scattering, and the visual proportions of continents and oceans as seen from cislunar space (Poole, 2008, *Earthrise: How Man First Saw the Earth*, Yale University Press). LRO's periodic Earth imaging has served calibration purposes but has also produced scientific data about the lunar environment and provided comparative datasets for understanding Earth's reflectance as seen from other bodies (Petro et al., 2016, *Icarus*).

The question of what is uniquely valuable in human observation versus robotic observation is a genuine scientific and cultural question that the Artemis programme raises with particular acuity. Supporters of human spaceflight argue that astronaut observers provide real-time adaptability, contextual judgement, and a human presence that generates knowledge robotics cannot replicate. Sceptics of the costs of human spaceflight note that robotic missions have produced comparable or superior imagery and scientific data at a fraction of the cost and risk. Both positions are defensible, and the popular claim about Artemis II's photographs — "first since Apollo" — is in one dimension a simplification that participates in this broader argument, attributing uniqueness to human observation that, in strictly photographic terms, was not absolute.

The history of Earth-from-Moon photography is also longer than popularly remembered. Beyond Apollo and LRO, the era of commercial and international lunar missions has produced multiple Earth views: JAXA's Kaguya orbiter (2007–2009), China's Chang'e series (2007–present), India's Chandrayaan-1 (2008–2009), and the dual robotic Artemis I mission itself in 2022, which produced automated digital photography of Earth during its lunar flyby. The Artemis II images are therefore the most recent in a continuous if intermittent series of Earth portraits from the lunar vicinity, not the first in a long gap.

What makes April 2026 distinctive — and what the popularly circulating claim accurately captures in its emotional register, if not in its precise wording — is the return of a human body to 252,000 miles from Earth and the reconstituted possibility of a human being choosing to lift a camera and take a photograph of home.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Artemis II Earth photography claim has not been formally addressed by Snopes, PolitiFact, or comparable mainstream fact-checking organisations, almost certainly because it is substantially true and does not attach to any false or misleading viral content in the typical sense. The claim circulated primarily as an accurate and celebratory report on a genuine achievement, and the nuance requiring correction — the existence of LRO's prior Earth photography — does not rise to the level of urgency that drives formal fact-check assignments.

Where the claim has required refinement, the correction has come primarily from science journalists and space-beat correspondents who either independently knew the LRO record or who received clarifications from space scientists in the days following the Earthset image release. The qualification standard in this case is the distinction between human-crewed observation and robotic observation — a distinction that is meaningful in cultural and phenomenological terms even when it does not divide "photograph" from "not photograph" in strictly technical terms.

For fact-checkers and media literacy educators, the Artemis II photo claim offers a useful case study in the dynamics of claim-simplification around genuine achievements. Most cases of misinformation involve false claims; this case involves a true claim that is slightly overstated through a standard journalistic compression that strips out an important qualifying term. The appropriate response is not "this claim is false" but rather "this claim is mostly true and here is what it omits." That calibration — between outright falsity and incomplete accuracy — is among the most demanding and most important skills in the fact-checking toolkit.

The claim also illustrates how robotic scientific infrastructure tends to be culturally invisible in ways that human spaceflight is not. LRO has been orbiting the Moon continuously since 2009, performing detailed lunar surface mapping, searching for water ice, and occasionally photographing Earth. Its scientific accomplishments are substantial. Yet in the popular imagination, "photographs of Earth from the Moon" remains a category defined by human presence, and the robotic contribution to that visual record is not embedded in cultural memory in any comparable way. Addressing that asymmetry — celebrating the robotic record as well as the human record — would produce a more complete and accurate picture of the history of Earth photography from cislunar space.
