## §1 · Claim & Verdict

In December 2015, a cluster of sensationalized news headlines declared that an asteroid would come "dangerously close" to Earth on Christmas Eve and that the event posed potential catastrophic consequences, including the induction of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions through gravitational influence. Specific claims circulated that Asteroid 2003 SD220, described as "colossal" in some reports, might either strike Earth directly or cause major seismic events due to its gravitational pull during close approach. One representative headline cited by Snopes read: "Asteroid that 'Could Cause Disaster' Headed Toward Earth on Christmas Eve." The body text of such articles described the asteroid as "so huge that some claim its gravitational pull could cause earthquakes and make volcanoes erupt."

Snopes rated this claim **False**. The actual closest approach of Asteroid 2003 SD220 on December 24, 2015 was approximately 6.8 million miles from Earth — roughly 28 times the Earth–Moon distance. NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) confirmed there was no hazard; the asteroid would not be visible to the naked eye; and the physical mechanism invoked (gravitational induction of earthquakes and volcanic eruptions) is not scientifically supported by the object's actual size, composition, or flyby distance. The "disaster" framing was manufactured by the headline writers; the underlying astronomical event was a routine near-Earth object (NEO) flyby of the kind that occurs many times per year.

## §2 · Context & Background

Asteroid 2003 SD220 was catalogued by astronomers in 2003 as part of the systematic survey efforts to identify near-Earth objects (NEOs) that might pose long-term impact risk to Earth. It is classified as an Aten-class asteroid — a group whose orbits cross Earth's orbit with semi-major axes slightly smaller than Earth's. The asteroid's radar characterization, refined in conjunction with the 2015 flyby, suggested a highly elongated shape approximately 1,100 meters (about 3,600 feet, or roughly 0.68 miles) in length. Some media reports described it as "1.5 miles wide," a figure Snopes noted was inaccurate; Space.com's reporting cited NASA's more accurate figure of approximately 1.24 miles (2 km), though the precise dimensions and shape have been iteratively refined by subsequent radar observations.

For context, NASA JPL's Centre for Near Earth Object Studies (CNEOS) tracks thousands of near-Earth objects and issues assessments of their impact probability, flyby distance, and physical characteristics. Paul Chodas, manager of NASA's Center for NEO Studies at the time, issued the explicit reassurance: "There is no cause for concern over the upcoming flyby of asteroid 2003 SD220 this Christmas Eve. The closest this object will come to Santa and his eight tiny reindeer is about 28 times the distance between Earth and the moon." JPL spokesperson Guy Webster confirmed to Snopes: "It's going to miss Earth by a wide swath, and not be any hazard."

The 2015 flyby was a predictable and anticipated event. It followed previous Earth flybys and was expected to be followed by additional close approaches — NASA noted that a closer approach was expected in 2018 at approximately 1.8 million miles (still safely distant), and subsequent approaches were projected through 2070, all well outside impact risk parameters. The 2015 flyby was specifically valuable to astronomers as it allowed the Arecibo Observatory's radar telescope to obtain detailed high-resolution images of the asteroid's shape and rotation, contributing to the planning for the 2018 observation campaign. The asteroid had a scientific value for NEO characterization precisely because it passes by Earth on a relatively regular schedule.

The disaster-prediction media environment in 2015 was not anomalous; asteroid and space-disaster sensationalism follows recognizable patterns in which real astronomical events are embedded in threat-inflating framing to generate page views. Marris (2013, *Nature*) documented patterns of media amplification of asteroid risk following the 2013 Chelyabinsk airburst, in which a 17–20 meter object entered Earth's atmosphere over Russia and produced a shockwave injuring approximately 1,500 people (Brown et al., 2013, *Nature*). The Chelyabinsk event was a real and unexpectedly powerful space weather incident that demonstrated genuine vulnerability to sub-threshold asteroid strikes; however, subsequent media coverage systematically used it to amplify alarm about entirely different object classes and flyby events, including 2003 SD220, that posed no comparable risk.

## §3 · Epistemic Analysis & Novelty

This case illustrates a distinctive variant of science misinformation that might be termed **scalar collapse in risk communication** — the journalistic compression of physically meaningful differences in distance, size, and proximity into emotionally equivalent "near miss" or "danger" framings that obliterate the quantitative distinctions that determine actual risk.

The epistemic novelty here involves examining why scalar information (28 times the Earth–Moon distance; gravitational force varies with the inverse square of distance) is so readily discarded in popular risk communication while qualitative framing ("close approach," "could cause disaster") persists. Research in risk cognition indicates that human risk perception is primarily driven by qualitative categories (will it hit or not?) rather than quantitative magnitude assessments (how far will it miss?) — a finding with foundations in the dual-process models of Kahneman (2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*) and the affect heuristic analyzed by Slovic et al. (2004, *Psychological Science*). When an object is framed as "potentially causing disaster," the affective response activated overrides quantitative probability assessments even for numerically sophisticated audiences.

The specific mechanism here is a rhetorical one, not merely a cognitive one: the articles that spread the alarm were not simply translating accurate information imperfectly — they were selectively omitting the information (6.8 million miles, 28 Earth–Moon distances) that would have immediately defused the alarm, while amplifying the qualitative framing ("could cause disaster"). This represents a departure from ordinary science communication failure (which might involve misunderstanding of probabilities or distances) into something closer to deliberate selective omission for rhetorical effect. The Snopes fact-check appropriately identifies this by providing the omitted quantitative context and showing that it entirely changes the reader's assessment of the event.

A second epistemic dimension involves the gravitational-earthquake claim, which has a more specific physics falseness worth examining. The claim that flyby of 2003 SD220 could "cause earthquakes and make volcanoes erupt" through gravitational pull represents a category error: tidal forces from a passing mass scale with the cube of the inverse distance and with the mass of the passing object. The Moon, at 3.84 × 10⁸ meters distance and mass 7.34 × 10²² kg, does produce measurable tidal effects on Earth, including contributing marginally to tidal triggering of shallow-fault seismicity (Métivier et al., 2009, *Earth and Planetary Science Letters*). Asteroid 2003 SD220, at 1.1 × 10¹⁰ meters distance and mass approximately 3 × 10¹² kg (estimated for an elongated ~1 km body at mean asteroid density), produces a tidal force on Earth's surface approximately 10¹⁵ times smaller than the Moon — a number so small as to be entirely negligible relative to any geological process (Paolicchi et al., 1997, *Asteroids, Comets, Meteors*). No peer-reviewed research has proposed a plausible mechanism by which sub-kilometer asteroid flybys at tens of millions of miles distance could trigger seismic or volcanic events.

The persistence of this false category of claim (asteroid flyby → earthquakes/volcanic eruptions) is interesting precisely because it fails by many orders of magnitude rather than failing marginally. One contributing factor is that the general intuition behind gravitational triggering is not entirely wrong — very close lunar approaches (perigee) do produce measurable if small contributions to tidal seismicity (Cochran et al., 2004, *Earth and Planetary Science Letters*), and this correct intuition about real phenomena is then incorrectly extended to objects 28 times more distant and orders of magnitude less massive.

## §4 · Scientific Evidence

The quantitative physics underlying the Snopes verdict is simple and noncontroversial. A systematic review of relevant data follows.

**Asteroid 2003 SD220 characteristics:** The object was first discovered in 2003 as part of the Spacewatch survey. Radar imaging during the December 2015 flyby from Arecibo Observatory and the Goldstone Solar System Radar established an elongated shape approximately 1,100 meters in length (Benner et al., 2015, IAU Circular). Subsequent 2018 flyby observations further refined the shape model, confirming a highly elongated body possibly resembling a contact binary. Its absolute magnitude H ≈ 16.7 corresponds to an estimated diameter in the range 1–2 km depending on assumed albedo. This places it well below impact consequence thresholds for continental-scale effects; 1 km objects represent an approximate boundary between regional and civilization-scale impacts (Chapman & Morrison, 1994, *Nature*), and the object's actual flyby distance of 6.8 million miles rendered any impact discussion moot regardless.

**Planetary defense and impact probability:** The Torino Scale — a standardized communication tool for assessing asteroid impact risk (Binzel, 2000, *Planetary and Space Science*) — assigns values from 0 (no risk) to 10 (certain global catastrophe). Asteroid 2003 SD220 was rated Torino Scale 0 for all projected future close approaches, including the 2015 Christmas Eve flyby, the 2018 approach, and a 2070 approach. The CNEOS impact risk table reflects a 0.0% probability of impact for all known close approaches.

**Tidal force calculation:** The tidal force F_t exerted by a mass M at distance r on a body of radius R is approximately F_t ≈ 2GMmR/r³, where m is the test mass and G is the gravitational constant. Using M ≈ 3 × 10¹² kg for 2003 SD220, r ≈ 1.1 × 10¹⁰ m for the 2015 flyby, and R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m for Earth's radius, the tidal acceleration at Earth's surface is approximately 4 × 10⁻²⁵ m/s² — twenty-five orders of magnitude below terrestrial surface gravity (9.8 m/s²) and physically incapable of influencing geologic processes. This calculation confirms that the earthquake/volcanic-eruption claim has no physical basis.

**Near-Earth Object monitoring:** NASA JPL's CNEOS surveys over 30,000 catalogued NEOs as of 2023, with observation arc lengths sufficient to project orbits decades into the future for potential impact assessment (Farnocchia et al., 2021, *Icarus*). The systematic survey coverage for objects larger than 1 km is estimated to exceed 95%, meaning nearly all objects of the size that could cause regional impact damage have been identified and their orbits are known (National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine, 2019, *Finding of the Report: Planetary Science and Astrobiology Decadal Survey*).

## §5 · Verdict Evaluation

Snopes' "False" verdict is well-supported. The factual claims in the sensationalized reporting were wrong in multiple specific ways: the asteroid was not "colossal" (at approximately 1.1 km length, it is a small-to-moderate NEO and, while potentially regionally hazardous in an impact scenario, is not planet-scale); the flyby distance was 6.8 million miles, not "dangerously close"; there was no plausible physical mechanism for earthquake or volcanic triggering at that distance and for that object mass; and NASA had publicly and explicitly stated "no cause for concern" before the fact-check was published.

The fact-check is concise and directly addresses the factual errors with authoritative NASA source material and journalistic verification (Webster's direct statement to Snopes reporters). An area where the analysis could have added depth is explaining *why* the gravitational earthquake claim was impossible — providing even a simple order-of-magnitude comparison between the asteroid's estimated mass and the Moon's tidal influence would have made the physics accessible to lay readers and, more importantly, would have provided readers with a transferable decision-making tool for evaluating future asteroid scare stories. As the article stands, it refutes the specific false claim but doesn't fully equip readers to evaluate the next one.

The fact-check also appropriately noted that 2003 SD220 would make additional flybys in 2018 and 2070, both safely distant — providing temporal context that prevents the refutation from seeming limited to this single event and demonstrating the systematic, predictable, and non-threatening nature of this particular asteroid's orbital behavior.

## §6 · Epistemic Gap & Further Research

The primary epistemic gap identified by this entry is the lack of systematic media analysis characterizing the "asteroid disaster" journalism genre — its frequency, trigger conditions, specific rhetorical moves, and correlation with actual object risk levels. Research on science journalism more generally (Entwistle, 1995, *Journalist Quarterly*; Bubela et al., 2009, *PLOS Medicine*) has documented patterns of sensationalism and risk amplification, but the asteroid/space-disaster genre has not received dedicated systematic treatment to the author's knowledge.

A second gap concerns public risk numeracy for astronomical distances and masses. Research in risk communication (Lipkus, 2007, *Patient Education and Counseling*; Schwartz & Woloshin, 2013, *JAMA Internal Medicine*) has documented that most people have very limited ability to make meaningful comparative judgments across orders of magnitude. For asteroid risk communication specifically, the challenge is compounding: Earth–Moon distance is already not intuitively grasped by most audiences, and "28 times the Earth–Moon distance" requires a two-step abstract spatial reasoning chain. Research on graphical or analogical representations that successfully convey asteroid flyby distances in terms that activate appropriate non-concern (rather than concern) would contribute to public risk communication for NEO events.

Third, the post-2015 landscape of planetary defense science and policy has advanced substantially, and this entry's focus on a 2015 event should be situated in that development. The Double Asteroid Redirection Test (DART) mission in 2022 successfully demonstrated kinetic impactor deflection of an asteroid (Dimorphos), representing the first intentional alteration of an asteroid orbit (Cheng et al., 2023, *Nature*). The successful DART demonstration has strengthened the credibility of planetary defense as a practical near-term capability rather than a purely theoretical concept, which in turn should inform public communication about asteroid risk: the appropriate public takeaway from NEO monitoring is neither "no asteroids matter" nor "we are helpless against asteroids" but rather "asteroid impact risk is being systematically characterized and manageable deflection capabilities are being developed." This more accurate framing is absent from both the sensationalized disaster reporting and, necessarily given its 2015 publication date, the Snopes fact-check.

Researchers interested in the current state of NEO science and planetary defense should consult: the CNEOS database (maintained by JPL, publicly accessible); Cheng et al. (2023, *Nature*) on DART results; Farnocchia et al. (2021, *Icarus*) on orbit determination for potentially hazardous objects; and the National Academies report on planetary science and astrobiology (2019) for policy context.
