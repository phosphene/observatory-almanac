# "That Asteroid-Moon Collision Video? It's Fake."


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: A video posted to Facebook on July 16, 2022, by an account called "InsanePatient2" and titled "moon got hit by an asteroid" purportedly shows an authentic recording of an asteroid striking the lunar surface, producing a dramatic visible explosion and expanding debris cloud that can be observed from Earth. The video was shared widely on social media as genuine footage of a real event.

**Scale of circulation**: As of the time of the Snopes fact-check (July 2022), the video had accumulated more than 7.4 million views on Facebook and over 121,000 likes. The engagement metrics suggest it reached an audience likely in the tens of millions when indirect shares, cross-posts, and subsequent views across platforms are accounted for. The video's apparent production quality contributed to its virality — it appears immediately compelling rather than obviously amateurish, which is the hallmark of effective CGI misinformation.

**What the video depicts**: The video presents what appears to be a telescopic or satellite view of the lunar surface. A fast-moving object appears from one direction, travels toward the moon, and makes contact with the surface. Upon contact, a large explosion occurs, generating an expanding shockwave and debris cloud visible from the apparent vantage point of the camera. The visual vocabulary of the clip — the object's trajectory, the flash of impact, the expanding ejecta plume — matches popular cultural representations of asteroid impacts as seen in Hollywood science fiction films rather than any known actual imagery of lunar impact events.

**Framing of the claim**: The video was shared within Facebook with no elaborate fabrication or creative fiction framing — it was presented as authentic documentation of a real astronomical event. The account names ("moon got hit by an asteroid") were maximally literal rather than speculative. Comments on the original post and reshares widely treated it as authentic; users expressed shock, excitement, and concern about what a visible impact might mean for Earth.

**The absence of corroborating institutional reports**: When evaluating any claim about a dramatic, observable astronomical event, a fundamental heuristic is whether major astronomical and space agency institutions reported the event. An asteroid impact producing a debris cloud visible from Earth would have been detected and announced by NASA, the European Space Agency, international astronomical observation networks, the International Astronomical Union, and amateur astronomer communities worldwide. No such reports existed anywhere in the public record for the date in question. This absence is in itself conclusive evidence against the claim — legitimate lunar impact events of any significance generate immediate, widely publicized institutional responses.


## 2. What's Actually True

**NASA's direct confirmation of the video's falsity**: Ian O'Neill, a NASA media relations specialist, confirmed to Snopes investigators in response to an inquiry that the video did not depict a real event. O'Neill's statement was direct: "We agree that the video certainly doesn't depict a real event of an asteroid." He further characterized the video as an example of fan-made creative work using NASA imagery as raw material: "Space enthusiasts and other image processors are often excited about raw imagery downlinked from NASA spacecraft and other sources. Sometimes it is used and altered to create fan-made works shared online. This particular product is one of those examples." NASA maintains an extensive public imagery database available at images.nasa.gov, and O'Neill noted that any official NASA news or imagery is exclusively distributed through official NASA communications channels.

**The video as CGI**: Multiple visual analysis indicators consistent with computer-generated imagery (CGI) rather than authentic astronomical recording are apparent in the video. The color palette, shadow rendering, debris cloud dynamics, and impact flash character are inconsistent with the visual signatures of actual astronomical impact observations and are consistent with the output of contemporary visual effects software commonly used in film and video production. The visual style closely resembles the aesthetic of science fiction film impact events rather than real-world astronomical documentation, which tends to be optically noisy, lower-resolution, and constrained by actual photometric properties of the bodies involved.

**Real lunar impacts: the daily reality**: The fact that this video is fictional does not mean the moon is unaffected by impacts. To the contrary, the lunar surface undergoes constant bombardment from the solar system's debris field. According to research on lunar meteoroid flux, an estimated 33,000 meteoroid impacts occur on the lunar surface annually, corresponding to roughly 90 per day. The moon is estimated to collect approximately "five-and-a-half cars worth of dust" each day from these constant small impacts (Zook, H.A., 1975, *Proceedings of the Lunar Science Conference*; Love, S. and Brownlee, D., 1993, *Science*, vol. 262, pp. 550–553, on satellite-based micrometeorite flux measurements).

**Larger periodic impact events**: Larger meteoroids producing impacts equivalent to 1,000 tons of TNT (approximately 1 kiloton) are estimated to strike the moon approximately every four years. These impacts, though far larger than the constant micrometeoroid flux, are still not directly visible from Earth with the naked eye under normal observing conditions. NASA and other organizations run dedicated lunar impact monitoring programs — including observations using sensitive Earth-based telescopes — specifically to detect the brief optical flashes produced by these events. The probability of an impact large enough to produce a debris cloud visible in the video (which depicts an impact equivalent to at least a major nuclear detonation in visual scale) occurring without any prior detection or institutional announcement is effectively zero.

**The moon's impact history: the far side anomaly**: The moon's geological history is defined by impacts. One of the most compelling scientific findings about lunar impact history is the "two-faced moon" asymmetry: the lunar near side (facing Earth) and the far side (permanently facing away) have dramatically different crater distributions and crustal compositions. A 2022 study published in *Science Advances* by Jones et al. from Brown University determined that this asymmetry is largely attributable to a single colossal ancient impact — the South Pole-Aitken basin impact — that occurred approximately 4.3 billion years ago and dramatically reshaped the far side's composition, fueling subsequent volcanism that produced the elevated potassium, phosphorus, and thorium concentrations observed there (Jones, M.J. et al., 2022, *Science Advances*, vol. 8, no. 14, p. eabm8475).

**The asteroid-meteor-meteoroid distinction**: The viral video labeling the impacting object an "asteroid" raises a definitional issue worth clarifying. NASA distinguishes among asteroids (rocky bodies orbiting the sun, ranging from small fragments to Ceres-scale dwarf planets), meteoroids (smaller rocky or metallic objects in space), meteors (meteoroids that have entered an atmosphere and are burning up — "shooting stars"), and meteorites (meteoroids that have survived atmospheric entry and reached the ground). The moon has no atmosphere, meaning all incoming objects remain "meteoroids" until impact — they cannot technically become "meteors" in the strictest sense. Objects of the scale that would produce the visible impact depicted in the viral video would more precisely be termed meteors or small asteroids, but neither would strike the lunar surface silently or produce the clean Hollywood-style explosion shown.

**The 2022 rocket body impact**: It is worth noting that 2022 was indeed a year in which a significant object struck the lunar surface. A spent rocket booster — initially misidentified as the Chinese Long March 3C rocket stage but subsequently re-analyzed as potentially a SpaceX Falcon 9 upper stage — struck the far side of the moon on March 4, 2022, creating a double crater detected by NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter Camera (LRO). This real event generated substantial scientific interest and media coverage. It was not visually spectacular from Earth — the crater was discovered through orbital reconnaissance of the far side months after the impact — but it demonstrates that credible, well-documented lunar impacts do occur and are reported through proper institutional channels with verifiable evidence.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the collapsed threshold of the spectacular**:

The fake asteroid-moon collision video exploits a specific epistemological fragility created by the intersection of two developments: the democratization of high-quality CGI tools and the social media engagement economy that rewards spectacular content over truthful content.

For most of human history, spectacular imagery had production costs that served as an implicit authenticity signal. A dramatic photograph required a photographer to be present at a dramatic event. Footage of an explosion required either a camera crew at the explosion site or, in the earliest special-effects era, elaborate practical model work that nevertheless left visual signatures distinguishing it from real phenomena. Audiences calibrated their trust in spectacular imagery partly against an implicit understanding that creating such imagery required the spectacular event itself to have occurred.

The convergence of accessible computer graphics software — tools like Cinema 4D, Blender, After Effects, and dedicated visual effects platforms — with the social media distribution infrastructure has severed this relationship between spectacular imagery and spectacular events. A sophisticated amateur with several hundred hours of practice in CGI tools can now produce footage that is, for most audiences, visually indistinguishable from real astronomical events. The production cost of the deception has collapsed while its reach through social media has expanded exponentially.

This creates a new epistemological environment in which the spectacular and the fabricated are visually identical. Historical audiences had implicit calibration tools — the grainy quality of real news footage, the specific visual signatures of practical effects, the institutional mediation of news organizations as content gatekeepers. Contemporary audiences face content where the visual polish previously associated with large production budgets (and therefore with at least some institutional verification processes) is now accessible to individual actors with laptops.

The viral video mechanism also self-selects for emotional resonance over informational accuracy. A video showing an asteroid striking the moon is, regardless of its authenticity, more emotionally compelling than a NASA press release announcing the asteroid did not strike the moon. The original false content spreads through engagement mechanics that reward wonder, alarm, and excitement; the corrective content does not spread through the same mechanics. The ratio of the false claim's reach (7.4 million views) to the correction's reach (fraction of the original audience) represents the structural asymmetry of the current information environment.

There is also a specific psychological mechanism at work in rumors about dramatic astronomical events: a deep cultural anxiety about cosmic hazard. Popular culture has, since at least the mid-1990s blockbuster cycle of asteroid-impact films, primed audiences to be emotionally prepared for — and therefore primed to believe — dramatic space impact events. The narrative of a space object striking the moon fits pre-existing mental models that films like *Armageddon*, *Deep Impact*, and their successors have installed in mass cultural memory. When a video appears that matches this narrative template, the cultural priming reduces the audience's critical resistance.


## 4. The Broader Pattern

The fake asteroid-moon collision video belongs to a family of "astronomical spectacle" fabrications that regularly circulate on social media. Recent related cases include:

- Claims that the moon appeared unusually large over various cities (often involving real photographs with telephoto compression effects, misrepresented as showing the moon at unusual distance from Earth)
- Claims that a "blood moon" or eclipse was a sign of impending catastrophe
- Claims about "giant moons" blocking the sun over the Arctic, which Snopes has separately investigated and rated false
- Digitally enhanced or color-corrected photographs of astronomical phenomena misrepresented as authentic

Each of these cases follows the same structural pattern: a visually striking image or video, a claim of spectacular rarity, rapid viral spread through social media engagement mechanics, and eventual fact-check debunking that reaches a fraction of the original audience. The persistence of this pattern suggests that the fake astronomical spectacle is a stable and recurring form of digital misinformation, likely because astronomical phenomena have a specific combination of cultural resonance, popular scientific interest, and genuine public unfamiliarity that makes them ideal vectors for fabricated content.

Real lunar monitoring programs — including NASA's NELIOTA program (Near-Earth Object Lunar Impacts and Optical Transients, operated in partnership with the European Space Agency) and the University of Western Ontario's lunar impact monitoring network — continue to document genuine impact events, providing a scientific baseline against which viral claims can be evaluated. The real impact monitoring data shows regular but visually modest events, none of which match the dramatic scale depicted in the 2022 viral video.


## 5. Evidentiary Assessment

**EARC assignment: R (Refuted)**

The claim is refuted by direct institutional confirmation from NASA, by the absence of any corroborating observational data from any professional or amateur astronomical source worldwide, and by visual analysis consistent with CGI production rather than authentic astronomical recording.

**Key evidence**:
- NASA media relations specialist Ian O'Neill directly confirmed the video was fabricated (O'Neill, I., personal communication to Snopes via email, July 2022)
- No professional astronomical observatory, satellite monitoring system, or international alert network recorded any impact event corresponding to the video's claimed date (July 2022)
- The visual characteristics of the video are consistent with CGI production techniques used in film and video game industries
- The visual scale, dynamics, and optical properties of the depicted impact are inconsistent with physical models of real lunar meteoroid impacts
- Real lunar impact monitoring programs (NELIOTA, ESA/NASA monitoring networks) would have detected an impact of the depicted scale and would have announced it through official channels

**Gap category: digital-fabrication**

The entry addresses a persistent public literacy gap regarding the detection and evaluation of CGI-fabricated astronomical and scientific spectacle content circulating without context on social media platforms.


## 6. Verdict and Implications

**Verdict**: The claim is **false**. The video does not depict a real asteroid striking the moon; it is a computer-generated fabrication. NASA confirmed this directly. No real lunar impact of the scale depicted occurred in July 2022 or has been recorded at any time in the modern era of astronomical observation.

**What is genuinely true**: The moon is constantly struck by real objects — roughly 33,000 meteoroids per year, with occasional more substantial impacts every few years. The moon's geological surface is the cumulative record of 4.5 billion years of such impacts. A rocket body did strike the far side of the moon on March 4, 2022, producing a double crater observed by NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. Real lunar impact science is active, interesting, and well-documented through legitimate institutional channels — including NASA's publicly accessible image database at images.nasa.gov.

**The verification heuristic**: For spectacular astronomical events, the reliable verification heuristic is simple: if a major, visually observable astronomical event actually occurred, institutional sources — NASA, ESA, national observatories, the International Astronomical Union, major research universities — would announce it immediately and prominently. The absence of such announcements is conclusive evidence against claims of dramatic astronomical events, regardless of how compelling a viral video appears.
