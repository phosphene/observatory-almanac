## The Claim

In April 2019, a wave of international news coverage suddenly descended on a story that had been operational fact in Australia for four years. Outlets from the UK's Independent to CNN to CBS News ran articles announcing that Australia was planning to kill two million feral cats by 2020, and that the primary or central method was to be the aerial delivery of sausages laced with poison. The CBS News headline read "Australia is Trying to Kill Millions of Stray Cats by Airdropping Poisoned Sausages." CNN reported that "Australia is at war — with feral cats." The Independent's headline framed it as "Australia Plans to Kill Millions of Feral Cats by Airdropping Sausages Laced With Poison." The circulation of these reports was driven by a feature published by The New York Times on April 25, 2019, which revived international interest in a policy that had been in place and operational since 2015.

The claims in question were complex: Was Australia actually planning this? Was sausage the primary method? Was the target figure 2 million? Were those cats truly a threat of the severity claimed? And what, precisely, was in the sausage?

---

## What's Actually True

The Australian government's feral cat culling program is entirely real, was underway well before the 2019 coverage, and the 2-million cat culling target was genuine. The description of poisoned sausages as a method is accurate, but the reporting implied that this was the primary or exclusive method — when in fact shooting was still the dominant culling technique as of the most recent available figures, and the purpose-designed sausage bait, known as "Curiosity," had not yet received full regulatory registration as of April 2019.

**The policy origin.** In 2015, the Australian Government launched the Threatened Species Strategy, a multi-year, multi-program framework aimed at reversing the decline of Australia's most imperilled native species. Feral cats were identified as the highest-priority threat addressed by the strategy: the government's own documentation described feral cats as "one of the greatest threats to Australia's land-based mammals" and stated they had been "a major contributor to the extinction of at least 27 mammals since they were first introduced to Australia." The strategy document cited feral cats as currently imperilling "at least 142 species or more than one third of our threatened mammals, reptiles, frogs and birds." The feral cat component of the strategy set a target of culling 2 million feral cats by 2020 and the complete eradication of feral cats from five specified Australian islands.

**Scale and methods.** By the end of 2016, researchers from RMIT University in Melbourne had estimated that approximately 211,000 feral cats had been culled as part of the initiative. Critically, these researchers estimated that approximately 83% of culled cats had been shot rather than poisoned. Conventional culling methods — shooting, trapping with foothold and cage traps, poison baiting using existing toxins — remained the primary techniques while the new Curiosity bait was under development and regulatory review.

**The Curiosity bait.** The sausage described in international media reports was the product of a $5.1 million development program by Australia's Department of the Environment and Energy. The Curiosity bait consists of a small meat-based sausage containing a small hard plastic pellet encapsulating para-aminopropiophenone (PAPP), a toxicant that converts haemoglobin to methaemoglobin, reducing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity and causing death through oxygen deprivation. PAPP was selected over alternative toxicants for several reasons: it acts through a mechanism considered more humane than existing options (primarily 1080/sodium fluoroacetate), it produces death in a shorter timeframe (minutes to hours rather than hours to days), its mechanism is specific enough that an antidote (methylene blue) can reverse the toxicosis if non-target animals are exposed and treated promptly, and the Royal Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals in Australia endorsed it as a clear improvement in humaneness over previously deployed toxins.

Importantly, the Curiosity bait was designed to exploit a specific physiological difference between cats and native Australian fauna: cats tend to swallow food in large pieces (feline dental anatomy is optimised for tearing rather than grinding, cats having only limited molar teeth), while most Australian native animals nibble and chew more thoroughly. The PAPP pellet was designed to dissolve only in a stomach environment — it is enclosed in a slow-dissolving plastic capsule — meaning animals that chew the sausage thoroughly and reject the pellet are protected. The Snopes article noted that the claim in the government document that cats lack molar teeth entirely was a factual inaccuracy (cats do have molars, though only two on each side and used for tearing rather than grinding); the functional claim — that cats are more likely to swallow large food pieces whole relative to Australian native fauna — appears sound based on the field trial evidence.

**Regulatory and distribution status as of April 2019.** At the time of the 2019 news wave, the Curiosity bait had not yet received final registration from the Australian Pesticides and Veterinary Medicines Authority. However, the government had already begun soliciting commercial manufacturing partners in November 2018, indicating confidence that registration would be granted. In field trials, the bait had been distributed both from aircraft and by ground vehicles in target areas.

## Why People Believe This (and Why the Framing Was Misleading)

The April 2019 news cycle exemplifies a structural problem in science communication about conservation policy: the gap between when a policy is created and when it receives international media attention can produce the false impression of novelty where there is only belated discovery.

**The New York Times as catalyst.** The New York Times' April 25, 2019 feature article on the feral cat program catalysed the subsequent international coverage wave. This is a known pattern in science and policy journalism: a major outlet devoting serious feature-length attention to an ongoing story can cause a cascade of derivative coverage from other outlets, each presenting the story as though it were newly discovered. Readers who had not previously encountered the story (which is to say, most international readers) experienced the coverage as breaking news, when it was actually belated documentation of a four-year-old policy. The policy itself had not changed; only the media attention had.

**The sausage framing.** The framing of aerial sausage delivery as the primary or defining method — rather than one innovative element of a multi-method culling program — reflects both the intrinsic vividness of the image and the way news selection favours the unusual over the routine. "Australia is shooting feral cats" is informationally accurate regarding the dominant culling method as of 2016, but it is not a compelling headline. "Australia is airdropping poisoned sausages" is striking, evocative, and immediately memorable. The framing prioritised narrative vividness over proportional accuracy, creating an impression that the sausage delivery was the central story when it was an innovative component of a larger program.

**The emotional register of feral cats.** Domestic cats (*Felis catus*) occupy a uniquely contested position in human emotional and cultural life. They are the world's most popular pet animal — an estimated 600 million domestic cats globally — and are subjects of extraordinary cultural affection, extensive social media content, and in many contexts strong emotional protectiveness. A government program to kill millions of them, even feral ones with no domestic history, activates this protectiveness in ways that differ from, say, news about the culling of feral pigs or cane toads. The international reaction to the Australian feral cat program included substantial opposition from animal rights organisations and general audiences, and the framing of the story by some outlets appeared calibrated to this audience — emphasising the scale and method of killing in ways that foregrounded its emotional offensiveness rather than its ecological rationale.

**The ecological rationale.** The environmental case for feral cat control in Australia is, by the standards of conservation science, unusually strong. Australia has the worst mammal extinction record on Earth: since European colonisation in 1788, approximately 33 mammal species have gone extinct, representing about one-third of all mammal extinctions globally since 1500 (Woinarski, Burbidge, and Harrison, 2015, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, which comprehensively documented Australian mammal extinction drivers). Feral cats are implicated in the majority of mammal extinctions that can be attributed to predation. Dickman (1996, *Overview of Impacts of Feral Cats on Australian Native Fauna*, Department of the Environment, Sport and Territories) documented feral cat predation across the full range of Australian habitats and found that individual cats in some conditions kill more than 1,000 native animals per year; a feral cat in arid environments may have a daily hunting requirement met by small mammals, birds, and reptiles that are themselves rare or threatened.

## Scientific Evidence: What the Literature Says

**The scale of the feral cat problem in Australia.** Edwards, Scalvenzi, and Dickman (2015, *Biological Conservation*) provided revised estimates of feral cat population density and distribution across Australia, finding that feral cat populations are present in virtually all Australian habitats including the harshest arid zones, with density estimates ranging from 0.04 to 4 cats per square kilometre depending on habitat quality and prey availability. National population estimates range from 2 million to 6 million feral cats depending on rainfall-driven fluctuations in prey availability. Years of high rainfall and subsequent small mammal population booms are followed by large increases in feral cat populations, creating what ecologists call "numerical responses" — predator population growth driven by prey abundance.

**Mammal extinction in Australia.** Woinarski, Burbidge, and Harrison (2015, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, vol. 112, no. 15, pp. 4531–4540) is the primary reference for the claim that Australia has lost 33 mammal species since 1788 — the highest national mammal extinction rate on Earth. The study identified predation by feral cats and foxes as the dominant cause of extinction for small and medium-bodied mammals (those in the "critical weight range" from 35 grams to 5.5 kilograms), and noted that habitat loss and altered fire regimes compounded predation pressure. The paper directly informed the Australian Threatened Species Strategy's identification of feral cats as the highest-priority extinction driver.

**PAPP toxicology and humaneness assessment.** Johnston, Eisemann, O'Brien, and Brown (2011, in *Wildlife Research*, vol. 38, pp. 665–673) published the results of field trials of PAPP-based bait for feral cat control, documenting kill rates, non-target uptake, and time-to-death. The study found mean time to death of approximately 2.5 hours following ingestion of a full dose, compared to 8–24 hours for 1080-based baits. The antidote efficacy (methylene blue administered intravenously within the toxic phase) was confirmed to be high for non-target animals treated promptly. The study established the basic toxicological and humaneness profile that underpinned regulatory consideration.

**Island eradication as conservation benchmark.** Nogales et al. (2004, *Conservation Biology*, vol. 18, no. 2, pp. 310–319) reviewed feral cat eradication projects on islands globally, finding that eradications had been achieved on islands up to approximately 3,000 hectares using combination methods including trapping, shooting, and poison baiting. This literature established that feral cat removal from bounded, manageable landscapes was technically achievable, and provided the conceptual foundation for the Australian strategy's island eradication targets. The mainland eradication components of the Australian program are considerably more challenging than island programs given the absence of geographic boundaries, and the 2-million-cat culling target for mainland Australia was explicitly a culling target rather than an eradication target — reducing population density in high-priority conservation areas rather than eliminating the species entirely from the continent.

**The ecological outcomes of feral cat removal.** Hayward et al. (2016, *Biological Conservation*, vol. 204, pp. 302–311) conducted a meta-analysis of studies examining native wildlife recovery following feral cat removal, finding significant positive responses in native bird, reptile, and mammal populations in the majority of cases where feral cats were removed and outcomes monitored. Native mammal reintroduction success rates were substantially higher in fenced reserves from which feral cats had been eliminated. The study provided empirical grounding for the claim that feral cat control is effective at producing conservation outcomes — not merely a policy aspiration.

## The Epistemic Gap: What Fact-Checking Doesn't Settle

Snopes confirmed the program as True in all material respects: the policy was real, the 2-million-cat target was genuine, and the Curiosity sausage bait was a genuine element of the strategy. The fact-check correctly noted that the program had been underway for years before the 2019 coverage, that shooting had been the dominant method, and that the sausage bait was not yet fully registered as of the coverage dates.

What the fact-check does not address, and what constitutes a genuinely interesting and unresolved set of questions, concerns the program's actual outcomes and the ethical tensions it surfaces.

**Did the program meet its targets?** The 2-million-cat culling target was set for completion by 2020. The most recent available figures in the public record as of the Snopes investigation showed approximately 211,000 culled by end-2016. Whether the subsequent years closed the gap, substantially exceeded it, or fell significantly short is not clearly documented in publicly available reporting. The Threatened Species Strategy progress reports produced by the Australian government provide partial data, but tracking outcomes with the same clarity as the original targets is a consistent weakness of large-scale conservation programs.

**The ethics review that doesn't appear in fact-checks.** The claim that PAPP is "humane" because death occurs faster than with 1080 is a relative comparison that requires scrutiny. Mean time-to-death of 2.5 hours (Johnston et al., 2011) is not instantaneous or painless by any ordinary standard — it involves progressive hypoxia affecting an animal that may remain mobile and cognitively active for part of that period. Animal rights organisations including PETA have contested the "humaneness" characterisation. The RSPCA's endorsement is based on a comparative assessment (better than previous toxins) rather than an absolute one (genuinely painless). Fact-checks validate "is this true?" but the normative question — is this the right thing to do? — involves value trade-offs that truth/false verification cannot resolve. The tension between the welfare interests of the feral cats being killed and the welfare interests of the native fauna they are preying to extinction is a genuine ethical dilemma that reasonable people weigh differently.

**The non-target species question.** The Curiosity bait's design is intended to reduce non-target uptake, but the validation studies were conducted under specific field conditions. The extent to which quolls, bilbies, and other native carnivores that might investigate baits are genuinely protected by the pellet design under diverse real-world conditions — including in areas where native fauna are less familiar with the bait presentation — remains a question in the ongoing research. Critically, quolls (*Dasyurus* spp.) are themselves among Australia's most threatened native animals, and any bait program that incidentally kills significant numbers of quolls would represent a perverse conservation outcome. This question is being actively managed and studied, but field validations at national program scale were not fully completed at the time of the 2019 coverage.

## Verdict

**True.** Australia's Threatened Species Strategy genuinely included a target of culling 2 million feral cats by 2020, using multiple methods including a purpose-designed meat sausage bait ("Curiosity") containing para-aminopropiophenone (PAPP) distributed in part by aircraft. This policy was real, was in progress for years before the 2019 media wave, and was grounded in a robust scientific case for feral cats as the primary driver of Australia's catastrophic mammal extinction rate. The sausage airdrop framing was accurate but disproportionately emphasised relative to shooting (which was the dominant actual culling method as of available figures) and the Curiosity bait was not yet fully registered as of April 2019. The international surprise was a function of belated media attention rather than any novelty in the program itself.
