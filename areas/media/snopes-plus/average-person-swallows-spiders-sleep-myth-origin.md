# The "Swallowing Spiders in Sleep" Myth Was Invented to Show How Misinformation Spreads

## 1. The Claim

There are actually two nested claims here, and both have achieved wide circulation.

**Claim A (the base myth):** The average person swallows eight spiders in their sleep each year. Variants put the number at four, twelve, or simply "several"; some specify that you swallow them without knowing; others add that spiders crawl into sleeping mouths for warmth, moisture, or because the breath draws them in.

**Claim B (the meta-myth):** Claim A was deliberately fabricated as a scientific or journalistic experiment to demonstrate how misinformation spreads. In this telling, a researcher, columnist, or internet prankster invented the spider statistic and planted it somewhere online or in print to observe whether people would believe and share it without checking—a controlled demonstration of gullibility. The meta-myth is often delivered with a knowing, winking quality: "Did you know that the thing everyone believes is false? And the reason it exists is that someone *wanted* you to believe it to prove a point about you?" It feels like an upgrade, a piece of behind-the-scenes epistemological sophistication.

Both claims circulate widely. Claim A appears in listicles, pub trivia, and "facts you didn't know" compilations. Claim B circulates as a debunking, typically shared by people who want to demonstrate they are not credulous. The irony—which is the central subject of this entry—is that Claim B is also false, and shares with Claim A the exact feature it purports to expose: it spreads because people repeat it without checking.

## 2. What's Actually True

**On Claim A: The spider statistic is biologically implausible.**

Domestic spiders are prey animals. They are acutely sensitive to vibrations, air currents, and chemosensory cues. A sleeping human produces all of these: snoring and breathing generate irregular pressure waves, the chest rises and falls, the bed creates vibration during movement. Spiders do not seek enclosed warm spaces occupied by large vibrating mammals—this would be antithetical to their survival behavior. House spiders (most commonly *Tegenaria domestica* or *Parasteatoda tepidariorum* in temperate climates) spend the overwhelming majority of their lives near their webs, in wall gaps, under furniture, or in ceiling corners. They do not wander onto faces as a foraging strategy.

Spider behavior researcher Rod Crawford at the Burke Museum in Seattle, who maintains an authoritative database on spider myths, has stated plainly that there is no scientific evidence that humans ingest spiders during sleep and that the behavior described would require spiders to act in ways contrary to everything known about their movement patterns and threat-response behaviors. An open sleeping mouth would emit breath, vibration, and chemical compounds from the body—all of which would register to a spider as a large predator or at minimum as a threat, not as an attractive shelter. The spider would flee, not enter.

**On Claim B: The meta-myth has a traceable but mundane origin.**

The claim that the spider statistic was deliberately invented as a misinformation experiment appears to trace to a 1993 article in the computing magazine *PC Professional*, written by Lisa Holst. The column reportedly listed several dubious "facts" that people forward in chain letters, with the spider statistic among them, and was later interpreted as having "invented" those facts. However, there are significant problems with this origin story.

Snopes researcher David Mikkelson traced Holst's column and found that the original article is essentially inaccessible for direct verification—it is cited at several degrees of remove, and attempts to locate the original print issue have not produced the text. The claim that Holst invented the spider statistic specifically as an experiment in misinformation spread requires inferring her intent from her framing, and that framing itself is known only through secondary citations. What Holst appears to have been doing was listing dubious facts already circulating in chain letter culture—not introducing a brand-new fabrication. If this reading is correct, the spider statistic predates the Holst column and was already circulating before she listed it.

More to the point: even if Holst invented the statistic, this does not constitute a "controlled experiment to show how misinformation spreads." That framing implies scientific design—a hypothesis, a controlled release, measurement of propagation. Holst's column was a humor piece for a computing magazine. The meta-myth layers an epistemologically sophisticated narrative onto what appears to have been a casual journalistic choice, transforming it into a deliberate intellectual provocation that it was almost certainly not. Mikkelson and Snopes rate the meta-myth claim as false, and there is no credible contemporaneous documentation of any researcher or journalist deliberately planting false information as a controlled public persuasion experiment at this time.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The base myth (Claim A) demonstrates one set of cognitive seductions. The meta-myth (Claim B) demonstrates a different, more interesting one. Together they form a nested system of false beliefs in which the cure is as fake as the disease.

The base myth spreads through **disgust salience and visceral imagery**. Spider-swallowing is deeply unpleasant to contemplate. This is not coincidental. Research in social psychology by Heath C, Bell C, and Sternberg E (2001, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology) demonstrated that "social transmission of beliefs" is substantially predicted by the emotional arousal the belief generates, particularly disgust. Stories that provoke strong disgust emotions are shared more frequently, remembered more vividly, and less critically evaluated than neutral material. The spider statistic functions like an emotional hook—once lodged in the mind, it is hard to dislodge because dislodging it requires overcoming both memory salience and the social reward of being the person who delivers a disgusting surprising fact. The evolutionary psychology of disgust is highly sensitive to contamination threats, and a statistic claiming you are being contaminated by spiders during your most vulnerable unconscious state activates this system immediately and forcefully.

There is also the pseudo-precision of a specific number. "Eight spiders a year" is more believable than "many spiders" or "some spiders." Specific numbers suggest measurement, and measurement suggests scientific authority. Cialdini R (1984, *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*, William Morrow) documented the automaticity of credibility attribution to apparently precise data; pseudo-precision is a primary vehicle for the propagation of false statistics because it mimics the appearance of empirical grounding. Four spiders, eight spiders, twelve spiders—any specific number signals that someone counted, even when no counting occurred.

The meta-myth operates through a completely different mechanism, and understanding it requires identifying a distinct cognitive vulnerability: **the credibility bonus of self-aware skepticism**. When a claim arrives in the form of "did you know that the thing you believe is false?", it confers status on the person sharing it. You gain the social position of the person who knows better, who has seen through the illusion, who has done the intellectual work of checking. This social incentive to share is at least as powerful as the disgust incentive that drives the base myth—perhaps more powerful in certain social environments where epistemic sophistication is a status marker.

What makes Claim B particularly insidious is that it is **epistemically dressed**. It is not simply wrong; it is wrong in a way that uses the language of meta-cognition and critical thinking. The framing of "this was invented to show how misinformation spreads" imports the vocabulary of media literacy—source checking, controlled experiments, deliberate planting—into what is itself an unchecked rumor operating exactly like the thing it claims to expose. Calling attention to your own epistemological frame while committing the error the frame supposedly prevents is a structural irony that is almost never noticed in the moment of sharing.

The philosopher Harry Frankfurt's analysis of bullshit in *On Bullshit* (2005, Princeton University Press) is relevant here, though the concept must be repurposed slightly. Frankfurt distinguishes between lying (asserting what you believe to be false) and bullshitting (speaking without regard for truth). The meta-myth circulates as bullshit in Frankfurt's sense: most people sharing it have not verified its origin, do not know who Lisa Holst is, have no access to the 1993 PC Professional article, and are indifferent to the truth conditions of their claim because the social and psychological value of the claim does not depend on its accuracy. The meta-myth's function is to signal epistemic sophistication, and that function is independent of whether the claim is actually true. This is the precise mechanism Frankfurt identified, and the spider meta-myth is a nearly ideal specimen.

There is a still deeper level here that few discussions of this myth reach: the meta-myth's persuasiveness depends on a **coherence cascade**—a chain of mutually reinforcing elements that together create an impression of solidity even though each element is itself unverified. The chain runs approximately: *someone deliberately invented this* → *they were testing misinformation spread* → *the experiment succeeded* → *your belief in the original myth was the expected outcome* → *now you know better*. Each step in this chain feels like it supports the others, creating the impression of a robust narrative, even though the chain's first link (the deliberate invention) is the only one that matters and it is the one that is unverified. Cognitive psychologists Levine TR and colleagues have documented this kind of coherence-driven belief in deception contexts; the narrative coherence of a story is among the strongest predictors of its acceptance independent of its actual evidentiary base.

## 4. Verdict

**False (for both claims)**

The base myth (you swallow eight spiders in your sleep each year) is false on biological and behavioral grounds. Spiders do not enter sleeping human mouths; their sensory apparatus and threat-response behaviors make this scenario essentially impossible outside of extreme circumstances. There is no study, no entomological survey, no dataset that supports any version of the statistic.

The meta-myth (this statistic was invented to demonstrate how misinformation spreads) is also false. The most plausible traceable origin is a 1993 PC Professional column by Lisa Holst that was cataloguing already-circulating dubious facts rather than inventing new ones, and which in any case was not a controlled experiment. No documentation of a deliberate plant-and-monitor experiment exists. The "experiment" framing is itself an unfounded claim that has propagated through exactly the mechanisms it purports to expose—social sharing without verification, motivated by the psychological reward of appearing epistemically sophisticated.

The double falsehood is not a coincidence. It is structurally generated by the social dynamics of debunking culture, in which knowing the "real story" behind a myth confers status—and in which that status incentive is just as capable of spreading false information as the disgust incentive that spread the original myth.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The spider meta-myth is a case study in what might be called **second-order misinformation ecology**—the way that debunking culture can generate its own misinformation layer that is structurally harder to correct than the original false belief. First-order misinformation circulates because people find it interesting, surprising, or emotionally compelling. Second-order misinformation circulates because people find it validating—it confirms their self-image as critical thinkers who don't just forward chain letters. The social reward structure in the second tier is actually stronger than in the first, because it offers identity affirmation rather than mere entertainment.

This pattern is well documented in media literacy research. Lewandowsky S, Ecker UKH, and Cook J (2017, Journal of Applied Research in Memory and Cognition) identified what they called the "backfire effect" (later revisited and partially revised by Wood T and Porter E in 2019, Political Behavior)—the finding that corrections to false beliefs can, under certain conditions, strengthen those beliefs by increasing their salience and by triggering defensive identity processing. More relevant here is the work of Pennycook G and Rand DG (2019, Trends in Cognitive Sciences) on the "lazy, not biased" hypothesis—the finding that susceptibility to misinformation tracks analytical thinking deficits more closely than motivated reasoning. The spider meta-myth provides a natural experiment: it travels primarily through communities that pride themselves on analytical thinking, suggesting that the social identity reward of "knowing better" is sufficient to bypass the actual analytical process of checking sources.

The ecosystem implications are significant for media literacy education. If corrections can be as false as the things they correct—and can spread through the very epistemic mechanism they invoke as their authority—then any framework that treats debunking as a reliable terminal state is insufficient. The goal of information hygiene is not to believe fewer things; it is to calibrate confidence to evidence across all claims, including the claims one makes about other claims. The spider myth and its meta-myth are illustrative of the full cycle: a false claim generates a false debunking, which generates another round of credulous sharing, which eventually generates another round of debunking (this one possibly correct), which is still subject to the same possibility of unchecked propagation.

The biology of spiders deserves more than a footnote here, because the myth inadvertently carries a real cost beyond its epistemic interest: it contributes to arachnophobia and the general stigmatization of spiders as threatening, invasive, and malevolent. Most house spiders pose no threat to humans. They are among the most effective allies in controlling mosquito and fly populations in domestic environments. The persistent image of spiders as creatures that crawl into your mouth while you sleep reinforces a phobia that costs spiders their lives in domestic settings and contributes to the broader, ecologically costly devaluation of invertebrate life. This is a case where a false belief has direct ecological downstream effects—small in any individual instance, large when aggregated across a culture that systematically misrepresents spider behavior and threat level.

The 1993 Holst column, whatever its precise relationship to the spider statistic's origins, does illuminate a real phenomenon: the chain-letter transmission of false "facts" was already a documented and culturally remarked-upon problem before the internet vastly amplified it. The specific format—numbered lists of surprising facts without sources—has structural features that strip claims of their attribution history, making each new recipient a potential amplifier with no access to evidence. This structural feature of list-format information transmission is more important than any individual false claim, because it creates an environment in which all claims circulate on equal epistemic footing regardless of their actual provenance. The spider statistic and the claim about swallowing eight of them per year exist in the same format and with the same apparent authority as the claim that a day has 86,400 seconds—and within a list, the reader has no efficient mechanism to distinguish them.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addresses the spider-swallowing statistic as false and provides biological reasoning about spider behavior, including the sensory threats a sleeping human presents to any nearby spider. It correctly identifies the evolutionary implausibility of the scenario and references expert sources in spider biology. The Snopes treatment is direct and well-reasoned.

Snopes also addresses the meta-myth—that the spider statistic was deliberately planted—and rates this claim as equally false. This is one of the stronger examples of Snopes applying recursive scrutiny: not just debunking the base claim, but identifying and debunking the popular debunking of it. The entry notes the problems with tracing the Holst column and distinguishes between "appearing in a list of circulating myths" and "being deliberately invented as a misinformation experiment."

What fact-checkers generally do well on this entry they struggle with elsewhere: the application of the same standard of evidence to the debunking as to the original claim. The spider case benefits from having a clear biological argument against the base myth and a traceable (if unverifiable) documentary trail for the meta-myth's origin. Topics where neither the base claim nor its popular debunking has clear empirical resolution are harder to handle with the binary verdict format.

The deeper methodological lesson this myth offers to fact-checkers is the importance of **recursive skepticism**—the practice of applying the same evidential standards to a claim about a myth that are applied to the myth itself. A culture of fact-checking that is reflexive in this way is harder to game, because it does not provide a simple shortcut for anyone who wants to spread false information under the protective coloring of debunking. Snopes earns credit here for making the recursive move; most other media literacy resources that discuss the spider myth stop at the first tier and inadvertently propagate the second-tier falsehood.
