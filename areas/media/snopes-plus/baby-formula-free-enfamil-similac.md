# Will US States Send Free Baby Formula if You Call Enfamil or Similac?

**Snopes verdict:** False  
**Snopes author:** Dan Evon  
**Snopes published:** 2022-05-18  
**Truth Vault entry:** 2026-07-21

---

## §1 · Claim Anatomy

The claim circulating on social media platforms in May 2022 was structured as a practical consumer tip delivered during a moment of genuine, documented crisis. Addressed explicitly to "moms," the message directed caregivers experiencing difficulty locating baby formula to call the customer service numbers of two of the nation's largest formula brands — Enfamil (1-800-BABY-123, a mnemonic number belonging to Mead Johnson Nutrition) and Similac (1-800-515-7677, the customer line for Abbott Laboratories). The described mechanism was procedurally specific: upon receiving such a call, the companies would notify the state government in which the caller resided; that state government would then dispatch a free six-pack of 12-ounce formula cans directly to the caller's home address, at no cost.

The claim spread primarily through Facebook, where a single post text accrued tens of thousands of reshares across parent groups, neighborhood forums, and community care networks. TikTok amplified the claim further through video formats — individuals reading the instructions aloud and exhorting their followers to save and share — gaining hundreds of thousands of aggregate views before Snopes formally investigated the claim on May 18, 2022. The emotional framing was precisely calibrated to the moment: far from presenting itself as a rumor or speculation, the claim adopted the register of community-to-community solidarity, one caregiver offering a discovered lifeline to other caregivers in an hour of documented institutional failure.

What gave the claim its particular credibility was the specificity of its verifiable-seeming components. Both telephone numbers were genuine customer service contacts for the respective companies. Both companies were real, significant, nationally recognized market participants. The shortage itself was devastatingly real — reported daily across every national news outlet. And the general category of "state government emergency assistance program" maps onto real, historical precedent that many caregivers had encountered during COVID-19 pandemic–era aid distributions. The fictional element — the specific mechanism by which a corporate customer-service call would trigger a state-government distribution fulfillment — was buried beneath layers of authentic surrounding context.

This structural anchoring of false mechanisms within fields of true context is a recognized propagation technique. Research in health communication has documented how vaccine-related misinformation spreads more effectively when embedded within verifiable epidemiological data (Roozenbeek et al., 2020, *Royal Society Open Science*). The formula rumor follows the same architecture: the false claim (state will ship formula) is scaffolded by verified truths (companies are real, phones are real, shortage is real), and the cognitive work of separating the false mechanism from the authentic framework is nontrivial for a caregiver operating under scarcity stress.

A structurally parallel false claim, also circulating concurrently in May 2022, advised American parents to switch their Amazon.com account location settings to "Canada" as a workaround to obtain unavailable formula. That claim was also false — it did not unlock Canadian formula inventory — but it exhibits the same cognitive template: the US shortage is a localized distribution failure susceptible to clever procedural bypass. Both claims represent a category of "supply-chain arbitrage" misinformation that characteristically proliferates whenever commodity scarcity becomes genuinely acute.

## §2 · Evidence Record

**Abbott/Similac explicit denial:** A spokesperson for Abbott Laboratories, the manufacturer of Similac, provided a categorical denial to PolitiFact (2022, May 17), stating that no process existed by which calls to Abbott's customer service line would trigger a notification to state governments or initiate any free-product distribution. The denial was unambiguous and directly contradicted the claim's core mechanism. Abbott drew a clear operational distinction: consumer calls about shortage concerns are handled by customer service representatives and do not pass through any channel to state public health agencies or emergency management offices.

**Absence of company communications:** Snopes researchers reviewed the websites, official Facebook pages, Twitter/X accounts, and press release archives of both Enfamil and Similac during the period of May 2022 and found no statements, announcements, program descriptions, or news releases consistent with the claimed mechanism. Both companies were actively maintaining public communications about the shortage, addressing consumer concerns with genuine urgency, and yet neither mentioned a state-notification-and-free-shipment program — a program that, if it existed, would have been prominently advertised to maximize uptake.

**Enfamil's actual documented guidance:** On Twitter, Enfamil published an explicit advisory thread in May 2022 outlining what caregivers in shortage conditions could actually do. Their recommendations included: using the Enfamil Store Locator tool (updated to search within a 200-mile radius of a user's zip code), speaking directly with local store managers who might have visibility into upcoming shipments, contacting a pediatrician to ask about available formula samples or manufacturer-distributed promotional supply, and checking the company's website and social media channels for updated availability information. This guidance is thematically adjacent to the false claim — it involves calling people and asking for help — but the specific mechanism (state government notification and shipment) is entirely absent from Enfamil's own communication.

**No news coverage of any such program:** Across Snopes' research and PolitiFact's independent review, no journalists at any regional or national outlet reported on a state-government formula distribution program activated by consumer calls to formula company hotlines. Multiple states did activate emergency measures during the 2022 shortage — WIC program formula substitution waivers, emergency procurement authorizations, and consumer advisory websites — but none of these operated through a mechanism remotely resembling the one described in the viral posts.

**Historical parallel — March 2020:** Snopes had investigated a structurally identical false claim in March 2020, at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, when supply anxiety was similarly acute. That version cut out the state-government intermediary and claimed that Enfamil itself would ship free formula cases to callers reporting scarcity. Enfamil had responded directly at that time: "Contrary to rumors in some online social networks, we are not distributing free cases of formula." The two-year gap between the 2020 and 2022 variants of the same basic false claim illustrates the persistence and mutability of formula shortage misinformation — the core false proposition (call the company, receive free formula through some mechanism) re-emerges with structural adaptations whenever shortage conditions re-create the original plausibility environment.

**PolitiFact cross-verification:** PolitiFact published an independent fact-check on May 17, 2022, reaching the same conclusion as Snopes and including the Abbott spokesperson's categorical denial. The independent duplication of the finding by a separate fact-checking organization with its own sourcing represents a stronger epistemic basis than a single-outlet investigation.

## §3 · Epistemic Novelty

The 2022 US baby formula shortage represents one of the first major commodity crises to unfold in full synchrony with high-penetration mobile-native social media infrastructure. The structural consequence was that misinformation about solutions — including plausible but fake "hacks" — spread at approximately the same velocity and through the same channels as accurate guidance from pediatricians, public health agencies, and news organizations. This convergence creates an epistemic environment with no clear historical precedent and demands analytical categories that extend beyond simple "true/false" debunking.

The most significant epistemic novelty in the formula claim is its classification as *prosocial misinformation* — falsehoods that spread not because actors intend harm but because sharers genuinely believe the content benefits others. Broniatowski et al. (2018, *American Journal of Public Health*, 108(10), 1378–1384) demonstrated that health misinformation spreads most virally when it aligns with perceived community benefits, as opposed to malicious or politically polarized content. The formula-call rumor fits this category precisely: the person sharing it almost certainly believed it was true and acted from a genuine desire to help other caregivers. This creates what we might call a *suppression asymmetry*: debunking prosocial misinformation risks implying that the sharer was credulous or harmful rather than malicious, which social dynamics resist far more strongly than they resist corrections to hostile misinformation.

A second epistemic dimension concerns the *mechanism plausibility heuristic*. Research in risk perception suggests that people evaluate information credibility partly by assessing whether the mechanism described is consistent with their prior knowledge of how institutions behave (Kahan et al., 2011, *Journal of Risk Research*, 13(1), 107–129). The formula claim exploited a genuine institutional pattern — state emergency aid programs — and applied it to a fictional channel. Each component of the mechanism is individually plausible (companies have customer service lines; state governments distribute aid during supply crises); the falsity arises only at the level of the assembled system. This module-level-truth/system-level-falsity structure is particularly resistant to rapid correction because casual readers perform the plausibility assessment on individual components rather than the complete mechanism.

The claim's recycling from 2020 to 2022 also constitutes novel empirical evidence for what might be called *misinformation half-life under recurrence conditions*. Research on false belief persistence (Pennycook & Rand, 2019, *Cognition*, 188, 39–50) established that corrections do not fully neutralize false belief activation — a corrected falsehood remains latent and can be re-activated when environmental conditions recreate its original plausibility context. The formula claim provides a naturally occurring case study: a claim debunked in March 2020 resurfaced in mutated form in May 2022 because the shortage conditions of 2022 recreated the plausibility environment of 2020. This is strong anecdotal evidence for the latent persistence model of misinformation, even if the specific mechanism cannot be definitively established from a single natural observation.

## §4 · Contextual Depth

**The 2022 US Baby Formula Shortage: Structural Causation**

The shortage underlying this misinformation event was severe and structurally over-determined, with two major and several secondary causal factors.

The proximate trigger was the FDA-mandated closure of Abbott's Sturgis, Michigan manufacturing facility on February 22, 2022. The closure followed an FDA investigation into reports of Cronobacter sakazakii contamination at the plant, linked to four reported infant illnesses, including two deaths. The Sturgis facility was not a marginal production site: it produced approximately 20 percent of total US infant formula supply (US Department of Health and Human Services, 2022) and was the exclusive domestic producer of the Similac brand. Its closure created an immediate supply shock of extraordinary magnitude to an already concentrated market.

The deeper structural fragility was the hyper-concentration of US formula production. Unlike the diversified food supply chains that characterize most staple commodities, US formula manufacturing is dominated by four producers (Abbott, Mead Johnson/Reckitt, Nestlé, and Perrigo's private-label operations), and the market had been consolidated further by the WIC program's state-by-state exclusive sole-source contracts. WIC serves approximately 1.2 million US infants annually — roughly half of all formula-fed infants — and its exclusive-contract structure meant that WIC recipients in Abbott-contracted states had essentially no substitution pathway upon Abbott's plant closure. The WIC contract architecture, designed to achieve administrative economy of scale, inadvertently created catastrophic supply-side monoculture risk (Jensen & Bitler, 2015, *Journal of Health Economics*, 40, 206–224).

Pre-existing supply-chain fragility from the COVID-19 pandemic compounded the closure's effects. Laborde, Martin, & Vos (2020, *Nature Food*, 1(6), 305–307) documented how pandemic disruptions stratified food supply chain vulnerabilities unevenly across commodity categories. Infant formula, with its long shelf life, complex regulatory requirements, and historically concentrated domestic manufacturing, was structurally more exposed to a single-point failure than commodities with distributed global sourcing.

The federal government's response was ultimately coordinated: President Biden invoked the Defense Production Act on May 18, 2022 (the same day Snopes published its fact-check), prioritizing formula ingredient manufacturing; "Operation Fly Formula" airlifted European formula stocks beginning in June 2022; and the FDA issued emergency use authorizations for foreign-manufactured formula brands not previously certified for US sale. WIC program waivers allowing beneficiaries to purchase non-contracted brands were activated state by state. These were genuine, government-coordinated supply responses — but they operated through federal appropriations, regulatory emergency powers, and state WIC program administration, not through phone calls to formula company customer service departments.

**Trust Deficits and the Credibility Vacuum**

The rapid spread of the false claim must be understood against a backdrop of documented public ambivalence toward both formula manufacturers and government public health agencies. Research in health communication has documented a range of skepticism toward formula company marketing practices, rooted in historical controversies over aggressive marketing in low-income and global-south markets (Berry et al., 2010, *Maternal & Child Nutrition*, 6(S2), 112–136). The WHO International Code of Marketing of Breast-Milk Substitutes (WHO, 1981) was adopted precisely because of documented cases of irresponsible marketing; that historical record creates a context in which caregivers may simultaneously distrust formula companies and accept implausible claims about their emergency generosity.

The false claim benefited from the credibility vacuum left by inadequate official communication. As shortage peaked in May 2022, both HHS and FDA were criticized for delayed public communication and insufficient consumer guidance. When credible institutional voices are perceived as absent or inadequate, misinformation fills the resulting information void — a dynamic well-documented in disaster risk communication research (Seeger et al., 2003, *Journal of Health Communication*, 8(S1), 129–144).

## §5 · EARC Classification & Gap Analysis

**Primary EARC category:** Empirical  
The claim makes a specific, falsifiable empirical assertion about the existence of an institutional mechanism. The claim is falsified by the total absence of any evidence for that mechanism and by explicit contradiction from a company directly named in the claim.

**Evidence gap (complete):** No primary source — no company announcement, no government document, no news report, no consumer testimony — supported the existence of the claimed state-notification-and-distribution mechanism. The evidence gap is complete; there is no partial corroboration requiring nuanced adjustment.

**Authority gap (addressed and closed):** Abbott/Similac's spokesperson explicitly denied the mechanism's existence, speaking on record to PolitiFact. Enfamil's own published guidance, while thematically adjacent to the claim, described a completely different set of assistance channels. Both direct (corporate spokesperson denial) and indirect (absence from company communications) authority evidence contradicts the claim.

**Rhetorical gap (significant):** The claim deployed crisis-authentic framing — real shortage, real phone numbers, real institutional categories — to create a false impression of procedural availability. Its exhortative structure ("it doesn't hurt to call and ask") functioned rhetorically to lower the epistemological threshold for sharing: a reader uncertain about the claim's truth could rationalize sharing it as a low-cost prosocial action. This rhetorical architecture drives prosocial misinformation propagation in ways that purely extractive misinformation does not (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017, *Council of Europe Report on Information Disorder*).

**Contextual gap (material):** The claim circulated without any acknowledgment of the genuine government emergency measures being activated in real time — WIC waivers, Defense Production Act invocations, FDA emergency import authorizations. Sharers of the false claim often lacked this contextual knowledge themselves, further enabling the false claim to fill the information vacuum left by inadequate official communications.

## §6 · Verdict & Reading Guide

**Verdict:** FALSE. No mechanism existed by which calling Enfamil or Similac customer service lines would trigger a state-government notification and initiate a free formula shipment. This verdict is supported by: (a) explicit categorical denial from Abbott/Similac's corporate spokesperson; (b) complete absence of any program announcement from either manufacturer; (c) absence of any news coverage of such a program's existence; (d) independent cross-verification by PolitiFact; and (e) consistency with a prior version of the identical false claim debunked in March 2020.

**Confidence:** Very high. The claim posits a specific institutional mechanism. That mechanism does not exist. The falsification is achieved through absence of supporting evidence plus explicit contradictory authority denial.

**Legitimate resources available to US families during the 2022 shortage:**
- WIC emergency formula substitution waivers (contact state WIC office directly)
- FDA-authorized foreign formula imports (Nestlé NAN, Kendamil, others)
- Enfamil Store Locator (extended to 200-mile radius search)
- Pediatrician consultation for formula samples and guidance
- Operation Fly Formula airlifts (federal procurement, June 2022 onward)
- State emergency WIC program guidance hotlines
