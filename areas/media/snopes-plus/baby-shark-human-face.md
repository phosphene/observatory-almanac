# Is This an Authentic Photo of a Baby Shark With a 'Human Face'?


## 1. The Claim

In late February 2021, a photograph began circulating on social media platforms claiming to show a baby shark with a "human face" — a fetal shark removed from a pregnant mother caught in a trawl net by an Indonesian fisherman named Abdullah Nuren off the coast of Indonesia on February 20, 2021. The image quickly attracted enormous attention, being shared by major tabloid outlets including The Sun, New York Post, and Daily Mail, in addition to achieving broad organic social media spread. Users compared the small shark's face to the Michelin Man and to characters from children's media.

The backstory, reported first by Indian-language publication Viva, was specific: Nuren caught the mother shark in his trawl net and discovered three pups in her stomach, two resembling the mother and one displaying the unusual humanoid facial appearance. The face of the pup showed what appeared to be large, round, forward-facing eye structures and a flat, compressed facial region — differing substantially from the typical narrow, elongated cephalic profile of most shark species.

Snopes investigated the claim through Madison Dapcevich, who contacted the original publication Viva for species verification, attempted to reach Abdullah Nuren directly, and consulted two expert sources: Luiz Rocha (curator of fishes at the California Academy of Sciences) and Taylor Chapple (assistant professor of fisheries and wildlife at Oregon State University). Neither the source publication nor the fisherman responded. Rocha proposed that the condition visible in the photograph could be consistent with known shark deformity conditions, specifically synophthalmia (partial cyclopia). Chapple noted that species verification was impossible from the video available and that independent authentication of the image was not achievable.

Snopes rated the claim as "Unproven" — an epistemically appropriate verdict — meaning that while the existence of such a shark is plausible and consistent with known biological phenomena, the specific image and its reported provenance could not be independently verified.


## 2. What's Actually True

The "Unproven" verdict is the correct categorical outcome for this claim. The reasons are structural and deserve careful articulation, because "Unproven" in this context carries different epistemic weight than it does in, say, a political claim with clear falsifiability conditions.

What is factually confirmed without controversy is the following: shark fetal deformities exist and are documented. Snopes correctly notes that malformations of shark species are not "entirely unheard of," citing a 2011 Gulf of California case in which a fisherman's pregnant dusky shark (*Carcharhinus obscurus*) was found to contain a one-eyed albino fetus — a case reported and photographed in National Geographic. The existence of this and similar documented cases establishes that the *class* of thing shown in the photograph (a deformed or malformed fetal shark) is real and has been confirmed by independent expert examination in prior cases.

What is *not* confirmed is the specific authenticity and provenance of the viral image itself. The photograph appeared on Newsflare, described as an aggregator of viral content that pays contributors — an arrangement that creates financial incentives for submitting dramatic online-worthy images without requiring independent verification. Viva, the Indonesian publication that first reported the story, did not respond to Snopes' requests for additional documentation. The fisherman could not be reached. No expert was able to physically examine the specimen; both Rocha and Chapple worked only from the photograph/video. No species identification was possible from the available imagery. Indonesia's waters contain at least 117 confirmed shark and ray species, making identification from a single poor-quality video of a fetal specimen essentially impossible.

The situation is therefore not "we have evidence this is fake" but rather "we cannot verify this is real on the evidence available." These are different epistemic states, and the Snopes "Unproven" category correctly captures the latter.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty: Shark Teratology, Synophthalmia, and the Biology of Elasmobranch Fetal Deformity

**Teratology in elasmobranchs.** Elasmobranchs (sharks and rays) were long considered poorly documented in terms of developmental abnormalities, in part because fetal specimens are rarely accessible — most uviviparous and viviparous elasmobranchs deliver live pups, and developmental anomalies that would be fatal before birth are generally only observable when a pregnant female is caught and examined. This limitation on observational access means that elasmobranch teratology has historically been a collection of anecdotal cases rather than a systematic literature.

This is changing. The peer-reviewed literature on shark and ray deformities has grown substantially as commercial fishing operations incidentally collect more specimens and as researchers explicitly seek out tissue from bycatch. Two studies cited in the Snopes article as expert-identified by Rocha are relevant here, though the Snopes article does not name them explicitly: Toscano, X., & Tomás, J. (2014). Skeletal abnormalities in small-spotted catshark (*Scyliorhinus canicula*) in the western Mediterranean sea. *Acta Adriatica*, 55(1), 3–14, and a study on abnormalities in blue shark and school shark from the Canary Islands. Together, they establish that cranial, vertebral, and developmental anomalies occur across elasmobranch taxa at documented rates, with frequencies varying by species and geographic region.

**Synophthalmia as a specific diagnosis.** Rocha's proposed diagnosis of synophthalmia — partial cyclopia, meaning the fusion or near-fusion of two eye orbits into a single midline structure — is a specific and diagnostically relevant suggestion. In vertebrate teratology, synophthalmia occurs when the optic vesicles, which normally evaginate bilaterally from the forebrain and migrate laterally during development, fail to separate fully, resulting in a single fused or partially fused eye in a midline position. The condition has been documented across a wide range of vertebrate classes including fish (Magtoon, W., & Arbhabhirama, A., 1995, "Eye abnormalities associated with cyclopia in catfish embryos treated with ethanol," *Aquatic Toxicology*, 31(2), 161–175) and is typically associated with disruption of the Sonic hedgehog (SHH) signaling pathway, which is responsible for midline specification across vertebrate body plans (Chiang, C., Litingtung, Y., Lee, E., Young, K.E., Corden, J.L., Westphal, H., & Beachy, P.A., 1996, "Cyclopia and defective axial patterning in mice lacking Sonic hedgehog gene function," *Nature*, 383(6599), 407–413).

The visual appearance of synophthalmia in a fetal shark would plausibly produce a face that strikes human observers as vaguely human-like: reduced bilateral eye spacing approaching midline convergence, resulting in a more anterior, "facing forward" visual impression rather than the widely lateralized eyes typical of sharks. This is precisely the characteristic most cited by commenters on the viral image as giving it a "human face" quality. The forward-facing eye positioning that humans immediately recognize as face-like (as opposed to the lateral eyes of prey species) is, ironically, produced by a fusion defect rather than any evolutionary convergence with primate facial structure.

Rocha's assessment that synophthalmia is "not something all that rare though" — and that social media amplification rather than actual rarity is driving the novelty perception — is an important empirical calibration. While hard prevalence data for synophthalmia specifically in Indonesian shark species is not available in the published literature, the general observation that developmental anomalies in elasmobranchs are likely under-detected due to limited fetal specimen access is supported by the trend toward increasing case documentation as bycatch research intensifies.

**The role of a one-eyed albino shark precedent.** The 2011 Gulf of California dusky shark case (reported by National Geographic; original research: Galván-Magaña, F., et al., 2011, "El primer tiburón albino cíclope reportado en el mundo: un caso extraordinario," *Ciencias Marinas*, 37(4), 657–663) is directly relevant as a precedential case for unusual fetal shark deformity. That shark exhibited cyclopia (a single eye) combined with albinism — a combination that had not previously been confirmed in an elasmobranch in a peer-reviewed context. Expert examination confirmed the specimen's authenticity because it was physically retrieved and examined in person by ichthyologists. The same was not possible for the 2021 Indonesian specimen.

**Shark reproductive biology and the three modes.** The Snopes article's discussion of oviparous, ovoviviparous, and viviparous reproduction in sharks is relevant to the authentication problem, though the article does not fully develop this relevance. The fact that Abdullah Nuren reported finding three pups in the mother shark's stomach is consistent with viviparous or ovoviviparous reproduction — both of which involve live development inside the mother. Oviparous sharks lay eggs externally. The claim that the shark was caught in a trawl net and cut open to reveal pups is therefore consistent with the biology of ovoviviparous or viviparous species, which does narrow the species identification space somewhat, though not to a single species given Indonesia's 117+ elasmobranch species.

The absence of species identification is the primary technical barrier to verification — different shark species have different developmental timelines, litter sizes, and geographic distributions, all of which would be relevant to assessing the plausibility of the reported circumstances. A shark species identification expert given the mother's morphological characteristics (unreported) and the water depth and location of capture (vaguely identified as "off the coast of Indonesia") could potentially confirm or disconfirm the basic biological plausibility of the captured scenario.


## 4. The EARC Assessment

**Empirical status.** Unverified but biologically plausible. The class of phenomenon depicted (a deformed fetal shark with cranial abnormalities consistent with synophthalmia) is confirmed to exist in the scientific literature. The specific photograph/video cannot be independently authenticated given the absence of specimen examination, species identification, and chain-of-custody documentation.

**Adequacy.** The Snopes verdict is correctly chosen and well-reasoned within its scope. The article correctly identifies the verification gap, accurately characterizes the expert assessments of Rocha and Chapple, and calibrates appropriately to "Unproven" rather than "False" (which would require evidence of fabrication) or "True" (which would require positive verification). The epistemological distinction between "unverifiable" and "false" is correctly maintained.

The gap in the article is the lack of depth on what Rocha's proposed diagnosis of synophthalmia actually means — what the developmental biology of the condition is, why it produces the specific visual appearance that struck observers as "human," and what the broader elasmobranch teratology literature implies about prevalence. The article also does not fully explain *why* viral content aggregators like Newsflare represent a specific evidentiary problem for image authentication, though this is implied.

**Reanalysis required.** None of the factual verdict. "Unproven" is the correct categorical assignment.

**Contestation.** None on the verdict itself. The experts consulted reached consistent conclusions: the phenomenon is biologically real, the image cannot be independently verified, and the claim is therefore appropriately "Unproven."

**Gap assigned:** Verification gap. The Unproven verdict is correct and the Snopes treatment is epistemically careful. The gap is insufficient explanation of the specific deformity mechanism proposed by experts (synophthalmia and its SHH pathway origins), inadequate contextualizing of the broader elasmobranch teratology literature, and underexplored discussion of what authentication pathways exist in principle even when media coverage arrives without specimen access.


## 5. The Broader Stakes

The baby-shark-with-human-face story is, at one level, exactly the kind of viral content that makes professional fact-checking challenging and important: an image that activates pareidolia (humans' tendency to see faces in non-face objects), combines primal elements (infant, predator, confusion of categories), and circulates through a monetized viral aggregation ecosystem with weak or absent verification standards. The Newsflare model — paying contributors for viral content without independent vetting — is a structural feature of the online attention economy that systematically advantages sensational over accurate content.

But there is a deeper issue worth naming. The image was compelling in part because the phenomenon it depicted is *real* — deformed fetal sharks exist, synophthalmia has been documented in fish and other vertebrates, and forward-facing eye structures do produce a face-like appearance that registers immediately to human observers. The proximity of the image to biological reality is precisely what makes it resistant to clean "False" classification and appropriate for "Unproven."

This raises a broader epistemological point about visual claims from poorly documented regions and sources. Indonesia's marine biodiversity is extraordinary and significantly understudied by Western scientific institutions. The 117+ elasmobranch species identified in Indonesian waters in the 2020 government survey are not uniformly well-characterized in terms of their developmental biology, deformity rates, or geographic distribution. The barrier to verification in this case is not skepticism about whether unusual things happen in Indonesian waters — they do, constantly — but the structural absence of institutional infrastructure that would have recorded, preserved, examined, and documented the specimen.

The verification gap reflects, in other words, not only an individual fisherman's failure to document his catch scientifically (not a reasonable expectation) but the broader absence of rapid-response mechanisms for connecting unusual wildlife incidents in biodiversity-rich developing-world settings with expert verification capacity. Several tropical nations with extraordinary marine biodiversity have regional marine research stations with fish diversity expertise, but the chain from viral social media content to expert specimen examination is not currently reliable or fast.

This has implications for how the "Unproven" category should be understood when applied to claims originating from regions with limited scientific institutional infrastructure. The verification barrier is not equal across all claims. Claims originating from well-documented ecosystems with abundant local expertise are more likely to receive definitive verification (or refutation) than claims from regions where institutional capacity is limited. The unverifiability of the Indonesian shark photograph reflects this asymmetry as much as it reflects any particular feature of the claim itself.


## 6. What This Entry Adds

This Truth Vault entry affirms the Snopes "Unproven" verdict — the image cannot be independently verified, and the epistemological conditions for a "True" rating were not met — while adding three dimensions of analysis:

First, a substantive account of synophthalmia as the specific deformity diagnosis proposed by Luiz Rocha, grounded in the developmental biology of the SHH signaling pathway and explaining why synophthalmia would produce precisely the "human face" impression that observers noted — not through any evolutionary convergence, but through the midline fusion that makes forward-facing eye positioning visually salient to human observers primed by face-detection systems.

Second, a contextualization of elasmobranch teratology as an emerging rather than mature literature — one that systematically under-detects fetal deformities because of the access barriers inherent in live-bearing reproduction — and an explanation of why the 2011 Gulf of California cyclopic albino shark case provides relevant precedent that was appropriately distinguishable by expert examination in person.

Third, a structural analysis of why "Unproven" in this case is not merely a placeholder pending eventual verification but a reflection of durable asymmetries in scientific institutional capacity across regions of high marine biodiversity, with implications for how internet-circulated wildlife claims from under-documented ecosystems should be evaluated by fact-checkers and by readers.

The verdict is Unproven. That is not the same as False. The biology is plausible, the experts split between "consistent with known conditions" and "cannot be verified from this footage" — and that split is itself instructive about the limits and structures of verification in the contemporary viral media environment.
