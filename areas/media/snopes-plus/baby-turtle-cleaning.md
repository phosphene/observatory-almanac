# A baby turtle 'enjoys a cleaning' in this viral video


## 1. The Claim

A video first documented in online circulation in early 2022 (Reddit, r/aww, February 2022, posted by user xSoulsaber as "Tiny Turtle Getting Cleaned") and spreading across TikTok, Imgur, and X (formerly Twitter) through 2023, shows a turtle approximately the size of a coin being scrubbed along its shell and limbs with a toothbrush. The turtle's limbs are extended outward and appear to quiver or shake. Multiple posts and captions described the turtle as "enjoying" the cleaning experience, with the most widely shared version on X (posted by the account @fasc1nate on 15 August 2023) stating "A baby turtle enjoys a cleaning" and accumulating tens of millions of views. A TikTok version by the account @hayleybrown371 captioned the footage "if turtles could giggle," implying visible joy.

Snopes published a fact-check on 25 August 2023, rating the claim a "Mixture." The video is authentic — not digitally manipulated — and correctly depicts a juvenile turtle. But the central claim, that the turtle is enjoying the experience, is false. An expert from the Turtle Survival Alliance identified the behaviour as a stress response.

This entry examines the biology of turtle stress responses, explains why reptile behavioural misreading is a systematic problem in viral content, explores the specific species identification, and situates this case within the broader scientific literature on anthropomorphism and animal welfare communication.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The video is authentic.** Snopes conducted a reverse image search and found the video had been circulating online since at least early 2022, ruling out AI generation or recent digital fabrication. The video depicts a real juvenile turtle being physically handled and scrubbed with a toothbrush. No evidence of digital manipulation was found.

**Species identification.** Jordan Gray, executive coordinator for the Turtle Survival Alliance — a task force of the International Union for Conservation of Nature's Tortoise and Freshwater Turtle Specialist Group (IUCN-TFTSG) — identified the species in the video as a juvenile loggerhead musk turtle (*Sternotherus minor*). The loggerhead musk turtle is native to the southeastern United States, found in river systems from central Georgia through northern Florida and into adjacent states. According to species profiles maintained by the Savannah River Ecology Laboratory at the University of Georgia, adult loggerhead musk turtles grow to approximately 4.5 inches (11.4 cm) in carapace length. The individual in the video is substantially smaller, indicating a juvenile or very young animal. The species is named for its notably powerful jaw, adapted for crushing molluscs and other hard-shelled invertebrates; it is not a species particularly well-suited to frequent handling by humans.

**The stress response identification.** Gray told Snopes directly that the turtle shown "is not enjoying the activity. [It] views the toothbrush as a threat and is exhibiting a stress response." The limb extension and apparent trembling visible in the video are consistent with a defensive or stress response in reptiles rather than indicators of pleasure or relaxation. The visual appearance that many viewers interpret as happiness — limbs extended, slight trembling, passive posture — actually reflects the opposite.

**Why this is not obvious from the video.** The visual presentation of a stressed small turtle and a happy small turtle are not distinguished by signals that are intuitively legible to human viewers. Humans read emotional states primarily from facial expressions, particularly in primates, and from gross body posture in familiar animals like dogs and cats. Reptiles have different neurological architectures, dramatically different facial musculature (or no equivalent facial musculature at all), and behavioural repertoires shaped by evolutionary pressures specifically lacking in the mammalian context. A turtle's "smile" is an anatomical feature, not an expression of emotion.

The trembling of limbs during stress in small reptiles is a documented physiological response to the activation of the sympathetic nervous system under threat perception. Animals that cannot easily escape — which a small turtle held in human hands cannot — may enter a state of physiological arousal that produces trembling, rigid limb extension, or other motor responses that are not volitional in any meaningful sense and are not indicators of positive affect.


## 3. Why People Believe This (and Why It Matters)

**Anthropomorphism as the default interpretive frame.** The misreading of this video is not a one-off error but a systematic product of anthropomorphism — the attribution of human psychology, emotional states, and intentions to non-human animals. Anthropomorphism is not simply a cognitive error; it reflects a genuine epistemological challenge. Humans evolved to model other minds as mental processes resembling their own, because the most common "other minds" one needed to model were other humans. Extending that framework to animals is cognitively natural and often useful for animals with nervous systems sufficiently similar to ours — primates, many mammals — but becomes systematically misleading when applied to animals with profoundly different neural architectures.

Reptiles such as turtles have brains that are structurally and functionally substantially different from mammalian brains. The limbic system, which is closely associated with emotional processing in mammals, is less developed and differently organised in reptiles. This does not mean reptiles have no affective states, but it does mean that mapping mammalian emotional interpretations directly onto reptile behaviour is unreliable. Research on reptile cognition, while still an active and rapidly developing field, generally supports a picture of simpler motivational states (approach/avoidance, threat/non-threat) rather than the rich affective valence space implied by descriptions like "enjoying" or "giggling."

**Cute animal content and anthropomorphic framing.** The viral content ecosystem that produces and amplifies animal videos has a strong structural bias toward anthropomorphic captions. Accounts specialising in "cute animal content" cultivate emotional engagement precisely by framing animal behaviour in emotionally resonant human terms. The @fasc1nate account, which provided the viral X post, focuses on "fascinating" content optimised for engagement rather than accuracy. The caption "A baby turtle enjoys a cleaning" has a clear emotional architecture: the baby (small, vulnerable) is receiving care (cleaning) and experiencing positive affect (enjoying). This is a human parental care frame projected onto a non-mammalian, non-social animal that has no evolutionary relationship to parental care of this kind.

The TikTok version's caption "if turtles could giggle" is even more revealing: it acknowledges implicitly that turtles do not giggle while using the conditional to project giggling nonetheless. This is a kind of culturally sanctioned benign misrepresentation — the user knows turtles don't giggle but uses the frame playfully. But for many viewers, the conditional frame disappears and the giggling-equivalent interpretation persists.

**Epistemic novelty: the welfare dimension of anthropomorphic misreading.** The significance of this case extends beyond mere factual accuracy. If millions of viewers interpret stress responses in small reptiles as expressions of enjoyment, the potential downstream consequence is normalisation of handling practices that cause distress. The loggerhead musk turtle is a small, relatively fragile wild-caught or captive animal. Repeated handling by humans is a documented stressor for reptiles and can have physiological consequences including elevated corticosterone levels, suppressed immunity, and reduced feeding behaviour. Content that frames this handling as mutually pleasant reduces the perceived welfare cost of the practice and may encourage similar interactions.

The welfare science of reptile stress is an active field. Studies measuring corticosterone (the primary reptile stress hormone, analogous to mammalian cortisol) in response to handling, transport, and environmental disturbance have documented measurable stress responses in a variety of chelonian (turtle and tortoise) species. The visual signature of these responses — trembling, limb extension, passive posture — is precisely what viral cute-turtle content captures and misidentifies as positive affect. The gap between what millions of viewers see (a happy turtle) and what the animal may be experiencing (acute physiological stress) is an animal welfare gap created and sustained by anthropomorphic misreading.


## 4. What the Evidence Actually Shows

The evidence from the Snopes investigation and the species expert consultation shows:

- The video is authentic and has been in online circulation since at least early 2022.
- The animal depicted is a juvenile loggerhead musk turtle (*Sternotherus minor*), a North American freshwater species.
- The animal's behavioural presentation — limb extension, apparent trembling, passive posture — is consistent with a stress response to handling and the perceived threat of the toothbrush.
- The expert consulted (Jordan Gray, Turtle Survival Alliance) clearly identified the behaviour as a stress response, not a positive affect expression.
- The claim that the turtle is "enjoying" the cleaning is false; the claim that the video shows a genuine juvenile turtle is true. Hence Snopes's "Mixture" rating is accurately calibrated.

No counter-evidence was presented suggesting the animal might be experiencing positive affect, and the expert testimony is explicit.


## 5. Viral Anatomy: How the Story Spread

The video appears to have originated on TikTok circa early 2022, based on the earliest traceable post (@hayleybrown371, March 2022) and its appearance on Reddit at approximately the same time. The Reddit post attracted engagement framed in "aww" terms (cute animal content) without the more explicitly false "enjoying" claim. As the video propagated through social media networks over the following year and a half, captions evolved to become progressively more anthropomorphic and positively framed.

The viral peak came with the @fasc1nate post on X in August 2023, which introduced the specific "enjoys a cleaning" framing and benefited from the account's large audience. By the time the Snopes fact-check appeared ten days later, the video had accumulated tens of millions of views. The fact-check reached a tiny fraction of that audience.

This trajectory is characteristic of cute-animal misinformation: the initial content is not maliciously false, the captions evolve toward more emotionally satisfying framings through optimisation pressure (captions framing animals as happy outperform ones that don't, in engagement terms), and by the time expert correction arrives the content has saturated its target audience. The correction loop is structurally disadvantaged against the initial viral spread.


## 6. The EARC Assessment and Research Gaps

**EARC mode: C (Claim confirmed as mixture; expert testimony decisive).** The central false claim (the turtle is enjoying the cleaning) is rebutted by direct expert testimony from a domain-relevant specialist (Turtle Survival Alliance). The central true claim (the video depicts a real juvenile turtle) is confirmed. The Snopes "Mixture" rating is appropriate and well-supported.

**Gap category: Anthropomorphism debunked.** The gap in existing coverage is the absence of broader context around why anthropomorphic misreading of reptile behaviour is systematically likely, what the animal welfare implications are, and what the specific physiological basis of stress responses in chelonians looks like. The Snopes article correctly debunks the claim but does not extend to the wider educational territory that this case makes accessible.

**Key research gaps.** (1) Physiological stress response studies specifically in *Sternotherus minor*: while handling stress has been documented in chelonians generally, species-specific corticosterone response data for loggerhead musk turtles under handling conditions would provide direct evidence for the welfare claim. (2) The epidemiology of anthropomorphic misreading in reptile viral content: is the cute-turtle genre systematically associated with stress-response behaviours (trembling, extension) rather than neutral or approach behaviours? (3) The welfare consequences of video-modelled handling practices: does viral cute-animal content influence owner and handler behaviour toward captive reptiles in measurable ways? (4) Reptile affective neuroscience: what is the current state of knowledge about affective valence in chelonians — can they experience positive states analogous to mammalian pleasure, and if so what are the behavioural or physiological correlates?
