# The Badlands National Park Twitter account went rogue and posted climate change facts after Trump took office


## 1. The Claim

On January 24, 2017 — four days after Donald Trump's inauguration as the forty-fifth President of the United States — the official Twitter account of Badlands National Park, a unit of the National Park Service operated by the US Department of the Interior, began posting a series of tweets presenting factual information about carbon dioxide concentrations in Earth's atmosphere and their relationship to climate change. The tweets cited specific measurements and stated established scientific conclusions without commentary, advocacy, or political framing.

The claim that circulated most widely was that the Badlands account "went rogue": that a park ranger or staff member, acting in implicit or explicit defiance of the incoming administration's posture toward climate science, used the official government account to broadcast scientific facts that the new administration's representatives had signalled a desire to suppress or contest. The tweets were deleted within hours of posting, which reinforced the interpretation that someone in authority had ordered their removal and that the original posts had been unauthorised. The episode was covered by major national and international news organisations, received millions of social media engagements, and became a symbol of what was subsequently called "the resistance" within the federal bureaucracy.

The core claim — that the Badlands account posted climate facts and those facts were subsequently deleted — is factually accurate. The episode happened, is documented, and is not disputed by any of the parties involved. The interpretive questions that give the story its cultural weight — why the posts appeared, who authorised or unauthorised them, whether they were a planned act of political resistance or a posting-as-usual routine that happened at a charged moment — are more complex and received varying accounts in different reporting.

Understanding the episode in full requires examining the documented facts, the political context that gave them meaning, and the structural questions about federal agency social media governance that the episode brought into sharp focus.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The tweets.** The Badlands National Park Twitter account (@BadlandsNPS) posted at minimum three climate-related tweets on January 24, 2017. The specific content included statements such as: "Today, the amount of carbon dioxide in the atmosphere is higher than at any time in the last 650,000 years"; "Burning of fossil fuels has increased atmospheric concentrations of CO2 by more than a third since the Industrial Revolution"; and figures referencing temperature anomalies. The language used was consistent with standard scientific communications produced by NOAA (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration) and the USGS (United States Geological Survey) for public outreach, and the information itself was not novel or controversial within scientific literature — it reflected well-established findings documented in sources including the IPCC's Fifth Assessment Report (Stocker, T.F., et al., eds., 2013, *Climate Change 2013: The Physical Science Basis*, Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change) and in NASA and NOAA annual climate assessments.

Screenshots were taken by several Twitter users within minutes of the posts appearing, so the content was archived even before the tweets were deleted. This preserved a full documentary record despite the subsequent removal. The National Park Service confirmed that the tweets were posted and then deleted; the agency's public affairs response at the time described the posting as having occurred "before the current restrictions on social media were lifted."

**The political context.** The Badlands tweets appeared during an extraordinary week for federal government communications. Shortly after Trump's inauguration on January 20, 2017, a series of changes to federal agency digital communications occurred that were widely reported and documented. The White House website's climate change pages were removed. The Environmental Protection Agency's website removed references to climate science and specific climate programmes. Reports circulated that employees at the EPA, USDA, and other agencies had been ordered to pause external communications — social media posts, press releases, public communications — pending review of messaging alignment with administration priorities. The specific origin and authority of these directives was disputed and varied by agency, but their general existence was documented by multiple reporting outlets citing agency employees.

The Department of the Interior confirmed it had issued a temporary freeze on grantor-funded social media while a communications review occurred. Whether the Badlands tweets appeared before the freeze was communicated to park staff, or in defiance of a freeze already communicated, or as part of a posting schedule set up before the inauguration, was reported with varying accounts by different outlets. The Washington Post, The Guardian, and BuzzFeed News all covered the episode in real time with slightly different reported interpretations of how the posts were authorised.

**Who posted them.** The precise identity of the individual who scheduled or published the Badlands tweets was not made formally public by the National Park Service. Reports citing park staff and former staff attributed the tweets variously to a former employee who had remaining scheduling access and was posting as a quasi-independent act, to current park staff following a pre-established content calendar, or to staff acting in deliberate awareness of the political tension. The most widely credited post-hoc account — based on reporting by Earther and by The Atlantic — was that a former employee who no longer had formal employment with the park had retained social media access and posted the tweets as a deliberate act, and that the posts were removed once the relevant supervisor or NPS communications authority became aware. This account is not definitively confirmed in official records and should be treated as the most plausible reported explanation rather than established fact.

**The climate facts themselves.** The specific scientific claims posted by the Badlands account were accurate and are not contested by any competent scientific authority. The statement that atmospheric CO₂ at the time of the tweets (~404 ppm) was higher than at any point in the past 650,000 years is supported by ice core records from Antarctica (Lüthi, D., Le Floch, M., Bereiter, B., et al., 2008, "High-resolution carbon dioxide concentration record 650,000–800,000 years before present," *Nature*, 453, 379–382) and from the EPICA Dome C ice core, which extended the record to 800,000 years. The statement about fossil fuel combustion increasing atmospheric CO₂ by more than a third since the Industrial Revolution is consistent with NOAA atmospheric monitoring data (Tans, P., & Keeling, R., 2017, Mauna Loa CO₂ annual mean data, NOAA/ESRL). The scientific accuracy of the posts is not the contested element of the episode.

**Subsequent federal agency "rogue" accounts.** The Badlands episode was followed within days by a series of unofficial or semi-autonomous Twitter accounts created by or attributed to current or former federal employees to disseminate climate and environmental science data independently of administration-controlled channels. These included @RogueNASA, @AltEPA, @AltNatParkService, @altUSFWS (Fish and Wildlife Service), and @ungaggedEPA, among others. Some of these accounts were operated by current government employees posting anonymously; others were operated by former employees or outside activists using the "Alt" prefix. The Badlands episode is generally credited with triggering this wave of "alt-agency" accounts, which collectively accumulated millions of followers within weeks.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The epistemic novelty: institutional speech as political act.** The Badlands episode reveals something genuinely novel about the governance of scientific communication in democratic polities where executive authority over federal agencies can change rapidly and where scientific findings are contestable political objects for some administrations. The standard legal and governance framework for federal agency communications assumes that the executive branch directs agency speech, that this direction is politically legitimate and within constitutional authority, and that the content of official communications reflects policy rather than independent scientific assessment. What happens when scientific findings that the agency is professionally obligated to report are also politically inconvenient to the administration directing that agency is a question that the Badlands episode made vivid in real time.

Zegart, A., & Morell, M. (2019, "Spies, Lies, and Algorithms: The History and Future of American Intelligence," Princeton University Press) and related work on intelligence and bureaucratic resistance have documented a broader phenomenon of what might be called "principled resistance" within federal agencies — where career civil servants resist or circumvent political direction they regard as inconsistent with their professional or legal obligations. The Badlands episode can be read as a specific instance of this broader phenomenon, though its character as posting publicly available, non-classified scientific consensus rather than leaking sensitive documents places it in a different moral category from many resistance narratives.

Braman, D. (2009 in the law review literature, and more accessibly Jasanoff, S., 2012, *Science and Public Reason*, Routledge) has examined the structural tension between scientific objectivity norms within government agencies and the political direction that executives are constitutionally authorised to impose. The Badlands episode exemplifies this tension: park rangers and NPS communications staff are trained to communicate established science accurately to the public; they operate within an agency whose mission explicitly includes scientific interpretation; and they simultaneously serve under a political hierarchy that may contest the political implications of that science. The question of who has governing authority over what a federal agency Twitter account says — and whether scientific consensus can be authorised by executive political direction to be omitted from official communications — was not resolved by the Badlands episode but was sharpened by it.

**The "going rogue" framing.** The specific cultural frame of "going rogue" — implying deliberate individual defiance of institutional authority — was the dominant media frame for the Badlands episode. This framing is plausible and may be accurate in the reported version involving a former employee with residual access, but it is not the only plausible interpretation of the documented facts. An alternative reading is that the posts represented routine science communication of a kind that NPS accounts regularly produced before the administration transition, that their appearance at this particular moment was a coincidence of posting schedule rather than deliberate defiance, and that the deletion reflected nervous communications managers over-correcting rather than genuine suppression of prohibited speech. The "going rogue" frame fits the political narrative better than this more prosaic alternative, which may partly explain its dominance.

**Why the deletion was itself significant.** The decision to delete the tweets — rather than simply not post further climate content — became itself a point of interpretation. Deleting scientific facts from a government account is not legally analogous to suppressing classified information, but symbolically, in a media environment primed to interpret any administration communications restriction as evidence of broader information suppression, the deletion confirmed the framing. The deletion acknowledged that someone in authority considered the posts problematic, which validated the interpretation that there was something to resist. Had the tweets simply been ignored and not deleted, the episode would likely have attracted less attention.

**The broader pattern of federal science communication restriction.** The Badlands episode was not isolated. Subsequent well-documented cases included the removal of climate data from EPA websites (Milman, O., 2017, "EPA removes climate change data from website," *The Guardian*, April 29); reports of USDA researchers being instructed to avoid using the phrase "climate change" in official communications (Domonoske, C., 2017, NPR reporting on USDA climate language guidance); and the broader administration stance toward the Paris Agreement withdrawal. These subsequent developments provided retrospective contextualisation that made the Badlands episode look prescient rather than coincidental. The pattern of evidence that emerged across 2017 supported a reading of the Badlands tweets as an early data point in a larger pattern of federal science communication restriction, which in turn reinforced the significance retrospectively attributed to the Badlands moment.


## 4. Verdict

**True — and the facts are not the interesting part.** The Badlands National Park Twitter account did post climate change facts in January 2017. Those facts were deleted. The episode happened as reported at its basic level of description. Snopes and other fact-checkers have rated the core claim true, and that rating is correct.

The more interesting critical evaluation is not whether the claim happened but what it means — and here the analysis requires more nuance. The "going rogue" frame is plausible but not the only plausible interpretation. The identity of the individual responsible was not publicly confirmed with certainty. The distinction between "deliberate act of political defiance" and "posting-as-usual at an inopportune political moment" matters for interpreting the episode, and the available evidence does not definitively establish which of these better describes the original intent.

What is clearly established is: the tweets were posted, they contained accurate scientific information, they appeared during a period of genuine tension about federal agency climate communications, they were deleted within hours, the deletion was confirmed by the agency, the episode received massive media coverage, and it precipitated a wave of "alt" agency accounts. All of these elements are documented. The moral and political significance attributed to the episode — whose interpretation varies greatly depending on political perspective — is built on this accurate factual foundation.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Badlands episode is part of a longer history of tension between political direction and scientific communication within the US federal government. The history of what critics have called "science suppression" or "politicisation of science" in US executive agencies has been documented across multiple administrations, though the scale and character varied considerably. Union of Concerned Scientists surveys of federal scientists documented patterns of political interference in scientific findings during the George W. Bush administration (Donaghy, T., & Wang, J., 2008, *Interference at the EPA: Science and Politics at the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency*, Union of Concerned Scientists). Similar documentation efforts under the Trump administration found significant evidence of interference in climate, environmental, and public health communications (Halpern, M., 2017, "Trump's Assault on Science," *Scientific American*, 317(4)).

The Badlands episode contributed to the development of what became known as the March for Science movement. The March for Science — held on April 22, 2017 (Earth Day), simultaneously in Washington D.C. and hundreds of other cities worldwide — explicitly invoked the threat to federal science communication as a motivating grievance. Event organisers cited the climate communications restrictions, the Badlands deletion, and the agency social media freezes as evidence that political interference with scientific communication had reached a threshold requiring public response. The march attracted hundreds of thousands of participants globally (January, C.T., & Bhatt, D.L., 2017, editorial in *Circulation: Cardiovascular Quality and Outcomes*, one of many scientific societies endorsing the march).

The governance question raised by the Badlands episode did not have a clean resolution. Federal agency social media accounts exist in an ambiguous position: they are official government communications channels, which means their content is subject to executive direction; but they are also professional scientific communication channels, which means their content is expected by their professional audiences to reflect scientific consensus rather than political preference. The tension between these two sets of expectations is structural, not incidental, and the Badlands episode made it briefly visible to a very large audience. Subsequent deliberations about federal science communication policy have not produced institutional changes that resolve this structural tension; it persists as a latent conflict that any politically charged administration transition can reactivate.

The episode also placed social media governance of government accounts in the spotlight. Federal agency social media policies varied widely by agency and were not systematically codified at the time. The Office of Science and Technology Policy, the General Services Administration, and individual agencies have published social media guidance documents, but these do not address the question of whether scientific consensus findings may be validly suppressed by political direction — a question that is both constitutional (touching on government speech doctrine) and administrative. Balkin, J.M., & Levinson, S. (2007, "The Processes of Constitutional Change: From Partisan Entrenchment to the National Surveillance State," *Fordham Law Review*, 75, 489) and related First Amendment scholarship on government speech provides limited direct guidance on the internal speech acts of agency employees, which exist in a different constitutional domain than external government speech directed at citizens.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated the Badlands climate tweets claim as true, and that rating holds. The factual record is documented and uncontested. The appropriate additional context fact-checkers should add — and that some did, though less consistently — involves three elements that simple true/false adjudication leaves on the table.

First, the uncertainty about the intentionality of the act: whether the tweets represented deliberate political defiance by a former employee with residual access, or routine posting that happened at a charged moment, or something in between, matters for understanding what actually happened even if it does not affect the true/false verdict on whether the tweets existed.

Second, the scientific accuracy of the posted claims: fact-checking treatments should affirm that the climate information in the tweets was accurate and drawn from mainstream scientific consensus. This contextual affirmation is important because part of the episode's political charge came from the implication that the administration's preferred posture toward federal climate communications was inconsistent with scientific evidence. Affirming the accuracy of the posted facts is part of evaluating the episode, not peripheral to it.

Third, the structural issue of executive authority over agency scientific communication: fact-checkers who engage with whether the deletions were "appropriate" or "inappropriate" need to reckon with the constitutional and administrative framework within which they occurred. The executive has broad authority to direct agency communications; whether that authority extends to omitting scientific consensus findings from official channels is a question on which constitutional lawyers, science policy experts, and public administration scholars have different views, and fact-checks that treat the deletion as unambiguously improper without engaging this complexity are themselves making a contested normative claim dressed as factual adjudication.

The Badlands episode is thus a productive case for media literacy instruction precisely because the simple factual verdict (true) is the beginning rather than the end of substantive engagement. The interesting questions — about institutional authority, scientific communication norms, political resistance, and the governance of government speech — are not resolved by the true verdict and require a richer analytical vocabulary than binary fact-checking typically provides.
