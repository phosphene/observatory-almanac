## The Claim

A photograph first published in September 2018 by Australian freediver and photographer Adam Stern has had an extraordinary viral afterlife. In October 2024, a version of the image shared on X (formerly Twitter) by an account called @JamesLucasIT accumulated more than 55.3 million views. Its caption read: "The terrifying beauty of the ocean... Sitting on the edge of an underwater cliff."

The image shows a person wearing a dive mask, seated on a white sand ledge at the edge of a dramatic oceanic drop-off, with vivid blue water plunging below and around them. The effect is visually vertiginous — the composition creates a clear impression of sitting at the edge of an enormously deep chasm, with the sandy shelf providing a precarious perch above what appears to be an abyss.

The claim embedded in the image's caption is twofold: that the photograph is authentic and shows what it purports to show; and that the setting is an "underwater cliff." Snopes reporter Madison Dapcevich investigated and confirmed the image's authenticity, while clarifying the minor geographical inaccuracy in its popular description. The setting is not a cliff — technically, it is a blue hole: an underwater sinkhole called Dean's Blue Hole in The Bahamas.

## What's Actually True

The photograph is authentic. It was taken by Adam Stern, a professional freediver who holds eight Australian freediving records, and first published on his Facebook page on September 29, 2018. Stern identified the man in the image as Michael Board, an international freediving competitor who has held multiple British freediving records. Board himself shared the image in a December 2018 Facebook post, where he noted that he had taken part in the "Vertical Blue" freediving competition — a prestigious, invite-only event held annually at Dean's Blue Hole in The Bahamas.

The location was identified by Stern in the comments section of his original Facebook post: Dean's Blue Hole, Long Island, The Bahamas. The image was picked up by outdoor sports media company Outside Inc., which shared it on Facebook on October 4, 2018, with the caption "A whole new meaning to living life on the edge." This early amplification by a major outdoor sports media outlet contributed to the image's widespread initial circulation, from which it has never entirely disappeared.

Dean's Blue Hole is, as the Bahamian tourism authority accurately states, the third deepest blue hole in the world, plunging to approximately 663 feet (202 metres). It is located off Long Island in the southern Bahamas (not to be confused with Long Island, New York). The hole is enclosed on three sides by a natural rock amphitheater, with the fourth side opening to a shallow turquoise lagoon and a white sand beach. The Vertical Blue free diving competition, which Board was participating in when the photograph was taken, has been held at Dean's Blue Hole annually since 2008 and is the most prestigious invitational freediving competition in the world, consistently drawing world record attempts.

Snopes contacted Adam Stern for additional comment and noted it would update the article upon receiving a response.

## The Science of Blue Holes: Underwater Sinkholes and Deep Marine Habitats

The epistemic heart of this story lies partly in the geography that makes the image so striking — the blue hole — which is a geologically and ecologically significant phenomenon that deserves more detailed treatment than its colloquial "underwater cliff" misidentification implies.

Blue holes are underwater sinkholes: vertically oriented cavities that penetrate through the seabed, often dropping hundreds of feet into the earth. They are formed by the same processes that produce terrestrial sinkholes — dissolution of soluble bedrock, typically limestone or dolomite, by acidic groundwater or seawater. The Bahamas archipelago is built almost entirely on carbonate rock platforms that formed over millions of years from the accumulation of shallow-water marine sediments (Neumann & Hearty, 1996, *Geology*). These carbonate platforms are exceptionally soluble, and the prolonged sea-level fluctuations of the Pleistocene epoch (the last ice age and its interglacials) subjected the platforms to alternating periods of subaerial dissolution during low sea stands and submarine dissolution during high sea stands.

Blue holes are therefore geologically ancient features: most formed during periods of lower sea level, when the areas now submerged were above the waterline, and rainwater percolating through the porous limestone gradually dissolved pathways to the aquifer below. When sea levels rose at the end of the last glacial maximum (approximately 10,000–12,000 years ago), these sinkholes flooded and became the dramatic underwater cavities we observe today (Dill, 1977, *Marine Geology*).

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) describes blue holes as ecosystems of exceptional diversity — "oases in an otherwise barren seafloor" — hosting corals, sponges, mollusks, sea turtles, sharks, and unusual communities of chemosynthetic bacteria in the deep, anoxic bottom layers (NOAA Office of Ocean Exploration and Research, 2020). The seawater chemistry within blue holes is highly unusual and stratified: the upper reaches are well-oxygenated and support standard reef communities, while the lower sections transition to an anoxic hydrogen-sulfide-rich environment that kills most aerobic organisms below a certain depth but provides conditions for specialised anaerobic microbial ecosystems. In some blue holes, the anoxic zone has preserved organic material for thousands of years, providing exceptional archives of past environments and sea-level history.

Dean's Blue Hole in particular has a distinctive geometry. Unlike many blue holes that are deep, circular shafts with near-vertical walls throughout, Dean's Blue Hole broadens significantly below the entrance, transitioning from approximately 25–35 metres wide at the surface to approximately 100 metres wide below 20 metres depth. This creates a chamber-like interior that contributes to its appeal for freedivers — the sensation of dropping into an expanding, luminous cave with walls retreating in all directions.

The scientific study of blue holes has historically been limited by the extreme difficulty of access. The opening of many blue holes is tens to hundreds of metres below the surface, precluding conventional SCUBA diving; and the bottom depths exceed the limits of breath-hold diving. Remotely operated vehicles have been used to survey some blue holes, but their narrow apertures often prevent entry by standard vehicles. NOAA's 2020 blue hole exploration mission noted that "the first reports of blue holes did not come from scientists or researchers, but actually came from fishermen and recreational divers" — a testament to the genuine frontier status of these environments.

Dean's Blue Hole is the freedivers' blue hole par excellence. Its depth of 202 metres makes it one of the deepest accessible single-breath-hold diving sites in the world. William Trubridge, the New Zealand freediver who holds numerous freediving world records, has used Dean's Blue Hole as his primary training and competition site since 2007. Many freediving world records have been set here, including Trubridge's 2016 unassisted no-fins dive to 102 metres. The vertical clarity of the water column, the stability of the site, and the protection offered by the rock amphitheater from wind and swell make it uniquely suited for the extreme depth dives that define competitive elite freediving.

## Why the Image Has Such Extraordinary Viral Longevity

The 55.3 million views accumulated by the October 2024 X posting of this six-year-old photograph demand explanation. The image's viral longevity is not accidental — it taps into a specific and very powerful set of human psychological responses.

**Vertiginous visual structure.** The composition is geometrically designed (whether intentionally or by the photographic angle) to maximise the impression of precarious elevation — or, rather, its underwater equivalent. Board is positioned at the edge of the sandy ledge, and the camera angle captures both the ledge from which he sits and the blue abyss dropping away below and around him. The visual processing of "sitting at the edge of a long drop" is not analytically mediated in the first pass — it is processed through the same neural circuits that produce reflexive fear responses to unprotected height. The fact that this is underwater, and that Board is a professional freediver for whom this depth is controlled and familiar, does not initially register. The visual cue for "dangerous precipice" fires before the contextual cues.

**The concept of psychrophobia and cold depths.** Thalassophobia — fear of deep, open, dark water — is among the most commonly reported specific phobias in surveys of unusual human fears, and is frequently cited by internet communities dedicated to documenting visually anxiety-inducing underwater imagery. The image operates squarely in this idiom: the deep blue of the hole's interior, the transition from light shallow water to darkness below, and the human figure perched at the boundary create a visual package perfectly calibrated for thalassophobic response. The image's repeated viral cycling through subreddits like r/megalophobia (fear of large things) and r/thalassophobia contextualises it within a specific aesthetic community that deliberately seeks out and shares such imagery.

**The comfort/danger paradox.** Part of the enduring fascination of the image is that it shows a human being in apparent comfort and relaxation at a location that the viewer's nervous system interprets as catastrophically dangerous. Board's seated posture — composed, unhurried, wearing a dive mask, apparently undisturbed by his situation — creates a cognitive dissonance between the viewer's autonomic fear response and the visible evidence that the person in the image is not experiencing distress. This paradox is fundamental to the appeal of extreme-sports media generally: the pleasure of vicarious proximity to mortal danger from a position of safety. The underwater ledge setting makes the paradox unusually stark.

**The accumulated caption lore.** Over six years of circulation, the image has accumulated a series of captions and framings that have shaped subsequent perception. The "sitting on the edge of an underwater cliff" description has become so closely associated with the image that it functions as a kind of built-in narrative — viewers who encounter it are already primed to read it as an image of extreme height, even in the absence of the caption. The slight inaccuracy (blue hole vs. cliff) has been absorbed into the image's cultural life and barely impedes engagement.

**The formal freediving context adds stakes.** For audiences who subsequently investigate the image and discover that the man in it is a professional freediver participating in a world-class competition, the stakes are recalibrated: this is not a random diver having an adventure but an athlete whose training involves routinely descending to depths of 50–100+ metres on a single breath. The revelation of this context does not reduce the image's power — it amplifies it by adding a layer of extraordinary human capacity to the already extraordinary geological setting.

## Freediving, Blue Holes, and the Ethics of Underwater Photography

The relationship between freediving culture, underwater photography, and social media virality is worth exploring as a phenomenon distinct from simple image authenticity questions. Adam Stern's professional identity is structured around producing underwater imagery of the kind exemplified by this photograph — imagery that compresses the extraordinary experience of deep, clear, oceanic environments into formats that can be shared with audiences who will never enter them.

The ethical dimensions of underwater photography at sites like Dean's Blue Hole are managed within the freediving community principally through the Vertical Blue competition's framework and through individual guide-led operations that bring paying visitors to the site for recreational freediving. Dean's Blue Hole is a popular tourist attraction accessible by boat from Long Island. The white sand shelf from which Board is photographed — visible at the mouth of the hole at depths of 5–10 metres — is the standard platform from which Vertical Blue competitions proceed and from which recreational visitors experience the site.

There are no marine protected area restrictions at Dean's Blue Hole that would preclude freediving or underwater photography at this location. The image was taken entirely within the normal parameters of the Vertical Blue competition in which Board was participating, by a photographer (Stern) whose professional practice centres on underwater documentation of freediving.

NOAA's characterisation of blue holes as environments whose distribution and abundance remain "unknown" points toward a genuine scientific frontier. While well-known blue holes in The Bahamas (Great Blue Hole, Andros Blue Hole, Dean's Blue Hole, and others), Belize (the Great Blue Hole), and elsewhere in the Caribbean have been documented, systematic mapping of global blue hole distribution has never been completed, and it is estimated that many significant blue holes remain uncharacterised by science — known to local fishermen but not formally studied. The image of Board at Dean's Blue Hole is, in this sense, an advertisement for an entire class of geological and ecological environments that remain scientific frontiers.

## Verdict

**True, with a precision note.** The photograph of a man sitting on the edge of what appears to be an underwater cliff is authentic. It was taken by Australian freediver and photographer Adam Stern in September 2018 at Dean's Blue Hole, Long Island, The Bahamas, and shows British freediver Michael Board seated on the sand ledge at the mouth of the blue hole during the Vertical Blue freediving competition. The image has been verified through multiple independent sources — Stern's original post, Board's own sharing of the image, the Vertical Blue competition records, and the geographic characteristics of Dean's Blue Hole.

The setting is not technically a cliff but a blue hole — an underwater sinkhole formed by carbonate rock dissolution during past sea-level low stands, which plunges vertically to 663 feet, the third deepest such structure in the world. This terminological distinction does not undermine the image's authenticity, but it does invite a richer understanding of what the image actually depicts: not simply a dramatic scenic composition but a world-record freediving venue, a geologically ancient structure, and a window onto one of the more extreme expressions of human aquatic capability.

The image's viral return 55+ million views in a single 2024 post — six years after its original publication — is a case study in the persistent power of imagery that combines extreme scale with extreme human composure, and in the internet's appetite for aesthetic vertigo.
