## §1 — Claim & Verdict Overview

The "Bambulance" legend — archived on Snopes under the slug `bambulance` and rated **Undetermined** — occupies an unusual position in the urban legend corpus: unlike most legends whose falsity is demonstrable or whose factual basis is clearly absent, this one has a disputed but not clearly fabricated evidentiary anchor in the form of an actual audio recording. The recording has circulated on traded cassette tapes since at least the 1970s and subsequently online, and multiple competing accounts exist for its origin. Snopes author **David Mikkelson** published and last updated this assessment (publication August 20, 2000; final update August 3, 2011) with a verdict of **Undetermined** — meaning the available evidence does not permit confident confirmation or denial that the call reflects a genuine incident.

The narrative itself is a cascade of improbabilities: an intoxicated driver hits a deer; believing it dead, he loads the carcass into his back seat; the deer (only stunned) revives inside the car and begins thrashing violently, biting the driver on the neck; the driver stops at a phone booth to call 911; a hostile stray dog attacks him during the call; the driver must dart in and out of the phone booth to fend off the dog, locate street signs, and communicate his position to the 911 dispatcher — all while explicitly requesting a "bambulance" (his inebriated pronunciation of "ambulance") and increasingly incoherent with panic and pain. The call transcript is a set piece of comedic desperation.

The **Undetermined** verdict is intellectually honest under the circumstances: the recording exists, multiple origin stories exist, external corroboration of the definitive "original" is absent, but so is definitive proof of hoax fabrication. This fact-check entry is among the most epistemically complex in Snopes's catalog.

## §2 — The Legend's Structure and Its Variants

**Dual Evidence Track: Audio and Narrative**

The Bambulance legend is distinctive in operating on two parallel evidential tracks. Most urban legends circulate as verbal narratives and lack any physical artifact. The Bambulance legend has an audio artifact — the recording itself — which both corroborates that some version of the call event occurred and complicates verification because multiple competing "original" recordings exist.

**Core Narrative Structure**

The narrative is structured as a **compounding disaster sequence**: each attempt to resolve one crisis introduces a new crisis. The driver:
1. Hits the deer (incident)
2. Loads the "dead" deer into the car (decision error)
3. The deer revives en route (complication 1)
4. The deer attacks the driver inside the car (complication 2)
5. The driver stops at a phone booth (resolution attempt)
6. A stray dog attacks during the call (complication 3)
7. The driver's intoxication progressively impairs communication (complication 4)
8. Each crisis reveals itself via the audio recording to the 911 dispatcher (documentation)

This compounding structure produces what narrative theorists describe as an **escalating stakes architecture**: no element of the disaster resolves before the next arrives. The intoxicated caller is simultaneously managing injury, animal threat, legal exposure (drunk driving), and the linguistic failure embedded in "bambulance."

**Variant Geography**

Mikkelson's analysis identifies multiple geographic variations of the call's origin: versions claiming Missouri, New York (Poughkeepsie), Connecticut, and Houston have all circulated at different times. Geographic specificity is a standard feature of legend credibility enhancement — people are more inclined to believe a story with a named city — but the migration of the "origin city" across the United States across different tellings is itself evidence of legendary status.

**The Tommy Boy Cinematic Version**

Snopes notes the 1995 film *Tommy Boy*, starring Chris Farley and David Spade, deploys an exact structural parallel: the protagonists run into a deer, load it into their car, the deer revives and destroys the interior. This cinematic embedding is significant: filmmakers use cultural material that audiences can be expected to recognize. The Bambulance structure (stunned deer loaded into vehicle, deer revives catastrophically) was sufficiently well-known by 1995 to function as an unannounced comedic reference to existing audience knowledge.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Evidential Paradox of the Contested Artifact

The Bambulance case presents a genuinely unusual **evidential paradox**: the existence of an audio recording typically increases our confidence that an event occurred, but in this case, the multiplicity of versions and the discordance between competing origin accounts makes the recording itself evidence of uncertainty rather than resolution.

This is an instance of what **Ragin, Charles C.** (*The Comparative Method*, University of California Press, 1987) would recognize as a **within-case evidence conflict**: multiple data points within the same case pointing in incompatible directions. The Poughkeepsie dispatcher Al Clouser claims the call was real in 1974; the 9-1-1 Magazine account (Larson, Randall D., "The Deer, the Dog, and the Bambulance," *9-1-1 Magazine*) claims it was a deliberate prank first pulled by Mickey Dawes in 1980 in Newburgh, New York, then repeated in Connecticut and Houston. These accounts cannot both be primary — and the 1974 Poughkeepsie claim is undermined by period-inconsistent internal evidence (the recording references "911" despite Poughkeepsie lacking 911 service in 1974).

The internal evidence anomaly is the strongest available epistemic wedge. **Carrier, Richard** (*Proving History: Bayes's Theorem and the Quest for the Historical Jesus*, Prometheus Books, 2012) identifies anachronistic internal details as among the most reliable negative evidence in historical source analysis. A recording that includes details inconsistent with its claimed time and place has been either fabricated, altered, or attributed to the wrong time and place. The 911 reference in the Poughkeepsie 1974 version is such a detail.

This produces a further epistemically interesting layer: even if the Poughkeepsie dispatcher's memory of receiving such a call is accurate, the recording he received may not be the "original" — someone may have produced a version of the call joke for the Poughkeepsie dispatch center that was itself a performance, not a documentation of a genuine incident. The relationship between the "event," the "original recording," and the circulating tapes is potentially a three-node rather than two-node chain.

**The Media Debunking Replication Problem**

Mikkelson notes a remarkable irony in the Reuters April 1996 investigation: a Reuters article explicitly debunking the falling cow legend (taxonomically different from the Bambulance legend but structurally analogous) was misread by subsequent distributors as a news account confirming the event. This "debunking converted to confirmation" dynamic is documented in the academic literature on correction effects. **Schwarz, Norbert, Lawrence Sanna, Ian Skurnik, and Craig Yoon** ("Metacognitive Experiences and the Intricacies of Setting People Straight: Implications for Debiasing and Public Information Campaigns," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 2007) demonstrate empirically that corrections can paradoxically strengthen belief in the corrected misinformation under specific conditions — a finding that the Reuters Bambulance-adjacent case illustrates in an early non-experimental form.

## §4 — Historical Provenance and the Evidence Chain

**Competing Origin Accounts**

Two primary origin accounts are documented in Snopes's sources:

**Account 1: Poughkeepsie, February 1974**  
Retired officer Al Clouser of the Poughkeepsie (New York) Police Department claims to reporter Elaine Viets (*St. Louis Post-Dispatch*, January 3, 1989) that he personally fielded the bambulance call in February 1974. However, Clouser's claim is undermined by the fact that Poughkeepsie lacked 911 service in 1974, yet the most widely circulated version of the recording references 911. This anomaly suggests that either Clouser's memory conflates a real dispatch call with the later circulating recording, or the circulating recording is not the Poughkeepsie call.

**Account 2: Houston, 1991**  
A 1999 article in *9-1-1 Magazine* by **Larson, Randall D.** identifies the most commonly circulated version as a 1991 prank call to Cypress Creek EMS (Houston area) by Mickey Dawes, a software representative testing a computer-aided dispatch system. Larson's account gives Dawes a prior history: a police officer in Newburgh, New York who first pulled the prank in 1980 with a fellow officer (calling it in to Poughkeepsie — which could explain Clouser's memory), then repeated it in Connecticut in 1982, and again in Houston in 1991.

**Reconciliation Attempt**

If Larson's account is accurate, the 1974 Poughkeepsie date is wrong (or refers to a completely different and genuine incident), and the 1980 Newburgh prank — called in to Poughkeepsie — created Clouser's memory of receiving the call. The recurring structure would be: Dawes creates the prank call (1980 Newburgh/Poughkeepsie), circulates it among dispatch communities as a joke; the Poughkeepsie dispatcher remembered receiving unusual calls; the Houston 1991 version became the widely circulated recording. This reconciliation is speculative; Mikkelson himself acknowledges the accounts are incompatible in key respects.

**Brunvand Documentation**

The legend is documented in two Brunvand volumes:
- **Brunvand, Jan Harold.** *The Baby Train*. W. W. Norton, 1993. ISBN 0-393-31208-9. (pp. 270–272)
- **Brunvand, Jan Harold.** *The Truth Never Stands in the Way of a Good Story*. University of Illinois Press, 2000. ISBN 0-252-02424-9. (pp. 76–83)

The later Brunvand treatment (2000) is more extensive, suggesting the legend continued to develop during the 1990s with the rise of internet distribution.

## §5 — Social and Psychological Function

**The Intoxicated Narrator as Comedy Instrument**

The Bambulance legend's comedy depends critically on the caller's intoxication as both an explanation for his predicament (he loaded the "dead" deer because his judgment was impaired) and as a communications impairment (his garbled speech generates the titular "bambulance"). This double-function of intoxication is narratively elegant: it provides diegetic coherence (why did he load the deer?) while furnishing the sonic comedy (distorted speech signals).

The portrayal of emergency services — the dispatcher's patient, professional responses to increasingly chaotic information — creates a structural contrast that amplifies the comedy through juxtaposition. **Oring, Elliott** (*Engaging Humor*, University of Illinois Press, 2003) identifies incongruity between register and content as a primary engine of humor: the dispatcher's calm official protocol grinding against the caller's escalating incoherence is this incongruity in near-perfect form.

**The Moral Ambiguity of the Deer Loading Decision**

Unlike most urban legend protagonists whose triggering error is unambiguously a violation of norms (the hostess who serves pet-sampled food, the driver who picks up a hitchhiker after dark), the bambulance caller's initial decision — loading a presumably dead deer rather than wasting it — has a pragmatic logic, particularly in rural cultures where deer meat is a valued resource. The decision only becomes error in retrospect; at the decision point, it was a reasonable response to apparent circumstances. This moral ambiguity is one reason the story functions as comedy rather than cautionary tale: we laugh with the caller rather than at a norm-violating villain.

**The Phone Booth as Temporal Anchor**

The narrative's setting in a phone booth is a significant temporal specificity: in the mobile phone era, the scenario (driver stops at a phone booth, locked inside against a dog, unable to relocate freely to identify landmarks) is structurally implausible. This temporal anchorage to the pre-mobile phone era both dates the legend's active circulation period and creates a growing anachronism gap that may be attenuating the story's transmission in contemporary oral networks.

## §6 — Research Gaps and EARC Assessment

**EARC Category: Empirical**

The primary research gap in the Bambulance case is **Empirical** — specifically, the question of whether the disputed origin accounts can be adjudicated through available physical evidence. The recording itself, if sufficient copies of both the Poughkeepsie-attributed and the Houston-attributed versions survive with adequate provenance documentation, could in theory be subjected to audio forensic analysis.

**Gap 1: Audio Forensics**

Modern audio forensics — including spectral analysis, microphone signature identification, room acoustics modeling, and voice biometrics — could potentially distinguish between multiple versions of the recording and establish which (if any) is the earlier tape. **Koenig, Bruce E. and Damir Lacey** ("Forensic Audio Examination of the Bambulance Tape" — hypothetical title, no such study exists) would be the paradigm for this work. No such analysis has been published.

**Gap 2: Dispatch Record Research**

Both the Poughkeepsie Police Department and the Cypress Creek EMS (Houston) presumably maintain dispatch records from the relevant periods. A public records investigation could either confirm or deny that any dispatch incident matching the Bambulance description was logged in February 1974 (Poughkeepsie) or 1991 (Houston). Neither the Snopes investigation nor the Brunvand documentation reports such a records check; all accounts rely on testimonial memory.

**Gap 3: Pre-Internet Tape Distribution Network**

The claim that the recording was circulating on traded cassette tapes since the 1970s implies the existence of an informal content distribution network among police dispatchers, CB radio enthusiasts, and similar communities. No study has systematically mapped this informal distribution network, which predates file-sharing infrastructure and internet distribution. Understanding this network would clarify the legend's transmission mechanism in its formative pre-digital phase.

**Gap 4: Differential Humor Processing**

The Bambulance call has been described by the Snopes article as having provided "amusement to thousands of listeners over the years." No study has examined whether the call functions differently for audiences with emergency dispatch professional experience vs. lay audiences, or whether awareness of the probable-prank status of the call modifies humor response. Research by **Martin, Rod A.** (*The Psychology of Humor: An Integrative Approach*, Elsevier Academic Press, 2007) suggests that context knowledge modulates humor appraisal; the Bambulance case would provide a natural experiment opportunity.

**Epistemic Confidence: Low (by design — Undetermined verdict)**. The competing origin accounts, the internal anachronism in the Poughkeepsie version, the multiple extant tape variants, and the absence of corroborating dispatch records collectively justify Snopes's appropriately cautious Undetermined verdict. This is unlikely to be resolvable without either archival dispatch records or forensic audio analysis of surviving tape versions.
