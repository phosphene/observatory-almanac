## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

Photographs of bananas displaying an unusual red or dark reddish-brown discoloration at their centers have circulated online for a decade with multiple layers of escalating alarm. The earliest iterations framed the images as evidence of purchase-time contamination, with captions suggesting the red discoloration might be blood and urging consumers to inspect produce before eating. Later iterations made the claim more explicit and more alarming: the red material was described as deliberate injections of HIV-infected blood, intended to spread the virus to consumers through produce consumption.

Snopes investigators Dan Evon and David Mikkelson (2015) assigned the claim the verdict **False**. The red discoloration visible in the photographs is not blood and has no connection to HIV or any other human pathogen. It is instead the characteristic symptom of one or more documented plant diseases: Nigrospora spp. fungal infection (which causes central browning to dark reddish coloration in the banana fruit); Moko disease (bacterial wilt caused by *Ralstonia solanacearum* Race 2, which causes vascular discoloration including reddish-brown staining in fruit tissue); and blood disease bacterium (BDB, caused by *Ralstonia syzygii* subsp. *celebesensis*), a related bacterial pathogen endemic to certain tropical growing regions. All of these are plant pathogens with no capacity to infect humans.

Furthermore, even in the hypothetical scenario in which HIV-infected material were injected into a banana, the biology of HIV transmission makes consumer infection through this route essentially impossible: HIV is highly labile outside the human body, inactivated by ambient air exposure, digestive enzymes, and stomach acid, and has no documented transmission pathway through food consumption under any conditions other than pre-chewed food fed to infants by an HIV-infected caregiver.

## §2 The Plant Pathology: What Actually Causes Red Banana Discoloration

The Canadian Food Inspection Agency's (CFIA) fact sheet "Red Fungus Inside Bananas" (2016, updated) provides the authoritative consumer-level description of the plant disease mechanisms underlying the photographs. The CFIA identifies four principal causes of red or dark discoloration in banana pulp:

**Nigrospora spp.** (Order: Pleosporales, Family: Pleosporaceae): A fungal pathogen affecting banana plants in tropical and subtropical growing regions, particularly where temperature and humidity conditions support fungal colonization of wounds or natural openings in the banana peel. Nigrospora infection causes the center of the banana to develop a dark reddish to brownish coloration that, when the banana is cut open longitudinally, can appear as a distinct red streak through the otherwise yellow pulp. The fungus colonizes the vascular tissue of the fruit and the red color reflects tissue degradation and the presence of pigmented fungal metabolites. Turner (1971, *Diseases of Banana, Abacá and Enset*, Commonwealth Mycological Institute) identified Nigrospora as among the most common post-harvest pathogens in bananas from the Americas and Southeast Asia.

**Moko disease** (*Ralstonia solanacearum* Race 2): A bacterial wilt disease of Musaceae (the banana family) and certain Heliconiaceae species, caused by a host-specific race of *Ralstonia solanacearum* that does not infect humans or animals. Moko is among the most economically damaging banana diseases globally, with particular prevalence in Central and South America and parts of Africa. Sequeira and Buddenhagen (1962, *Phytopathology*, vol. 52, no. 9) described the characteristic symptom progression: the bacterium colonizes the vascular tissue of the banana plant through wounds or insect vectors, and in ripening fruit causes internal discoloration ranging from pale yellow to dark brown-red, often visible when the fruit is cut across the center. Infected fruits may externally appear normal until quite advanced stages of infection, which explains why consumer discovery of the discoloration at the point of consumption is common.

**Blood Disease Bacterium (BDB)**: *Ralstonia syzygii* subsp. *celebesensis*, a close relative of the Moko disease pathogen, causes a clinically similar disease in bananas primarily in Southeast Asia (Indonesia in particular). Eden-Green (1994, *Musa Disease Global Review*, INIBAP) documented BDB as a distinct disease entity from Moko despite similar macroscopic symptoms, with the key distinguishing feature being its specific geographic distribution and the absence of plant-to-plant transmission via insect vectors common to Moko.

All three of these pathogens are plant-specific organisms that have been studied for decades in the phytopathological literature. None has any documented capacity to infect human hosts. *Ralstonia solanacearum* in particular has a narrow host range within the plant kingdom; while it is a highly damaging crop pathogen, it does not belong to any bacterial taxon with zoonotic or foodborne pathogen potential, and the human body's immune system, digestive environment, and cellular biology present no meaningful infection route for a soil-dwelling plant bacterium adapted to colonizing plant vascular tissue.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Claim Escalation Cycle and HIV Transmission Misconceptions as Narrative Drivers

The Ferrero Rocher case (see companion entry) illustrated supply-chain attribution error; this entry illustrates a different epistemic mechanism: *claim escalation cycles*, in which an initial modest claim is iteratively replaced by more alarming versions that share the same visual evidence but differ completely in the causal mechanism asserted.

The original version of the red banana claim was epistemically modest: "unusual discoloration—could be blood—consumers should check their produce." This is a reasonable precautionary consumer communication, even if mistaken in identifying the discoloration as blood. Its replacement by the HIV injection claim represents a categorical escalation: from an uncertain observation about produce appearance to a specific, named viral pathogen, a deliberate human actor (someone injecting the bananas), and a specific intended harm (HIV transmission). The visual evidence—photographs of red banana pulp—is identical across both versions of the claim. Only the interpretive framework changes.

This escalation cycle is driven, in part, by pre-existing HIV transmission misconceptions that are themselves documented in public health literature as persistent and consequential. Herek and Capitanio (1997, *Health Psychology*, vol. 16, no. 6) documented that a substantial proportion of the American adult public holds factually incorrect beliefs about HIV transmission routes, including beliefs that HIV can be transmitted via food, water, toilet seats, insect bites, and casual food-handling contact. These misconceptions are resistant to correction and are activated by ambiguous stimuli—such as an unexpected red substance inside a food product—that are consistent with the feared route of transmission in the observer's mental model.

The HIV injection narrative specifically conflates two separate categories of risk: the remote but not zero risk of needlestick injury with HIV-contaminated blood (a documented occupational hazard for medical workers, studied extensively by Cardo et al., 1997, *New England Journal of Medicine*, vol. 337, no. 21) with the biologically implausible route of oral consumption of HIV-contaminated material. These are mechanistically distinct. Needlestick transmission bypasses the skin barrier and delivers virus directly into the bloodstream at concentrations sufficient to establish infection; oral consumption of HIV-contaminated material exposes the virus to saliva (which contains HIV-inhibiting compounds including secretory leukocyte protease inhibitor; Shugars et al., 1996, *Journal of Infectious Diseases*, vol. 174, no. 6), stomach acid (which inactivates HIV within seconds at pH 1.5–3.5; McDougal et al., 1985, *Science*, vol. 227, no. 4683), and intestinal proteases, creating a transmission efficiency of effectively zero under normal circumstances.

The CDC's own guidance (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, HIV Transmission, updated 2021) explicitly states that HIV "does not live long outside the human body" and that the virus cannot be transmitted via foodstuffs with the single narrow exception of pre-chewed food fed by an infected caregiver to an infant—a route requiring direct oral transfer of saliva containing freshly expressed virus with an immunologically naïve pediatric gut.

The epistemic novelty: the claim escalation cycle that transformed "possibly blood" into "HIV injection" was successful precisely because of a specific, well-documented public health knowledge deficit. Understanding why the claim converted so readily reveals both the mechanism of the misinformation and the specific public health education intervention points that would make populations resistant to this class of false food scares: accurate, accessible information about HIV transmission biology.

## §4 The "Blood" Misidentification and Cognitive Disgust Mechanisms

The initial misidentification of the red plant disease symptom as blood is understandable from a perceptual psychology standpoint. The color of oxygenated human blood—a bright cherry red to crimson depending on concentration and age—overlaps substantially with the color range produced by Nigrospora infection and, in some cases, Moko disease tissue degradation. For a consumer with no knowledge of banana plant pathology (a reasonable prior for the general consumer), blood is the most culturally salient explanation for red material discovered unexpectedly inside a normally yellow food.

Disgust psychology, as studied by Rozin and colleagues (Rozin, Haidt, and McCauley, 1993, *Psychological Review*, vol. 100, no. 4, "Disgust"; Rozin and Fallon, 1987, *Psychological Review*, vol. 94, no. 1), has established that human disgust responses to potential food contamination are extremely sensitive and governed by several heuristics that do not require accurate knowledge of the contaminating material. The "law of contagion" in disgust psychology holds that brief contact between any object associated with a contaminant transforms the host object into something disgusting regardless of any physical residue. Applied to the banana case: once the possibility that the red substance is blood is introduced, the contamination frame persists even in observers who are subsequently told the substance is fungal in origin.

This persistence of the contamination frame is relevant to the claim escalation cycle: once "could be blood" is established as a frame of reference, subsequent re-tellings fill the frame with the most alarming content available. HIV is the culturally dominant blood-associated disease in western public health discourse, making it the natural candidate to fill the "dangerous blood contaminant" narrative slot. The accuracy of the underlying identification is irrelevant to this process; what matters is the activation of the contamination frame and the availability of a named, fear-associated pathogen.

## §5 Banana Plant Disease: Broader Context and Consumer Implications

Banana production is one of the most economically significant agricultural sectors in the developing world. The global banana trade, dominated by the Cavendish cultivar (*Musa acuminata* AAA group), faces a documented existential disease threat from Tropical Race 4 (TR4) of *Fusarium oxysporum* f.sp. *cubense*, a fungal pathogen currently spreading through banana-producing regions of Asia, Australia, the Middle East, and Africa and generating substantial agricultural research concern (Dita et al., 2018, *Frontiers in Plant Science*, vol. 9). Moko disease and BDB are separately documented threats to banana production in their respective regions of prevalence.

The consumer implications of Moko-infected bananas specifically are relevant to this claim. As noted by the CFIA (2016), the plant diseases causing red discoloration in bananas "are not a threat to human health." Even if a consumer were to eat a banana infected with *Ralstonia solanacearum* Race 2, the bacterium has no pathogenic mechanism in human hosts. This is consistent with the established principle that host-range-restricted plant pathogens, which have evolved in association specifically with plant cellular biology including plant cell wall components, plant-specific signaling molecules, and plant immune evasion mechanisms, do not possess the molecular machinery needed to colonize human tissue. Roberts et al. (2009, *Microbiology and Molecular Biology Reviews*, vol. 73, no. 4) reviewed plant pathogen effector proteins and confirmed no overlap with known human virulence factors for the *Ralstonia* clade.

The consumer-level advice given by the CFIA is appropriate and proportionate: "while unappealing to eat, these diseases affecting bananas are not a threat to human health; however, when in doubt, throw it out or compost them." This formulation acknowledges both the safety reality (no health risk) and the aesthetic reality (a reasonable consumer preference not to eat diseased fruit) while providing clear guidance.

## §6 Conclusion: Produce Safety, Biologically Implausible Claims, and the HIV Rumor Ecosystem

The false claim that red-discolored bananas are infected with HIV exemplifies a category of biological misinformation in which the actual phenomenon (plant disease causing unusual visual appearance) becomes the pretext for a completely fabricated harm narrative (deliberate HIV contamination). The two parts of the claim—visual evidence and causal mechanism—are entirely independent; the visual evidence neither supports nor requires the causal narrative, which is constructed from fear, pre-existing HIV transmission misconceptions, and the ambiguity of the initial "this looks like blood" misidentification.

The false/mixture family of claims about HIV in food products is well-documented in public health misinformation research. Simbayi et al. (2006, *AIDS Care*, vol. 18, no. 5) documented HIV-food transmission fears in South African communities, finding that widespread belief in food-based HIV transmission was associated with reduced HIV testing and care-seeking behavior—demonstrating a concrete public health harm from biologically implausible transmission myths.

For the specific banana claim, the harm is proportionate to the claim's viral reach: millions of exposures to a false association between a common fruit and a feared disease, combined with a false model of HIV transmission that inflates perceived risk of incidental contact and potentially displaces accurate risk assessment for actual transmission routes. Correcting this claim with the specific biology—plant disease, not blood; HIV cannot survive stomach acid; the CDC endorses this position explicitly—provides both the factual correction and the mechanistic explanation needed for durable knowledge change rather than mere skepticism of the specific claim.
