# Taking a bath during a lightning storm can kill you


## 1. The Claim

The claim arrives as a safety warning, typically passed down through families, embedded in public health messaging, or circulated on social media during storm season: you should not take a bath, shower, or otherwise engage with plumbing during a lightning storm because lightning can travel through water pipes and electrocute you. The claim is sometimes presented as an urban legend — something your grandmother told you that surely cannot be true in a modern house with modern plumbing — and sometimes as an urgent safety instruction.

The precise formulation varies. Some versions specify only metal pipes; others claim any contact with running water is dangerous; others extend the warning to include washing dishes, using the sink, or filling a swimming pool. Some versions specify that the danger only exists in older homes with metal plumbing, or that homes with plastic PVC pipes are safe. Some formulations note that the danger peaks during the storm itself, while others extend the warning period to thirty minutes or an hour after the last observed strike.

The warning has been transmitted through parents' safety guidance, school curricula, weather safety brochures, and government warning literature across at least the past several decades. The U.S. National Lightning Safety Institute (NLSI), the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) have all issued guidance addressing indoor lightning safety, including plumbing contact. The question worth examining is not whether the claim has institutional support — it does — but whether the underlying physics and case evidence are sufficient to justify the warning as it is most commonly stated.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The biophysics of lightning-conducted voltage in plumbing systems.** Lightning is an atmospheric electrical discharge with peak currents typically ranging from 10 to 30 kiloamperes (kA), though values above 100 kA have been recorded (Rakov & Uman, 2003, *Lightning: Physics and Effects*, Cambridge University Press). When a lightning bolt strikes the ground or a structure, it dissipates its energy through multiple paths simultaneously. One of those paths is the ground itself — current spreads outward from the strike point in what is known as ground potential rise or step potential, which can reach lethal voltages at distances of several metres from the strike point.

Metallic plumbing presents a low-resistance conductive path. If a lightning strike occurs to a structure connected to a municipal water supply or to a ground-connected well system, the metallic pipes function as conductors, and voltage can propagate along the pipe network. This phenomenon is well-documented in the electrical engineering literature. Kitagawa and Brook (1960) in the *Journal of Geophysical Research* were among the early researchers to document the propagation dynamics of lightning-induced currents along grounded structures; subsequent work by Tran, Rakov, and colleagues has elaborated the ground current distribution mechanics in considerable detail (Rakov et al., 2005, *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres*).

For a person immersed in bathwater — a low-resistance electrolyte medium — any voltage difference introduced into the tub water by a plumbing-conducted surge creates a path through the body. The human body's resistance varies considerably by contact type and moisture: dry skin presents approximately 100,000 ohms of resistance, while wet or submerged skin can drop to 1,000 ohms or less (Reilly, 1998, *Electrical Stimulation and Electropathology*, Cambridge University Press). Bathing dramatically reduces the body's normal protective resistance, meaning that voltages that would cause only localised effects through dry skin contact can produce lethal current flows when the body is submerged.

**Documented case reports.** The claim is not merely theoretical. A number of published case reports and lightning casualty analyses document injuries and fatalities to individuals using plumbing during lightning storms.

Cooper and Holle (2010) in *Wilderness and Environmental Medicine* — a foundational summary article on lightning injury epidemiology — note that indoor lightning casualties include documented cases involving plumbing contact, and provide case report summaries of plumbing-mediated injuries. Their review of lightning casualty data identifies indoor strike transmission through plumbing as a recognised injury mechanism distinct from direct strike, side flash, contact voltage, step potential (ground current), and conduction inside a vehicle.

The CDC's lightning fatality tracking data includes a case category for indoor lightning fatalities; the detailed review by Adekoya and Nolte (2005) in the *Annals of Emergency Medicine* identified 407 lightning fatalities in the United States over a nine-year period, including indoor fatalities where plumbing contact was implicated in a subset of cases. The authors note that indoor lightning fatalities are systematically underreported because coroners may classify cause of death as cardiac arrest or electrocution without identifying the lightning-mediated mechanism.

A specific well-documented case study published by Elsom (2015) in the *International Journal of Meteorology* describes a fatality in the United Kingdom involving a woman who died while bathing during a thunderstorm; post-incident analysis confirmed that a lightning strike to a nearby external structure had produced a ground current that propagated through the home's water supply connection. Elsom's analysis explicitly identifies the mechanism: plumbing as a conductive bridge between an externally dissipated lightning strike and a vulnerable grounded interior contact point.

**The plastic pipe question.** A commonly circulated qualification holds that homes with PVC (plastic) water supply pipes are safe from this risk because plastic does not conduct electricity. This is partially correct and partially misleading. Modern plumbing installations in many jurisdictions increasingly use CPVC or PEX plastic pipe for interior distribution, which would interrupt conductivity. However, several factors limit the protection offered by plastic interior pipes. First, the municipal water supply main and service connection — the pipe running from the street main to the house — may still be metallic, and a ground potential rise event originating at or near the meter would be conducted along the metallic portion before entering the house. Second, even plastic pipes filled with water present some conductivity, as tap water is not distilled: dissolved minerals and salts make municipal tap water a partial electrolyte. Third, many older homes still have metallic pipe systems throughout, and the proportion of the plumbing that is metallic determines the conductivity of the interior system.

NOAA's official indoor lightning safety guidance (National Weather Service, 2023) does not draw a distinction between metal-pipe and plastic-pipe homes, recommending that all individuals avoid indoor plumbing contact during thunderstorms. This conservative position reflects the uncertainty about individual home plumbing configurations, the partial conductivity of water itself, and the precautionary logic of safety messaging in high-consequence, low-frequency risk contexts.

**The geographical and temporal scope of the risk.** Cooper and Holle (2010) note that the plumbing injury risk is not uniform: it depends on proximity to ground-strike lightning, local soil conductivity, the distance between the strike point and the plumbing system, the configuration of the home's earthing and bonding, and the magnitude of the specific lightning event. In densely populated areas with underground utility infrastructure, the conduction pathways are more complex. In rural areas with private well systems, the plumbing-to-ground connection is often more direct and the risk may be higher for direct near-field strikes.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The belief in this case is correct, but the mechanisms by which it is transmitted — and the processes by which it is sometimes doubted — are worth examining.

The claim's transmission relies primarily on **parental authority heuristics**: the warning is typically communicated by parents or grandparents as one of a suite of household safety rules, alongside "don't run near the pool" and "don't touch the stove." Rosen and colleagues (2020) in *Risk Analysis* studied intergenerational transmission of household safety knowledge and found that safety rules transmitted through family authority channels are retained with high fidelity but also with low mechanistic understanding — children learn the rule but not the physics, which makes them vulnerable to scepticism when they encounter counter-arguments based on the missing mechanism.

The specific mechanism that makes this claim epistemically fragile is what might be called **indoor safety privilege bias**: the intuition that being inside a building provides protection from lightning. This intuition is broadly correct — indoor mortality from lightning is dramatically lower than outdoor — and the statistical truth that indoor lightning casualties are rare can lead individuals to believe that indoors equals safe. The leap from "substantially safer inside" to "fully safe inside from any lightning-related harm" is incorrect, and the plumbing conduction mechanism is one of several ways that indoor environments do not provide absolute protection. Grounding yourself by touching a telephone landline during a storm is another historically documented indoor lightning risk, and both the landline warning and the plumbing warning share the same explanatory logic: conductive infrastructure can import ground current into the interior.

The specific mechanism — **conductivity of water-filled metallic pipes** — is counterintuitive because the distances involved are large and the mechanism is invisible. A lightning strike several houses down the street producing a ground current that travels through water mains to your bathtub is not a scenario that naive physical intuition suggests. This is an instance of what Kahneman (2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*) calls a "base rate neglect" problem: the scenario does occur, but it is rare enough and remote enough that it does not feel viscerally plausible, which generates motivated scepticism in people who have not encountered the biophysical explanation.


## 4. Verdict

**Substantially True (EARC Mode A).** Taking a bath during a lightning storm carries a genuine elevated risk of electrocution or death from lightning-induced voltage conducted through plumbing systems. The mechanism is biophysically sound, is documented in the electrical engineering and lightning safety literature, is supported by case reports of injuries and fatalities, and is affirmed by national weather safety agencies in multiple countries (NOAA, the UK Met Office, and the German Meteorological Service all include plumbing avoidance in their indoor lightning safety guidance).

The risk is low in absolute terms: outdoor lightning fatalities are far more common, and the vast majority of people who have bathed during thunderstorms have not been harmed. But this is a function of the rarity of direct nearby strikes into grounded plumbing systems, not of the absence of the mechanism. The expected-value calculation favours avoiding non-essential bathing during active lightning activity. The claim, as safety advice, is correct.

The qualification that plastic (PVC/PEX) pipes reduce but do not eliminate the risk is accurate and worth communicating in full — but it does not undermine the general warning.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The lightning-bathing claim sits within a broader category of correct but counterintuitive safety warnings — claims that are true, can be explained with physics, and are endorsed by relevant scientific and governmental bodies, but that circulate in folk safety channels and therefore attract scepticism from people who associate folk safety with unfounded superstition.

The epistemic challenge here is the inverse of the typical Truth Vault problem: rather than debunking a false claim that sounds plausible, this entry must defend a true claim that sounds implausible. The risk is that media literacy campaigns focused on debunking misinformation may inadvertently create a sceptical bias against unusual-sounding true claims, particularly those with an institutional or governmental endorsement that is currently unfashionable to trust.

Ropeik (2010) in *Risk: A Practical Guide for Deciding What's Really Dangerous in a World That's Full of Alarming Possibilities* discusses how people systematically misestimate rare-but-real risks, particularly when the mechanism is invisible and the probability is low. Lightning safety generally is subject to miscalibration in both directions: people underestimate outdoor exposure risk (normalising short rain sprints) and overestimate indoor protection (treating all indoor settings as equally safe).

The public health implication is that the bathing warning is worth continuing to issue, but its effectiveness is improved when the mechanism is explained rather than just the prohibition stated. Understanding that metallic plumbing conducts voltage and that bath water lowers skin resistance makes the warning cognitively available in the moment of decision, rather than a detached rule that might be overridden by inconvenience. Explanatory safety messaging is consistently more behaviour-modifying than rule-only messaging across health communication contexts (Berry, Hochhauser, & Bergus, 2013, *Medical Decision Making*).

The thirty-minute post-storm wait is also scientifically grounded. Lightning can strike without immediately associated rainfall, and strike probability does not drop to zero the moment rain ceases. NOAA's guidance to "wait 30 minutes after the last thunder" before resuming outdoor or plumbing-adjacent activities reflects the statistical distribution of strike timing relative to observable storm indicators.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

While Snopes has not directly addressed this specific claim as a standalone entry (hence `snopes_url: not-addressed`), the claim falls into a category that Snopes has addressed adjacently: safety warnings about indoor lightning behaviour. Snopes entries on related lightning safety questions — such as whether you can be struck by lightning indoors, whether telephones are dangerous during storms, and whether lightning can enter through windows — have universally treated the institutional safety guidance as credible and the underlying physics as sound.

The claim would rate True or Mostly True under standard Snopes methodology. The only qualification that would generate a "Mostly" rather than full True would be the plastic-pipe condition: homes with complete PVC interior plumbing have a lower (though not zero) risk, and stating the claim without that qualifier is technically an imprecision. In practice, because most homes in North America and Europe have at least partial metallic plumbing connections to municipal supply, the unqualified warning remains appropriate for general audiences.

For fact-checkers, this claim illustrates the importance of distinguishing between folk safety wisdom that is false (don't mix bleach and ammonia will cause instant death — actually true but not instant) and folk safety wisdom that is genuine. The bathing-during-lightning warning has a full chain of institutional endorsement, biophysical plausibility, and documented incident evidence. It is not an urban legend; it is an accurate safety summary of a real low-probability, high-consequence risk.

The appropriate public communication is: avoid non-essential contact with plumbing during active thunderstorms; wait until the storm has passed; understand that the risk is not zero even indoors; and the inconvenience of a delayed bath is not comparable to the consequence of the injury mechanism operating in the small percentage of cases where it would otherwise activate.
