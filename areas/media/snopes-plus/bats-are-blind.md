# Bats Are Blind

## 1. The Claim

The phrase "blind as a bat" is one of the most durable comparative idioms in the English language. It implies total or near-total blindness—that bats navigate purely by sound and have no meaningful visual capacity. The claim appears in casual conversation, children's books, pop science articles, and even some science education materials. When people invoke the expression, they typically mean to describe someone stumbling around without visual guidance, drawing on the intuition that bats are creatures of pure echolocation, their eyes vestigial at best, useless at worst.

This belief is wrong. Not approximately wrong, not wrong in nuance only—straightforwardly incorrect as a factual description of bat visual biology.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

Every known species of bat can see. The order Chiroptera contains more than 1,400 described species, and not one of them is blind. Vision varies enormously across the clade—from the large, well-developed eyes of Old World fruit bats to the smaller but still functional eyes of microbats—but the claim of blindness applies to none of them.

**Megabats: vision as a primary sense**

The suborder Yinpterochiroptera includes the Old World fruit bats (family Pteropodidae), sometimes called flying foxes. These are among the largest bats in the world, with wingspans reaching 1.5 meters in *Pteropus vampyrus*. Fruit bats do not use echolocation at all (with the exception of one genus, *Rousettus*, which uses a primitive tongue-click system). Instead, they navigate and forage almost entirely through vision and smell.

Fruit bat eyes are large relative to body size and are structurally adapted for scotopic (low-light) vision. Their retinas are rich in rod photoreceptors, which operate well in dim conditions. Research by Suthers and Wallis (1970) on the Australian grey-headed flying fox (*Pteropus poliocephalus*) demonstrated that these animals have visual acuity comparable to many diurnal mammals when tested under appropriate light conditions. They can resolve fine spatial detail and show good contrast sensitivity. Far from being blind, fruit bats rely on their eyes the way a nocturnal primate does—adapted for darkness, not absent from the scene.

**Microbats: echolocation and vision as complementary systems**

The microchiroptera (now split across several phylogenetic groupings, but traditionally distinguished by their use of laryngeal echolocation) are the bats most people picture when they think of bats at all: small, fast-flying insectivores navigating in complete darkness. These are also the bats most likely to be described as "blind."

They are not blind either.

Microbat eyes are smaller, and some species have relatively reduced visual acuity compared to fruit bats. But "reduced" does not mean absent. Studies of visual system anatomy in microbats consistently find functional retinas, optic nerves, and visual cortex. Electroretinography studies—which measure the electrical response of the retina to light—show normal photoreceptor function across echolocating bat species tested.

More importantly, microbats use vision in ways echolocation cannot replace. Gorresen et al. (2015), studying Hawaiian hoary bats (*Lasiurus cinereus semotus*), demonstrated that the animals showed consistent behavioral responses to visual landmarks during navigation, even in situations where echolocation alone would have been sufficient. The bats were not ignoring visual information—they were integrating it.

Several converging lines of evidence support the conclusion that microbats use vision selectively and strategically:

- **Long-range navigation**: Echolocation is effective only over short distances, typically a few meters to tens of meters depending on frequency. Over the longer distances involved in migration or ranging to foraging sites, bats rely on celestial cues, magnetic sense, and visual landmarks. Blind animals could not use star positions or horizon features for orientation. Bats demonstrably do.

- **Predator detection**: Multiple studies have shown bats altering flight behavior in response to visual predator stimuli—owl silhouettes, for instance—at distances beyond the effective range of echolocation. Visual threat detection is functioning.

- **Roost selection and social signaling**: Within roosts, bats engage in visual communication—posture, wing display, facial expressions—that requires some degree of vision to perceive and produce adaptively.

**Color vision**

Bat color vision is limited but present. Most bat species studied have two types of cone photoreceptors (dichromacy), sensitive to ultraviolet and longer wavelengths. This is less chromatic richness than humans (who are trichromats) but more than zero. Some bat-pollinated flowers have evolved UV reflectance patterns thought to function as visual guides for their pollinators—an evolutionary partnership that implies functional bat color perception.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The misconception is not random. It tracks something real about bat behavior and biology, even though it overshoots the mark significantly.

**Echolocation is genuinely remarkable—and unusual**

Bats' biosonar system is one of the most sophisticated sensory apparatuses in the animal kingdom. Laryngeal echolocation in microbats involves emitting ultrasonic pulses—often above 20 kHz, beyond human hearing—and interpreting the returning echoes to construct a three-dimensional map of the local environment at millisecond resolution. Some species can discriminate objects separated by fractions of a millimeter, detect wing-beat frequencies of individual insects, and track multiple targets simultaneously while suppressing their own outgoing calls to hear returning echoes.

This is so impressive that it crowds out the complementary senses. When people learn that bats navigate in complete darkness through sound alone, the natural inference is that vision must be unnecessary—and if unnecessary, perhaps absent. The logic is plausible but wrong. Evolution rarely eliminates costly structures immediately; vision has many uses that echolocation cannot serve; and many bats live in environments where light is available for at least part of their activity cycle.

**The idiom came first**

The phrase "blind as a bat" predates any scientific investigation of bat visual biology. It appears in print by at least the early 19th century and likely circulated orally earlier. Idioms encode folk perceptions, and folk perceptions of bats in temperate Europe and North America were shaped by encounters with small, fast-flying insectivores appearing at dusk—animals that seemed to dart around obstacles with uncanny precision, apparently without using their eyes. The conclusion was reasonable from casual observation; it just turned out to be wrong.

Once an idiom enters common currency, it resists revision. People who encounter the scientific correction may accept it intellectually while continuing to use the phrase, because the phrase functions as a cultural artifact rather than a sincere factual claim.

**Conflation with other senses**

More broadly, the myth reflects a zero-sum model of sensory systems: if a creature is very good at sense X, it must not need sense Y, and therefore must not have it. This model is wrong across biology. Animals routinely maintain multiple overlapping sensory systems even when one is dominant. Humans have a well-developed visual system and also functioning olfaction, proprioception, and hearing. Bats have a well-developed echolocation system and also functioning vision. The senses are complementary, not exclusive.

## 4. Verdict

**False — with a grounded kernel.**

No bat species is blind. All bats have functional eyes, and many species—particularly the Old World fruit bats—rely primarily on vision for navigation and foraging. Microbats use echolocation as their primary short-range navigation tool but supplement it with vision for long-range orientation, predator detection, and social signaling.

The kernel of truth in the myth: echolocating microbats can navigate effectively in total darkness without using vision, which is a form of visual independence that could be described as not *needing* to see in those conditions. But not needing something in a specific context is categorically different from lacking it entirely.

"Blind as a bat" is a failed analogy. The correct formulation would be something like "uses sound the way a bat does"—but that idiom never caught on.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The bat blindness myth is an instance of a broader class of animal cognition misconceptions that attribute radical sensory limitation or impairment to creatures whose dominant sense differs from our own. Dogs are sometimes described as "color blind" in a way that implies near-total color absence (they see color, just dichromantically). Moles are said to be blind (they have small, light-sensitive eyes). Snakes are "deaf" (they detect vibration through their jawbones and have functional cochleae). In each case, the animal's sensory specialization gets reframed as a deficit relative to human norms.

This matters beyond zoological pedantry. It reflects a persistent anthropocentrism in how we model animal minds—an assumption that departures from human sensory architecture are deficiencies rather than specializations. Bats did not evolve toward blindness; they evolved a supplementary sense of extraordinary power. Their visual systems did not atrophy in the way that cave-dwelling fish lose eyes over generations of complete darkness. They are full-sensory animals navigating a world we can barely imagine perceiving.

The bat blindness myth also illuminates how idioms function epistemically. Phrases like "blind as a bat" carry cognitive content—they shape actual beliefs about the world, not just poetic registers. When idioms encode false beliefs, they create low-level misinformation that spreads through culture without requiring anyone to intend to deceive. The correction requires explicit counter-learning, which idioms resist because their value as cultural objects is independent of their literal truth.

Finally, bats as a group are ecologically crucial and dramatically underappreciated. They are the primary predators of nocturnal insects in many ecosystems, providing pest control services worth billions of dollars annually to agriculture. They are major pollinators of tropical plants, including several economically important species. And their immune systems—which tolerate viral loads that would kill other mammals—are of intense interest to infectious disease research. The myth of bat blindness, by constructing bats as weird and limited, contributes to their cultural marginalization and the neglect of their conservation needs.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The "blind as a bat" claim rarely receives formal fact-checker attention because it circulates primarily as an idiom rather than as a sincere factual assertion. When it does surface in fact-checking contexts, the treatment is consistent: direct refutation based on basic mammalian anatomy, with citation to the ubiquity of functional bat eyes across the order.

Science communication outlets have covered the correction repeatedly. National Geographic, the BBC's science coverage, and academic outreach publications from bat research groups all carry straightforward corrections. The American Bat Conservation Society and Bat Conservation International include the vision correction in their public education materials.

The challenge for fact-checkers here is a genre problem rather than an evidence problem. When the false claim appears as an idiom, applying fact-checking methodology feels category-mismatched—you're assigning a truth value to a figure of speech. The practical solution most communicators use is to debunk the literal content while acknowledging that the idiom serves a social function, and to redirect attention toward what bats actually do that is genuinely remarkable: not their blindness, but their biosonar.

From a methodology standpoint, the bat case illustrates the limits of claim-based fact-checking when the relevant misinformation is embedded in folk knowledge rather than in discrete, attributable assertions. No one "said" bats are blind in a checkable public statement. The belief just exists, distributed across culture, self-reinforcing through repetition. Effective correction requires sustained science communication rather than a single debunking article.

### The Science Communication Opportunity

The bat blindness myth, precisely because it is false in an interesting way, offers a productive entry point for public engagement with sensory biology. When a communicator explains that bats are not blind, the natural follow-up question is: so what do they use their eyes for? That question opens onto a genuinely fascinating area of neuroscience and evolutionary biology.

The answer involves a cross-modal integration story: bats don't choose between echolocation and vision, they fuse them. Research in neuroethology has demonstrated that in species like *Rhinolophus ferrumequinum* (the greater horseshoe bat), the superior colliculus—a midbrain structure involved in orienting responses—receives input from both the auditory and visual systems. The bat's brain is literally combining acoustic and visual spatial maps into a unified representation of space. This is not vision or echolocation; it is a sensory architecture humans don't have and can barely imagine.

Communicators who reframe the correction around this positive content—not "bats aren't blind" but "bat perception is richer and stranger than you think"—find better audience engagement and retention than those who simply negate the myth. The correction becomes a door rather than a wall.

### Taxonomy of Bat Vision Across the Clade

To appreciate why "blind as a bat" fails as a description of any bat, it helps to understand the range of visual investment across the approximately 1,400 bat species:

**Large-eyed megabats** (Pteropodidae and relatives): Visual acuity comparable to many diurnal mammals. Some species show cone densities in the foveal region that rival those of primates. Navigation and foraging are vision-driven. Echolocation absent or vestigial.

**Cave-dwelling microchiroptera** (various families): Smaller eyes with greater rod density, adapted for low-light rather than high-acuity vision. Echolocation-dominant navigation. Vision used for orientation at longer ranges and for social behavior within roosts.

**Insectivorous temperate-zone bats** (Vespertilionidae, including common North American species like *Eptesicus fuscus*, the big brown bat): Eye size moderate, vision functional. Studies with *Eptesicus fuscus* have demonstrated that these bats can learn visually-cued tasks in laboratory settings, suggesting more capable vision than their small eyes might suggest.

**Vampire bats** (Desmodontidae): Functional vision, with evidence suggesting better acuity than most microchiroptera. Hunt at night using multiple sensory cues including infrared heat detection, acoustic localization, and vision.

Across all groups: functional eyes. Within groups: variation in emphasis and acuity that reflects ecological niche. The "blind bat" does not exist in any taxonomic category.

### Conservation Implications of Misrepresentation

The relationship between how animals are perceived and how they are treated has conservation implications. Bats are among the most ecologically important and most threatened mammalian groups. In North America, white-nose syndrome—a fungal disease caused by *Pseudogymnoascus destructans*—has killed more than six million bats since its detection in 2006, with some hibernating species suffering population declines exceeding 90 percent. In Europe, bat populations face pressure from habitat loss, wind turbine collisions, and roost disturbance.

Public support for bat conservation—and public willingness to support bat-friendly land management, reduce roost disturbance, and back protective legislation—correlates with accurate understanding of bat biology and ecological role. Myths that construct bats as creepy, limited, and alien reduce the empathy and identification that drive conservation behavior. The correction of the blindness myth is, in this sense, not merely academic: it is part of the larger project of building a public relationship with bat biology that supports the animals' survival.

Bats also occupy a distinctive position in disease ecology. Their unusual immune tolerance for viral loads that would be lethal to other mammals makes them important reservoir hosts for viruses including coronaviruses, filoviruses, and henipaviruses. Understanding bat immunology is therefore directly relevant to pandemic preparedness. This research program depends on sustained scientific attention to bats as full biological subjects—not as creepy marginal creatures defined by what they lack, but as complex mammals with sophisticated biology worth studying in depth. Accurate public understanding of basic bat biology, beginning with the correction of the blindness myth, supports the cultural conditions in which that science can proceed.
