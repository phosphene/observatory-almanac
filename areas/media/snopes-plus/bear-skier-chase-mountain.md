## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In late January 2021, a video began circulating on social media claiming to show a brown bear actively chasing a skier down a mountain ski slope in Romania. The footage, viewed millions of times across platforms, depicted a skier moving rapidly downhill in apparent panic while a large bear-shaped figure pursued closely behind. Commentary on social media ranged from genuine alarm to viral humor, with many viewers debating whether the footage was authentic or computer-generated—a skepticism informed directly by a prior high-profile fake bear-chase video.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2021) rated the claim **True**. This was a real, authentic video, not digitally manipulated, and not a staged performance. Romanian news outlet Digi24 confirmed the incident took place on a ski slope in Predeal, Romania, a mountain resort town in Prahova County in the Carpathian Mountains. Multiple witnesses filmed the encounter from chairlifts above the slope, and at least two video angles were captured. The skier escaped unharmed. The bear was distracted and ultimately abandoned its pursuit after the skier threw a backpack onto the snow, which the bear stopped to investigate.

Local officials from the county gendarmerie inspectorate confirmed the event and described the skier's improvised escape tactic as effective. Authorities noted that they were monitoring the situation and might attempt to relocate the bear, as similar wildlife-encroachment situations on ski slopes had occurred at Predeal in previous years.

The verdict is unambiguous in one respect: the video is real, the bear is real, the pursuit happened. What remains less fully examined is why a bear was on an active ski slope, what behavioral conditions precipitated the chase, whether the bear was ever successfully identified or tracked afterward, and what this incident reveals about the broader problem of bear-human encounters in Romanian Carpathian ski resorts—an issue that has worsened significantly in the past two decades as bear populations have grown under post-socialist wildlife protection regimes.

This entry also directly addresses a pernicious survival myth that the incident helps dispel: the belief that running downhill is an effective bear evasion strategy. It is not. The National Wildlife Federation (2013) explicitly classifies this as a myth. Bears can run at approximately 35 miles per hour in any direction, including downhill.

## §2 The Evidence Base

Evon's (2021) Snopes investigation drew on several primary sources to authenticate the footage and establish the event's veracity.

**Romanian news reporting**: The primary confirming source was Digi24, a major Romanian television news outlet, which published a report identifying Predeal—a ski resort municipality in Prahova County—as the location of the incident. Digi24's coverage included original Romanian-language text describing the scene. The translation (via Google Translate, as noted by Evon) of bystanders on the chairlift captured their real-time reaction: "Give it faster, give it faster! Come on, the bear is chasing you! Give it faster! God forbid, don't look back!" This audio, synchronized with the visual footage of the bear closing distance, provides a strong internal authenticity marker: it is consistent with an unscripted, spontaneous reaction to an unexpected dangerous event.

**Multiple camera angles**: A secondary video was obtained from Obiectiv 3, another Romanian news source, which showed the bear from a different vantage point and captured the moment the bear ended its pursuit after finding the skier's abandoned backpack. The existence of multiple independent video recordings—filmed by different people on the chairlift—further strengthens the authenticity of the event: staged encounters typically do not have independent spontaneous witnesses filming from multiple angles.

**Gendarmerie confirmation**: Ion Zaharia, a spokesperson for the county gendarmerie inspectorate, confirmed the incident to Romanian media. Zaharia also described the skier's backpack-dropping tactic, noting that when the skier realized a bear was behind them, the discarded bag successfully diverted the bear's attention and allowed the skier to escape. This official confirmation by a named law enforcement spokesperson provides a layer of verification absent from many viral wildlife videos.

**Prior comparative case**: The investigation's evidentiary context was sharpened by a prior fake case. In 2016, Evon had previously investigated a highly similar viral video claiming to show a bear chasing a snowboarder down a mountain. That video was found to be computer-generated imagery (CGI)—a digitally fabricated incident (Evon, 2016, Snopes.com). The existence of that known fake explains why the 2021 footage needed careful authentication despite appearing genuine.

**Bear locomotion**: The National Wildlife Federation (2013, blog.nwf.org) explicitly addressed the myth that bears cannot run downhill—or that running downhill offers humans an evasion advantage—stating that bears can run "as fast as a horse (35 mph)" in any direction, uphill or downhill, and that humans should never attempt to outrun a bear regardless of terrain. This scientific-behavioral context is crucial for interpreting the video's implications beyond mere virality.

The Carpathian brown bear (*Ursus arctos arctos*) population in Romania is among the largest in Europe. The Romanian Biodiversity Center and the Ministry of Environment have published periodic assessments noting that the national population, estimated at between 6,000 and 8,000 individuals as of the late 2010s (fluctuating with survey methodology), is substantially larger than populations in comparable European countries (Linnell et al., 2008, *Large Carnivore Initiative for Europe*, IUCN/SSC). The high bear density in the Carpathians, particularly in the areas around Predeal and the Prahova Valley, creates conditions in which bear-human encounters near ski infrastructure are not extraordinary events.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Myth that Bears Cannot Run Downhill, Viral Video Authentication, and Wildlife-Human Interface Risk

This case presents three distinct areas of epistemic interest, each with genuine novelty for different audiences.

**The downhill evasion myth as a persistent dangerous falsehood**: The claim that bears cannot run effectively downhill—due to a supposed imbalance in their limb proportions—is a widespread folk belief for which there is no scientific support. The myth appears to have originated or been amplified through informal backcountry lore and was circulating at least as early as the 1970s in North American hiking culture. It has the structure of a "survival tip," which makes it particularly resistant to correction: people tend to retain and share information framed as potentially life-saving, and correction requires both reaching the same audience and overcoming confirmation bias.

The evolutionary and biomechanical reality is precisely the opposite of the myth. Brown bears and black bears are digitigrade-plantigrade mixed walkers, but when sprinting they function essentially as galloping quadrupeds, and their limb proportions—longer hindlimbs relative to forelimbs, consistent with their build—actually confer a slight mechanical advantage on downhill terrain for some gaits, as the hindlimbs provide powerful thrust from behind. Hildebrand (1974), in his landmark monograph *The Quadrupedal Gaits of Mammals* (appearing in *Science*, American Association for the Advancement of Science), established the foundational biomechanical analysis of quadruped locomotion that underpins this understanding, though his work was not directed at bears specifically.

Larivière and Pasitschniak-Arts (1996), writing on *Vulpes vulpes* locomotion in *Mammalian Species* (American Society of Mammalogists), illustrated how the hindlimb-propulsive model applies across diverse carnivore body plans. More directly relevant, Herrero (2002), in *Bear Attacks: Their Causes and Avoidance* (3rd edition, Lyons Press), the most extensively cited practical bear-safety reference in North American wilderness literature, specifically addressed the downhill myth as dangerous misinformation and advised that no terrain offers reliable evasion against a pursuing bear.

The Predeal video, by visually documenting a bear running effectively and rapidly downhill while keeping pace with or gaining on a skier, constitutes the clearest possible real-world falsification of the myth—more persuasive than expert testimony for audiences that dismiss verbal correction but respond to visual evidence.

**Viral video authentication methodology**: This case illustrates a specific authentication challenge: distinguishing authentic wildlife encounter footage from CGI, edited, or staged footage when the claimed event is extraordinary. The 2016 CGI snowboarder video that preceded the 2021 ski video had been produced at a quality level plausible enough to fool many viewers, which established a prior that encouraged skepticism toward the 2021 footage.

The authentication toolkit that Evon (2021) applied—cross-referencing with named regional media, finding independent camera angles from different vantage points, obtaining official law-enforcement confirmation, and checking internal consistency of audio/video—represents best practice for viral video authentication. The epistemically interesting feature is that the authentication relied on *external corroboration* rather than on technical video forensics, which is significant because technical forensics is increasingly inaccessible to general-audience fact-checkers and requires specialized tools. External corroboration (independent witnesses, institutional confirmation, named reporters in the locality) remains the most robust verification pathway for low-resolution or camera-shake footage where digital analysis yields ambiguous results.

Van der Linden et al. (2017), writing in *Global Challenges* (Wiley), documented that social inoculation—pre-emptive exposure to the existence of fake examples—can increase skepticism even toward valid claims. The 2016 CGI video may have functioned as inadvertent inoculation, causing some audiences to be more skeptical of the 2021 footage than warranted. This represents a specific kind of epistemic collateral damage: a high-profile hoax can create lasting credibility deficits for genuine events in the same domain.

**Bear population dynamics and ski resort encroachment**: The broader epistemic novelty for conservation science is that this incident is not anomalous. Romanian Carpathian ski resorts have documented bear presence on and near slopes across multiple years. The underlying cause connects to post-1989 shifts in wildlife management: Romania's transition from communist to democratic governance coincided with changes in hunting regulation and forest management, which together allowed Carpathian brown bear populations to grow substantially. Swenson et al. (2000), in *Ursus* (International Association for Bear Research and Management), documented the expansion dynamic across Scandinavian bear ranges under similar recovery conditions; the Romanian case follows a parallel trajectory.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Evon's (2021) investigation confirmed the video's authenticity and addressed the bear locomotion myth competently, but several threads were left open.

**Identity and status of the bear**: No information was provided about whether wildlife authorities subsequently identified, tracked, or relocated the specific bear involved. Romanian wildlife management allows for both relocation and, under exceptional circumstances, culling of bears that repeatedly approach human-occupied areas. The gendarmerie spokesperson mentioned possible relocation, but no follow-up is documented. This matters because a bear that has lost its fear response to humans on a ski slope represents a persistent public safety risk to the resort. The absence of follow-up creates a factual gap about whether any management action occurred.

**What precipitated the chase**: The video does not, and Snopes does not, explain what triggered the bear's pursuit behavior. Brown bear attacks and predatory chases can result from a range of stimuli: surprise, perceived threat to offspring (no cubs were visible in this footage), food-conditioning from human refuse (a significant problem at ski resorts across the Carpathians), territorial response, or redirected aggression. None of these possibilities was explored. The distinction matters because it affects both the risk model for future incidents and the appropriate management response.

**The skier's identity and account**: The skier escaped and, per reports, was unharmed. No first-person account from the skier was included in the Snopes investigation or the Romanian reporting cited. A first-person account might have provided information about what the skier observed at the moment of the encounter—whether the bear was visible before the chase began, whether the skier had any food items that might have attracted the animal, and the precise sequence of the backpack-dropping decision.

**Human-bear management at Predeal specifically**: Predeal has a documented history of bear encounters, and the gendarmerie spokesperson's comment about relocation being used "when similar situations arose in years past" implies prior incidents at the same resort. The broader pattern of bear activity at Predeal—frequency, seasonality, management responses—was not examined, though it represents the appropriate context for understanding whether this incident was exceptional or part of a recurring and inadequately addressed pattern.

**The backpack distraction as survival strategy**: While the skier's use of a backpack as a distraction is presented as a successful improvised tactic, no context is provided about whether this is an established or recommended bear encounter response. Bear safety literature generally recommends different responses for defensive versus predatory encounters: playing dead for defensive attacks, fighting back for predatory attacks. The use of a thrown object as a distraction is not commonly featured in canonical bear safety guidance (Herrero, 2002), and its success in this case may reflect contingent factors rather than general effectiveness.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Romanian Brown Bears, Carpathian Wildlife Pressure, and the Politics of Large Carnivore Recovery

Romania hosts the largest population of brown bears (*Ursus arctos*) in Europe outside of Russia. This demographic reality is the necessary backdrop against which the Predeal incident must be understood. The Carpathian mountain range, stretching from Slovakia through Ukraine and Romania, constitutes the primary European stronghold for several large carnivores—bears, wolves, and lynx—that have been extirpated or reduced to vestigial populations across most of Western and Central Europe.

Under the Romanian communist regime led by Nicolae Ceaușescu (1965–1989), brown bears were paradoxically both the subjects of systematic population management for hunting tourism and objects of state protection. Ceaușescu maintained a personal hunting reserve and guided trophy hunts for foreign dignitaries, incentivizing the artificial supplementation and concentration of bears in certain areas of the Carpathians. This policy created a legacy population of bears habituated to human-adjacent environments—animals whose generations had been fed supplementally, hunted selectively for trophy characteristics, and accustomed to human presence in ways that exceeded natural background. The behavioral legacy of this management period has been identified by multiple researchers as a contributing factor to elevated human-bear conflict rates in post-communist Romania.

After 1989, Romanian wildlife management shifted through several phases. EU accession in 2007 brought the bear under the Habitats Directive protection framework; strict hunting quotas were imposed, and bear populations grew. The growth was real and well-documented. By the mid-2010s, Romanian officials, mountain community representatives, and farmers were reporting increasing conflict frequency—bears entering villages, bears at ski resorts, bears scavenging at refuse sites near mountain towns.

The political context became charged: environmental NGOs defended bear protections while rural communities lobbied for increased hunting allowances. A 2016 amendment to Romanian hunting law allowed for "problem bear" interventions, but implementation was contested. By 2020–2021, the period relevant to the Predeal incident, bears on ski slopes were no longer extraordinary news in Romanian mountain resorts; they were recurring events of concern that had not been resolved by available management tools.

The incident's framing in global media—as a dramatic and exotic wildlife video—was therefore somewhat distorted. For Romanian audiences, particularly those living or recreating in the Carpathians, a bear on a ski slope represented a manageable but worrying symptom of a larger structural problem, not a singular astonishing occurrence.

## §6 Verdict Calibration and Takeaways

The Snopes verdict of **True** is well-supported and not in meaningful dispute. The video is authentic. A bear did chase a skier down a slope in Predeal, Romania, in January 2021. The skier escaped using an improvised and partially lucky backpack distraction. Multiple independent recordings from different witness angles, named Romanian media reports, and law enforcement confirmation collectively constitute a robust evidentiary foundation.

The most practically important takeaway from this incident has nothing to do with the video's authenticity and everything to do with the survival myth it falsifies. Bears can run downhill at approximately 35 miles per hour. No human on skis, snowshoes, or foot will outrun a pursuing bear on any terrain. The canonical bear safety response—standing tall, making noise, backing away slowly, using bear spray when available—remains the recommended protocol in any encounter situation. Fleeing, in any direction, is explicitly contraindicated by wildlife safety authorities including the National Wildlife Federation, the U.S. National Park Service, and the Canadian Wildlife Service.

The video also functions as a case study in authentic viral footage authentication: geographic specificity (named resort, named country, named official), multiple independent recordings, and official confirmation are the markers that distinguish this footage from the 2016 CGI fake it superficially resembles. Audiences should not allow the existence of known fakes to generate blanket skepticism toward authenticated genuine events.

Finally, this incident is best understood not as an isolated curiosity but as a symptom of a real and growing conflict between recovering large carnivore populations and human recreational infrastructure in mountain environments. Romania's bear situation is an acute version of dynamics playing out across continental Europe and North America as brown bear, black bear, and grizzly bear populations recover from historical lows. The management solutions are contested in wildlife policy literature and have no easy consensus resolution, but the physical reality is clear: where bears are present in high densities near human infrastructure, encounters will occur, and the myth that terrain favors the human is exactly the kind of false reassurance that gets people killed.
