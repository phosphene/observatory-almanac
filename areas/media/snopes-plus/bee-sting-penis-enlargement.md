## The Claim

On November 16, 2019, an entertainment website called Ihlayanews published an article claiming that bee stings to the penis could cause permanent enlargement. The article attributed the purported technique to "the royals of ancient Egypt" and quoted a fictional figure named "John Corona" — identified as "the leader of the bee sting to the penis research" — who was said to be "still shocked by the results." The article included the phrase "The Asian guys can't handle their new mandingle, it's awesome."

The article is obviously satirical — its source website carries the Afrikaans tagline *nuusparodie waarvan jy hou* ("news parody you like") and its Facebook page states explicitly that it is "a news parody and satire website" whose articles are "meant for mature audience with a dark sense of humour." It was rated "Labeled Satire" by Snopes in November 2019.

This entry treats the claim seriously not because the article was serious, but because the convergence of several factors — the persistence of body-modification folklore, the existence of an actual practice called "bee venom therapy," the documented phenomenon of deliberate genital stinging in some body-modification communities, and the anatomy and immunology of insect envenomation — makes the subject worth a rigorous audit. The satirical vehicle does not render the underlying question meaningless; it makes the question more interesting to examine carefully.

The claim: a bee sting to the penis causes permanent enlargement.

## What's Actually True

It is necessary to work through this claim at the biological level, because doing so illuminates both why it is false and what limited, very different version of a related claim might have biological grounding.

**What a bee sting actually does.** Honey bee venom (*Apis mellifera*) is a complex mixture. Its major active components include:

- **Melittin** (~50% of dry venom weight): an amphipathic peptide that disrupts cell membranes, causes mast cell degranulation, and is primarily responsible for the burning pain of a sting.
- **Phospholipase A2** (~12%): an enzyme that cleaves membrane phospholipids, releasing arachidonic acid and triggering the inflammatory cascade.
- **Hyaluronidase** (~3%): an enzyme that breaks down hyaluronic acid in connective tissue, effectively spreading the venom through tissue.
- **Apamin** (~3%): a small peptide that crosses the blood-brain barrier and acts on central nervous system ion channels.
- **Mast cell degranulating peptide**: causes histamine release.
- Minor components: various enzymes, biogenic amines (including histamine and serotonin present at low concentration in venom itself), and small proteins.

The immediate tissue response to envenomation is a controlled inflammatory cascade: vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, mast cell degranulation releasing histamine and cytokines, leucocyte infiltration, and local oedema. This produces swelling. In well-vascularised, connective-tissue-rich regions, the swelling can be dramatic.

**The swelling is acute, not permanent.** This is the fundamental biological fact that refutes the claim. Inflammatory oedema is transient. The interleukins, prostaglandins, and histamine that cause fluid to accumulate in tissue are metabolised and cleared. The leucocytes finish their work and exit. Vascular tone normalises. Barring complications — tissue necrosis, secondary infection, or an unusual local immune response — the swelling resolves over hours to days. No component of bee venom causes permanent structural remodelling of the penis. There is no fibrosis, no tissue growth, no new cellular matrix being laid down.

**The claim of ancient Egyptian royal use is fiction.** There is no archaeological or textual evidence from Egyptology supporting any form of deliberate bee envenomation for sexual enhancement. Egyptian medicine, which is reasonably well documented through the Ebers Papyrus, the Edwin Smith Papyrus, and other sources, included extensive use of honey (a genuine antimicrobial wound treatment), beeswax, and bee products — but nothing resembling deliberate therapeutic stinging for anatomical modification.

**Apitherapy is a real practice, though its evidence base is disputed.** Apitherapy refers to the therapeutic use of bee venom and bee products. It has been practised in various forms in traditional medicine in Asia, Eastern Europe, and parts of South America. Modern interest has focused primarily on:

- **Arthritis and joint inflammation**: Some practitioners and patients report symptomatic relief. A 2005 review in *Arthritis & Rheumatism* found that bee venom has anti-inflammatory properties in animal models via the phospholipase A2 pathway, but clinical trial evidence for humans remains limited and methodologically mixed.
- **Multiple sclerosis**: There is interest in bee venom's effects on neuroinflammation. A 2019 Cochrane review found insufficient evidence to support or refute apitherapy for MS.
- **Cancer**: Melittin has cytotoxic properties in cell culture, which has driven research into potential anti-cancer applications. This does not translate to apitherapy for cancer treatment.

None of this evidence base involves or implies permanent tissue enlargement of any body part.

**The deliberate envenomation body-modification community is real.** This is perhaps the most genuinely obscure piece of context for this entry. There is a small but documented community of people who deliberately sting themselves or have themselves stung with bees as part of body modification practices. This is distinct from apitherapy in motivation (aesthetic or experience-seeking rather than therapeutic). It is also documented in entomologist researchers: Justin Schmidt, who created the Schmidt Pain Index ranking of insect stings by pain intensity, described deliberately repeating stings to document his subjective experience. Andrew Kronauer, a Harvard myrmecologist, has written about similar self-experimentation.

In male genital body modification communities — which include an extraordinary range of practices from simple piercings to subincision — bee stinging of the glans or the shaft has been documented anecdotally online. Practitioners describe the swelling as temporary and the experience as extreme. This is medically inadvisable for reasons beyond the obvious: the immunological risks are significant (discussed below), and the tissue of the glans penis is richly innervated and highly vascularised, making severe local reactions more likely than in less sensitive regions.

**The actual danger.** This is the most important section of the biological analysis, because the satire article's framing — *it's awesome, easy, great results* — obscures that deliberate genital bee stinging is medically dangerous on multiple axes:

1. **Anaphylaxis.** An estimated 0.3–7.5% of the general population will develop sensitisation to bee venom after initial exposure — subsequent stings can trigger anaphylaxis, which is life-threatening without immediate epinephrine treatment. The genital region's high vascularity means faster systemic venom distribution.
2. **Severe local reactions.** Even without systemic allergy, repeated stings can cause large local reactions with significant tissue oedema and necrosis. Stings to the penis specifically carry risk of urethral oedema.
3. **Secondary infection.** The disruption of skin integrity, combined with inflammatory oedema, creates a favourable environment for bacterial colonisation. In the genital region, with its warm, moist microenvironment, this risk is elevated.
4. **No therapeutic benefit.** There is no evidence that any form of temporary swelling produces lasting anatomical change in penile tissue. Penile enlargement surgery — which is genuinely performed and which carries its own documented risks — works through entirely different mechanisms (ligament division and fat grafting, or dermal matrix procedures) and achieves modest results.

## Why People Believe This

Even clearly satirical content about this subject spreads in the way this article did because it touches several intersecting anxieties and fascinations.

**Penile enlargement anxiety is commercially exploited at industrial scale.** The penis enlargement industry — pump devices, herbal supplements, jelqing advice, exercises, and surgical procedures — is a multi-billion-dollar global market built primarily on anxiety rather than evidence. The market's baseline message is that enlargement is possible and that natural or low-risk methods may achieve it. This creates an interpretive filter: claims about enlargement-adjacent phenomena are processed through an existing desire for them to be true.

**Folk medicine frameworks legitimise painful treatments.** Across many cultures, treatments that hurt are expected to work — the pain is evidence of effectiveness. Ben venom stinging is used in traditional medicine for joint pain; the leap to other applications feels internally logical within a folk-medicine framework even when it is biologically nonsensical.

**Pseudohistorical claims ("ancient Egyptians did it") add perceived credibility.** Appeals to ancient practices — particularly Egyptian, Chinese, or Amazonian indigenous knowledge — function as authority claims in wellness and alternative medicine marketing. The "ancient royals" framing bypasses critical scrutiny because it implies time-tested verification rather than asking for evidence-based evaluation.

**Novelty and absurdity drive engagement irrespective of truth value.** The social psychology of viral sharing is well-documented: emotionally arousing content shares faster than neutral content, and humour/absurdity is a positive valence that competes with outrage for engagement. The article exists as deliberate satire, and people sharing it mostly knew it was a joke. The danger is not that many people believed it literally but that it circulates in the ecosystem of "enlargement claims," potentially reaching individuals who encounter it decontextualised.

## Verdict

**The claim is entirely false.** A bee sting to the penis does not and cannot produce permanent enlargement. The biological mechanism is wholly absent: bee venom produces acute inflammatory oedema that resolves within hours to days, and no component of honey bee venom or the immune response it triggers causes lasting anatomical tissue change.

The ancient Egyptian attribution is fictional. The research figure is fictional. The claimed effect is not merely unproven but biologically impossible under normal physiological conditions.

**The broader claim that bee stings could produce noticeable temporary swelling in genital tissue is trivially true** — bee stings produce temporary oedema in all tissue — but this is not remotely what the satirical article claims, and temporary swelling that constitutes painful soft-tissue inflammation is not a desirable outcome by any medical or cosmetic standard.

**Deliberate bee stinging of the genitals carries genuine medical risk**, including anaphylaxis, severe local reaction, and secondary infection. No credible medical authority recommends it for any purpose.

## The Wider Picture

This entry sits at the intersection of three phenomena worth separating out.

**The satire/misinformation boundary.** Snopes' category "Labeled Satire" acknowledges a specific type of content: pieces that are clearly fictional by design but that circulate as though they were factual. The ihlayanews piece was clearly labelled on its source website, but like all satirical pieces it can spread decontextualised. The *Poe's Law* phenomenon — the impossibility of distinguishing sincere extremism from satire without author intent as a cue — applies in the milder form: once a claim detaches from its satirical context, it is processed by recipient logic, not authorial intent.

**The enlargement pseudoscience landscape.** The penis enlargement market is a useful case study in how medically unsupported practices sustain themselves. A 2019 systematic review in *BJU International* examined clinical evidence for non-surgical penile augmentation and found low-quality evidence for modest effects from some vacuum erection device protocols, limited evidence for injection of hyaluronic acid filler (a legitimate aesthetic medicine procedure), and no evidence for herbal supplements, exercises, or traction devices achieving meaningful permanent change. The gap between the proliferation of products/claims and the thinness of evidence is striking. Into this gap, bee-sting satire and bee-sting body-modification folklore alike flow easily.

**Apitherapy and the evidence problem.** The existence of genuine scientific interest in bee venom as a bioactive substance — its anti-inflammatory properties, its explored applications in arthritis management, the melittin research in oncology — creates a confusing halo effect. People who know that "bee venom has medical uses" may be predisposed to accept claims about other bee-venom applications that have no scientific support. The existence of real apitherapy research is not evidence for all apitherapy claims; it is specifically not evidence for permanent tissue enlargement from envenomation.

The interesting epistemological lesson from this entry is precisely that the biological claim — permanent structural change from inflammatory response — is not a matter of "not enough evidence" but rather of "the mechanism does not exist." This is a different type of refutation than many fact-checks produce. The claim is not unproven; it is biologically incoherent. There is no possible study design that could confirm it, because the cellular machinery for doing what it describes is absent. Distinguishing between "unproven," "disproven," and "mechanistically impossible" is important for clear epistemic communication.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated the claim "Labeled Satire" and provided a brief contextualisation: the article originated on a satirical website with an explicit disclaimer, and the claim it made was not factual.

The Labeled Satire category is a relatively light-touch rating designed primarily to attribute the claim's origin correctly and advise readers that it is not a factual news report. It does not, by design, engage with the underlying science, the body-modification context, or the broader landscape of enlargement pseudoscience that the claim inhabits.

**The gap this entry fills (Enhance):** The source article covers provenance but not biology. By establishing the pharmacological mechanism of bee venom, the immunology of envenomation, the limited evidence base for apitherapy, and the actual risk profile of deliberate genital stinging, this entry transforms a three-sentence satire notation into a substantive examination of why the claim is false, what is true in its vicinity, and what genuine risks the claim's humorous framing obscures.

There is also a meta-level observation for fact-checkers: "Labeled Satire" as a verdict can be misleading if interpreted as "this doesn't require further investigation." A satirical claim that is also biologically false, medically dangerous if acted upon, and embedded in a broader pseudoscience ecosystem benefits from investigation precisely because the "it's just a joke" framing can serve as a barrier to taking the underlying science seriously. Not every satirical article is worth this level of analysis, but those that involve health behaviours with real risk profiles — including any subject touching on enlargement practices, self-medication, or deliberate physical risk-taking — deserve more than provenance annotation.

The appropriate editorial decision is case-by-case. For this particular claim, the brevity of the Snopes entry was proportionate to a genuinely silly satirical piece that circulated in 2019. The value of the extended analysis is less about correcting a specific false belief that many people held, and more about demonstrating how claims about body modification pseudoscience should be assessed: not at the level of "is the source legitimate" but at the level of "what would biology and medicine have to look like for this to be true, and does it?"
