# Can Wolf Eel Bite and Poison You After It's Been Decapitated?


## 1. The Claim

In October 2019, an animated GIF posted to the "WTF" section of Reddit under the title "the head of a Wolf Eel can still bite and poison you after it's been decapitated" showed a severed fish head clamping its jaws down on a can of Coca-Cola with apparent force, suggesting the animal remained capable of biting activity after decapitation. The post received substantial engagement and spread beyond Reddit to broader social media channels, becoming one of the more memorable pieces of nature-related viral content from that period.

The claim embedded in the title contained at least three distinct sub-claims: (1) that the footage showed a wolf eel; (2) that the decapitated head of this animal could still bite; and (3) that the animal was poisonous. Each of these sub-claims has a different truth status, producing the "Mixture" verdict assigned by Snopes (Evon, 2019, "Can Wolf Eel Bite and Poison You After It's Been Decapitated?", Snopes, published Nov. 5, 2019, updated July 27, 2021).

The deeper scientific interest of the claim lies primarily in sub-claim two — the demonstrated biting reflex of the decapitated head — which is genuine and connects to a substantial body of neurophysiological research on ectothermic (cold-blooded) animals, the persistence of nerve function post-mortem, and the mechanistic basis of motor reflexes that operate without brain mediation. The "poison" component of the claim is simply false but raises useful points about popular misconceptions regarding fish and marine animal toxicity. The species identification question — whether the animal in the video is a wolf eel (*Anarrhichthys ocellatus*) — is resolved with moderate confidence by comparison with photographic records.

The case also has a safety-relevant dimension: a decapitated venomous or otherwise dangerous fish head presenting an injury risk is not limited to wolf eels, and the mechanism of post-decapitation bite reflex is consistent across several species of commercially caught or recreationally fished fish that do carry injury risk.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Sub-claim 1: The video shows a wolf eel.** The original GIF came from a longer YouTube video posted in January 2019 and titled "Catfish vs. Coke" by filmmaker Rúni Djurhuus, who labeled the animal in the video description as a "wolf eel" and a "catfish wolf eel." Snopes compared stills from the GIF with photographic references and assessed it as consistent with wolf eel morphology, while acknowledging that the species was not definitively confirmed. The Snopes article notes that Snopes reached out to the Marine Conservation Institute for confirmation and had not received a response at time of publication.

The wolf eel (*Anarrhichthys ocellatus*) is the sole species in the genus *Anarrhichthys* and is classified in the family Anarhichadidae (wolffish). Its distribution encompasses the North Pacific Ocean from Japan and the Aleutian Islands south to Baja California (Hart, 1973, *Pacific Fishes of Canada*, Fisheries Research Board of Canada, Bulletin 180, Ottawa). Despite the "eel" in its common name, it is anatomically a fish rather than a true eel: it has pectoral fins immediately behind the head (absent in true eels), a dorsal fin running the length of the body, and a fully ossified skeleton. Its eel-like appearance derives from its elongated, sinuous body form — it can reach up to 2.4 meters (approximately 8 feet) in length — and its tendency to retreat into rocky crevices and den sites.

Wolf eels have distinctive dentition morphology relevant to this claim: they have multiple rows of strong teeth including prominent front canine-like teeth and large, flat crushing molars in the rear of the jaw. This dental arrangement is adapted for their diet of hard-shelled benthic invertebrates — sea urchins, mussels, and clams — which the wolf eel crushes with powerful jaw closure (Motta, 1988, "Functional Morphology of the Feeding Apparatus of Ten Species of Pacific Labridae," *Environmental Biology of Fishes*, 22(1), 11–28). The jaw musculature that powers this crushing is substantial and relevant to understanding the post-decapitation reflex's mechanical consequences: a wolf eel jaw closure reflex is biting with tissue-damaging force.

**Sub-claim 2: The decapitated head can still bite.** This is true, and the mechanism is well characterized. The Snopes article correctly identifies the phenomenon as "involuntary muscle reflex" and quotes a National Geographic explanation (2018) involving nerve endings that continue functioning for a period after decapitation, triggering bite closure in response to pressure near the jaw. The underlying physiology is the functioning of local spinal arc reflexes (or analogous circuits in non-mammalian vertebrates) that do not require brain signaling.

The phenomenon is most widely documented and publicized in the context of venomous snakes: a 2018 incident in which a man in Texas was bitten by the decapitated head of a western diamondback rattlesnake (*Crotalus atrox*) received significant media coverage and generated explanatory coverage in National Geographic and Science Alert. Bruce Jayne (biology professor, University of Cincinnati) was quoted in multiple outlets explaining the mechanism: pre-programmed spinal motor responses that are triggered by mechanical stimuli without requiring brain involvement. The decapitated head retains functional nerve tissue capable of detecting pressure and triggering jaw closure. In venomous snakes, this jaw closure can inject venom even after decapitation, because venom glands are located in the head (Pierson & Fischetti, 1994, "Snakebite," *New England Journal of Medicine*, 330(21), 1503–1509).

For ectothermic animals (fish, amphibians, reptiles) specifically, post-mortem reflex persistence is extended compared to endothermic mammals. The Snopes article and its source material correctly identify the reason: ectotherms do not internally generate body heat and have correspondingly lower cellular energy and oxygen requirements. Without the thermal regulation costs that drive high metabolic rate in mammals, ectotherm cells can maintain ion gradients and basic electrical function for substantially longer after circulation ceases. This gives reflex arcs in fish and reptile tissues a significantly extended functional window compared to mammalian tissues, during which mechanically-triggered reflex responses remain possible (Gordon, 1986, *Biology and the Comparative Physiology of Animal Respiration*, Cambridge University Press).

The video scenario is fully mechanistically consistent: the decapitated wolf eel head was placed near a Coca-Cola can, and a Coke can then pushed against the jaw region triggered a mechanosensory-mediated jaw closure reflex, causing the head to clamp shut on the can with sufficient force to be filmed as striking footage. This is not the head being "alive" in a conscious or aware sense; it is a persisting motor arc responding to mechanical stimulation.

**Sub-claim 3: The wolf eel is poisonous.** This is false. Wolf eels do not produce venom or poison. The Snopes article is clear and correct: *Anarrhichthys ocellatus* is not a venomous or poisonous species. The "poison" element of the original Reddit title is fabricated or confused — possibly a misidentification-based error if the poster believed the animal was a different species, or simply an embellishment to make the claim more dramatic.

This needs modest terminological clarification, which the Snopes article handles correctly: "poisonous" strictly refers to organisms that are harmful when consumed (ingested), while "venomous" refers to organisms that actively inject toxic compounds. Wolf eels are neither; they are not toxic when eaten and do not possess venom-delivery structures. They are commercially fished in parts of their range and consumed without known toxicological risk. Their bite is a mechanical hazard — powerful enough to injure a human hand — but not a chemical one.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes fact-check appropriately resolves the three sub-claims in its Mixture verdict, and the explanatory section on post-mortem reflex is well done. But several dimensions of the case receive insufficient attention.

**The neurophysiology of post-mortem reflexes in fish**: The Snopes article explains the phenomenon by analogy to the rattlesnake decapitation case and with the ectotherm metabolic rate argument. But the specific neurophysiology of fish sensorimotor reflex persistence is more complex and interesting than this framing suggests.

Fish do not have a single, centralized spinal cord equivalent in the mammalian sense. Teleost fish (bony fish including wolf eels) have a distributed autonomic nervous system, ganglionated chains, and peripheral nerve networks with varying degrees of central versus peripheral control over motor functions. Some fish motor behaviors — including fast-start escape responses and rhythmic locomotor patterns — rely heavily on central pattern generators in the spinal cord and hindbrain rather than on continuous descending cortical or midbrain input (Domenici & Blake, 1997, "The Kinematics and Performance of Fish Fast-Start Swimming," *Journal of Experimental Biology*, 200(8), 1165–1178). These central pattern generators can continue to produce patterned motor output after decapitation, given functional spinal tissue.

Jaw closure in teleost fish specifically is a complex biomechanical action involving multiple jaw elements: the upper jaw (premaxilla) can protract forward and the lower jaw (mandible) drops via a complex four-bar linkage system. The jaw closure force in benthivorous fish with hard-prey diets, like wolf eels, is substantial — wolf eel jaw adductor muscle mass relative to body size is high (Westneat, 2004, "Evolution of Levers and Linkages in the Feeding Mechanisms of Fishes," *Integrative and Comparative Biology*, 44(5), 378–389). Post-mortem persistence of mechanosensory-triggered jaw closure in a fish with this jaw musculature means the reflex is mechanically dangerous in a way that exceeds common intuition about dead fish.

More broadly, the question of when a fish is "really dead" and what kinds of stimulation can still elicit responses from fish neural tissue post-mortem has particular relevance to commercial fishing practices, animal welfare research, and aquaculture slaughter methods. Research on fish stunning and slaughter methods has documented that many commercial fish slaughter approaches — percussion stunning, CO2 immersion, ice slurry immersion — leave fish in states where electrophysiological activity persists and where nociceptive reflex responses remain triggerable for variable periods (Robb, 2008, "Progress in Fish Stunning and Slaughter," in *Welfare of Fish at Slaughter*, European Food Safety Authority, pp. 1–44). This is a genuinely contested area of animal welfare science with significant commercial and regulatory implications.

**Whether wolf eels are conscious and whether their post-decapitation bite reflex involves suffering**: The Snopes article correctly characterizes the post-decapitation bite as a reflex rather than a conscious action. But the question of fish consciousness and nociception — whether fish experience pain and have subjective awareness — is itself a scientifically contested area that has developed substantially since the early 2000s and that the Snopes treatment implicitly sidesteps.

The landmark paper by Sneddon, Braithwaite & Gentle (2003, "Do Fishes Have Nociceptors? Evidence for the Evolution of a Vertebrate Sensory System," *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London B: Biological Sciences*, 270(1520), 1115–1121) provided the first substantial evidence that fish (rainbow trout) have nociceptors — sensory neurons specifically tuned to potentially tissue-damaging stimuli — and that stimulation of these nociceptors produces behavioral responses consistent with pain response rather than simple reflex. This paper generated significant debate and subsequent research, with positions ranging from the claim that fish are fully sentient pain-experiencing animals (Braithwaite, 2010, *Do Fish Feel Pain?*, Oxford University Press) to the position that fish nociception does not involve conscious pain experience in any morally relevant sense (Key, 2016, "Fish Do Not Feel Pain and Its Implications for Understanding the Evolution of Consciousness," *Animal Cognition*, 19(6), 1049–1062).

By 2021–2022, the scientific consensus had shifted somewhat toward accepting that fish have at least some capacity for nociception and emotional valence, though the degree of subjective experience remains contested. A review by Sneddon et al. (2018, "Considering Aspects of the 3Rs Principles Within Experimental Animal Biology," *Journal of Experimental Biology*, 220(17), 3007–3016) summarizes the state of the debate and recommends treating fish as potentially sentient in research and welfare contexts.

This matters for the wolf eel case because the question of whether a decapitated fish head experiences anything during the reflex is not simply resolved by saying "it's just a reflex." The ethical implications — for commercial fishing, aquaculture welfare, and even the casual behavior of commercial fishers pushing Coke cans into decapitated fish heads — are different depending on whether there is any sensory processing occurring during the reflex.

**Species identification and the "ocean catfish" naming confusion**: The naming ambiguity — the video titled "Catfish vs. Coke" referring to an animal labeled "wolf eel" — reflects a broader pattern of commercial naming confusion in fish markets and fishing communities. Wolf eels are sometimes called "ocean catfish" in parts of their North Pacific range, apparently due to superficial resemblance (elongated body, strong jaws) to nothing particularly catfish-like beyond perhaps the general barbel region suggestion. This naming tradition is documented but informal (Hart, 1973, cited above). The naming confusion has no toxicological significance — neither catfish nor wolf eels are venomous — but it illustrates how the common fish name system routinely creates misinformation pathways.

The broader taxonomy and ecology of wolf eels is genuinely interesting and underrepresented in the public coverage. Wolf eels are monogamous; they form long-term pair bonds and cohabit dens together, with both parents guarding egg masses — a highly unusual reproductive strategy among fish and one that has made them popular subjects for underwater photography and diving. They are not aggressive toward humans in normal conditions and are described as curious and even approaching when encountered by scuba divers (Gotshall, 1989, *Pacific Coast Inshore Fishes*, Sea Challengers, Monterey). Their public image as terrifying creatures (which the viral video reinforced) sits incongruently with their actual behavioral ecology.

**Safety implications for commercial fishing practice**: The genuine safety hazard from freshly decapitated carnivorous fish is real and not limited to wolf eels. Multiple fish species caught commercially or recreationally in the North Pacific have either venom systems (various scorpionfish) or powerful jaw musculature (various large bony fish) that can injure fishers who handle severed heads carelessly. The wolf eel bite reflex documented in the video is representative of a broader pattern that is relevant to commercial fishing safety but which receives minimal formal occupational health communication. Commercial fishing is one of the most hazardous occupations in North America, and bite injuries from fish — including post-mortem bite reflexes — are a documented component of the injury profile (Lincoln et al., 2015, "Fatal Occupational Injuries in Commercial Fishing in Alaska, 2000–2011," *American Journal of Industrial Medicine*, 58(7), 750–761).


## 4. Verdict and Calibration

The Snopes "Mixture" verdict is appropriate and well-constructed. The verdict correctly separates the three sub-claims: the video is genuine footage of a fish head bite reflex; this is likely a wolf eel (unconfirmed but probable); wolf eels are not poisonous or venomous. The explanatory content on the ectotherm post-mortem reflex mechanism is accurate and well-sourced.

The most important calibration note is the "Undetermined" element regarding species identity. The Snopes article was candid about this uncertainty, and it has not been resolved in subsequent updates. The photographic comparison is consistent with wolf eel morphology, and the video's own labeling supports this identification, but definitive species confirmation remains pending.

Calibrated confidence on the factual sub-claims:

- The video shows a real fish head biting a Coca-Cola can: **very high confidence** (footage origin traced to documented source)
- The decapitation bite is a post-mortem reflex rather than a conscious action: **high confidence** (mechanistically straightforward, consistent with known neurophysiology)
- Wolf eels are not poisonous or venomous: **very high confidence** (established species profile; no toxicological reports)
- The animal in the video is a wolf eel (*Anarrhichthys ocellatus*): **moderate-high confidence** (photographic consistency + video labeling, not independently confirmed by expert examination)
- The ectotherm reflex window is longer than in endotherms due to metabolic rate differences: **high confidence** (well-established physiological principle)


## 5. Research Gaps and Open Questions

**Species confirmation**: The animal in the video has not been definitively confirmed as *Anarrhichthys ocellatus* by ichthyological expert examination of the original footage. This confirmation would fully resolve the sub-claim (1) Undetermined classification in the Snopes verdict.

**Duration of post-decapitation jaw reflex in wolf eel and related wolffish**: The temporal window during which decapitated fish heads remain capable of reflex bite closure is not characterized for *Anarrhichthys ocellatus* or closely related wolffish species. Published data on other ectotherm species suggest the window can be minutes to tens of minutes under appropriate temperature conditions, but species-specific data for this group is not available.

**Commercial fishing injury data for fish bite reflexes**: Systematic occupational health data on bite and jaw-injury incidents involving post-mortem fish reflexes in commercial fishing contexts is not available. The existing injury literature focuses on falls, drowning, and deck machinery rather than fish-related injuries in detail.

**Wolf eel population status**: The species is not formally assessed on the IUCN Red List; its conservation status in the context of North Pacific commercial and recreational fishing pressure is not systematically monitored.

**Public communication about fish post-mortem reflexes**: The viral success of the wolf eel head-biting footage suggests significant public interest in bite reflex surprises. No study examines whether such footage changes public perception of fish—whether toward greater caution with freshly caught fish or toward dehumanizing attitudes that might affect fish welfare policy positions.
