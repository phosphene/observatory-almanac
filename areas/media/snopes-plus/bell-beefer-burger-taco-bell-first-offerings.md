# Was the 'Bell Beefer' Burger Among Taco Bell's First Offerings?

## 1. The Claim

The Bell Beefer is among the most well-remembered discontinued items in American fast-food history. Described most accurately as a sloppy-joe-style burger — ground beef seasoned with Taco Bell's signature taco spicing, served in a hamburger bun rather than a taco shell — the Bell Beefer occupied a distinctive location in the Taco Bell product catalogue: a burger sold by a chain whose entire branding and market positioning was based on Mexican-inspired food. For devoted fans, the Bell Beefer represents a lost artifact of a more experimentally eclectic era in American fast food.

The claim examined here — whether the Bell Beefer was among Taco Bell's original first offerings when the chain launched in 1962 — surfaces in multiple contexts: in nostalgic discussions of the item's discontinuation, in petitions for its return, and in general food-history commentary. The implicit assertion is that the Bell Beefer was present at the very beginning of Taco Bell, making its eventual disappearance not merely a product discontinuation but the erasure of a founding-era menu item.

Snopes journalist Taija PerryCook investigated the claim, published October 12, 2023. The investigation drew on newspaper archives, historical menu images, Taco Bell corporate communications, and podcast reporting on the Bell Beefer's history. Taco Bell's head of public relations, Matt Prince, was directly quoted in a podcast cited in the investigation.

The claim's verdict is Mostly True: the founding menu did include a direct antecedent — the "chili burger" — but the Bell Beefer name itself dates to 1977, fifteen years after Taco Bell's opening.

## 2. What's Actually True

**Taco Bell's founding and original menu:** Glen Bell founded Taco Bell in 1962, opening the first restaurant in Downey, California. The founding menu consisted of exactly five items, each priced at 19 cents:

1. Frijoles
2. Tostados
3. Burritos (available in red or green variants)
4. Tacos
5. Chili burgers

The five-item, 19-cent menu has been documented by multiple sources including NewsNation (reporting April 2022) and KTLA (April 2022), and is consistent with the historical record of early Taco Bell operations. The franchise Glen Bell built from this modest beginning had grown to a net worth calculated at over $33 billion by the time of the Snopes investigation.

**The chili burger, Bell Burger, and Bell Beefer — one product, three names:** According to Matt Prince, head of public relations at Taco Bell, as quoted in the "Cheesy Gordita Bunch" podcast (Episode 41, "Bell Beefer: Stank Festival"), the chili burger, the Bell Burger, and the Bell Beefer are all the same product — different names for the same core item across different eras of Taco Bell's history. The rebranding sequence unfolded as follows:

- **Chili burger (1962–early 1970s):** The original founding menu name. A seasoned ground beef mixture served on a hamburger bun — essentially Taco Bell's taco filling applied to a sloppy-joe format.
- **Bell Burger (early 1970s–1977):** By the early 1970s, the price for all five original menu items had risen from 19 cents to 25 cents. Simultaneously, the product was rebranded from "chili burger" to "Bell Burger." A 1974 Minnesota newspaper clipping reproduced at Newspapers.com shows Taco Bell menu listings from this era including the Bell Burger. The name change added brand identity (Bell = Taco Bell) without altering the product.
- **Bell Beefer (1977–mid-1990s):** By 1977, the product received its most-remembered name: the Bell Beefer. A 1980s Taco Bell menu image reproduced on Flickr shows the Bell Beefer listed at 89 cents. The Bell Beefer name appears to have been the most commercially distinctive iteration — the one that generated the most consumer loyalty and the nostalgia now associated with its absence.

**The Bell Beefer's discontinuation:** By the mid-1990s, the Bell Beefer appears to have been phased out of Taco Bell's national menu. The Taco Bell fandom wiki documents the product's removal. As of the October 2023 Snopes investigation, the Bell Beefer was not available at any Taco Bell locations in the United States.

**The fan campaign for the Bell Beefer's return:** The Bell Beefer's absence has generated what may be the most culturally elaborate discontinuation-mourning in American fast-food consumer history. Documented artifacts of the Bell Beefer revival campaign include:

- A dedicated Facebook group ("Taco Bell Please Bring back the Bell Beefer") with its own organized advocacy.
- A Change.org petition addressed to Taco Bell requesting Bell Beefer reinstatement.
- A dedicated podcast episode by the "Cheesy Gordita Bunch" podcast (Episode 41: "Bell Beefer: Stank Festival").
- In-person demonstrations — dubbed "Stank Festivals" — at Taco Bell locations in San Francisco, documented via X (Twitter) in 2022.
- Home recreation attempts by food bloggers, including a vegan version created by the food blog "Thee Burger Dude."

The intensity of the Bell Beefer nostalgia response is partly explicable by the item's conceptual distinctiveness: a burger at Taco Bell is an unexpected cultural artifact — a concession to the demographic possibility that some customers might want a hamburger-format option. Its existence signals something about the early, experimentally inclusive vision of Taco Bell's menu that has since been narrowed to a more focused Mexican-inspired product range.

**Comparison with the Mexican Pizza discontinuation:** The Snopes investigation drew a parallel with Taco Bell's 2020 removal of the Mexican Pizza, which generated similarly intense fan protest — including a campaign by rapper Doja Cat — eventually resulting in Mexican Pizza's return to the menu in 2022. The Bell Beefer recall campaign has not achieved similar success; as of the investigation, the item had not returned.

**No TV commercials documented:** Despite multiple historical menu images corroborating the Bell Beefer's existence across two decades, the Snopes investigation noted that no television commercials advertising the Bell Beefer had been found in the available historical record.

## 3. Why People Believe This — and Its Epistemic Novelty

This claim occupies an unusual position in the Truth Vault series because the underlying factual question is not primarily about misinformation. The Bell Beefer is a verification case about fast-food historical record — the kind of knowledge gap that is neither politically charged nor primarily driven by motivated reasoning, but rather reflects the simple inaccessibility of granular corporate menu history to ordinary consumers and journalists.

**The epistemic novelty of the Mostly True rating:** The "Mostly True" verdict is a more epistemically informative signal than a flat "True" or "False." The founding Taco Bell menu included a chili burger that is the direct antecedent to the Bell Beefer, making the general claim ("Bell Beefer was among the first offerings") defensible in spirit and genealogically accurate. But the Bell Beefer *name* itself was a 1977 coinage — fifteen years after the founding — meaning a strictly precise reading of the claim is false: the Bell Beefer was not, by name, among Taco Bell's first offerings. It was the *chili burger*, a product that was subsequently rebranded through two name changes before arriving at the Bell Beefer designation. The difference between "true" and "mostly true" here turns entirely on the level of specificity applied to the claim: if "Bell Beefer" refers to the named product, it is not an original offering; if "Bell Beefer" refers to the product identity (the sloppy-joe burger format using taco filling ingredients), it is present from day one.

This is a paradigmatic case of what scholars of historical claims might call *referential ambiguity* in folk historical memory: the popular claim is accurate to the underlying fact pattern while technically imprecise about the naming sequence. Popular food history tends to collapse branded product names with the underlying product concepts, which is a sensible shorthand for consumer identity purposes but creates technical inaccuracies when the naming history is examined at higher resolution.

**Fast-food menu archaeology and institutional memory:** The Bell Beefer case also illustrates a broader feature of fast-food corporate history: the radical incompleteness of the publicly accessible record. Major American fast-food chains have existed for decades and had multi-thousand-item menu histories, but systematic archiving of menu items, prices, regional variants, and temporal sequencing is fragmented and inconsistent. Much of what is known about historical fast-food menus derives from consumer archive projects (Newspapers.com, Flickr, fandom wikis), oral histories and podcasts like the Cheesy Gordita Bunch, and occasional journalist investigations, rather than from systematic corporate archiving. Taco Bell confirmed Bell Beefer history through a PR representative's podcast appearance — not through any publicly accessible official product history document.

The absence of Bell Beefer television commercials in the documented record is a specific illustration of this archival gap: the Bell Beefer ran on Taco Bell menus for approximately two decades across what was a period of explosive fast-food industry growth and heavy television advertising. That no commercial has been found does not mean none existed — it means either that fast-food television advertising from this era was not comprehensively archived, that the Bell Beefer specifically did not receive national broadcast attention, or that existing archives have not been systematically searched. The epistemic appropriate response is to note the absence of evidence while acknowledging the imperfection of the archival record.

**Nostalgia as epistemically active force in claim generation:** The Bell Beefer claim is not propagated by bad-faith actors pursuing financial or political goals — unlike the advertising arbitrage claims or the Youngevity product promotion claims examined in this series. It is propagated by genuine consumer nostalgia and the social amplification that follows from food-community identity formation around discontinued products. The question of whether the Bell Beefer was an original Taco Bell item is, for its advocates, a question of legitimacy — a claim that is worth making because it elevates the Bell Beefer from a mere discontinued menu curiosity to a founding-era artifact with the deepest possible roots in the brand's history. The slightly overstated version of the claim ("the Bell Beefer was an original offering") is epistemically motivated by affective investment, not epistemic negligence.

Research on nostalgia and consumer product attachment finds that consumers who form strong identities around specific discontinued products assign elevated historical status to those products — overestimating their vintage, their uniqueness, and their centrality to the brand's identity (Holbrook, 1993, *Journal of Consumer Research*). The Bell Beefer campaign exemplifies this pattern: for its devoted advocates, the Bell Beefer is not merely a discontinued item but a founding artifact whose absence represents a rupture in Taco Bell's authentic identity.

## 4. The Evidence in Full

1. **Original Taco Bell menu (1962):** Five items at 19 cents each — frijoles, tostados, burritos (red/green), tacos, and chili burgers. Documented by NewsNation (2022) and KTLA (2022), consistent with available historical records.

2. **Glen Bell as founder:** Glen Bell founded Taco Bell in Downey, California, 1962. The franchise's market valuation exceeded $33 billion by 2023.

3. **Three-name product continuity:** Taco Bell head of public relations Matt Prince confirmed in a podcast (Cheesy Gordita Bunch, Episode 41) that the chili burger, Bell Burger, and Bell Beefer are the same product with different names from different eras.

4. **Rebranding timeline:**
   - Chili burger (1962–early 1970s)
   - Bell Burger (early 1970s–1977): documented in 1974 Minnesota newspaper clipping (Newspapers.com)
   - Bell Beefer (1977–mid-1990s): documented in 1980s Taco Bell menu image (Flickr), priced at 89 cents

5. **Bell Beefer discontinued mid-1990s:** Documented in Taco Bell fandom wiki; not available at any U.S. location as of October 2023.

6. **No television commercials found:** The Snopes investigation found no TV commercials specifically advertising the Bell Beefer in available historical archives.

7. **Active fan campaign documented:** Facebook group, Change.org petition, Stank Festival sit-ins (San Francisco, 2022), podcast episode, vegan recreation by "Thee Burger Dude" food blog.

8. **Parallel to Mexican Pizza:** Taco Bell's Mexican Pizza revival (2022) following fan pressure, as context for understanding the Bell Beefer restoration campaign.

## 5. What Remains Uncertain

**Whether TV commercials for the Bell Beefer exist:** The absence of documented television commercials advertising the Bell Beefer across a twenty-year menu run is historically unusual. Fast-food chains were heavy television advertisers during the 1970s–1990s period. Whether no commercials were filmed, whether they were filmed but not archived, or whether existing archives are simply incomplete is undetermined.

**The exact discontinuation date:** Sources describe the Bell Beefer as having been phased out "by the mid-1990s." The precise date(s) of regional or national removal from the menu has not been established from primary documentation.

**Whether the Bell Beefer has appeared as a limited-run or regional revival:** Taco Bell periodically tests discontinued items in specific markets or for limited promotional windows. Whether any Bell Beefer test or limited revival has occurred since the mid-1990s official discontinuation is not documented in available sources.

**EARC classification:** Classified C (Content/Consensus). The claim is Mostly True — the founding menu included the direct antecedent product (chili burger) under a different name, and the corporate authority (Taco Bell PR head) confirms the product continuity. The technical imprecision involves equating the Bell Beefer brand name with the underlying product concept.

## 6. Verdict and Further Reading

**Verdict: MOSTLY TRUE.** The founding Taco Bell menu in 1962 included a product called the "chili burger" — a seasoned beef sloppy-joe-style sandwich on a hamburger bun — that is the direct antecedent of what became known as the Bell Beefer. Taco Bell's own head of PR confirmed that the chili burger, Bell Burger, and Bell Beefer are the same product, renamed across different decades. The Bell Beefer name itself was only adopted in 1977, making the strict claim — that the Bell Beefer was among Taco Bell's first offerings — technically imprecise: the product concept was there from day one, but the Bell Beefer name was a 1977 coinage, fifteen years after founding. The item was phased out by the mid-1990s and had not returned to the menu as of October 2023, despite an active and documented fan campaign including petitions, Facebook groups, sit-in protests, and podcast coverage. No television commercials advertising the Bell Beefer were found in the available historical record despite its approximately twenty-year menu tenure.
