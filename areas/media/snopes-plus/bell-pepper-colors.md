## 1. The Claim

The claim circulates every few years on social media, typically dressed up as a mind-blowing food revelation: those red, yellow, orange, and green bell peppers you see at the supermarket are all "the same pepper" — just harvested at different stages of ripeness. The green ones are unripe; the red ones are the fully mature version. A 2018 tweet from a lifestyle blogger put it most bluntly, saying bell peppers are all "the same pepper just less ripe." The post went viral, receiving millions of shares and generating confident, declarative agreement in comment sections. The idea resurfaced again in July 2024 when a viral X post garnered more than 2.5 million views with the same assertion.

The intuitive appeal is obvious. It feels like the kind of thing that "they never told you in school" — a secret botanical truth hiding in plain sight at the produce aisle. The claim implies you've been overpaying for ripe red peppers when you could simply wait for greens to ripen at home, or that there is fundamentally no difference between any of the varieties except the timing of harvest.

The reality is considerably more interesting, and considerably more complicated.

## 2. What's Actually True

### The Part That's Correct

Let's begin with what the viral claim gets right, because it isn't entirely wrong.

All bell peppers sold in the United States under the "bell pepper" market label belong to a single species: *Capsicum annuum*. The genus *Capsicum* contains more than 30 species, but *C. annuum* is by far the most economically important and widely cultivated, with its native range tracing back to tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas, particularly Bolivia, Peru, Brazil, and Mexico.

Within *C. annuum*, bell peppers are classified as the *Grossum Group*, characterized by large, blocky fruit with three or four lobes, thick walls, and low (or absent) capsaicin content — which is why they lack the heat of their *C. annuum* cousins like jalapeños, serranos, and cayennes.

So: yes, all commonly sold bell peppers are the same species.

It is also true that color can be influenced by ripeness. Green bell peppers are, in most commercially grown varieties, harvested before full maturity. The green color comes from chlorophyll, the same photosynthetic pigment found in leaves. As the fruit matures on the plant, chlorophyll breaks down and other pigments emerge, particularly carotenoids — giving peppers yellows, oranges, and reds. Mississippi State University Extension has confirmed this basic pattern: "Green bell peppers appear first and are the least ripe. As they mature, they usually turn to another color. This may be yellow, orange, red, or purple."

So the claim's core premise — that a green pepper *can* eventually ripen to another color — is correct, at least for many varieties.

### The Larger Truth That Gets Erased

Here is where the viral claim becomes misleading: it treats ripeness as the sole determinant of color, when in reality *genetic breeding* is an equally fundamental and often overriding factor.

James Meyers, an endowed professor of plant breeding at Oregon State University's College of Agricultural Sciences, explains the mechanics clearly. Bell pepper colors can be sorted into two categories: **immature colors** and **mature colors**. Immature colors range from white to dark green — but even within immature green, the depth and quality of that green is controlled by a gene called the "chlorophyll retainer." Different cultivars have different expressions of this gene.

When you get to mature colors — the final, fully ripe color of the fruit — you are looking at carotenoid chemistry directed by genetics:

- **Red bell peppers** owe their color to carotenoids like capsanthin and capsorubin, produced by the *Capsanthin-Capsorubin Synthase (Ccs)* gene. A mutation at this locus prevents those pigments from forming, and the pepper ripens yellow or orange instead.
- **Yellow bell peppers** contain carotenoids like violaxanthin; a recessive mutation at the *Ccs* gene ("y" locus) is responsible.
- **Orange bell peppers** can result from elevated β-carotene due to mutations in the *β-Carotene Hydroxylase 2 (BCH2)* gene.
- **Purple and black bell peppers** get their dark colors from anthocyanins — pigments from an entirely different chemical class — visible even in the immature fruit. Varieties like 'Purple Beauty' live in this space.
- **Brown or "chocolate" bell peppers** (like 'Chocolate Beauty') develop because the chlorophyll retainer (*cl*) mutation prevents normal chlorophyll breakdown: if the variety would otherwise ripen red, it ends up a rich brown-maroon.
- **White bell peppers** exist too, produced by cultivars that essentially lack carotenoids in ripe fruit.

Each of these color outcomes is largely encoded in the genome of the cultivar through deliberate selective breeding — not simply waiting longer on the vine. This is the crucial distinction the viral claim elides.

### What You Cannot Do at Home

A practical implication underscores the error: if you buy green bell peppers and leave them on your counter, hoping they'll ripen to red, the result depends entirely on which *variety* of green pepper you bought. If you happened to buy a variety bred to mature red, yes, it will eventually turn red — but it will also become soft and potentially rot before it gets there without proper growing conditions. If the supermarket green pepper you bought was a variety bred primarily for green harvest, it may never fully develop a rich red carotenoid profile because the plant's genetics weren't optimized for that outcome.

Some green bell pepper cultivars are specifically developed for flavor and structural quality *at the green stage* — the genetic programming for mature color was not the breeding goal.

Meyers notes that "all these traits are under separate genetic control and can be mixed and matched to produce cultivars with different characteristics." The bell pepper you buy in a store has a genetic ancestry shaped by decades of deliberate cross-breeding — for color, sweetness, wall thickness, disease resistance, and shelf life — not by letting nature take its course.

### Taste Differences Are Real, Not Just Ripeness

The flavor differences between colors are not merely a matter of ripeness affecting sugar levels. While it's true that green peppers (harvested early) are less sweet and have a slightly bitter, tangy edge because they haven't yet converted starches to sugars, yellow and orange peppers have distinct flavor profiles rooted in their specific carotenoid chemistry. North Carolina State Extension reports that orange peppers have a "tangy, fruit taste" with high juice content, while red peppers have "a nice flavor when grilled" and yellow peppers deliver sweetness with "high nutritional content." These aren't just ripeness effects — they reflect genuinely different biochemical compositions across cultivars.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The bell pepper ripeness claim is a perfect example of what we might call **partial-truth virality**. It contains a true claim (all are *C. annuum*; color is influenced by ripeness) embedded in a false generalization (color is *only* about ripeness; all peppers are interchangeable).

The appeal is structural. The claim offers the pleasure of secret knowledge: you've been duped by the produce industry; things aren't what they seem; the colorful peppers in the store are a marketing fiction. It's satisfying to believe you've pierced the veil.

There's also a human tendency to overgeneralize from simple categories. If you've ever watched a green tomato ripen to red, or a green banana go yellow and then brown, the idea that green = unripe and everything else = ripe is well-conditioned. The bell pepper world is messier — genetics and ripeness interact — but people apply the simpler mental model.

Additionally, food labeling and display conventions obscure the complexity. Supermarkets rarely distinguish bell pepper varieties by cultivar name; they simply sort by color and price. The consumer has no reason to suspect that the red pepper and the orange pepper are not the same cultivar at different stages, because there's no information in the retail environment to suggest otherwise.

## 4. Verdict

**Mixture — mostly misleading.** The claim is grounded in genuine botanical fact: all US bell peppers are *Capsicum annuum*, and ripeness does affect color in many varieties. But the claim that they are *all* "the same pepper just less ripe" is demonstrably false. Color in bell peppers is co-determined by genetics and breeding, and many color varieties have been specifically developed through selective cultivation, not simply allowed to ripen longer. The genes controlling red, orange, yellow, purple, white, and chocolate-brown bell peppers are distinct and heritable — they cannot be achieved merely by adjusting harvest timing.

Snopes rated this claim a "Mixture," which is accurate if somewhat charitable. The kernel of truth is real but the dominant takeaway from the viral posts — that pepper color is purely a ripeness effect — is incorrect and misleads people about the complexity of modern food plant genetics.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The bell pepper story is a window into the hidden science of domesticated plant genetics — one of the most consequential and underappreciated fields in human history.

*Capsicum annuum* has been cultivated and selectively bred for thousands of years. Archaeological evidence places *Capsicum* cultivation in Mexico as far back as 6,000 years ago. Modern bell pepper varieties are the product of intensive 20th-century agricultural breeding programs designed to produce predictable color, uniform shape, consistent wall thickness, disease resistance, and shelf stability. The "California Wonder" cultivar, for example, introduced in 1928, became the standard for the large red and green bell pepper market.

The genetics of pepper pigmentation is now a subject of active scientific research, with implications for nutritional science as well as agriculture. Carotenoids in red and orange peppers — including beta-carotene, lycopene, capsanthin, and zeaxanthin — are associated with antioxidant activity and vitamin A precursors in the human diet. Understanding which genes produce which carotenoids, and in what quantities, informs breeding programs aimed at developing nutritionally optimized crop varieties.

The same genetic architecture that creates bell pepper color diversity is at work across the entire Capsicum genus. The characteristic red of a ripe chili pepper comes from the same pigment pathways. The white, yellow, and brown variants that appear in heirloom Capsicum collections reflect natural and intentional genetic variation — and serve as a reminder that "color = ripeness" is never quite as simple as it looks.

There's also an economic dimension. Red bell peppers typically cost significantly more than green ones in retail markets, precisely because they require *more* time on the vine to reach their mature color — which means more resources (water, labor, field space, fungicide risk) per unit of yield. The cost difference is real and is driven by production economics, not marketing fiction. If the ripeness-only claim were true, farmers would simply leave all their green peppers on the vine longer and sell them as reds at higher profit — but the cultivars best suited to green harvest are not identical to those bred for red maturity.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published a thorough fact-check in July 2024, consulting James Meyers at Oregon State University directly. Their "Mixture" rating reflects the genuine complexity here: the simplest version of the claim (same species, color influenced by ripeness) is not wrong, but the popular takeaway (all peppers are just ripening versions of the same thing) erases critical genetic nuance.

The fact-check does solid work by sourcing an actual plant breeding expert rather than relying solely on secondary sources. The Meyers interview is the anchor that gives the piece authority beyond Wikipedia-level synthesis.

One area where an independent analysis can add value: the Snopes piece doesn't fully develop the *consumer implication* angle — namely, that the "just let it ripen" advice commonly appended to the viral claim is not reliably actionable. The variety you buy at a supermarket as a green pepper is usually selected for green-stage quality, and may not produce a high-quality fully ripe pepper if left on a counter. The genetics aren't present to guarantee it. This is a practical caveat that matters for anyone who took the viral claim as a grocery-shopping strategy.

There is also a broader epistemological lesson here about the nature of partial-truth claims. The bell pepper claim does not survive botanical scrutiny because it conflates *necessary condition* with *sufficient condition*: the claim is "color = ripeness" when the actual relationship is "color is influenced by *both* ripeness and genetics, and neither alone determines the outcome." This structural confusion — treating one causal factor as the only one — is one of the most common mechanisms of misinformation, and it rarely announces itself as such.
