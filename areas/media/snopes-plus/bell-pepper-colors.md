# Bell Peppers Are All the Same Plant, Just Different Colors


## 1. The Claim

The claim, which resurfaces cyclically on social media with the reliability of a perennial crop, asserts that green, yellow, orange, and red bell peppers are not different varieties but are the same pepper harvested at different stages of ripeness. Green peppers are merely "unripe" versions of the colorful peppers sitting beside them in the produce aisle. The implication is straightforward: if you simply left a green pepper on the vine long enough, it would transform into a yellow, then an orange, then a red bell pepper — a botanical metamorphosis as elegant as it is misleading.

The claim gained mainstream traction in 2018 when the lifestyle blog Call Me Amy posted on X (then Twitter) that all bell peppers are "the same pepper just less ripe." The post went viral, and the idea has resurfaced regularly since, including a July 2024 post that garnered over 2.5 million views. The claim has also appeared in countless TikTok videos, Instagram infographics, and the kind of "mind-blowing facts" listicles that populate social media feeds when users run out of celebrity gossip.

At its simplest, the claim contains three embedded assertions: (1) all bell peppers belong to the same species; (2) color variation is entirely a function of ripeness; and (3) any green pepper, left unharvested, would eventually turn red. The first assertion is broadly correct but incomplete; the second is a distortion; and the third is demonstrably false for many cultivar lines. The resulting "fact" is a classic example of how a partial truth, stripped of its qualifying context, becomes functionally misleading.

What makes this particular myth interesting is not that it is spectacularly wrong — it isn't. It's that it's wrong in exactly the way that feels like it should be right. The idea that nature operates through a simple ripening gradient is aesthetically pleasing, conceptually tidy, and profoundly incomplete.


## 2. What's Actually True

The relationship between bell pepper color and biology is considerably more intricate than the viral shorthand suggests. Understanding it properly requires unpacking several layers of botanical genetics, agricultural breeding history, and the biochemistry of plant pigmentation.

### Species Identity: Same Species, Many Cultivars

The starting point is taxonomically accurate: bell peppers sold in the United States belong to the species *Capsicum annuum*, a member of the Solanaceae (nightshade) family that also includes tomatoes, potatoes, and eggplants. This species is native to the tropical and subtropical Americas, with the greatest genetic diversity concentrated in Bolivia, Peru, Brazil, and Mexico. The genus *Capsicum* comprises over thirty species, but *C. annuum* is by far the most widely cultivated and economically important.

However, saying all bell peppers are the "same species" obscures as much as it reveals. *Capsicum annuum* encompasses an enormous range of cultivated varieties — cultivars — that have been selectively bred over centuries for specific traits. The bell pepper "market class" itself is, according to James Meyers, an endowed professor of agriculture at Oregon State University, "generally described as large blocky fruit with three or four lobes, thick walls, and low capsaicin content." But within that market class, the genetic diversity is substantial. There are baby bells bred for small fruit size, high-pungency bell cultivars that challenge the typical "sweet pepper" assumption, and varieties with heart-shaped profiles that deviate from the classic blocky form.

The analogy to human biodiversity is instructive: all living humans belong to the species *Homo sapiens*, yet the phenotypic diversity within that species — height, skin color, facial structure, metabolic characteristics — is enormous and genetically determined. Saying bell peppers are "the same" because they share a species designation is approximately as useful as saying all dogs are the same because they're all *Canis lupus familiaris*.

### The Ripeness Factor: Partially True

Some cultivars of *C. annuum* do indeed change color as they mature. A green bell pepper of certain varieties is an immature fruit that, if left on the vine, will transition through intermediate stages to a mature color — often red. In this narrow sense, the viral claim is correct: some green peppers are indeed "unripe" red peppers.

The maturation sequence typically follows a predictable biochemical pattern. Immature peppers appear green because of the presence of chlorophyll in the fruit wall. As the fruit matures, chlorophyll degrades and carotenoid pigments accumulate. The specific carotenoids present determine the final color: capsanthin and capsorubin produce red, β-carotene and zeaxanthin produce orange, and lutein and violaxanthin contribute to yellow coloration. This chlorophyll-to-carotenoid transition is accompanied by changes in sugar content, flavor compounds, and vitamin levels — which is why red bell peppers taste sweeter than green ones and contain substantially more vitamin C and vitamin A precursors.

Mississippi State University Extension confirms this basic trajectory: "Green bell peppers appear first and are the least ripe. As they mature, they usually turn to another color. This may be yellow, orange, red, or purple." This is the kernel of truth that the viral claim correctly seizes upon.

### The Genetic Factor: Where the Claim Breaks Down

Here is where the viral oversimplification collapses. Pepper color is not solely a function of maturity — it is under independent genetic control. According to Professor Meyers, "all these traits are under separate genetic control and can be mixed and matched to produce cultivars with different characteristics." Some bell pepper varieties are specifically bred for their coloring, and their mature fruit color is genetically fixed regardless of how long the fruit remains on the vine.

The genetics of pepper pigmentation involve multiple loci:

**Immature fruit color** ranges from white to dark green, controlled in part by a gene called "chlorophyll retainer" that determines how much chlorophyll accumulates in the developing fruit. A pepper with low chlorophyll retention will appear white or ivory at the immature stage, while one with high retention will be dark green.

**Mature fruit color** is determined by an entirely separate set of genes governing carotenoid biosynthesis. The *CCS* (capsanthin-capsorubin synthase) gene is crucial: functional alleles produce red pigments, while loss-of-function mutations redirect the carotenoid pathway toward yellow or orange end products. The *y* locus controls overall carotenoid accumulation — recessive homozygotes produce dramatically less carotenoid, yielding mature fruits that remain pale or white.

**Anthocyanin accumulation** adds another dimension entirely. Purple or black-skinned peppers owe their coloration to anthocyanin pigments, which operate through a completely independent biochemical pathway from the carotenoids. These pigments are typically present in immature fruit and may fade as the pepper matures to its final carotenoid-determined color.

**Patterning genes** control the distribution of pigments within the fruit wall. Some cultivars display striping, variegation, or gradient coloration that has nothing to do with ripeness and everything to do with spatial gene expression patterns.

The practical upshot: a variety bred to produce yellow mature fruit (such as 'Golden California Wonder') will never become red no matter how long it stays on the vine. Its carotenoid biosynthetic pathway is genetically configured to produce yellow pigments, not red ones. Similarly, a variety bred to produce white mature fruit (such as 'White Cloud') lacks the carotenoid accumulation genes necessary to produce any warm color at all, and a black pepper variety owes its appearance to anthocyanins that have no ripeness-dependent pathway to red.

### Nutritional Differences Are Real

The color differences between peppers correspond to genuine nutritional variation, not merely aesthetic preference. Red bell peppers contain roughly eleven times more β-carotene (a vitamin A precursor) than green peppers and nearly twice the vitamin C. They also contain significantly more lycopene, an antioxidant associated with cardiovascular health benefits. Yellow and orange peppers fall between green and red on most nutritional metrics, with orange peppers being particularly rich in zeaxanthin, a carotenoid important for eye health.

These nutritional differences exist both because of ripeness effects (carotenoids accumulate during maturation) and because of genetic differences between cultivars optimized for different color profiles. The practical dietary consequence is the same either way: eating a variety of pepper colors provides a broader spectrum of micronutrients than eating only one color.

### The Commercial Reality

In commercial agriculture, green bell peppers are typically cheaper than colored varieties for two compounding reasons. First, green peppers can be harvested earlier, reducing the time the plant occupies field or greenhouse space and lowering the risk of crop loss from disease, pests, or weather. Second, some green pepper cultivars are specifically bred for harvest at the immature green stage and never develop appealing mature colors — they may turn a muddy brownish-red rather than the bright, saturated colors consumers expect. These are not "the same" as the cultivars bred for vibrant red, yellow, or orange maturation.

The price difference in the grocery store thus reflects genuine agricultural economics, not merely the time cost of leaving a single variety on the vine longer. The green pepper in the value bin and the red pepper in the premium display may literally be different cultivars with different genetic backgrounds, different growth requirements, and different optimization targets.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of the "same pepper, different ripeness" myth illustrates several cognitive tendencies that make simplified botanical claims irresistible.

### The Parsimony Trap

Humans have a documented preference for simple explanations over complex ones — a heuristic that is often useful but can lead astray when reality is genuinely complex. "All bell peppers are the same, just picked at different times" is dramatically simpler than "bell pepper color is determined by the interaction of multiple genetic loci governing chlorophyll retention, carotenoid biosynthesis pathway configuration, anthocyanin accumulation, and pigment patterning, modulated by environmental conditions and maturation stage." The simple version fits in a tweet. The accurate version requires a botany lecture.

This cognitive preference is compounded by what psychologists call the "illusion of explanatory depth" — the tendency to believe we understand complex systems better than we actually do. Most people interact with bell peppers exclusively as consumers. They see green, yellow, orange, and red peppers in the grocery store and observe that they taste different, cost different amounts, and look like they could plausibly be the same object in different states. The ripeness narrative provides a satisfying explanation that requires no specialized knowledge to evaluate.

### The Grocery Store Arrangement

The physical layout of produce sections inadvertently reinforces the myth. Bell peppers of different colors are typically displayed together, often in adjacent bins arranged in a gradient from green to red. This visual arrangement mirrors a ripening sequence, even when the peppers in any given bin were grown from entirely different seed varieties. Supermarket design teams optimize for visual appeal, not botanical accuracy, and the color-gradient display sells more peppers than randomly mixed bins would.

### The Tomato Analogy

Most people are familiar with the fact that tomatoes ripen from green to red — a genuine color-maturation sequence within a single fruit. The pepper myth essentially extends this known pattern to a closely related plant family (both are Solanaceae), creating a plausible-sounding analogy that happens to be only partially correct. The analogy is further reinforced by the experience of home gardeners, many of whom have watched individual pepper fruits change color on the vine — a real phenomenon that the myth incorrectly generalizes to all color variation.

### The "Mind-Blown" Economy

Social media platforms reward content that produces a sense of surprise or revelation. The bell pepper color claim belongs to a genre of viral content — "things you thought you knew but didn't" — that is optimized for shares and engagement rather than accuracy. The claim's virality is self-reinforcing: each time it goes viral, it reaches a new audience that finds it surprising, validates it through sharing, and embeds it deeper into the collective understanding.

The 2.5 million views on the July 2024 X post demonstrate the claim's enduring viral fitness. Each cycle of virality produces a new cohort of believers who will, years later, confidently tell friends at dinner parties that "all peppers are the same, you know — just different ripeness levels."

### Cultural Detachment from Agriculture

In societies where most consumers have no direct experience with crop cultivation, botanical knowledge becomes entirely mediated by secondary sources. Few people have observed pepper plants growing from seed to harvest, compared different cultivar lines side by side, or consulted a plant genetics textbook. In this knowledge vacuum, viral claims fill the role that agricultural experience once occupied. The pepper myth thrives precisely because its audience lacks the experiential baseline to evaluate it.


## 4. Verdict

**Distorted but grounded.** The claim contains a genuine botanical observation — some bell pepper varieties do change color as they ripen, and green peppers of those varieties are indeed less mature than their colored counterparts — but it fatally oversimplifies the genetics of *Capsicum annuum* by reducing all color variation to a single maturation variable. The claim ignores the extensive breeding programs that produce genetically distinct cultivars optimized for specific colors, the multiple independent genetic pathways that determine pepper pigmentation, and the commercial agricultural reality that green and colored peppers in the grocery store are often entirely different varieties.

Snopes rated this claim "Mixture," which is accurate as far as it goes but somewhat underweights the extent of the distortion. The claim is not merely a mix of true and false elements; it is a genuine observation (ripening affects color) inflated into a universal principle (all color variation = ripening) that misrepresents the actual genetics. This is a characteristic "distorted-but-grounded" epistemic structure: the claim could not exist without a real phenomenon at its core, but the popular version has been stretched far beyond what the evidence supports.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The bell pepper color myth is a minor entry in a much larger category of folk botanical beliefs that attribute complex genetic variation to simple environmental or developmental processes. This category includes claims like "all citrus fruits are the same species" (a more defensible assertion, given the complex hybridization history of *Citrus*), "seedless watermelons are genetically modified" (they are not — they are triploid hybrids produced through conventional breeding), and "organic produce is genetically identical to conventional produce" (it uses the same cultivars but not always, and cultivation practices affect gene expression).

### The Cultivar Concept Gap

At the heart of the pepper myth lies a broader public misunderstanding of what plant breeding has accomplished. Modern agriculture relies on thousands of cultivated varieties — cultivars — developed through decades or centuries of selective breeding. Each cultivar represents a specific combination of genetic traits optimized for particular growing conditions, market preferences, disease resistance profiles, and shelf-life characteristics. The concept of the cultivar is as central to modern agriculture as the concept of the breed is to animal husbandry, yet it remains almost entirely absent from public discourse about food.

When consumers encounter bell peppers in the grocery store, they see "green pepper" and "red pepper" as if these were fundamental categories. They do not see 'Blitz,' 'Revolution,' 'Golden California Wonder,' 'Chocolate Beauty,' 'White Cloud,' or any of hundreds of named cultivars, each with its own genetic profile, growth characteristics, and color trajectory. The cultivar label — the most informative piece of information about a pepper's biology — is stripped away at the point of sale, replaced by a simple color descriptor that invites exactly the kind of oversimplification the viral myth provides.

### What Breeding Has Actually Done

The history of pepper breeding is a story of remarkable human ingenuity in manipulating plant genetics through conventional (non-GMO) methods. Breeders have developed cultivars that produce blocky fruit in nearly every color imaginable: not just the familiar green-yellow-orange-red spectrum, but also ivory white, chocolate brown, deep purple approaching black, and even variegated patterns that display multiple colors simultaneously.

This chromatic diversity has been achieved by selecting for specific allelic combinations at the loci governing carotenoid biosynthesis, anthocyanin production, chlorophyll degradation, and pigment patterning. Each color represents a different configuration of the same underlying biochemical machinery — analogous to how different breeds of dog represent different configurations of the *Canis* genome, producing enormous phenotypic diversity within a single species.

The achievement is particularly impressive because breeders must optimize for multiple traits simultaneously. A commercially successful pepper cultivar needs not just the right color, but also adequate fruit size, wall thickness, disease resistance, heat tolerance, shelf life, and flavor profile. Color is just one variable in a multidimensional optimization problem, and achieving a specific color target while maintaining acceptable performance on all other metrics requires sophisticated breeding programs spanning many plant generations.

### The Simplification Pipeline

The pepper myth also illustrates how scientific information degrades as it passes through successive layers of popularization. The original botanical fact — "within certain *C. annuum* cultivars, chlorophyll degradation and carotenoid accumulation during fruit maturation produce a color change from green to a genetically determined mature color" — is accurate but unwieldy. Popular science articles simplify this to "green peppers are unripe colored peppers." Social media distills it further to "all peppers are the same, just different ripeness." Each compression step loses qualifying information, and the endpoint bears only a superficial resemblance to the starting point.

This informational compression is not unique to pepper biology — it is a universal feature of how scientific knowledge enters public consciousness. The same pipeline produces claims like "we only use 10% of our brain" (a distortion of neuroimaging studies showing specialized regional activation), "lightning never strikes twice" (a misunderstanding of probability that Benjamin Franklin could have debunked), and "evolution means survival of the fittest" (a drastic oversimplification coined by Herbert Spencer, not Darwin).

### Nutritional Implications

Understanding the true genetics of pepper color matters practically because it affects dietary advice. If all pepper colors were merely ripeness stages of the same cultivar, then the nutritional differences between them would be entirely attributable to maturation — and a consumer seeking maximum vitamin C could simply wait for the greenest pepper to ripen at home. In reality, the nutritional profile of a pepper reflects both its maturation stage and its cultivar-specific genetic endowment. Different cultivar lines produce different baseline levels of carotenoids, ascorbic acid, and other micronutrients, and these genetic differences interact with maturation effects in complex ways.

For consumers seeking to optimize nutritional intake from bell peppers, the accurate advice is not "buy green peppers and let them ripen" (many cultivars harvested green will not develop appealing colors or optimal nutrition at home) but rather "eat a variety of pepper colors from cultivars bred for each color" — which, coincidentally, is what most grocery stores already facilitate by stocking multiple cultivar-specific color options.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated this claim "Mixture" based on consultation with James Meyers of Oregon State University and supporting evidence from North Carolina State Extension and Mississippi State University Extension. This is a methodologically sound approach: the claim's partially-true structure makes a binary True/False verdict inappropriate, and consulting a domain expert in pepper genetics ensures that the assessment reflects current scientific understanding rather than popular simplification.

The Snopes article correctly identifies both the true element (ripeness does affect color in some varieties) and the false element (genetics play an independent and often dominant role in color determination). However, the article does not fully explore the extent of genetic diversity within bell pepper cultivars or the implications for the "same species" framing. The species-level identity is treated as the primary true element, when in fact the species designation is the least informative axis of comparison. Many cultivar-to-cultivar genetic differences within *C. annuum* produce more dramatic phenotypic variation than species-to-species differences in some other plant genera.

The article also does not address the psychological mechanisms that make this claim so persistent — the parsimony preference, the grocery store arrangement effect, and the "mind-blown" economy that rewards oversimplified factoids. Understanding why a claim persists is as important as understanding whether it is true, because debunking without psychological insight rarely changes minds.

### The "Mixture" Challenge

The "Mixture" rating illustrates a recurring challenge in fact-checking: how to handle claims that are partially true in ways that make the true parts seem to validate the false parts. The bell pepper claim's true element (ripeness affects color) is experientially verifiable — anyone who has grown peppers has seen this happen — which gives the entire claim an aura of empirical support. The false element (all color variation = ripeness) is harder to disprove experientially because most consumers never grow multiple cultivars side by side.

This asymmetry between verifiable-true-part and hard-to-disprove-false-part is a characteristic feature of claims that earn "Mixture" ratings. The true part provides immunization against quick debunking ("but I've SEEN my green peppers turn red!"), while the false part requires specialized knowledge to identify. Effective fact-checking of such claims needs not just a verdict but a clear explanation of *which* part is true, *which* part is false, and *why* the true part does not validate the false part — exactly what the Snopes article attempts, with reasonable success.

### What Good Coverage Would Add

An ideal treatment of this claim would include visual comparison of different cultivar lines at the same maturation stage (demonstrating that genetics, not just ripeness, determines color), a simplified genetic diagram showing the independent loci controlling immature color, mature color, and anthocyanin accumulation, and an acknowledgment that the claim's viral persistence reflects not just ignorance but a genuine gap in how agricultural knowledge reaches consumers. The cultivar concept — so central to modern food production — deserves a public profile commensurate with its importance. Until it gets one, the grocery aisle will continue to be a museum of invisible genetics, and oversimplified viral claims will continue to fill the explanatory vacuum.
