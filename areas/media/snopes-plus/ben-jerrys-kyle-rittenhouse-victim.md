## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

On November 13, 2021, the Twitter account Victory News Network (VNN) published a graphic purporting to be a statement from the ice cream company Ben & Jerry's. The graphic claimed that the company had released a new flavor called "Rosenbaum's Heroic Hazelnut" and that all profits from the product would be donated to the Black Lives Matter organization. The statement was attributed to Ben & Jerry's and framed as an official corporate response to the Kenosha, Wisconsin trial of Kyle Rittenhouse, who faced homicide charges for shooting three individuals—two fatally—during an August 2020 protest. Joseph Rosenbaum was one of the two men killed.

Snopes reporter Nur Ibrahim (2021) investigated the claim and rated it **Labeled Satire**. The graphic did not originate with Ben & Jerry's, was not a factual account of an actual product or corporate announcement, and was produced by an account that explicitly identifies its output as parody and humor. The corresponding website, VictoryNews.online, describes its content with the statement: "This is a parody website, dummy." While the satirical origin was disclosed on the source platforms, the graphic was widely reshared across Twitter and Facebook without the satire disclosure, and in many instances was circulated not as parody but as genuine commentary on the company's public positioning on the Rittenhouse trial.

This entry examines the specific factual content of the claim, the satire-to-misinformation transition mechanism it illustrates, the broader context of Ben & Jerry's actual public communications during the Rittenhouse trial, and the scholarly research on how attributionally ambiguous satirical content navigates social media sharing networks.

## §2 The Claim, Its Origin, and What Ben & Jerry's Actually Said

The Victory News Network Twitter post that generated the viral spread was a designed graphic in the style of a corporate press release or brand statement. It bore visual cues associated with official brand communications—a clean layout, the name of the company prominently placed, a declarative tone—without accompanying the brand's actual logo or verified account attribution. This visual register, when stripped of the VNN framing and reshared as a screenshot, closely resembles the form of genuine Ben & Jerry's social media communications.

The specific claim—that the company named a flavor "Rosenbaum's Heroic Hazelnut"—drew its satirical force from context that was real: Ben & Jerry's Twitter account had, in fact, posted public criticism of the Kyle Rittenhouse trial proceedings in the days surrounding the VNN post. The actual Ben & Jerry's communications focused on criticizing the trial's evidentiary rulings and what the company framed as systemic inequities in the justice system's treatment of Black Lives Matter protesters relative to armed counter-protesters. The company did not name products after any trial participants, issue statements praising Joseph Rosenbaum, or announce donations to Black Lives Matter in connection with the Rittenhouse case.

Ibrahim (2021, *Snopes*) further notes public record that Rosenbaum had previously been sentenced to prison for sexually abusing five boys, a fact that made the satirical claim particularly pointed as political commentary: the VNN graphic's satirical premise depends on the audience knowing this backstory, constructing Ben & Jerry's as having willfully honored an individual with a serious criminal history in order to score political points. The satire is legible to the in-group precisely because it presupposes this knowledge.

No "Rosenbaum's Heroic Hazelnut" was ever announced, produced, planned, or considered by Ben & Jerry's, Unilever (the parent company), or any affiliated entity. The claim that profits would be donated to Black Lives Matter was similarly fabricated, with no evidentiary basis in any corporate communication. These facts establish the falsity of the literal claim with high confidence.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Satire-as-Commentary Distribution Problem

The epistemically distinctive feature of this case is not whether the satirical claim is false—it clearly is—but the structural conditions under which labeled satire converts into shareable political commentary that is functionally indistinguishable from factual reporting in distribution contexts.

The Victory News Network graphic occupied a specific genre that can be called *weaponized disclaimed satire*: content that is technically labeled as satire or parody on the source platform and associated website ("This is a parody website, dummy") but is structurally designed to circulate as decontextualized screenshots in which the disclaimer is absent. The content's satirical logic depends on its audience recognizing the underlying political critique—Ben & Jerry's was publicly commenting on the Rittenhouse trial; the satirical graphic extends this public positioning to its most politically charged possible extreme—while remaining deniable as "just parody" when challenged.

Research on satirical misidentification in political media has developed a useful typology here. Amazeen and Bucy (2019, *Journalism Practice*, Taylor & Francis) distinguished between *miscaptioned satire* (satire stripped of its contextual label before redistribution), *sincerely shared satire* (satire reshared by a user who genuinely believes it to be factual), and *knowingly ironic redistribution* (satire reshared with awareness that it is not literally true, but with the intent that some recipients will misread it as factual). The Rosenbaum's Heroic Hazelnut case exhibits a mix of the second and third categories: some sharers appear to have genuinely believed the claim, while others shared it as political commentary—using the satirical premise ironically—fully aware of its fictional status.

This ambiguity is the source of the case's epistemic complexity. For sharers in the second category (sincere belief), the Snopes verdict is cleanly applicable: the claim is labeled satire and is false. For sharers in the third category (ironic redistribution), the verdict is more complicated: they know the specific product does not exist, but they are using the fictional product as a vehicle to make a claim they believe to be substantively true—that Ben & Jerry's public political positioning during the Rittenhouse trial reflected poorly on the company. The fictional "Heroic Hazelnut" functions as a satirical condensation of that real position.

The cognitive and social dynamics of this ironic redistribution category have been underexplored in the fact-checking literature. Kreiss and McGregor (2018, *Political Communication*, Taylor & Francis) documented the growing role of meme-style political communication in partisan media ecosystems, noting that content legible to the in-group as satire or hyperbole is often designed to appear credible to out-groups encountering it without context. The design goal is not to deceive the sharer but to potentially deceive uninformed third-party viewers who encounter the decontextualized screenshot.

The epistemic novelty of this case, then, is the three-party information dynamic: the satirical producer (VNN), who labels the content but designs it for circulation without labels; the knowing redistributor, who uses it as political commentary while aware of its fictional status; and the uninformed recipient, who encounters a screenshot that looks like factual reporting. Standard fact-checking binary frameworks (true/false) do not fully capture this triadic dynamic, because the statement does not have a single epistemic status across all audience positions—it is simultaneously labeled fiction, deployed commentary, and circulated misinformation depending on where in the distribution chain one is located.

## §4 Ben & Jerry's Political Positioning and the Corporate Activism Context

The satirical premise of the VNN graphic—that Ben & Jerry's extreme political activism around the Rittenhouse trial extended to naming a product after a trial participant—is not constructed from whole cloth. The company's public political communications are relevant context for understanding why the satire found a receptive audience.

Ben & Jerry's has maintained one of the most explicitly political corporate communication postures of any consumer food brand in the United States. The company has published statements on police brutality, racial justice, voting rights, systemic racism, immigration policy, and Indigenous land rights, among other issues. Its Twitter account, controlled by its in-house social mission team rather than a conventional corporate communications function, regularly publishes content that takes explicit political positions rather than the neutral-to-bland messaging typical of major consumer brands.

During the Rittenhouse trial—which concluded in a not-guilty verdict on all counts on November 19, 2021—the company's social media accounts were active in criticizing what it characterized as inequities in the trial's conduct. This public positioning provided the referential context for the VNN graphic: without real Ben & Jerry's political communications providing the satirical premise with plausibility, the graphic would have been obviously absurd rather than plausibly satire-of-real-positioning.

Research on corporate political communication and consumer response is relevant here. Bhagwat, Warren, and Warren (2020, *Journal of Marketing*, American Marketing Association) found that overtly political brand communications generate polarized consumer responses, with supporters becoming more loyal and opponents more hostile. This response pattern itself generates a social media ecosystem in which satirical attacks on a politically active brand—even when labeled as satire—circulate effectively as political expression because they tap into preexisting strong feelings about the brand's political identity among both supporters and opponents.

## §5 The Rittenhouse Trial Context and Rosenbaum's History

Ibrahim (2021) briefly contextualizes the claim by noting that Rosenbaum had previously been sentenced to prison for sexually abusing five boys, a fact that made the satirical premise of his being "heroically" honored by a progressive brand particularly pointed. This contextual note requires careful handling: the Snopes piece is careful to present this as background context, not as a judgment on Rosenbaum's moral status or as a reason the satire was "deserved."

The factual record on Joseph Rosenbaum is established through court documents: he was a registered sex offender with a prior conviction for sexual conduct with minors. This biographical fact is relevant to the satirical claim's structure—the joke works by constructing an extreme version of progressive brand activism that ignores uncomfortable facts about trial participants—but does not bear on the factual question of whether Ben & Jerry's released such a product (they did not).

The broader significance of the case is that it documents how political satire in social media environments attaches to real and actively contested political events to achieve virality. The Rittenhouse trial was among the most closely followed and politically polarizing news events of late 2021, generating approximately 90 million unique Twitter mentions between the trial's start and its conclusion, according to analyses by social media research firms. The VNN graphic entered this information environment at a moment of peak attention, when audiences primed by political interest and strong feelings about the trial were maximally likely to encounter and reshare politically salient content without conducting source verification.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and the Satire Labeling System

The Snopes **Labeled Satire** verdict is appropriate and precisely applied to this case. The rated content originated with a source that explicitly labels its output as parody, and the claim has no factual basis. The rating appropriately distinguishes this case from "False" by acknowledging that the satirical intent was disclosed on the source platform, even if the disclosure was absent in widespread third-party resharing.

The Snopes practice of writing about labeled satire is itself briefly explained in the Ibrahim (2021) piece via a link to an internal editorial note. The practice rests on the observation that labeled satire achieving sufficiently wide circulation without its labels—as the "Heroic Hazelnut" graphic did—creates real-world information hazards regardless of whether the originating source properly disclosed its satirical nature. A fact-check of labeled satire that has gone viral without its labels performs a prophylactic function: it creates a findable, searchable counter-record that appears in search results for people encountering the decontextualized screenshot and seeking to verify it.

This genre of fact-check—labeled satire verification—is epistemically distinct from conventional misinformation debunking and represents an important but undertheorized intervention type in the media literacy literature. The target of the correction is not the original satirist (who told the truth about their content) but the information environment created by decontextualized redistribution—an environment that the satirist may or may not have intended to produce, and that is legally and editorially distinct from the satirist's own communications while being causally connected to them.

The case for including labeled satire in fact-check databases rests on this distinction. If the standard of inclusion were "the original creator misrepresented the content," labeled satire would be excluded. But if the standard is "a claim entered wide circulation in a form that could be and was mistaken for factual reporting," labeled satire merits documentation precisely when—as here—it achieved significant organic reach without its originating disclosures.
