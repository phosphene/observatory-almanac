## 1. The Claim

The claim, which circulated widely on social media beginning around 2015 and resurfacing in amplified form in 2017, asserts that the SS *Cotopaxi* — a tramp steamer that departed Charleston, South Carolina on December 1, 1925 bound for Havana, Cuba, carrying a cargo of coal and a crew of thirty-two — mysteriously reappeared in Cuban territorial waters approximately ninety years after its disappearance. According to the narrative, the Cuban Coast Guard encountered the vessel drifting unmanned, its hull intact, with no trace of its crew but with the ship's log and cargo allegedly preserved. The story further claims that Cuban authorities and, in some versions, the United States Coast Guard confirmed the vessel's identity through hull markings and registration documents.

The claim's emotional architecture is carefully constructed. It grafts onto the Bermuda Triangle mythos — the loosely defined region of the western North Atlantic roughly bounded by Miami, Bermuda, and San Juan, Puerto Rico — which has served as a vessel for maritime mystery narratives since Vincent Gaddis coined the term "Bermuda Triangle" in a February 1964 article for *Argosy* magazine (Gaddis, V., 1964, "The Deadly Bermuda Triangle," *Argosy*). The *Cotopaxi* itself carries additional cultural freight: Steven Spielberg used the ship as a memorable Easter egg in *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* (1977), where it appears deposited in the Gobi Desert by extraterrestrial visitors — a fictional touch that subsequently fed back into the ship's popular mystique, blurring the line between cinematic invention and maritime folklore.

The specific article that launched the viral claim was published by World News Daily Report (WNDR), a website whose own disclaimer identified its content as satirical and fictional. WNDR operated as what media researchers classify as a "fake-news farm" — a site designed to generate advertising revenue through sensational, fabricated stories formatted to resemble legitimate journalism (Wardle, C. and Derakhshan, H., 2017, *Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policy Making*, Council of Europe). The *Cotopaxi* story was among its most successful products, achieving millions of shares across Facebook, Twitter, and WhatsApp, and spawning derivative articles on dozens of secondary clickbait sites that stripped out even the tenuous satirical framing.

## 2. What's Actually True

The factual substrate beneath this fabrication is well documented, and separating the real maritime history from its fictional accretions is both straightforward and instructive.

**The SS *Cotopaxi* was a real ship, and it really did disappear.** The vessel, a 2,350-ton steam-powered cargo ship built in 1918, departed Charleston on December 1, 1925 under Captain W. J. Meyer with thirty-two crew members. It was bound for Havana carrying a load of coal. Radio distress signals were received on December 1, indicating the ship was taking on water in heavy seas. No survivors were ever found, and the vessel was declared lost. Insurance claims were filed and settled. The disappearance was a genuine maritime tragedy — but it occurred during a documented tropical storm, not in calm or mysterious conditions.

**The ship's wreck has almost certainly been found.** In 2020, marine biologist and shipwreck researcher Michael Barnette led a team that identified a wreck sitting on the ocean floor approximately 35 nautical miles off St. Augustine, Florida. The wreck, previously catalogued as an unidentified object in NOAA survey data, matched the *Cotopaxi*'s known dimensions, construction profile, and timeline of loss. Barnette's identification was featured in the Science Channel documentary *Shipwreck Secrets* (2020). The wreck's location — on the sea floor, encrusted with marine growth, structurally degraded — is the definitive physical refutation of the "reappearance" claim. The *Cotopaxi* did not drift through some temporal anomaly; it sank in a storm and came to rest on the continental shelf, where it has remained for a century.

**The Bermuda Triangle itself is a manufactured mystery.** Lawrence David Kusche, a reference librarian and pilot, conducted the most systematic investigation of Bermuda Triangle claims, published as *The Bermuda Triangle Mystery — Solved* (Kusche, L.D., 1975, *The Bermuda Triangle Mystery — Solved*, Harper & Row). Kusche's method was straightforward and devastating: he cross-referenced Charles Berlitz's claims in *The Bermuda Triangle* (Berlitz, C., 1974, *The Bermuda Triangle*, Doubleday) against original Coast Guard reports, insurance records, weather data, and contemporaneous newspaper accounts. His findings revealed that Berlitz had systematically distorted the evidentiary record. Incidents that occurred far outside the triangle's boundaries were repositioned inside it. Ships and aircraft that disappeared during documented storms were described as having vanished in calm seas. Cases with known, mundane explanations were presented as unsolved. Some incidents were fabricated entirely.

**Lloyd's of London has never imposed a premium surcharge for vessels transiting the Bermuda Triangle region.** This actuarial fact, widely cited by skeptics and confirmed by Lloyd's own statements, represents perhaps the most parsimonious refutation of the Triangle hypothesis. Insurance underwriters, whose institutional survival depends on accurate risk assessment, have examined the loss data for this region and found no statistical anomaly warranting higher premiums. The U.S. Coast Guard has similarly stated that the number of incidents in the Bermuda Triangle is not disproportionate to any comparable region of heavy maritime traffic (United States Coast Guard, n.d., "Does the Bermuda Triangle really exist?," USCG Navigation Center FAQ).

**The *Cotopaxi* reappearance story conflates distinct maritime mysteries.** One genuinely puzzling case often entangled in Bermuda Triangle lore is that of the *Carroll A. Deering*, a five-masted commercial schooner found run aground on Diamond Shoals, off Cape Hatteras, North Carolina in late January 1921. When rescuers boarded, they found meals laid out in the galley, personal belongings undisturbed, lifeboats missing, and the crew entirely absent. The Deering case — investigated at the time by five federal agencies simultaneously — remains legitimately unresolved and has generated credible hypotheses involving piracy, rum-running hijacking, and mutiny (Hicks, B., 2003, *Ghost Ship: The Mysterious True Story of the Mary Celeste and Her Missing Crew*, Ballantine Books, provides comparative analysis of derelict vessel cases). But Diamond Shoals is off North Carolina, well outside even the generous boundaries of the Bermuda Triangle, and the Deering mystery has nothing to do with ships "reappearing."

## 3. Why People Believe This

The viral success of the *Cotopaxi* reappearance story is not attributable to any single cognitive bias; rather, it exploits a layered architecture of psychological vulnerabilities, cultural priming, and information-environment failures.

**Pre-existing narrative scaffolding.** The Bermuda Triangle operates as what cultural memory scholars call a "narrative schema" — a pre-built interpretive framework that organizes new information into familiar patterns (Wertsch, J.V., 2002, *Voices of Collective Remembering*, Cambridge University Press). When a story invokes the Bermuda Triangle, audiences do not evaluate it from an epistemic blank slate. They bring decades of accumulated cultural material: Berlitz's bestselling books (over 20 million copies sold in more than 30 languages), television documentaries, Hollywood films, and playground folklore. The schema converts the evaluation from "Is this plausible?" to "Does this fit the pattern?" — and a vanished ship reappearing fits the Bermuda Triangle pattern perfectly, even though that pattern itself was fabricated.

**The ghost ship archetype.** Maritime cultures worldwide harbor deep traditions of spectral vessels. The Flying Dutchman legend, attested in published form since at least the late eighteenth century, posits a ship condemned to sail the oceans eternally. Norse maritime folklore includes accounts of *draugr* ships crewed by the dead. The *Mary Celeste*, found derelict near the Azores in December 1872 with her crew missing but the vessel intact, became the template for the modern "ghost ship" narrative — though notably the *Mary Celeste* was found in the eastern Atlantic, nowhere near the Bermuda Triangle (Fay, C.E., 1942, *Mary Celeste: The Odyssey of an Abandoned Ship*, Peabody Museum). The *Cotopaxi* story activates this deeply rooted archetype: a ship lost at sea, returning from the void, crewless and inexplicable. The archetype's emotional resonance — the uncanny return of what was mourned as lost — predisposes audiences to want the story to be true, or at least to engage with it before critically evaluating it.

**Format mimicry and authority signaling.** The WNDR article was formatted to resemble a legitimate news report. It included fabricated quotes attributed to Cuban officials, referenced nonexistent government press releases, and employed the neutral, authoritative register of wire-service journalism. This format mimicry exploits what psychologists call "peripheral processing" — the tendency, identified in the Elaboration Likelihood Model (Petty, R.E. and Cacioppo, J.T., 1986, *Communication and Persuasion: Central and Peripheral Routes to Attitude Change*, Springer-Verlag), to evaluate messages based on surface cues (format, apparent authority, production quality) rather than argument quality when motivation or ability for careful evaluation is low. On social media, where content is encountered in rapid scroll and sharing is frictionless, peripheral processing dominates.

**The coverage-absence heuristic, inverted.** A genuinely reappearing ship — a vessel materializing intact after nine decades on the ocean floor — would constitute the single most extraordinary maritime event in recorded history. It would violate fundamental principles of physics, chemistry, and biology. It would be covered by every major news organization on Earth. The absence of such coverage from Reuters, the Associated Press, the BBC, *The New York Times*, or any credible outlet is itself dispositive evidence against the claim. Yet many consumers of the WNDR story never applied this heuristic. The social media information environment strips stories of their broader context; a Facebook post does not arrive accompanied by the silence of every legitimate newsroom. This inversion — where the absence of corroboration is invisible rather than informative — is a structural feature of platform-mediated news consumption.

**Epistemic novelty: Manufactured provenance chains**

The *Cotopaxi* reappearance story exemplifies a disinformation technique that deserves more analytical attention than it typically receives: the construction of *manufactured provenance chains*. This technique goes beyond simple fabrication. Rather than inventing a claim from whole cloth, the fabricator builds a chain of apparent sourcing that mixes genuine historical detail with invented official sources, creating an epistemic texture that mimics the feel of well-sourced journalism.

Consider the chain in the *Cotopaxi* story: (1) The ship is real — verifiable in Lloyd's Register, Coast Guard records, and maritime histories. (2) Its disappearance is real — documented in contemporaneous news reports. (3) Its association with the Bermuda Triangle is culturally established — via Berlitz, Spielberg, and decades of popular media. (4) Cuban waters are a plausible location — geographically proximate to the Triangle and politically remote enough that readers cannot easily verify claims about the Cuban Coast Guard. (5) The fabricated "discovery" is attributed to named (but invented) officials and agencies.

Each link in this chain serves a distinct epistemic function. The real historical details (links 1–3) provide *anchoring credibility* — a reader who fact-checks the ship's existence or its disappearance will find confirming evidence, which paradoxically increases confidence in the fabricated portions. The geographical and political remoteness (link 4) provides *verification friction* — a barrier to easy disconfirmation. The attributed officials (link 5) provide *authority mimicry*.

This technique is structurally analogous to what intelligence analysts call "perception management" through "anchoring in verified fact" (Heuer, R.J., 1999, *Psychology of Intelligence Analysis*, Center for the Study of Intelligence, CIA). It is far more effective than pure fabrication because it exploits the heuristic that partially verified claims are more likely to be entirely true. Fact-checkers encountering a manufactured provenance chain must do the laborious work of identifying exactly where the chain transitions from verified to fabricated — a task that requires domain expertise, archival access, and time. The asymmetry is pronounced: constructing the chain takes minutes; deconstructing it takes hours.

This technique has proliferated across the misinformation ecosystem. It appears in fabricated historical claims about ancient civilizations, in fraudulent medical advice that mixes genuine pharmacological data with invented clinical trials, and in political disinformation that embeds fabricated quotes within accurate accounts of real events. Recognizing the manufactured provenance chain as a distinct epistemic threat — rather than treating all misinformation as homogeneous — is essential for developing targeted countermeasures.

## 4. Verdict

**False.** The claim is fabricated. No ship reappeared.

The SS *Cotopaxi* sank during a storm on or shortly after December 1, 1925. Its wreck was identified in 2020 on the ocean floor off St. Augustine, Florida, approximately 35 nautical miles from shore (Barnette, M., 2020, as documented in *Shipwreck Secrets*, Science Channel). The vessel has been resting on the seabed for a century. It did not reappear in Cuban waters, was not intercepted by any coast guard, and was not found intact.

The reappearance claim originated with World News Daily Report, a self-described satirical website that functioned as a fake-news farm optimized for social media virality. The story was subsequently amplified by secondary clickbait aggregators that removed the satirical framing, presenting the fabrication as straight news.

Beyond the sourcing failure, the claim is physically impossible. A steel-hulled vessel submerged in seawater for ninety years would be profoundly degraded by corrosion, biofouling, and structural collapse. Marine taphonomy — the study of how organisms and artifacts decompose in marine environments — establishes that ferrous metals in oxygenated seawater corrode at rates of 0.1 to 0.5 millimeters per year, with accelerated degradation in tropical waters (Denny, M.W., 1988, *Biology and the Mechanics of the Wave-Swept Environment*, Princeton University Press; North, N.A., 1976, "Formation of Coral Concretions on Marine Iron," *International Journal of Nautical Archaeology*, 5(3), 253–258). Organic materials — wood, rope, fabric, food stores — would be completely consumed by marine organisms within years to decades. The notion of a ship emerging intact, let alone with preserved cargo or documentation, after nine decades of submersion is not merely unlikely; it contradicts well-established physical and biological processes.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The *Cotopaxi* fabrication sits at the intersection of several broader phenomena that merit examination.

**The Bermuda Triangle as an information cascade.** The Triangle's cultural status is itself a case study in manufactured mystery. The sequence is traceable: Gaddis's 1964 *Argosy* article coined the term and planted the seed. Berlitz's 1974 book industrialized the claim, selling tens of millions of copies with dramatic accounts that Kusche (1975) subsequently demonstrated were systematically distorted. But Kusche's debunking sold modestly by comparison. The asymmetry between sensational claim and sober correction — what information scientists call the "debunking deficit" (Lewandowsky, S., Ecker, U.K.H., Seifert, C.M., Schwarz, N., and Cook, J., 2012, "Misinformation and Its Correction: Continued Influence and Successful Debiasing," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 13(3), 106–131) — meant that the Triangle mythology became self-sustaining. Each new generation of writers could draw on Berlitz's distorted corpus as though it were established fact, while Kusche's corrections remained confined to a much smaller readership.

This dynamic has a compounding quality. By the time the *Cotopaxi* reappearance hoax appeared in 2015, the Bermuda Triangle had been culturally "real" for half a century. The hoax did not need to argue for the Triangle's existence; it could simply presuppose it, borrowing credibility from an edifice of prior misinformation. This is the information cascade at work: each subsequent claim is easier to believe because of the accumulated weight of prior claims, regardless of those prior claims' evidentiary status (Bikhchandani, S., Hirshleifer, D., and Welch, I., 1992, "A Theory of Fads, Fashion, Custom, and Cultural Change as Informational Cascades," *Journal of Political Economy*, 100(5), 992–1026).

**The fake-news farm business model.** World News Daily Report was not an ideological operation; it was a commercial one. Its business model was straightforward: produce sensational fabrications formatted as news, optimize headlines for social media sharing, monetize the resulting traffic through programmatic advertising. The *Cotopaxi* story was profitable because it was shareable, and it was shareable because it activated pre-existing narrative schemas (the Bermuda Triangle, ghost ships, the uncanny) while mimicking the formal properties of legitimate journalism. This commercial incentive structure means that as long as social media platforms reward engagement over accuracy, and as long as programmatic advertising pays by the pageview without regard to content veracity, fake-news farms will continue to produce stories like the *Cotopaxi* reappearance (Allcott, H. and Gentzkow, M., 2017, "Social Media and Fake News in the 2016 Election," *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 31(2), 211–236).

**Marine archaeology as epistemic closure.** The 2020 identification of the *Cotopaxi* wreck by Michael Barnette's team illustrates how empirical investigation can definitively close epistemically open questions. The wreck's physical presence on the ocean floor is not merely evidence against the reappearance claim; it is the kind of evidence that leaves no room for reasonable disagreement. The ship is *there*, in a specific location, in a state of degradation consistent with a century of submersion. Marine archaeology, sonar surveying, and underwater imaging technologies have transformed our ability to resolve maritime mysteries that once persisted indefinitely. The *Cotopaxi* case demonstrates that the persistence of maritime myths often reflects not the absence of answers but the absence of investigation — and that when investigation occurs, the answers tend to be mundane.

**Film and folklore feedback loops.** Spielberg's decision to include the *Cotopaxi* in *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* created a peculiar feedback loop between fiction and folklore. The film did not claim the *Cotopaxi* was actually taken by aliens; it used the ship as a piece of playful world-building. But the film's enormous cultural impact (it grossed over $300 million worldwide and became one of the defining science fiction films of the 1970s) ensured that millions of people encountered the ship's name in an explicitly supernatural context. When the WNDR story appeared decades later, the *Cotopaxi* was not just an obscure lost freighter — it was a ship that audiences already associated with inexplicable events, even if the source of that association was avowedly fictional. This film-to-folklore pipeline, in which fictional treatments of real events become part of the cultural memory surrounding those events, is a recurring pattern in the misinformation ecosystem.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The *Cotopaxi* reappearance claim is a methodologically instructive case for understanding how professional fact-checking organizations approach fabricated viral content.

**Source identification and upstream tracing.** The first step in Snopes's methodology, representative of the broader fact-checking community's approach, is to trace a claim to its earliest identifiable source. For the *Cotopaxi* story, this upstream trace led quickly to World News Daily Report. Identifying WNDR as the origin is immediately informative: the site's own disclaimer, its history of fabricated stories, and its structural characteristics (no bylines traceable to real journalists, no editorial contact information, advertising-saturated layout) all signal that it operates outside the norms of legitimate journalism. Snopes and similar organizations maintain internal databases of known fake-news sources, which allows rapid identification — but the technique is replicable by any reader willing to trace a claim to its source rather than evaluating it in the decontextualized form in which it appears on social media.

**Cross-referencing against the documentary record.** Fact-checkers verify the factual substrate of a claim by consulting primary sources: ship registries, Coast Guard records, contemporaneous newspaper archives, and academic maritime histories. For the *Cotopaxi*, the documentary record confirms the ship's existence and disappearance but contains no evidence of any reappearance. The absence of coverage in any authoritative source — Cuban government bulletins, U.S. Coast Guard records, wire-service archives, maritime trade publications — is treated as meaningful negative evidence. This approach operationalizes the coverage-absence heuristic: an event of this magnitude, had it occurred, would have generated an enormous documentary footprint.

**Physical plausibility assessment.** Fact-checkers consult domain experts or established scientific literature to evaluate whether a claim is physically possible. The *Cotopaxi* claim fails this test comprehensively. Marine engineers, naval architects, and marine biologists can all confirm that a vessel submerged for ninety years cannot resurface intact. The corrosion kinetics of ferrous metals in seawater, the biological degradation of organic materials, and the structural mechanics of prolonged submersion all preclude the claimed outcome. Fact-checkers need not be marine scientists themselves; they need only recognize when a claim makes an empirically testable physical prediction and consult the relevant expertise.

**The definitive archaeological evidence.** Since 2020, fact-checkers addressing the *Cotopaxi* claim have a decisive piece of evidence that was unavailable when the hoax first circulated: the wreck identification. Barnette's team matched the wreck's dimensions (approximately 250 feet in length), construction characteristics, and location against the *Cotopaxi*'s known specifications and last reported position. The wreck sits in approximately 40 feet of water on the continental shelf — precisely where a coal-laden steamer taking on water in heavy seas would be expected to come to rest. This archaeological evidence transforms the fact-check from a probabilistic assessment (the claim is almost certainly false because the source is unreliable and the physics are impossible) into a definitive one (the claim is false because we have found the ship, and it is on the ocean floor where it sank).

**Inoculation and prebunking.** Contemporary fact-checking theory increasingly emphasizes *prebunking* — preemptive exposure to misinformation techniques — as more effective than reactive debunking (Roozenbeek, J. and van der Linden, S., 2019, "Fake News Game Confers Psychological Resistance Against Online Misinformation," *Palgrave Communications*, 5, 65). The *Cotopaxi* case is an ideal candidate for prebunking exercises because its misinformation techniques — manufactured provenance chains, format mimicry, exploitation of pre-existing narrative schemas — are generalizable. Teaching audiences to recognize these techniques using the *Cotopaxi* as a concrete example builds transferable critical-evaluation skills that apply far beyond maritime hoaxes.

**Limitations of the fact-checking model.** It is worth noting what fact-checking cannot fully accomplish. The Snopes article on the *Cotopaxi* claim is thorough, well-sourced, and unambiguous in its verdict. But its audience is overwhelmingly composed of people who are already skeptical of the claim and sought out verification — a phenomenon known as "preaching to the choir" (Shin, J. and Thorson, K., 2017, "Partisan Selective Sharing: The Biased Diffusion of Fact-Checking Messages on Social Media," *Journal of Communication*, 67(2), 233–255). The millions who shared the original WNDR article are largely unreached by the correction. This structural asymmetry — fabrication travels through social networks via emotional engagement, while corrections travel through deliberate information-seeking — remains the central unsolved problem in the fact-checking ecosystem. The *Cotopaxi* case, with its millions of shares and its comparatively modest correction footprint, is a microcosm of this broader challenge.

The ship did not come back. It was always at the bottom of the sea. The real mystery was never the ocean — it was why we so readily believed otherwise.
