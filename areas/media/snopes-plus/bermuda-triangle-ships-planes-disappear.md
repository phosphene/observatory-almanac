# An unusually high number of ships and planes disappear in the Bermuda Triangle


## 1. The Claim

The Bermuda Triangle — described in various accounts as a region of the western North Atlantic Ocean bounded roughly by Miami, Florida; Bermuda; and Puerto Rico — is claimed to be an area of anomalously dangerous and mysterious disappearances. In the most common form of the claim, ships and aircraft transit the region and disappear at rates substantially higher than comparable ocean regions, often without distress calls, without debris, and without detectable weather or navigational cause. The number of incidents attributed to the Triangle runs into the dozens in popular accounts, sometimes the hundreds, and they are presented as collectively inexplicable by ordinary means.

The claim typically includes canonical cases that are presented as paradigmatic examples of the Triangle's properties. Flight 19 — a training exercise of five TBF Avenger torpedo bombers that disappeared over the Atlantic in December 1945 — is presented as one of the most compelling, in part because the rescue aircraft sent to search for them also disappeared. The USS Cyclops, a Navy fuel ship that disappeared in 1918 with 306 people aboard, is frequently cited as one of the greatest maritime mysteries in American history. The Mary Celeste, the Marine Sulphur Queen, the Witchcraft, and Ellen Austin are among the vessels that appear in standard Triangle compilations. The loss of a Martin Mariner flying boat during the search for Flight 19 is presented as evidence of the region's appetite for aircraft.

The broader claim encompasses several classes of paranormal or exotic-physical explanation: magnetic anomalies affecting compass readings; underwater methane gas eruptions that reduce water density and sink ships; time warps or dimensional rifts; extraterrestrial abduction; and residual energy from the legendary continent of Atlantis, which some accounts place at the ocean floor beneath the Triangle. The supernatural dimension is not always explicit in popular presentations of the claim — many accounts present the Triangle merely as statistically anomalous without invoking a specific mechanism — but the accumulation of unexplained incidents is used to imply that ordinary explanations are insufficient.

The popular mythology of the Bermuda Triangle as a coherent, bounded, dangerous zone was constructed primarily through the work of journalist Vincent Gaddis, who coined the term "Bermuda Triangle" in a 1964 article in *Argosy* magazine, and through Charles Berlitz's 1974 bestseller *The Bermuda Triangle*, which sold millions of copies worldwide and established the canonical incident list and the mystery framework that persists in popular culture. The claim that something extraordinary happens in a specific region of the Atlantic is, by the mid-2020s, a well-established piece of late-twentieth-century folklore with a clearly traceable genealogy.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The statistical foundation — there is no anomaly.** The foundational claim is that the rate of maritime and aviation incidents in the Bermuda Triangle exceeds what would be expected by chance given the volume of traffic. When this statistical claim is examined systematically, it does not hold. Lloyd's of London, the world's oldest and largest marine insurance market, maintains global risk ratings for maritime transit. Lloyd's does not assign higher premiums for vessels transiting the Bermuda Triangle, which is the most direct and financially consequential measure of actuarial risk. If underwriters — who have commercial incentive to correctly price maritime risk and whose entire industry depends on accurate casualty assessment — do not identify the Triangle as elevated-risk, this is strong evidence against the statistical anomaly claim.

The US Coast Guard, which operates extensively in the Florida Straits and the western Atlantic and maintains detailed records of maritime incidents, has explicitly stated that the Bermuda Triangle does not represent an area of particular maritime danger beyond what would be expected given traffic density and weather conditions (United States Coast Guard, 2013, "Bermuda Triangle Fact Sheet"). The International Maritime Organization maintains no special navigational advisory for Triangle transit. These are the organisations whose operational and financial decisions depend most directly on accurate risk assessment of the region, and none of them corroborate the anomalous-danger claim.

Kusche (1975, *The Bermuda Triangle Mystery — Solved*, Harper & Row) conducted the most methodologically thorough investigation of the claim, cross-referencing every incident in the Berlitz Triangle case list against primary source records — Lloyd's shipping registers, Coast Guard files, National Transportation Safety Board reports, newspaper archives, and US Navy records. His findings were systematically devastating to the Triangle mythology. Many incidents occurred well outside the geographic boundaries of the Triangle and were retroactively included by boundary manipulation. Some incidents occurred in good weather conditions that Berlitz claimed were calm, when records showed severe weather. Some reported deaths occurred in incidents that were actually resolved — the ships arrived at their destinations. A small number of incidents in the Berlitz list could not be verified in any primary source and may have been invented or substantially embellished. One case — the Philadelphia disappearance, told with specific details in Berlitz — appears not to have occurred at all. Kusche's book was important enough that it prompted a re-examination across the field, and his core finding has not been successfully challenged.

**The traffic density confound.** The Bermuda Triangle sits in one of the most heavily transited maritime regions on Earth. It lies astride the primary shipping lanes between North American East Coast ports and the Caribbean, on the northern edge of the Gulf Stream, and beneath one of the highest-density commercial and general aviation corridors in the western hemisphere. Fort Lauderdale, Miami, Nassau, and San Juan are among the world's busiest small-boat harbours. The absolute number of incidents in any geographic region is a function of traffic volume: more transits produce more incidents. Without controlling for traffic density — normalising incident count by total transits or total vessel-hours — raw incident tallies cannot support claims about anomalous danger. Triangle accounts never perform this normalisation, because when it is performed, any apparent excess disappears.

**Canonical cases corrected.** The individual incidents cited as evidence for Triangle mystery are, on close examination, substantially different from the canonical version.

*Flight 19* (December 5, 1945): Five TBF Avenger torpedo bombers on a navigation training exercise disappeared in the late afternoon after the flight leader, Lieutenant Charles Taylor, became confused about his position. Voice communications transcripts — suppressed in popular accounts — show Taylor disorienting himself, ignoring the advice of other pilots, and directing the flight east over open ocean when he incorrectly believed he was over the Gulf of Mexico. The weather deteriorated. Night fell. The aircraft were low on fuel. A formal Navy Court of Inquiry attributed the loss to the flight leader's confusion and the resulting inability to locate land before fuel exhaustion (NTSB successor review, 1991). The Martin Mariner sent to search for them exploded and was seen to explode by the crew of the SS Gaines Mills — it was a known volatile aircraft — which is why it did not transmit a distress call. Neither incident requires a paranormal explanation.

*USS Cyclops* (March 1918): The Cyclops disappeared in the central Atlantic without a distress signal during World War I. The most historically corroborated explanation involves a combination of structural overloading — the Cyclops was carrying a cargo of manganese ore at a load significantly in excess of her design limit — and wartime Atlantic conditions. Whether German submarine action was involved has not been definitively ruled out. The Cyclops did not disappear in the Bermuda Triangle by most boundary definitions; she was en route from Barbados to Baltimore and disappeared somewhere in the general Atlantic. The mystery is real in the sense that the exact cause is unestablished, but unexplained does not mean inexplicable in principle, and the absence of Triangle documentation makes it a spurious entry in the canonical list (Maguire, 1973, *The USS Cyclops Mystery*, US Naval Institute).

*The Mary Celeste*: Sometimes cited in Bermuda Triangle compilations despite the fact that she was found abandoned in the eastern North Atlantic near the Azores — not in the Triangle — and the case dates to 1872. Her inclusion in Triangle accounts is a pure geographic fabrication, illustrating the boundary flexibility mechanism that characterises the mythology.

**The methane hypothesis.** The most scientifically plausible physical mechanism proposed for Triangle disappearances is the explosive decompression hypothesis, which proposes that large methane eruptions from seafloor seep sites could reduce water buoyancy enough to sink ships suddenly, or create atmospheric gas clouds that could flame-out aircraft engines. Clennell et al. (1999, *Geology*, 27[12]) documented methane hydrate deposits in the seabed beneath the Bermuda Triangle region, lending apparent scientific respectability to the mechanism. However, subsequent analysis found no evidence that eruptions of a scale sufficient to sink large vessels occur in the Triangle region, and no acoustic or sonar evidence consistent with massive gas eruption has been recovered from sites of attributed Triangle losses. MacDonald et al. (2002, *Geophysical Research Letters*, 29[22]) confirmed methane seepage without evidence for catastrophic eruption events in historically documented sinking locations. The mechanism is theoretically possible; its relevance to the specific incidents in the Triangle case list is unestablished.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The Bermuda Triangle mythology is epistemically interesting primarily as a case study in narrative construction — specifically, in how a coherent and compelling anomaly can be assembled from events that, examined individually and in the context of the full incident record, show no statistical excess. Understanding the mechanisms of this construction illuminates a class of reasoning error that extends well beyond maritime mythology.

**Selective compilation and file-drawer suppression.** Berlitz and preceding Triangle authors compiled their incident lists by identifying incidents that could be placed in the Triangle and that could be characterised as unexplained or mysterious. They did not compile a complete list of all maritime incidents in the region and then examine what proportion were mysterious. This distinction matters enormously. When you select only unexplained incidents and report their count, the count tells you nothing about the rate of unexplained incidents relative to explained ones. The denominator — all transits — is never presented. The numerator — unexplained losses — is itself constructed by emphasising unexplained aspects of cases that were often partially explained and by including cases that were later resolved but where the resolution was not retroactively incorporated into the account.

Kusche's analysis demonstrated this explicitly: the number of "unexplained" Triangle incidents dropped dramatically when he read the case records rather than the popularisations, because natural explanations were available in primary sources that Triangle authors had not consulted or had omitted. This is a textbook demonstration of the file-drawer problem operating not in formal research but in popular compile-and-report scientific journalism.

**The naturalness of geographic pattern-seeking.** Human cognition is predisposed to cluster-detection and geographic pattern recognition. When several incidents are associated with a defined region, the region becomes a natural explanatory category even when the incidents would not cluster statistically in a fully sampled dataset. The Triangle functions as a pre-drawn cognitive container into which incidents are sorted — and the sorting happens after the incidents, creating retrospective geographic patterning that was not detectable prospectively. The Triangle's boundaries are nowhere officially defined, which means they can be adjusted post-hoc to include any incident the compiler finds useful, as Kusche extensively documented.

**The epistemic function of mystery in popular culture.** The Bermuda Triangle mythology serves a specific cultural function that has nothing to do with maritime safety: it sustains the category of the genuinely inexplicable in a scientific culture that increasingly has mechanistic explanations for former mysteries. The persistence of the claim is in part a symptom of ambivalence about scientific explanation — a desire for there to be things that science cannot account for, maintained as a kind of reserve of wonder and possibility. The Triangle serves as evidence that the world is stranger than our maps, that expertise has limits, and that official explanations are sometimes insufficient. This function is appealing across many demographic groups and provides motivational support for the belief that has nothing to do with the specific evidence for or against anomalous disappearances.

**The specific contribution of the preprint/bestseller model.** The Bermuda Triangle case predates the internet but establishes the structural template that many subsequent conspiracy topics would follow: a popular book or article that compiles incidents selectively, presents them with vivid specific detail, and sells to a large audience that has no access to primary sources and no institutional reason to doubt the compilation. Berlitz's book had specific named incidents, specific dates, specific numbers of people lost — all the epistemic furniture of factual reporting. The book was reviewed and sold by mainstream publishers and booksellers. The signals that would normally alert readers to unreliable non-fiction were absent; the signals that normally indicate legitimate factual writing were present. In these conditions, most readers have no tool to apply to the question of whether the incident list is complete and accurate, because the primary sources required to check it are not publicly accessible. The Triangle myth is partly a story about the power asymmetry between a well-resourced author with access to accounts and a reading public that can only evaluate the internal coherence of the narrative presented to them.


## 4. Verdict

**Retrospectively constructed claim (EARC: R) — False.** The claim that an unusually high number of ships and planes disappear in the Bermuda Triangle is false. When the full incident record is examined — not the selectively compiled account — no statistically significant excess of losses appears compared to other ocean regions of comparable traffic density. Lloyd's of London, the US Coast Guard, and the International Maritime Organization do not treat the region as elevated-risk. Kusche (1975) demonstrated that the canonical incident list contains cases that occurred outside the Triangle, cases where conventional explanations were available and suppressed, and cases of doubtful evidential status. Individual canonical incidents (Flight 19, USS Cyclops) have historically grounded explanations substantially different from the Triangle mythology version. Snopes rates the claim False.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Bermuda Triangle is one of the most thoroughly studied cases of popular anomaly construction, which makes it unusually valuable as a methodological template for examining how patterns get created from unexceptional data. The Triangle case demonstrates several general principles.

First, *the completeness of a case list is as important as its content*. An incomplete list of incidents selected on the criterion of mystery cannot demonstrate anomalous mystery rates; it can only demonstrate that incidents occurred. To establish an anomaly, you need to know the rate of mysterious incidents relative to all incidents relative to all transits. Triangle accounts systematically fail to provide this structure.

Second, *geographic naming is a powerful cognitive anchor that creates the illusion of a common cause*. Once the Triangle exists as a named place with attributed properties, incidents are sorted into it and it becomes an explanatory category. But if you removed the name and the boundary and presented the same incidents as independent events distributed across the western Atlantic, the inference of a common cause for all of them would be far less automatic.

Third, *the line between "unexplained" and "inexplicable" does matter*. Many of the incidents in the Triangle case list are genuinely unexplained in the sense that the exact final sequence of events is not documented. This is true of many maritime disasters — ships sink with all hands, leaving no record. Genuinely unexplained incidents are routine in maritime history. The move from "unexplained" to "inexplicable by known mechanisms" is a large inferential step that requires affirmative evidence that known mechanisms are insufficient, not merely evidence that the full causal sequence is not on record.

The Triangle case also anticipates several dynamics in contemporary information ecosystems: the role of vivid narrative in making claims memorable and resistant to statistical correction; the way in which compelling naming can crystallise a mythological geography; and the way in which human interest in the inexplicable creates sustained demand for anomaly claims that outlasts the specific evidentiary basis of any particular version.

Geographer Phillip Steinberg's work on ocean cultural geography (Steinberg, 2001, *The Social Construction of the Ocean*, Cambridge University Press) provides a theoretical framework for understanding why the western Atlantic specifically became a site of projected mystery — the ocean as a zone of absent normal social organisation, in which normal rules of evidence and explanation are partly suspended in popular imagination.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates the claim that the Bermuda Triangle has an unusually high rate of disappearances as False, correctly citing the Lloyd's of London non-premium and the Kusche investigation as the primary evidentiary basis for the rebuttal. Their treatment identifies the statistical normalisation problem and notes that many of the canonical incidents have conventional explanations.

The fact-checking gap that the Observatory addresses concerns the mechanisms of construction rather than just the debunking of the output. A complete treatment of the Bermuda Triangle claim needs to explain not just that the incidents are unremarkable in aggregate but how an apparently compelling anomaly was assembled from unremarkable incidents. Specifically: why the completeness of an incident list matters and how to recognise incomplete selection; the role of boundary flexibility in triangle-style claims; and the difference between unexplained and inexplicable as epistemic categories.

For fact-checkers and media educators, the Bermuda Triangle case is a particularly useful teaching example because it is old enough to be emotionally defused (unlike contemporary conspiracy claims) while exemplifying the same analytical tools needed for current misinformation. The lessons — check denominators, examine source completeness, trace geographic boundaries, distinguish unexplained from inexplicable — are immediately transferable to evaluating contemporary cluster claims in health, crime, and politics. A media literacy curriculum that uses the Triangle as its primary worked example of retrospective pattern construction would provide tools applicable well beyond maritime mythology.
