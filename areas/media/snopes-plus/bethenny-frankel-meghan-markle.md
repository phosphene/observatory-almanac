# Bethenny Frankel exposed or feuded with Meghan Markle


## 1. The Claim

The claim, as it typically circulates, holds that reality television personality and entrepreneur Bethenny Frankel — best known for her appearances on *The Real Housewives of New York City* (Bravo, 2008–2010, 2022–2023) and her SkinnyGirl brand — has "exposed" Meghan Markle, the Duchess of Sussex and former senior working member of the British Royal Family, or has been involved in a sustained feud with her. In its most amplified form, the claim suggests that Frankel possesses or has revealed insider knowledge about Markle — about her motivations, character, or conduct — that constitutes a genuine disclosure of hidden information.

The claim travels in several registers. In entertainment news coverage and social media, it is typically framed as a celebrity conflict story: two prominent women in public life in opposition, with Frankel characterised as the one willing to say publicly what others only think privately. In tabloid and monarchist-adjacent online communities, Frankel is frequently cited as an authoritative critical voice about Markle and the Sussex household — her opinions on Markle elevated to the status of insider revelation by virtue of Frankel's own celebrity and her willingness to speak where others are silent. In the Sussex-critical media ecosystem that developed particularly after the March 2021 Oprah Winfrey interview with Harry and Meghan and the December 2022 Netflix documentary *Harry & Meghan*, Frankel's commentary has been circulated as substantiating evidence for negative assessments of Markle's character.

Bethenny Frankel (born Bethenny Frankel, 4 November 1970, in New York City) is not a member of the Royal Family, not a personal friend or associate of Meghan Markle, not a former palace employee, and not a person with any documented professional or social connection to Markle that would position her as a source of insider knowledge. She is a media personality and businesswoman who has expressed opinions about a much more prominent public figure through the media — including social media, podcasts, and television appearances. Understanding the claim requires understanding both what Frankel actually said and what epistemic authority her commentary does or does not carry.

The claim compounds over time in a way characteristic of media-generated feuds: Frankel's individual commentary statements are aggregated into a narrative of sustained opposition, individual criticisms are framed as "exposures," and the cumulative impression is of a major interpersonal conflict with a factual disclosure component — neither of which is accurate to the underlying events.


## 2. What's Actually True

**What Bethenny Frankel actually said.** Bethenny Frankel began publicly commenting on Meghan Markle in 2019 and has continued periodically since then. Her documented statements have included: criticism of Markle's public positioning as a victim while simultaneously maintaining a high media profile; comparisons between Markle and herself (Frankel) in terms of how women with fame and resources present themselves publicly; commentary on Markle's Oprah interview (aired CBS, 7 March 2021) expressing scepticism about some of its claims and framing; criticism of what Frankel characterised as Markle's strategic use of victimhood narratives; and later commentary, particularly from 2023 onwards, in which Frankel made more pointed criticisms of Markle's public credibility.

These statements are documented across Frankel's social media accounts, in podcast appearances including her own podcast *Just B with Bethenny Frankel*, and in interviews with entertainment press including *Extra*, *E! News*, and various tabloid publications. They constitute a documented record of Frankel's publicly expressed opinions about Markle.

None of these statements constitutes an "exposé" in the journalistic or investigative sense of that word — a disclosure of previously unknown information obtained through reporting, documentation, or firsthand knowledge. Frankel's statements are opinions, comparisons, and speculative character assessments. She has not claimed to have been in a room with Markle. She has not cited documentary evidence supporting her characterisations. She has not presented herself as a source with direct knowledge of the Sussex household, palace conversations, or any specific events in Markle's private life. Her commentary is evaluative rather than revelatory.

**The degree of mutuality.** A "feud" typically implies bilateral conflict — two parties in active opposition to each other. The documented record of Markle's direct engagement with Frankel's commentary is minimal. Meghan Markle has not, to the documented public record, directly addressed Bethenny Frankel in any interview, social media post, or public statement. The interaction is almost entirely one-directional: Frankel commenting on Markle, not Markle engaging with Frankel. The characterisation of this as a "feud" significantly overstates the mutuality of the exchange and, implicitly, overstates the equivalence in public prominence between the two women.

Markle, as the wife of Prince Harry and a former senior working member of the British Royal Family, is among the most covered individuals in global media. Her public life is documented across hundreds of millions of words of press coverage, documentary film, interviews, and books. Bethenny Frankel is a moderately prominent American reality television personality and entrepreneur whose level of public recognition, while substantial within the reality television audience, does not approach Markle's global media footprint. The framing of their non-interaction as a "feud" imposes a false symmetry on an asymmetric situation.

**The 2023 escalation.** In 2023, Frankel's commentary on Markle intensified in connection with the launch of Markle's American Riviera Orchard lifestyle brand and Markle's public communications strategy. Frankel posted social media content directly critical of Markle's brand launch and of her broader positioning as a public figure, and these posts received substantial media coverage. This constitutes the period in which the "feud" framing achieved its widest circulation. The coverage was factually accurate insofar as it documented what Frankel said; it was misleading insofar as the framing of mutual celebrity war implied a back-and-forth that the record does not show.

**The substantive content of Frankel's criticism.** Assessing whether Frankel's criticism is valid is a separate question from assessing whether the claim that she "exposed" Markle is accurate. On the substantive merits: Frankel has made claims about Markle's public credibility that are opinions rather than provable propositions; she has made comparisons between herself and Markle that are self-serving in the sense that they frame Frankel's own public conduct as a superior model; and she has offered assessments of Markle's character, motivations, and public strategy that — consistent with the genre of public personality commentary — reflect Frankel's own subjective interpretation rather than access to insider facts.

Research on public figure commentary in celebrity media (Turner, Graeme, 2010, *Ordinary People and the Media: The Demotic Turn*, Sage Publications) distinguishes between celebrity commentary that is substantive critique based on publicly available information, celebrity commentary that is opinion with limited evidential basis, and celebrity commentary that is primarily self-promotional in its use of another celebrity's name. Frankel's commentary combines elements of the first two categories while also having properties of the third: her public criticism of Markle increased Frankel's own media visibility during periods when she was otherwise less prominently placed in the celebrity news cycle.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The "exposé" framing as amplification.** The transformation of opinion commentary into "exposé" follows a well-documented pattern in celebrity media coverage. Media coverage of celebrity opinion statements routinely uses the language of exposure, revelation, and disclosure — "Frankel DESTROYS Markle," "Star EXPOSES Royal Hypocrisy" — because this language maximises click-through rate and share incentives. The language imports the evidential weight of investigative journalism into a content format that is fundamentally opinion-based. Over repeated exposure to this framing, readers encode Frankel's commentary as revelatory rather than evaluative, which is a false encoding but one that the media environment actively produces.

Research on framing effects in media consumption (Entman, 1993, "Framing: Toward Clarification of a Fractured Paradigm," *Journal of Communication*) demonstrates that the words used to introduce and describe commentary shape how that commentary is processed and remembered. "Bethenny Frankel criticises Meghan Markle" and "Bethenny Frankel EXPOSES Meghan Markle" describe the same event but produce substantially different assessments of the evidential weight and significance of Frankel's statements. The entertainment press systematically uses the higher-intensity framing for its engagement properties, regardless of whether the content justifies that framing.

**Parasocial credibility transference.** Frankel's extended run as a cast member of *The Real Housewives of New York City* produced a substantial parasocial relationship between Frankel and her audience. Parasocial relationships — relationships in which media audiences develop a sense of familiarity and connection with public figures who are unaware of their existence (Horton & Wohl, 1956, "Mass communication and para-social interaction: Observations on intimacy at a distance," *Psychiatry*) — are particularly strong for reality television cast members, because the genre presents itself as unmediated access to real lives.

Reality television audiences who have spent years watching Frankel navigate personal and professional dynamics on *RHONY* have developed a parasocial sense of knowing her — her personality, her judgement, her directness, her reliability as a read on social situations. This parasocial familiarity is then imported into her celebrity commentary: because audiences feel they know Frankel to be perceptive and candid, her assessments of other celebrities are credited with greater authority than those of less familiar commentators. Her criticism of Markle benefits from the parasocial credibility built across years of reality television, even though that credibility derives from a completely different and unrelated context.

This is a form of halo effect — the cognitive bias identified by Thorndike (1920, "A constant error in psychological ratings," *Journal of Applied Psychology*) by which a positive evaluation of a person in one domain is incorrectly applied to their performance in an unrelated domain. Knowing Frankel well (as a reality television participant) does not confer evidential weight on her opinions about Markle (who she does not know and about whom she has no special information), but the parasocial familiarity produces the feeling that it does.

**Epistemic novelty: the structural role of polarisation in celebrity commentary.** The Frankel-Markle dynamic illustrates a feature of celebrity polarisation economics that deserves distinct analysis. In media ecosystems organised around partisan audience identity — and the online discourse around the Royal Family, particularly around the Sussex family, became one of the most intensely partisan celebrity discourse environments of the 2020s — commentary that validates the preferred narrative of one audience faction is amplified through that faction's networks regardless of its evidential basis.

The Sussex-critical audience constituted a substantial and highly engaged media consumer segment throughout the period 2019–2025. This audience consumed content that validated negative assessments of Meghan Markle with unusual intensity — sharing, bookmarking, and repeatedly circulating commentary that confirmed their existing views. Bethenny Frankel, as a credentialled celebrity who expressed criticism with personality, became a hero figure in this ecosystem. Her commentary was amplified not because it was evidentially superior to other commentary but because it was useful to the ecosystem's narrative function.

This is what media researchers have called "affinity-based information selection" (Stroud, 2010, "Polarisation and partisan selective exposure," *Journal of Communication*): audiences select information that confirms prior attitudes, and the amplification of any given piece of content is determined largely by its confirmatory value for particular audiences rather than by its accuracy or evidential weight. Frankel's commentary was optimally positioned for amplification in the Sussex-critical ecosystem because it was critical, vivid, came from a named celebrity source, and was delivered in Frankel's characteristically blunt personal style. None of these properties are evidential properties.

**The "feud" as celebrity media template.** Celebrity media operates with a limited vocabulary of narrative formats, and "feud" is among the most durable and commercially reliable. Research on celebrity media content (Marshall, 2014, *Celebrity and Power: Fame in Contemporary Culture*, University of Minnesota Press) identifies feuds as a fundamental narrative unit — alongside "romance," "scandal," and "redemption" — that celebrity media repeatedly and formulaically applies to relationships between famous people regardless of whether the relationship actually conforms to the template.

Once the Frankel-Markle commentary dynamic acquired sufficient media volume, it was cast into the "feud" template because that template is available, recognisable, and effective. The feud frame requires two named celebrities, a conflict, and preferably ongoing escalation with latest developments. The lack of genuine bilateral conflict was not an obstacle: it was papillated over by framing Markle's non-response as strategic silence and characterising every new Frankel comment as an escalation of existing hostilities. The template was applied to a situation that did not meet its structural requirements, and audiences received the template framing rather than an accurate description of the relationship.


## 4. Verdict

**Distorted but grounded — real commentary, significantly exaggerated in framing.** The claim that Bethenny Frankel "exposed" or "feuded with" Meghan Markle distorts a genuine and documented set of events — Frankel publicly and repeatedly criticising Markle across multiple platforms from 2019 onwards — by applying framing that misrepresents the nature, epistemic status, and mutuality of the commentary. Frankel expressed opinions; she did not expose previously hidden facts. The commentary was predominantly one-directional; it was not a bilateral feud. Frankel's celebrity provides no special access to information about Markle that she does not have; her commentary draws on the same public information available to any observer.

The underlying claim that Frankel has been critical of Markle is true and documented. The claim that this constitutes an exposé with evidential weight is false and unsupported. The claim that this constitutes a feud in the conventional sense misrepresents the degree of mutual engagement between the two parties. The distortion is significant and systematic because the more dramatic framing — exposé, feud — is what circulates most widely and generates the greatest belief in the underlying claim's significance.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Frankel-Markle commentary dynamic is legible as a case study in how celebrity media ecosystems use opinionated personalities as proxy validators for pre-existing audience positions. The Sussex discourse of 2019–2025 was unusual in the intensity of its polarisation and in the degree to which normally distinct celebrity media audiences converged around partisan positions on the Sussex family. Tabloid royalism, American reality television fandom, and social media culture intersected in a particular way that produced an unusual demand for sympathetic celebrity voices willing to criticise Markle publicly.

Frankel was well suited to fill this role for several reasons: she is American, so she can speak to Markle's American background and claimed American universalism; she is successful, so she can make invidious comparisons without appearing purely envious; she is associated with female empowerment through her career narrative while also being willing to criticise another woman, which defuses the "misogyny" counterargument; and she is confrontational and vivid in her communication style, which makes her commentary shareable.

These structural qualifications explain her prominence as a Markle critic more fully than the evidential content of her commentary does. She is useful to the Sussex-critical ecosystem because of her positioning and personality, not because she knows things others don't know. The misattribution of insider knowledge to her commentary reflects the ecosystem's need for credentialled validators, not the actual content of her statements.

This pattern — celebrity opinion commentary elevated to insider revelation status through the needs of a partisan audience ecosystem — is likely to recur. The conditions that produced it (extreme celebrity polarisation, parasocial credibility transference, framing effects from entertainment press exposure language, and affinity-based information selection) are structural features of contemporary celebrity media rather than anomalies. Similar dynamics have been observed around partisan celebrity ecosystems in political contexts, in fan communities, and in documented influencer-media relationships. The Frankel-Markle case is unusual only in the intensity of its polarisation and in the degree to which a relatively minor celebrity (Frankel) acquired outsized significance as a validator within a discourse ecosystem organised around a major one (Markle).

Media scholars studying celebrity discourse and political communication have documented these dynamics in adjacent contexts — the use of celebrity validators in political campaigns (Garthwaite & Moore, 2012, "Can celebrity endorsements affect political outcomes? Evidence from the 2008 US Democratic presidential primary," *RAND Journal of Economics*), the amplification of counter-narrative voices in celebrity news ecosystems, and the systematic elevation of opinionated commentators over silent parties in binary celebrity conflict framing. The Frankel-Markle case adds to this literature a particularly clear example of how one-sided celebrity commentary is transformed into a "feud" narrative and how the transformation serves audience and media ecosystem needs irrespective of factual correspondence.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Bethenny Frankel/Meghan Markle claim represents a category of content that is simultaneously fact-checkable and resistant to formal fact-check treatment. It is fact-checkable because specific verifiable claims can be assessed: Did Frankel make the statements attributed to her? (Yes, documented.) Did Markle respond? (No, not in documented record.) Does Frankel have insider access to the Sussex household? (No, no documented connection.) Is the characterisation of "exposé" accurate to the content of Frankel's statements? (No, statements are opinion rather than disclosure.)

It is resistant to formal fact-check treatment because no specific falsifiable proposition is typically presented — "feud" and "exposed" are framing words rather than truth-apt claims, and the bulk of the content in circulation is aggregative commentary rather than specific factual assertion. Fact-checking organisations optimised for discrete true/false verdicts on specific claims are structurally ill-suited to assessing the cumulative framing distortion that characterises this type of celebrity discourse.

The claim is not addressed by Snopes, PolitiFact, or equivalent organisations, which reflects both the entertainment-rather-than-political orientation of the subject matter and the framing-rather-than-factual-claim nature of the distortion. The gap is real and significant: a substantial proportion of celebrity biographical misinformation operates through framing distortion rather than factual fabrication, and current fact-checking infrastructure is not optimised to assess it.

For fact-checkers or media literacy educators using this case, the pedagogically valuable elements include: distinguishing between the language of "exposé/reveal/expose" and the actual content category (opinion commentary); applying the mutuality test to "feud" claims (has both parties visibly engaged?); assessing the epistemic basis for the claimed insider knowledge (what access does the commentator actually have?); identifying the amplification mechanism (which audience ecosystem is driving circulation and why?); and asking whether the claim's prominent framing is an accurate description of the underlying events or a template imposed onto them for narrative and commercial reasons. These are skills applicable across a wide class of celebrity media claims for which the formal true/false fact-check is inadequate and the framing assessment is the more useful intervention.
