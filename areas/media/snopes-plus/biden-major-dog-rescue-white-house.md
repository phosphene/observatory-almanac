## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In November 2020, following the projection that Joe Biden had won the U.S. presidential election, considerable media and social media attention turned to the president-elect's personal life — including his two German shepherds, Champ and Major. Multiple news outlets and social media users circulated the claim that Major, the younger of the two dogs, would become "the first rescue dog in the White House." Headlines from NBC News and other major outlets framed this as an established historic first. The claim circulated extensively during the transition period from November 2020 through the Biden inauguration in January 2021.

Snopes fact-checker Nur Ibrahim (2020) assigned this claim the rating **Mixture**. The claim is partially true and partially false, depending on the specific definition applied to the phrase "rescue dog":

- **What's True**: Major Biden was adopted from a shelter — specifically the Delaware Humane Association — making him the first dog in White House history to arrive from an animal shelter adoption. This distinction is accurately described as a first.
- **What's False**: Major was not the first "rescue dog" broadly construed to reside in the White House. President Lyndon B. Johnson's dog Yuki, a mixed-breed dog found at a Texas gas station on Thanksgiving Day 1966 by Johnson's daughter Luci Nugent, predates Major as a "rescued" dog living in the White House — though Yuki was not adopted from a shelter.

This entry examines the specific factual distinctions at stake, the history of presidential canine companions and their acquisition stories, the definitional ambiguity surrounding the word "rescue" as applied to animals, and the media literacy questions raised by the rapid spread of a claim that required only minor semantic precision to verify or falsify.

## §2 The Factual Record: Major Biden and the Delaware Humane Association

The story of Major Biden's path to the White House is documented through multiple verified sources, including the Delaware Humane Association's own public communications and contemporaneous press coverage.

Major was a German Shepherd puppy brought to the Delaware Humane Association (DHA), an animal care and adoption organization based in Wilmington, Delaware, after being exposed to something toxic in his family home that endangered his health. The DHA, as standard practice for animals needing medical recovery, placed Major in a foster network. The Bidens served as Major's foster family, and in 2018, they formalized the arrangement by officially adopting him. The Delaware Humane Association publicly acknowledged the adoption in a tweet dated November 7, 2020, the day major news organizations called the presidential race for Biden.

Champ Biden, the older German shepherd, was acquired through a different channel: the Bidens purchased Champ from a Pennsylvania breeder in December 2008, shortly after Biden was elected vice president. This fact is significant for the claim: only Major's shelter origins are at issue, not Champ's breeder origins.

Nur Ibrahim's (2020) fact-check clearly established: (1) Major was adopted from a licensed animal shelter; (2) no prior presidential dog has been documented to have been adopted from a shelter; (3) therefore, Major's shelter-adoption status is a genuine historical first.

The distinction between "shelter adoption" and "rescue" more broadly is where the claim becomes a Mixture rather than a True or False.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Definitional Problem of "Rescue Animal" and How Linguistic Ambiguity Drives Misinformation

The epistemic novelty in this case is a well-defined type of definitional misinformation: claims that appear false or true depending on which definition of a contested term the speaker and audience share. This phenomenon is structural rather than intentional — neither the news organization that published the original "first rescue dog" headline nor the fact-checker who identified its limitations was operating in bad faith. The error was definitional, driven by the popularization of the word "rescue" in animal welfare contexts over the preceding two decades.

The word "rescue" as applied to companion animals has undergone significant semantic broadening in American English since approximately the 1990s. In its strict historical usage, "rescue" described animals taken from abusive or neglectful situations by humane enforcement officers or animal control agencies. In contemporary popular usage, "rescue" has expanded to cover any animal obtained through adoption from a shelter or rescue organization — and by further extension, any animal with an origin story involving distress, abandonment, or informal discovery, regardless of institutional intermediation.

This semantic broadening created two simultaneously valid definitions in public circulation in November 2020:
1. **Narrow definition**: A rescue animal is an animal formally adopted through a licensed shelter or rescue organization.
2. **Broad definition**: A rescue animal is any animal whose origin involved being "saved" from a dangerous, abandoned, or distressed situation, regardless of process.

Under the narrow definition, Major is the first. Under the broad definition, Yuki the LBJ dog — found at a Texas gas station by Johnson's daughter on Thanksgiving 1966 — qualifies as rescued, and Major is not the first.

The problem Ibrahim (2020) identified is that the news outlets running "first rescue dog" headlines were using the broad definition (and celebrating the shelter-adoption framing), while the historical record Yuki established a prior claim under the same broad but not narrow reading. Snopes's Mixture rating correctly captures this exactly: the shelter-adoption first claim is true; the uncaveated "first rescue dog" claim requires specification.

This pattern — where definitional imprecision in a news claim creates structured partial truth — appears frequently in political fact-checking. Nyhan and Reifler (2010), writing in *Political Behavior* (Springer), documented how even well-intentioned corrections can fail to update audience beliefs when the correction hinges on definitional distinctions that audiences find remote or technical. In this case, the distinction between "from a shelter" and "technically rescued but not from a shelter" may strike many audiences as minor — but the role of animal shelters as institutions has cultural weight in American animal welfare discourse that the distinction makes nontrivial.

The EARC designation of R (Representational gap) is appropriate because the core knowledge gap is not factual but representational: audiences lack a clear mental model of the spectrum from "breeder-acquired" to "shelter-adopted" to "informally rescued," and the news coverage did not help them build one.

## §4 Historical Context: Presidential Dogs and Acquisition Stories

The history of presidential pets at the White House is well documented through presidential library records, National Park Service archives, and multiple historical studies. Major Biden's case invites a survey of how previous presidents — and specifically their dogs — were obtained, which contextualizes the significance of the shelter-adoption first.

**George Washington** owned numerous hounds, primarily used for fox hunting, and selectively bred them at Mount Vernon. These were working animals acquired through breeders and gifted from foreign dignitaries — notably, the Marquis de Lafayette sent Washington French staghounds from France.

**Theodore Roosevelt** is often described as the most prolific White House pet owner; his menagerie included multiple dogs of various breeds, many of them gifts. His terrier Skip and bull terrier Pete were acquired through more informal channels.

**Franklin D. Roosevelt** is most associated with Fala, a Scottish Terrier who became one of the most famous presidential pets in American history. Fala was given to Roosevelt by his cousin Margaret Suckley in 1940 and was acquired directly — not from a shelter.

**Lyndon B. Johnson** owned multiple dogs, but Yuki deserves specific treatment for this fact-check. Yuki was a cream-colored mixed-breed dog found by Luci Nugent (Johnson's daughter) at a gas station in Stonewall, Texas, on Thanksgiving Day in 1966, as she was en route to the LBJ Ranch. Luci initially kept Yuki, but during a White House visit, Yuki attached himself to the president, and Johnson eventually claimed him as his own companion. The LBJ Presidential Library affirmatively documents this history, including photographs of Johnson and Yuki interacting in the Oval Office. Yuki was found, not purchased and not adopted from a shelter — he was, in the broad popular sense, a rescue.

**Richard Nixon** famously received Checkers, a Cocker Spaniel, as a gift from a supporter. Nixon's emotional 1952 television address defending the dog against accusations of unethical gift reception — the "Checkers speech" — transformed the dog into a cultural and political landmark.

**Bill Clinton's** Buddy, a Labrador Retriever, was purchased from a breeder in 1997. The Clintons also had Socks the cat, who was taken in from a neighborhood as a stray kitten by Clinton's daughter Chelsea — another informal rescue.

**Barack Obama** acquired Bo, a Portuguese Water Dog, in 2009. Bo was a gift from Senator Edward Kennedy and had been purchased originally from a breeder; he had been returned to the breeder, making him a "rehomed" dog but one with breeder origins. The Obamas later adopted Sunny, also a Portuguese Water Dog, in 2013 — but Sunny was purchased from a breeder, not a shelter.

The pattern across presidential history is consistent: dogs were primarily purchased from breeders or received as gifts. No documented presidential dog prior to Major was adopted through a formal shelter or rescue organization. Major's distinction is established.

## §5 The Delaware Humane Association and the Institutional Significance of Shelter Adoption

The Delaware Humane Association (DHA), from which Major was adopted, is a nonprofit animal welfare organization established in 1889 — one of the oldest humane organizations in the United States. Its founding predates the formal federal recognition of animal cruelty as a legal category in many states, tracing to the broader 19th-century humane movement that established organizations including the American Society for the Prevention of Cruelty to Animals (ASPCA, founded 1866) and the American Humane Association (founded 1877).

The institutional fact that Major came from the DHA — a formal, chartered nonprofit with a licensed shelter function — is precisely what distinguishes his arrival at the White House from Yuki's informal discovery at a gas station. Shelters serve specific institutional functions that informal rescue does not: they provide veterinary screening and care, behavioral assessment, vaccination records, microchipping, and legal transfer of animal ownership. An animal adopted from a shelter arrives with documentation of its health status and a verified ownership chain. An animal informally acquired from a gas station parking lot arrives with none of this institutional record.

Scott and Serpell (1994), in *The Domestic Dog: Its Evolution, Behaviour and Interactions with People* (Cambridge University Press, edited by Serpell, J.), argued that the formal shelter system plays a distinct social function beyond pet placement: it serves as the primary institutional mechanism for tracking animal population dynamics, disease prevalence, and welfare standards in companion animal populations. When the president adopts from a shelter, the symbolic message — endorsement of the shelter system as a source of companion animals — has measurable effects on adoption rates, as documented by Weiss et al. (2012) in the *Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science* (Taylor & Francis). Studies of "celebrity adoption endorsements" have consistently found that public figures' public pet adoptions correlate with localized upticks in shelter inquiries and adoptions in the weeks following media coverage.

Biden's Major adoption was actively promoted by the Biden campaign and transition team as a symbolic statement about animal welfare values, and the Delaware Humane Association used the occasion to drive fundraising. The institutional significance of this first is therefore not purely ceremonial; it carried real-world effects on public behavior toward shelter adoption.

## §6 Media Literacy Lessons, Claim Spread Dynamics, and Post-White-House Context

The viral spread of the "first rescue dog" claim in November 2020 illustrates several recognizable patterns in how factually complex claims propagate through modern news ecosystems.

**Speed and sentiment**: The Biden election result was called on November 7, 2020, by major news organizations. Major's "first rescue dog" framing appeared in news coverage within 24 to 48 hours — during a period when emotional investments in the outcome were high and readers were receptive to positive stories about the president-elect. Ciampiconi et al. (2021), in a pre-print analysis of Twitter engagement patterns following the 2020 election result (later published in *Social Media + Society*, SAGE), documented that feel-good stories associated with the Biden transition achieved above-average engagement during this period because of the affective context. Positive animal stories have a well-documented engagement premium (Jonkman et al., 2020, *Journalism*, SAGE).

**Definitional shortcuts in headlines**: Headline word limits structurally discourage definitional precision. "Will Biden's dog Major be the first shelter-adopted dog to live in the White House?" is accurate but approximately 40 percent longer than "Will Biden's dog Major be the first rescue dog in the White House?" — and the shorter headline is what got written.

**Easy verifiability the claim afforded**: Ibrahim's (2020) correction required only two pieces of information: (1) what "rescue dog" means and (2) who Yuki was. Both are in public historical record. The claim's Mixture status could have been established with a brief library or internet search. The fact that it propagated uncorrected through major news outlets before Snopes's fact-check reflects the environment of high-volume, low-individual-verification publication that characterizes digital news cycles.

**Post-White-House note**: Major Biden had a complicated White House tenure. Multiple reports in early 2021 described Major as having bitten Secret Service agents and other White House staff. By April 2021, both Major and Champ had been moved out of the White House and returned to the Biden family's Delaware residence, and Champ died in June 2021. The Biden family later acquired Commander, a new German Shepherd, who also experienced behavioral challenges at the White House. Major's actual residence at the White House was therefore brief. This post-verification development does not affect the accuracy of the historical first claim established in November 2020 but adds ironic texture to the heavily symbolic framing that attended Major's transition-period coverage.

The Snopes Mixture verdict remains the appropriate assessment. The shelter-adoption first is real and documented. The uncaveated "first rescue dog" claim requires the historical footnote of Yuki. Both things are true simultaneously, and the epistemic value of the Mixture verdict lies precisely in its refusal to flatten this complexity into a simple True or False.
