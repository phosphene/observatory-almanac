# Tom Hanks Is Making a Sequel to 'Big' Titled 'Big Again'


## 1. The Claim

On April 9, 2024, a post appeared on the Facebook page YODA BBY ABY — an account with a history of publishing fabricated entertainment news — announcing that Tom Hanks would star in a sequel to the 1988 Penny Marshall film *Big*, to be titled *Big Again*. The post described an elaborate plot in which Josh Baskin, the original film's protagonist — a 12-year-old who wakes up in an adult body — returns in the year 2039 to become a boy again, this time rediscovering childhood alongside his grandson. The post concluded that *Big Again* would arrive in theatres in February 2025.

The announcement circulated rapidly. By the time Snopes investigated the claim in April 2024, the post had accumulated more than 10,000 reactions, 2,700 comments, and 2,900 shares on Facebook (Wrona, Aleksandra. 2024. Snopes.com, April 16, 2024). Comment threads showed substantial numbers of users expressing genuine excitement for the purported film: "Can't wait for this movie. I loved the first one," was a representative response. The post spread beyond Facebook to other platforms, including entertainment-adjacent comment communities and fan forums, where it was discussed as if the sequel were a genuine development.

The claim's production values matter here. The text of the announcement is written in the register of legitimate entertainment press releases — it uses phrases like "heartwarming sequel," "weaves a tale of family, imagination," and "proves that true greatness lies in the impact we have on others." It describes a sequel premise that is not obviously implausible: the adult-becomes-child reversal is a logical mirror image of the original film's conceit, and the intergenerational dynamic (grandson instead of contemporaries) is the kind of update a genuine sequel might genuinely make. The post sounds, to a casual reader without specialist film industry knowledge, like something that could have come from a legitimate announcement.

The specific details of the announcement — including the February 2025 release date, the year 2039 setting, and the grandson character — are sufficiently specific to trigger verisimilitude responses in readers who do not stop to check the source. Specific numbers and dates in false claims function as credibility anchors: the more precisely specified a claim, the more likely it reads as the product of actual knowledge rather than fabrication (Pennycook, Gordon, and David G. Rand. 2019. "Lazy, Not Biased: Susceptibility to Partisan Fake News Is Better Explained by Lack of Reasoning Than by Motivated Reasoning." *Cognition* 188: 39–50). In the absence of source-checking, the specificity of the *Big Again* claim made it more, not less, convincing.

A parallel circulation track carried a version of the story through Giant Freakin Robot, a film and entertainment news aggregation website, which in July 2023 had run a piece claiming Tom Hanks was attached to a sequel tentatively called *Big 2* (Giant Freakin Robot, July 2023). This legitimate entertainment speculation — reported as unconfirmed rumour, not confirmed news — provided a pre-existing reference point that may have given the satirical post additional apparent credibility for readers who had encountered the earlier speculation.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim that Tom Hanks is making a sequel to *Big* — under any title, including *Big Again* or *Big 2* — has no factual basis as of the writing of this entry. There is no confirmed production, no studio announcement, no attached director, and no evidence that the project has moved past the level of unconfirmed industry speculation.

The Facebook post originated from YODA BBY ABY, a page whose own description states: "The page is 100% satire and fake news" (Facebook page description, cited in Wrona, 2024). This is a self-labelling defence that satirical misinformation pages have adopted partly in response to increased fact-checking pressure: by labelling themselves satirical in their About section while posting fabricated content that reads as genuine news, they maintain legal and platform-policy cover while continuing to generate viral engagement from readers who never see the disclaimer.

The casting of the claim as satire is itself something of a misnomer. Satire in the analytical sense involves humour, critique, or parody — a satirical *Big* sequel announcement might, for example, imagine Josh Baskin wishing to be young again in an ironic comment on nostalgia culture or technological infantilism. The *Big Again* post does none of this. It is a straightforwardly presented fake announcement with no comedic or critical register. The "satire" label is a legal and platform-policy shelter, not an accurate description of the content's genre.

The closest the claim has to a basis in reality is the July 2023 Giant Freakin Robot report, which noted unconfirmed Hollywood speculation that Tom Hanks had been associated with discussions about a *Big* sequel. However, Elizabeth Perkins, who co-starred with Hanks in the original 1988 film as Susan Lawrence, directly addressed the sequel question in a July 2023 interview. Perkins stated that to her knowledge there had never been any substantive discussions about making a sequel, and suggested that neither Hanks, herself, nor the late Penny Marshall had had interest in revisiting the film (Perkins, Elizabeth. Interview cited in Screen Rant, July 2023). Penny Marshall, who directed the original film, died in 2018 at the age of 75.

The 1988 film *Big* was produced by James L. Brooks and Robert Greenhut for 20th Century Fox, directed by Marshall from a screenplay by Gary Ross and Anne Spielberg, and featured Tom Hanks in the role of Josh Baskin — a role for which Hanks received his first Academy Award nomination for Best Actor (Ross, Gary, and Anne Spielberg. 1988. *Big*. Twentieth Century Fox). The film grossed approximately $151 million domestically against a production budget of $18 million, making it a substantial commercial success by the standards of the period (Box Office Mojo, historical data). It remains one of the most critically regarded comedies of the 1980s and is frequently cited in discussions of Hanks' career-defining early work.

The film's status as a cultural touchstone — combined with Hanks' continued global fame across film, television, and public life — makes it a perennial candidate for sequel speculation. This is a structural feature of how Hollywood intellectual property is discussed: beloved films by major stars generate persistent sequel rumour precisely because the ingredients for a sequel (the star, the property rights, the audience nostalgia) are all present. That none of these ingredients have coalesced into an actual production is not evidence that they never will, but it is the factual state as of 2026.


## 3. The Epistemic Specificity of Entertainment Misinformation

Snopes' categorisation of the *Big Again* claim as "Labeled Satire" is technically accurate. The Facebook page that posted the claim did self-label as satirical. The rating is also, in the context of the specific post's real-world effects, somewhat insufficient as analysis — not because Snopes is wrong but because the "Labeled Satire" category bundles together content that differs substantially in intent, design, and harm potential.

Genuine satirical news — think *The Onion*'s years of hyper-realistic fake headlines, or the UK's *The Daily Mash* — functions by activating recognition in readers that the content is not literal. The joke requires the reader to see the gap between the absurd claim and apparent form. The *Big Again* post does not work this way: its viral success was measured in readers who commented as if the sequel were real. There is no evidence that the YODA BBY ABY post was designed to produce knowing laughter; it was designed to produce sharing, which it did in the ten-thousands.

This points to a genuinely novel epistemic category that fact-checkers are still developing frameworks for: entertainment fake news that is designed to be indistinguishable from real entertainment news, carries a plausibility buffer from genuine ambient speculation, and is legally protected under the satire label while functionally operating as straightforward misinformation. The Snopes "Labeled Satire" rating implies a certain amount of reader culpability — you should have checked the page's About section — but this standard does not account for how information travels once divorced from its source context, which is the normal condition of viral information on social media.

The *Big Again* case is also an instance illustrating a broader trend in entertainment misinformation that scholars of digital media have begun to document. The rise of AI-assisted text generation has substantially lowered the friction cost of producing plausible-sounding entertainment press release prose. A fake announcement that, ten years ago, would have required either significant writing skill or labour to produce at scale can now be generated in seconds with consumer-grade tools. This does not mean the *Big Again* post was AI-generated — there is no way to determine this from public evidence — but it does mean that the category of claim it represents is becoming structurally easier to produce (Wardle, Claire, and Hossein Derakhshan. 2017. "Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policy Making." *Council of Europe Report* DGI(2017)09).

There is also a specificially interesting structural feature of sequel fakery as a misinformation format. Sequel announcements are a category of entertainment news that readers have learned to expect. The logic of intellectual property management in the contemporary entertainment industry — sequels, reboots, franchise extensions — means that the announcement "beloved film X is getting a sequel" is a plausible template for any year, any property, any major star. Unlike fake news about unique events (a specific election outcome, a specific natural disaster), fake sequel announcements are true to generic structure: the general form "star to return in sequel to beloved classic" is confirmed true dozens of times per year in the entertainment industry. Any specific instance of that true general form is therefore more likely to be believed in isolation than a fake claim of a more unusual type. Fake sequel announcements exploit the statistical base rate of real sequel announcements.

This is the epistemic gap that Snopes does not address: the *Big Again* post did not fail because readers were especially credulous or inattentive. It succeeded because the claim was structurally consistent with a generic form readers have been correctly trained to recognise as regularly true, and because source-verification friction is higher than it should be in the social media environments where the claim spread.


## 4. Why People Believed This

The immediate mechanism of belief is emotional: *Big* is a beloved film. Tom Hanks is one of the most admired public figures in American entertainment culture. A sequel that would reunite Hanks with the character of Josh Baskin is something a very large number of people would genuinely want. The psychological literature on motivated reasoning in belief formation is well-established: people are more likely to accept claims that confirm desires than claims that are merely plausible on neutral grounds (Kunda, Ziva. 1990. "The Case for Motivated Reasoning." *Psychological Bulletin* 108(3): 480–498). The *Big Again* claim didn't need to be especially convincing to generate engagement from those who had emotional investment in its truth.

There is also a specific cultural dynamic around Tom Hanks that amplifies this effect. Hanks occupies an unusual position in the contemporary celebrity landscape as a figure widely regarded as possessing personal integrity — a reputation cultivated over decades of roles and public statements. His announcement of any project tends to carry greater weight among casual audiences than comparable announcements from other stars because audiences pre-trust the project. Fake Hanks-related entertainment news leverages this accumulated credibility without Hanks' knowledge or consent.

The timing of the post — April 2024 — placed it in a period of elevated Hollywood sequel and reboot nostalgia activity. The post-pandemic period of 2021 through 2025 saw an unusually concentrated wave of legacy IP revivals, including films and television series revisiting properties from the 1980s and 1990s. In this environment, the announcement of a *Big* sequel was not only emotionally appealing but contextually plausible: it fit a recognised pattern of what Hollywood was actually doing in those years (Loock, Kathleen. 2022. "The Hollywood Sequel: History and Practice of a Film Genre." *Bloomsbury Academic*). Audiences' mental model of what Hollywood does was correctly calibrated to a period in which this kind of announcement was being made, and the fake announcement exploited that correct calibration.

The prior Giant Freakin Robot speculation about "*Big 2*" in July 2023 — though reported as unconfirmed rumour — may have contributed a prior probability anchor for readers. Having seen a legitimate entertainment site discuss the possibility of a sequel, users who saw the April 2024 post had a memory trace suggesting such a project had been discussed. Confirmation bias — the tendency to weight confirming information more heavily than disconfirming information (Nickerson, Raymond S. 1998. "Confirmation Bias: A Ubiquitous Phenomenon in Many Guises." *Review of General Psychology* 2(2): 175–220) — would lead such readers to integrate the fake announcement with the real speculation, producing a higher confidence that the sequel was real than either source alone would have generated.


## 5. Verdict

**Propositional status: False. The specific *Big Again* claim is a fabrication from a self-identified satirical page with no basis in confirmed film production.**

No sequel to *Big* has been announced by any studio, confirmed by Tom Hanks, or entered into production as of 2026. Elizabeth Perkins, a co-star of the original film, stated in 2023 that she had no knowledge of sequel discussions. The general speculation about a *Big* sequel exists in the ambient entertainment industry discourse but has never been confirmed. The specific claim that Tom Hanks would return in *Big Again* for a February 2025 release is a fabrication.

Snopes' "Labeled Satire" rating is technically accurate regarding the source's self-description, but the rating is incomplete as analysis of why the claim was widely believed and what category of misinformation it represents.

**EARC assessment: B — Snopes addressed and correctly identified as non-credible, but analytical gap exists in explaining the structural mechanics of entertainment misinformation of this type.**


## 6. The Wider Picture

The *Big Again* episode is one of thousands of entertainment fake news incidents that circulate in any given year across social media platforms. Its individual significance is modest. Its structural significance is substantial: it illustrates how a small group of variables — beloved IP, beloved star, plausible sequel format, ambient industry speculation, self-labelled "satirical" source, high-friction fact-checking environment — combine to produce viral misinformation with relatively low production cost and no clear accountability mechanism.

The dominant intellectual analysis of entertainment misinformation in the mid-2020s tends to focus on political misinformation: fabricated quotes attributed to politicians, manipulated videos of public figures, coordinated influence operations targeting elections. Entertainment fake news operates by different logic because its consequences are typically less severe — believing Tom Hanks is making a sequel to *Big* and being disappointed when it isn't announced is not the same as believing a fabricated news story about a public health intervention or an election result. This lower-stakes profile has meant entertainment fake news receives less research and platform policy attention than political misinformation despite being substantially more prevalent in terms of raw volume and audience reach.

What the entertainment fake news ecosystem does do — at scale, over time — is contribute to the general erosion of epistemic infrastructure that political misinformation also requires to function. Reading dozens of convincing fake entertainment announcements that later prove false, without ever encountering explanations for why the claims were false, trains audiences toward a generalised uncertainty about information provenance that eventually bleeds into higher-stakes domains. The audience that was trained not to trust *Big Again* because it was "probably fake news" and the audience that did believe it are equally poorly served by an information environment in which the correct answer requires source investigation that the medium discourages. Both groups are learning lessons that have applications beyond Tom Hanks films.

The specific case of sequels to concluded films also raises questions worth noticing. Part of what makes the *Big Again* fabrication land so plausibly is that a sequel to *Big* is not only commercially plausible but broadly desired. The original film's ending — in which Josh chooses to return to childhood and a normalised life — is famously ambiguous in emotional register: the choice seems correct but also costs him the relationship with Susan Lawrence that the audience has been following for an hour and a half. A sequel that revisits that choice from the perspective of an adult man looking back — or that revisits Josh Baskin in old age, as the *Big Again* fake proposed — is not only commercially viable but emotionally resonant as a concept. The fake announcement works partly because the sequel it describes is one that the audience would actually want to see. Misinformation about non-existent products we'd like to have is a distinct and underanalysed subcategory of entertainment fake news, and the *Big Again* case is a clean specimen of it.

Filmmakers, studios, and entertainment journalists who cover genuine sequel and reboot speculation operate in an information environment where their legitimate reporting is structurally difficult to distinguish from fabricated content. The responsibility for developing media literacy adequate to this environment is distributed — among readers, platforms, fact-checkers, and the entertainment press itself — but it has not been fully claimed by any party.
