## §1 — Claim & Verdict Overview

In March 2021, U.S. legislators were debating a federal minimum wage increase to $15 per hour. A focal point of this debate was the observation that McDonald's workers in Denmark already earned approximately $22 per hour — a figure that Snopes had previously investigated and confirmed as substantially accurate (Snopes fact-check on McDonald's Denmark wages, confirmed). This confirmation triggered a secondary round of claims: if McDonald's workers in Denmark make $22 an hour, surely the consumer price of a McDonald's product in Denmark must be dramatically higher than in the United States to compensate?

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2021) rated the claim that a Big Mac in Denmark is "substantially more" expensive than in the United States **False**. Direct price comparisons via food-delivery platforms yielded a Big Mac at approximately 30.00 DKK (about $4.73 USD at the time) in Copenhagen, compared to approximately $4.82 in Tulsa, Oklahoma. The Economist's Big Mac Index — the standard international reference for cross-country Big Mac price comparison — confirmed the finding at a slightly different price point: 30.00 DKK in Denmark versus $5.66 in the United States, translating (after purchasing-power-parity adjustment by exchange rate) to approximately 13% *cheaper* per-unit cost in Denmark.

Evon noted that informal single-source estimates varied: *New York Times* opinion columnist Nicholas Kristof (2020) estimated a Big Mac cost about 27 cents more on average in Denmark than in the United States, and Danish politician Ida Auken wrote in the *Washington Post* (2021) that a Danish burger costs "roughly a dollar more." These estimates bracket the same general conclusion: whether Danish Big Macs are slightly cheaper or at most a dollar more expensive, they are emphatically not "substantially more expensive" in any sense that would validate the claim that high McDonald's wages necessarily produce dramatically higher Big Mac prices.

The case illuminates the intersection of economic intuition, wages-price causation, purchasing power parity, and the politics of minimum-wage discourse — all mediated by one of the most recognizable and intellectually interesting price benchmarks in international economics.

## §2 — The Big Mac Index: Origins, Method, and Structural Features

The Big Mac Index was introduced by *The Economist* magazine in 1986 as a "lighthearted guide" to whether currencies are at their "correct" level — a humorous gloss that has since become one of the most widely cited informal tools in international economics education. Its creator, *Economist* journalist Pam Woodall, selected the Big Mac because it is a standardized product (same ingredients, assembly method, and brand specifications across countries) sold in a large number of countries, making it a practical basket-of-goods comparison.

The formal economic concept the Big Mac Index approximates is **Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)** — the theory that in the long run, exchange rates should adjust so that identical goods cost the same across countries when prices are expressed in a common currency. A comprehensive and academically validated PPP approach uses a large basket of comparable goods; the Big Mac Index uses only one item, which makes it analytically crude but pedagogically accessible. Crucially, the Big Mac price reflects not only the cost of its ingredients but also local labor costs, commercial real estate prices, corporate and franchise tax structures, supply chain logistics, and consumer purchasing patterns. This multi-factor embedding is both its strength as a holistic price signal and its weakness as an analytical tool for isolating specific causal variables.

Empirical validation of the Big Mac Index as an exchange rate predictor is mixed. Pakko and Pollard (1996, *Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis Review*) found that while the index does not predict short-term exchange rate movements, it has shown moderate correlation with longer-run currency movements over periods of several years. Clements and Lan (2010, *Review of International Economics*) found that the Big Mac Index outperformed simple purchasing-power-parity calculations derived from more complex baskets in certain forecasting tests, largely because of its accessibility and the absence of methodological disagreements that complicate multi-good indexes.

The **Denmark figure** in the March 2021 debate was not simply pulled from random social media; it came specifically from the Economist's published Big Mac Index data, which at the time showed Denmark at 30.00 DKK. The GDP-adjusted version of the index showed Denmark's Big Mac as approximately 13% cheaper than the U.S. version — a result that seemed counterintuitive to many observers and requires understanding how GDP adjustment works. The GDP-adjusted Big Mac Index corrects for the fact that prices of traded goods should differ across countries with different income levels: poorer countries should have cheaper Big Macs not because of efficiency but because of lower general price levels. When Denmark is adjusted for its higher GDP per capita (and hence its higher general price level), its Big Mac is actually somewhat underpriced relative to what the naïve PPP calculation would predict.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Wage-Price Intuition Trap and the Denmark Case

The epistemic novelty in this case lies not in the Big Mac data itself — the price information was publicly available — but in the remarkably strong and widespread intuition that high wages *must* produce high prices, an intuition that persisted against directly available evidence.

The underlying economic intuition is not unreasonable in principle: wages are a production cost, and production costs contribute to final prices. Elementary cost-plus pricing logic suggests that if labor costs double, prices should rise to maintain margins. This logic is the foundation of a recurring objection to minimum wage increases: that they will produce inflation that erodes the real value of the wage gain. Yet the empirical evidence on minimum wage increases and price levels in developed economies consistently shows that the price effects are moderate and far smaller than the full labor cost increase would predict under naive pass-through assumptions.

The seminal modern literature here is Dube, Lester, and Reich (2010, *Review of Economics and Statistics*), which used paired-county comparison methodology along state borders to examine job effects of minimum wage increases. On the price side, Aaronson (2001, *The Economic Journal*) found that restaurant price increases following minimum wage increases in the United States were real but small — approximately 0.7% per dollar increase in the minimum wage — far smaller than the 4–7% increase that would be predicted by full labor-cost pass-through. The canonical theoretical framework reconciling these findings involves: (1) labor market monopsony, where wage increases reduce employer market power rents rather than fully passing into prices; (2) efficiency wages, where higher wages reduce turnover and training costs partially offsetting the direct cost increase; and (3) demand effects, where higher wages for low-wage workers increase consumer spending, supporting revenue even as unit margins tighten.

The Denmark case adds a second layer to the standard wage-price discussion: **cross-country institutional differences** substantially alter how labor costs translate into consumer prices. Denmark's higher labor costs at McDonald's are embedded in an institutional context of collective bargaining (Danish McDonald's workers are covered by the 3F trade union contract), comprehensive social insurance that reduces employer per-worker overhead costs for health and retirement relative to U.S. healthcare-inclusive compensation packages, a highly educated and low-turnover labor force, and a consumption-tax system (VAT at 25%) that affects price levels in ways orthogonal to labor costs. The Big Mac price therefore simultaneously reflects all these institutional factors, making it impossible to read off a simple labor-cost-to-price multiplier from the Denmark example.

The **epistemic trap** is the intuitive simplicity of wage → price causation relative to the institutional complexity that actually governs the relationship. A claim of the form "if workers make $22/hour, burgers must cost more" is psychologically compelling because it follows a familiar cost-plus logic and requires no institutional knowledge to generate. Its falsity requires institutional knowledge to demonstrate. This asymmetry — simple intuitive claim, complex institutional correction — makes the Denmark Big Mac case a paradigmatic example of what Kahneman (2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*) would classify as a System 1 error: a fast, confident, intuitive answer that is wrong in ways that only System 2 reasoning can reveal.

## §4 — Denmark's Labor Model and the McDonald's Wage Comparison

The $22/hour figure for McDonald's workers in Denmark requires context to interpret correctly in the U.S. debate. The figure primarily derives from a 2016 report and subsequent coverage noting that Danish McDonald's employees covered under the 3F union contract earn approximately 145–165 DKK per hour (roughly $21–24 USD depending on exchange rate), significantly above the U.S. federal minimum wage of $7.25 or effective fast-food wages in most U.S. states.

However, this headline comparison omits several structural features of Danish labor compensation. Stückelberger (2019, writing in *Labor History*) documented that total compensation packages in Danish fast food, when accounting for the Danish Arbejdsmarkedets Tillægspension (ATP — a mandatory labor market pension), the Feriegod holiday pay system (which adds approximately 12.5% to base wages as paid holiday entitlement), and the 3F union contract's scheduling and overtime provisions, represent a total compensation cost to employers substantially higher than the headline wage rate alone. By comparison, U.S. fast-food work often lacks employer-provided retirement contributions and paid sick leave in significant portions of the labor market.

Conversely, Denmark's employer-side social security contributions are structured differently from U.S. payroll taxes: Denmark's social insurance system is funded primarily through general income taxation and VAT rather than payroll taxes, which shifts the financing of social insurance away from a direct per-worker cost to employers. This institutional difference is one mechanism by which higher wage rates can coexist with consumer prices that are not proportionally elevated.

The "Denmark model" for fast-food labor has become a recurring reference point in U.S. minimum-wage advocacy, with proponents arguing that the Danish case demonstrates the feasibility of dramatically higher fast-food wages in a successful economy. Critics counter that Denmark's institutional context — smaller workforce size, union density approaching 67%, strong social insurance safety nets — is sufficiently different from the U.S. context that direct transferability is limited. Neither position is well served by the false claim that Denmark's Big Macs are dramatically more expensive; this claim misrepresents the factual landscape and tends to obscure rather than illuminate the actual mechanisms that allow Denmark to sustain higher fast-food wages.

## §5 — The Viral Minimum-Wage Discourse Context

The March 2021 resurgence of Denmark Big Mac claims occurred in a politically charged moment. Democratic legislators, including Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, were actively pushing for inclusion of a $15 federal minimum wage in the American Rescue Plan (which ultimately did not include it). Opponents of the increase, across social media and in some opinion journalism, seized on the Denmark high-wage/high-price narrative as evidence that wage increases inevitably produce inflation that offsets consumer gains.

The factual structure of this rhetorical move is important: the underlying claim about Denmark's McDonald's wages was accurately confirmed by Snopes; only the secondary claim about Big Mac prices was false. This "false implication from true premise" pattern is distinct from fabricated claims: the first part (high wages) is real, which gives the overall narrative chain a credibility coat that the second part (much higher prices) benefits from by association.

Evon's fact-check itself noted a 2021 article correction: the original version erroneously used Swedish Kronor (SEK) rather than Danish Krone (DKK) in its currency conversions, which affected the specific price figures cited but not the directional conclusion. This correction is methodologically notable: currency errors in price comparisons can produce large apparent discrepancies even when the underlying directional finding is correct. SEK/USD and DKK/USD exchange rates are not dramatically different at most historical moments, but using the wrong currency introduces unnecessary imprecision into fact-check research on cross-country price comparisons, and the correction illustrates the importance of currency verification in international economic fact-checking.

## §6 — Research Gaps and EARC Assignment

**EARC Assignment: A (Analytical)**

The Big Mac Index / Denmark minimum wage case exposes several significant research gaps at the intersection of economic fact-checking, labor economics, and public economic understanding.

**Gap 1: Public intuition accuracy about wage-price pass-through.** No systematic empirical study has directly measured lay population accuracy in predicting the magnitude of price increases following specific minimum wage increases in real historical cases. Survey-based research on public economic beliefs (Katz & Krueger, 1992, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*; more recent surveys by IGM Forum) has documented disagreement among economists but has not focused on comparing lay intuitions to documented empirical outcomes. A well-designed study could present respondents with wage increase scenarios and request predicted price impacts, then compare to observed outcomes.

**Gap 2: Institutional mediation in cross-country wage-price comparisons.** The existing literature on purchasing power parity and the Big Mac Index does not systematically decompose the sources of cross-country Big Mac price variation into labor cost, commercial real estate, supply chain, taxation, and demand-side components. Such a decomposition, using the multi-country Big Mac price panel available from the Economist data archive, would substantially enrich our understanding of how labor costs translate (or do not) into consumer prices across different institutional contexts.

**Gap 3: Correction dynamics for false economic implications from true premises.** The misinformation correction literature (Nyhan & Reifler, 2010, *Political Behavior*; Walter & Tukachinsky, 2020, *Advances in Political Psychology*) has examined how corrections affect belief in false claims. The specific case of false implications generated from true premises — where acknowledging the truth of the premise seems to validate the false implication — has not been specifically studied. The Denmark case is a natural paradigm: does correcting the false implication while confirming the true premise (high wages) produce backfire effects or confusion?

**Gap 4: Temporal stability of the Big Mac Index as a minimum-wage argument touchstone.** The Big Mac Index Denmark data fluctuates with exchange rates and menu price changes. A claim that was false in March 2021 (when the DKK/USD rate placed Danish Big Macs at approximately USD $4.73) could become true or false at different exchange rate configurations. No framework for distinguishing "stable falsity" from "exchange-rate-contingent falsity" in Big Mac Index-based claims has been developed in fact-checking methodology literature.
