# "Bill Gates Is Funding a Project to Block the Sun"


## 1. The Claim

The claim, circulating widely since approximately 2021, holds that technology billionaire Bill Gates is personally funding a secretive scientific project designed to physically block sunlight from reaching Earth—an audacious planetary intervention that would cool global temperatures, disrupt crop growth, and, in more conspiratorial tellings, constitute a covert form of population control or weather manipulation. The framing varies: some versions describe it as a direct effort to reduce global temperatures by bouncing sunlight back into space; others cast it as a deliberate dimming of the sun that would catastrophically harm agriculture and human health worldwide.

The claim gained particular traction after reporting about the Stratospheric Controlled Perturbation Experiment (SCoPEx), a research program at Harvard University co-led by professors David Keith and Frank Keutsch, which received funding from Gates and others. Media coverage of SCoPEx—often sensationalized—fed into existing narratives about billionaire technocrats pursuing dystopian terraforming schemes unaccountable to the public. Viral social media posts depicted the project as a conspiracy against ordinary people by a global elite.

This narrative intersects with several pre-existing anxieties: distrust of billionaire-directed philanthropy, concern about undemocratic techno-solutionism in climate policy, suspicion of stratospheric chemtrails, and fear that powerful private actors could unilaterally alter the planetary climate without global consent. These anxieties are not wholly irrational—they attach to genuinely contested questions about governance, legitimacy, and risk that the scientific community has not resolved.

What gives the claim its inflammatory resilience is not pure fabrication. Gates did fund solar geoengineering research. Harvard did prepare a stratospheric balloon experiment. Stratospheric aerosol injection does involve the idea of scattering incoming solar radiation. The charge of "blocking the sun" is therefore a distortion of real facts rather than an invention—making it a classic case where fact-checking must navigate the space between the false and the deceptive-but-grounded.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rates the core claim as a "Mixture," and this verdict is defensible in the broadest sense. Gates did provide funding for the Harvard Solar Geoengineering Research Program (HSGRP), which hosted SCoPEx. His Fund for Innovative Climate and Energy Research (FICER), established circa 2007, has awarded grants to a variety of climate energy projects including early solar geoengineering work led by David Keith at Harvard (Keith, D., 2013, *A Case for Climate Engineering*, MIT Press). This connection between Gates and the research program is verifiable fact.

However, the claim that Gates is "funding a project to block the sun" requires three leaps beyond the factual record, each of which significantly distorts what was actually proposed.

**First distortion — scale**: SCoPEx was a small-scale scientific experiment, not a large-scale deployment. The experiment planned to send a high-altitude balloon 20 kilometers into the stratosphere and release approximately 100 grams to 2 kilograms of calcium carbonate particles in a controlled puff to observe how these particles disperse and interact with stratospheric ozone (Dykema, J.A., Keith, D.W., Anderson, J.G., & Weisenstein, D., 2014, *Stratospheric controlled perturbation experiment*, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society A*, 372, 20140059). This is not "blocking the sun." The amount of material is minuscule compared to what would be needed for any meaningful radiative forcing.

**Second distortion — intent**: The stated purpose of SCoPEx was to gather observational data to improve computer models used in climate research—not to demonstrate the viability of a deployment system or to initiate any kind of sun-dimming process. As Keith and colleagues have written, fundamental questions about stratospheric chemistry under the influence of injected particles remain unanswered; the experiment was basic science, not planetary engineering (Keith, D.W., Weisenstein, D.K., Dykema, J.A., & Keutsch, F.N., 2016, *Stratospheric solar geoengineering without ozone loss*, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 113(52), 14910–14914).

**Third distortion — current status**: SCoPEx was suspended in August 2023 and officially canceled in March 2024 (Salata Institute for Climate and Sustainability, Harvard University, 2024). The platform developed for the experiment was reoriented toward basic atmospheric science unrelated to solar geoengineering. Any framing of Gates as presently "funding a project to block the sun" is therefore out of date in addition to being characterologically misleading.

What Snopes correctly handles is the factual scaffolding: the funding connection is real, the research program existed, the concept of solar radiation management is legitimate science, and the "Mixture" verdict appropriately signals that the claim is neither pure invention nor accurate description.


## 3. The Epistemological Gap

Snopes arrives at its "Mixture" verdict through a relatively conventional debunking approach: confirm the funding connection, establish the limited scale and scientific (rather than operational) nature of SCoPEx, note the cancellation, and resist the more inflammatory version of the claim. This approach is epistemologically sound as far as it goes. But the methodology treats the controversy primarily as a misinformation problem—a case of accurate facts being spun into a misleading frame—rather than as a window into genuinely unresolved questions in climate governance.

The deeper epistemological gap is this: the controversy about Gates and SCoPEx is also a legitimate political and ethical debate that fact-checking alone cannot resolve, and conflating the two can inadvertently trivialize the second while correcting the first.

Consider the governance lacuna. Solar geoengineering research does not currently exist within any internationally agreed regulatory framework. No treaty governs who can conduct outdoor stratospheric experiments, what thresholds of scale require multilateral approval, or how affected communities—particularly in the Global South, whose rainfall patterns would be most disrupted by large-scale stratospheric aerosol injection—can exercise meaningful veto or consent rights (Biermann, F., & Möller, I., 2019, *Rich Man's Solution? Solar Geoengineering, the 'Beautiful Risk,' and the Continued Hegemonial Privilege of the Global North*, *International Environmental Agreements*, 19(6), 797–809). When the Mexican government banned outdoor geoengineering experiments in early 2023 after a US startup, Make Sunsets, conducted unauthorized sulfur dioxide releases over Baja California, it was exercising exactly this kind of consent—but after the fact rather than before it.

The claim that Gates is "funding a project to block the sun" is epistemically false as a description of what SCoPEx was. But it is epistemically understandable as a political reaction to something that was real: a process in which a billionaire philanthropist, through non-transparent grant-making, provided resources for atmospheric experiments that had not been subjected to any democratic deliberation or international oversight. The folk political epistemology of the claim may be wrong about the facts while being correct about a structural problem.

This is where the Snopes methodology encounters its limits. The fact-check/false binary is well-suited to correcting false empirical assertions (Gates is not literally dimming the sun), but ill-suited to adjudicating the legitimacy concerns (should any private actor be able to fund research that could normalize, and eventually deploy, stratospheric aerosol injection without global consent?). A reader who walks away from the Snopes fact-check believing the controversy is simply misinformation has been given an accurate verdict about the literal claim and an incomplete picture about why that claim has such persistent purchase.

The epistemic novelty lies in recognizing that this is simultaneously a clear case of misinformation (the specific claim is distorted) and a legitimate regulatory concern (the structural situation that generated the claim is real and unresolved). Treating these as separable problems rather than intertwined phenomena is crucial for understanding why simple debunking fails to neutralize the controversy.


## 4. Why People Believe This

The claim about Gates blocking the sun achieves extraordinary virality by weaving together several distinct epistemic strands that each have their own evidential and psychological grounding. Understanding this anatomy is crucial for distinguishing between what is false and what is merely misframed.

**Techno-solutionism skepticism**: A substantial body of scholarship and policy analysis criticizes the tendency to pursue high-tech interventions for problems that are fundamentally political, economic, or social in origin (Winner, L., 1986, *Do Artifacts Have Politics?*, in *The Whale and the Reactor*, University of Chicago Press; Anderson, K., & Peters, G., 2016, *The Trouble with Negative Emissions*, *Science*, 354(6309), 182–183). The prospect of a billionaire-funded research program potentially normalizing planetary-scale climate intervention without democratic mandate fits this pattern of critique. This is not conspiracy—it is a mainstream concern in climate science and science-and-technology studies.

**Moral hazard doctrine**: The concern that solar geoengineering research could undermine mitigation ambition—by making a technological "Plan B" seem available, thus reducing political pressure to cut emissions—has been extensively theorized (Robock, A., Marquardt, A., Kravitz, B., & Stenchikov, G., 2009, *Benefits, risks, and costs of stratospheric geoengineering*, *Geophysical Research Letters*, 36, L19703). If the public believes that someone powerful is working on a sun-dimming fix, their engagement with decarbonization advocacy may diminish. This is a legitimate empirical concern with documented psychological analogues.

**Historical precedent for geoengineering governance failures**: The international community has repeatedly failed to establish proactive governance for transformative technologies before they reach deployment threshold. The history of nuclear technology, genetically modified organisms, and artificial intelligence each demonstrate that governance frameworks tend to lag behind technological development (Jasanoff, S., 2004, *The Idiom of Co-Production*, in *States of Knowledge*, Routledge). Public anxiety about solar geoengineering represents, among other things, a political demand for governance that does not yet exist.

**Chemtrail conspiracy precedent**: The claim fits neatly into pre-existing chemtrail narratives, which hold that airplanes routinely release chemical agents into the atmosphere for covert purposes (Shearer, C., West, M., Caldeira, K., & Davis, S.J., 2016, *Quantifying expert consensus against the existence of a secret large-scale atmospheric spraying program*, *Environmental Research Letters*, 11(8), 084011). The chemtrail framing is straightforwardly incorrect as empirical science, but its cognitive infrastructure—suspicion of stratospheric interventions, distrust of powerful actors, anxiety about respiratory and agricultural effects—maps easily onto geoengineering discussions.

**Private power over global commons**: The prospect of a wealthy private actor exercising significant influence over research that could affect the global atmospheric commons raises questions about concentrated power with no adequate institutional frame to contain or answer them. Bill Gates's philanthropic record is itself contested: critics argue that his foundation has wielded disproportionate influence over global health, agricultural, and educational policy in ways that bypass democratic deliberation (Birn, A.E., 2014, *Philanthrocapitalism, past and present: The Rockefeller Foundation, the Gates Foundation, and the setting(s) of the international/global health agenda*, *Hypothesis*, 12(1), e8). These critiques are empirically debatable, but they form the political substrate within which the "blocking the sun" narrative becomes credible to audiences who might otherwise dismiss it.

**Psychological amplifiers**: The human brain is wired to overweight agentic (intentional) explanations for large-scale phenomena—especially threatening ones. The appearance of a single powerful actor (Gates), a distinctive and technically real action (stratospheric experiments), and a plausible mechanism of harm (sun-dimming effect on crops and climate) activates agency detection heuristics with great effectiveness (Epley, N., Waytz, A., & Cacioppo, J.T., 2007, *On Seeing Human: A Three-Factor Theory of Anthropomorphism*, *Psychological Review*, 114(4), 864–886). The narrative is cognitively sticky because it fits human intuitions about how threatening change happens.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Solar radiation management (SRM)—the broad category of interventions to reduce incoming solar energy as a climate response—occupies one of the most contested borderlands in contemporary climate science, policy, and ethics. Understanding this terrain is essential to understanding why the Gates/SCoPEx controversy is both empirically manageable and politically explosive.

**The Basic Science of Stratospheric Aerosol Injection**: SAI is the most studied form of SRM. The principle is straightforward: particles injected into the stratosphere (15–25 km altitude) scatter a fraction of incoming solar radiation, reducing the amount that reaches the surface. Volcanic eruptions provide natural analogues—the 1991 Mount Pinatubo eruption in the Philippines injected roughly 20 million tonnes of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, producing an estimated 0.5°C of global cooling over the following year (Kremser, S., et al., 2016, *Stratospheric aerosol—Observations, processes, and impact on climate*, *Reviews of Geophysics*, 54(2), 278–335).

The appeal of SAI as a potential emergency intervention is evident: it could be deployed relatively quickly compared to decarbonization, and its radiative effects would be rapid. Its risks are equally evident: regional precipitation changes (particularly monsoon disruption in Asia and Africa), stratospheric ozone depletion, termination shock (rapid warming if SAI were suddenly halted), and complete absence of effect on ocean acidification (a CO₂ problem that SRM cannot address) (Irvine, P.J., et al., 2019, *Halving warming with idealized solar geoengineering moderates key climate hazards*, *Nature Climate Change*, 9(4), 295–299; Jones, A., et al., 2022, *Regional climate impacts of stabilizing global warming at 1.5°C or 2°C above pre-industrial levels*, *Nature Climate Change*, 12, 249–253).

**The Governance Vacuum**: International governance of solar geoengineering research remains in its infancy. The United Nations Environment Programme issued a report in 2023 acknowledging governance gaps but stopping short of a binding framework. The Convention on Biological Diversity has called for precaution, but without enforcement capacity. In 2024, a group of scholars published an op-ed in *Science* calling for a formal non-use agreement (Biermann, F., et al., 2022, *Solar Geoengineering: The Case for an International Non-Use Agreement*, *WIREs Climate Change*, 13(3), e714). The signatories included not fringe actors but respected climate scientists and governance specialists—signaling that legitimate concern about premature normalization is widespread within expert communities.

**The Make Sunsets Affair**: In late 2022, the startup Make Sunsets—founded by former Google engineer Luke Iseman—conducted unauthorized balloon releases of sulfur dioxide over Baja California, Mexico, to attempt to generate carbon credits by cooling the planet. This drew condemnation from the scientific community and led the Mexican government to announce a moratorium on solar geoengineering experiments within its territory in early 2023 (Meza, A., & Ramos, A., 2023, *Mexico bans geoengineering experiments after rogue startup launches balloons*, *Reuters*, January 11, 2023). Make Sunsets operated outside any established research framework and their releases were at such small scale as to be scientifically meaningless—but the episode illustrated the governance gap: anyone with a balloon and sulfur dioxide could claim to be doing solar geoengineering.

**The SCoPEx Story in Full**: Harvard's SCoPEx program, by contrast, operated within a research institution with a formal safety committee and external advisory body. The experiment was subject to significant public scrutiny, including opposition from the Saami Council, which objected to a planned balloon launch from Sweden on grounds of Indigenous sovereignty and inadequate consultation. This objection led to the cancellation of the Sweden launch in 2021 and contributed to the broader slowdown of the program (Saami Council, 2021, *Saami Council rejects plans for geoengineering experiment in Sápmi*). SCoPEx never conducted its planned calcium carbonate release experiment before it was officially canceled in March 2024.

The cancellation has been interpreted variously: some see it as the scientific community exercising appropriate self-governance in response to public concern; others argue it represents governance failure in the other direction—precautionary paralysis that prevents legitimate research into a technology that may be critically needed in a 3°C warming scenario. Both positions have merit, and the debate is unresolved.

**The David Keith Position**: David Keith, the principal investigator of SCoPEx's predecessor research and a leading figure in solar geoengineering scholarship, has consistently argued that small-scale outdoor experiments are necessary and defensible to reduce the massive uncertainty that currently surrounds SRM effects. He distinguishes sharply between research (which he supports, with appropriate governance) and deployment (which he regards as requiring international agreement and to which he has not committed). His book *A Case for Climate Engineering* (Keith, D., 2013, MIT Press) was notable for its ambivalence as much as its advocacy: Keith acknowledges the serious risks and governance challenges while arguing that the alternative—researching nothing—is itself a form of risk-taking under uncertainty.

**Implications for Fact-Checking Practice**: The Gates/SCoPEx case illustrates a category problem in mainstream fact-checking. The claim "Gates is funding a project to block the sun" is straightforwardly false in its most dramatic interpretation. But the underlying concern—that private actors with immense resources can shape the normative trajectory of planetary-scale interventions without democratic accountability—deserves engagement, not merely correction. When fact-checkers dispatch the claim without addressing the structural concern, they may inadvertently reinforce the perception that elite institutions circle the wagons to protect each other, which itself feeds the conspiracist ecosystem they aim to counter.

A more robust epistemological approach would both correct the false claim and engage with the governance vacuum it reflects. This does not mean treating all alarm as equally valid, but it does mean recognizing that "this specific fact is wrong" and "this specific concern is legitimate" can simultaneously be true.

**The Climate Stakes**: Solar geoengineering sits at the intersection of two existential risks: catastrophic climate change and the unintended consequences of large-scale technological planetary intervention. Neither of these risks admits simple solution. The fact-check framework, designed to adjudicate claims about facts, is useful but insufficient for navigating decisions about which technological research to pursue, under what governance conditions, and with what processes of consent. Those are genuinely political and ethical questions that require democratic deliberation, not fact-checking verdicts.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates the claim as a "Mixture," noting that while Gates has funded solar geoengineering research at Harvard, the characterization of this as a project designed to "block the sun" dramatically overstates the scale and intent of the research. Their fact-check establishes the Gates–SCoPEx funding connection, explains the limited nature of the proposed SCoPEx experiment (a small aerosol release from a high-altitude balloon to study stratospheric chemistry), and notes the project's suspension and cancellation.

The Snopes analysis is accurate in its core fact-checking function and appropriately resists the most sensationalized version of the claim. It correctly identifies the epistemic sleight of hand whereby legitimate small-scale research is transformed, through scale inflation and intent distortion, into a planetary intervention scheme.

However, the analysis is limited by the binary framing that standard fact-checking imposes. Snopes does not substantively engage with the governance questions that the controversy raises: whether any private actor should fund outdoor atmospheric experiments without multilateral oversight, what consent rights communities downwind of stratospheric interventions should hold, or whether normalizing the concept of solar geoengineering through incremental research steps creates path dependencies toward deployment regardless of intent. These are not fringe concerns—they are the subject of active debate in climate science, international law, and political philosophy.

Other fact-checkers have handled this topic with varying depth. PolitiFact and AFP Fact Check have addressed related claims about geoengineering, generally arriving at similar conclusions: the specific factual claims (Gates is blocking the sun now, it will destroy crops, it is an active deployment) are false; the background facts (funding, research, the existence of solar geoengineering as a concept) are accurate. None have systematically engaged with the governance gap as a legitimate element of the controversy.

The methodological verdict: the fact-check correctly adjudicates the empirical claims but does not fully reckon with the structural and political concerns that give those claims their cultural traction. The Snopes "Mixture" rating is defensible; a more complete analysis would pair it with substantive engagement with the governance debates that the claim—however distorted—puts on the table.
