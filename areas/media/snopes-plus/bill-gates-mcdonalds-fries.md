## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

At intervals throughout the 2020s, social media users have circulated a meme claiming that Microsoft co-founder and billionaire philanthropist Bill Gates owns farmland in Washington State that produces potatoes used in McDonald's french fries. The claim received renewed momentum on March 21, 2024, when a conspiracy-promoting account on X (formerly Twitter) reshared a version of the meme that had previously circulated on Facebook. Depending on the iteration, the meme attributed additional claims to the basic land-ownership fact: that the potatoes grown there were genetically modified (GMO), and that Gates' farm was the sole or primary supplier of potatoes to McDonald's worldwide.

Snopes investigator Alex Kasprak (2024) rated the core claim **True**. The farm in question—100 Circle Farms, located in Washington State near the Oregon border—is indeed a significant supplier of potatoes used in McDonald's french fries, and is owned by Gates through a chain of corporate holding companies ultimately traceable to his private investment firm, Cascade Investment. The GMO claim was rated false by Full Fact (2023), and the "sole supplier" framing is also false, as McDonald's sources potatoes from a variety of farms across the world. However, the stripped-down, accurate version of the claim—that Gates owns a farm that produces a large portion of McDonald's french fry potatoes—is factual.

This entry examines the specific corporate ownership chain connecting Gates to the farm, the epistemics of true-but-misleading claims that add false riders to accurate factual cores, the broader context of Bill Gates' land holdings and the conspiratorial ecosystem that those holdings have generated, and what the persistent virality of this claim reveals about the relationship between billionaire food-system influence and public concern.

## §2 The Ownership Chain: Cascade Investment, Angelina Ag, Oak River Farms, and 100 Circle Farms

The corporate structure connecting Bill Gates to 100 Circle Farms is a multi-entity chain of the kind commonly used in large agricultural acquisitions to manage liability, taxes, and operational complexity. Tracing this chain is essential to evaluating the truthfulness of the underlying claim.

In September 2018, a $171 million land sale in Benton County, Washington, was reported by the Tri-City Herald (Culverwell, 2018). The purchaser on record was a Louisiana-registered company named Angelina Ag. The Land Report—a media outlet specializing in land ownership and resource management—investigated the acquisition and reported that Angelina Ag was a subsidiary of Oak River Farms, which in turn was identified as a company managing agricultural holdings for Cascade Investment, Bill Gates' private investment vehicle (O'Keefe, 2018; O'Keefe, 2021, *The Land Report*). Cascade Investment, according to its own website, does not publicly discuss its asset portfolio, making independent third-party reporting like The Land Report's investigation essential to documenting the connection.

The Washington farmland acquired through this chain includes 100 Circle Farms, which is referenced by name on McDonald's official supplier information page. McDonald's describes 100 Circle Farms on its website: "100 Circle Farms grows potatoes in circles so big they're visible from space. Then our trusted processor, Lamb Weston, cuts them into fries at 70 miles per hour" (McDonald's, accessed March 2024). The farm's production scale is substantial: McDonald's notes that the farm can dig "anywhere from a thousand to twenty-four hundred tons of potatoes per day," with the majority destined for McDonald's french fries.

The potato processor connecting 100 Circle Farms to McDonald's products is Lamb Weston Holdings, Inc., one of the world's largest frozen potato processors, headquartered in Eagle, Idaho. Lamb Weston processes the raw potatoes grown at the farm into the frozen fries distributed to McDonald's locations (McDonald's supplier page, 2024). This intermediary relationship is important: 100 Circle Farms does not supply McDonald's directly with finished food products—it supplies the raw commodity that Lamb Weston processes. This distinction matters for evaluating exaggerated claims about Gates' direct control over the McDonald's supply chain.

No change in the ownership of 100 Circle Farms through the Cascade Investment / Oak River Farms / Angelina Ag structure had been reported as of the Snopes fact-check's publication in March 2024. The acreage of the Washington State purchase, estimated by The Land Report at roughly 14,500 acres in the Benton County transaction, represents a portion of Gates' total U.S. farmland holdings—which The Land Report calculated in January 2021 to make Gates the single largest private owner of U.S. farmland, at approximately 242,000 total acres across multiple states (O'Keefe, 2021, *The Land Report*).

When asked in a March 2019 Reddit AMA why his investment group was buying farmland, Gates provided a brief explanation: "My investment group chose to do this. It is not connected to climate. The agriculture sector is important. With more productive seeds we can avoid deforestation and help Africa deal with the climate difficulty they already face." This statement does not address the McDonald's connection specifically and does not acknowledge that the holdings would become a subject of conspiracy theories; it was made before most of the controversy had developed.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The True-but-Misleading Claim and the GMO Rider

The epistemically distinctive feature of this case is that the viral meme combines an accurate factual core with false add-on claims in a specific pattern that is significantly harder to debunk than purely false claims.

The base claim—Gates owns farmland that produces McDonald's french fry potatoes—is factually verified. This means that the meme carries what communication researchers call a *truth anchor*: at least one verifiable component that creates credibility for the broader claim package. When a debunker addresses the GMO rider as false, a significant portion of audiences correctly notes that the debunker has not denied the ownership claim, which they may interpret as tacit confirmation of the full claim including the false elements.

The GMO claim is the most significant false rider. No commercially approved GMO potato variety for McDonald's supply chain was in large-scale production in the Pacific Northwest as of 2024. The Simplot company has developed a GMO potato (the Innate potato) with reduced acrylamide and browning, but its commercial adoption by McDonald's had not been broadly implemented at the time of the meme's circulation (Full Fact, 2023). The inclusion of the GMO claim in the meme likely reflected a conflation of different conversations about food technology, Gates' agricultural investments, and his public statements about biotechnology in agriculture—none of which individually supports the specific claim that the Washington State potato farm produces GMO crops for McDonald's.

The "sole supplier" claim is straightforwardly false and is addressed in Kasprak's (2024) fact-check. McDonald's is a global enterprise sourcing food ingredients from thousands of suppliers across dozens of countries; the idea that a single 14,500-acre farm could supply the entire global McDonald's potato demand is numerically implausible. The claim appears to have entered circulation through rhetorical amplification: once the farm-to-McDonald's link was established (correctly), successive shares of the meme inflated the relationship from "significant supplier" to "primary supplier" to "sole supplier."

What Kasprak (2024) does not explicitly theorize—but which is epistemically important—is that this pattern of truth-anchor + false-rider construction appears repeatedly in food and health misinformation. The phenomenon is structurally analogous to what Pennycook and Rand (2019, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, Elsevier) call "bullshit receptivity": claims that are neither entirely false nor carefully accurate, but that use real data points to create compelling but misleading narrative frameworks. Kunda (1990, *Psychological Bulletin*, American Psychological Association) documented the motivated reasoning mechanism that allows audiences to selectively attend to truth-confirming elements of mixed claims while discounting disconfirming elements when the overall claim serves a pre-existing belief.

In the specific context of Gates-and-food claims, the truthful core—that Gates owns vast agricultural landholdings that supply major food companies—generates what is genuinely a legitimate topic for public policy debate: the concentration of agricultural land in the hands of billionaire investors. The false riders (GMO, sole supplier, implied malevolence) are grafted onto a real and important economic fact to transform a policy-relevant observation into a conspiracy narrative. This transformation is epistemically harmful precisely because it routes legitimate concern through demonstrably false claims, making the broader public less willing to engage with the underlying structural issue.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Kasprak's (2024) investigation correctly establishes the core factual truth and dispels both major false riders. Several additional threads merit attention for a comprehensive analytical record:

**Cascade Investment's full agricultural portfolio**: The Snopes article focuses on the Washington State purchase and 100 Circle Farms. However, Gates' full agricultural landholdings—across states including Louisiana, Arizona, Nebraska, and others—involve different crops, different supply chains, and, in some cases, different degrees of vertical integration. The claim specifically about McDonald's potatoes applies to a specific subset of those holdings, and this geographic and crop-specific precision is not systematically developed.

**Lamb Weston's role and ownership**: The fact-check correctly identifies Lamb Weston as the potato processor, but does not note that Lamb Weston Holdings, Inc. is itself a publicly traded company (NYSE: LW) that processes potatoes from many farms, not just 100 Circle Farms. Gates' farmland feeds into a commodity processor that serves multiple fast-food and retail clients; the McDonald's relationship is real but is mediated through a processing layer that is not under Gates' control.

**The environmental footprint of center-pivot irrigation farming**: 100 Circle Farms is named for the characteristic circular patterns visible from satellite imagery—signature formations created by center-pivot irrigation systems. These systems are highly water-intensive, drawing from the Columbia Basin aquifer system in an increasingly water-stressed region. This environmental dimension of Gates' agricultural holdings is not addressed in the Snopes fact-check but is relevant to the broader policy debate about large-scale land acquisition.

**Changes since 2024**: The fact-check notes no ownership changes as of March 2024. Subsequent changes in the Cascade Investment portfolio or in McDonald's supplier relationships would be relevant to updates of this entry.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Bill Gates, Land Ownership, and the Conspiratorial Food Narrative

The Bill Gates farmland controversy exists within a broader conspiratorial ecosystem that accelerated dramatically during the COVID-19 pandemic. Gates' investments in vaccine development, his public health communications, and his agricultural and food-technology holdings were bundled together in a coherent anti-elite narrative in which he was cast as a central node in a system of hidden control over human biology, food supply, and public health. This conspiracy cluster was remarkable for its combination of real facts (Gates does own substantial farmland; Gates Foundation does fund vaccine programs) with fictional frameworks (hidden microchips, population control, deliberate food manipulation).

Agricultural land concentration is a genuine and measurable trend. Nicolette Hahn Niman and others have documented the consolidation of U.S. farmland into larger and larger parcels owned by fewer entities (Lyson, 2004, *Civic Agriculture: Reconnecting Farm, Food, and Community*, Tufts University Press). A 2022 USDA Census of Agriculture report found that average farm size in the U.S. continued its multi-decade increase, while the number of small farms declined. In this context, Gates' position as the largest single owner of U.S. farmland is factually significant to ongoing policy debates about land reform, antitrust in agriculture, and the power of investment capital in the food system.

The conspiracy narrative, however, short-circuits engagement with these legitimate policy concerns by substituting false explanatory frameworks (GMO control, hidden population goals) for accurate descriptions of how agricultural investment actually works. This is a characteristic property of what Benkler et al. (2018, *Network Propaganda: Manipulation, Disinformation, and Radicalization in American Politics*, Oxford University Press) describe as "network propaganda": the co-optation of real concerns into demonstrably false narrative frameworks that compromise the credibility of those who raise the underlying concerns.

McDonald's supply chain dynamics are genuinely opaque to most consumers. The company sources ingredients globally through complex intermediary relationships, and those relationships are not easy to trace or audit without significant investigative effort. This opacity creates fertile ground for simplified (and frequently inaccurate) narratives about who controls what enters the food supply.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: TRUE** (with significant context)

Bill Gates, through his private investment vehicle Cascade Investment and its subsidiary entities (Oak River Farms, Angelina Ag), owns farmland in Washington State that includes 100 Circle Farms—a significant supplier of potatoes that are processed by Lamb Weston and sold to McDonald's for use in french fries. This is an accurate, verified factual claim.

The claim is **not** true in versions that add: (a) that the potatoes grown there are genetically modified—they are not; (b) that 100 Circle Farms is the sole supplier of McDonald's french fry potatoes—it is not, as McDonald's sources from a global network of suppliers; or (c) any implied claim about intentional malice or hidden control of the food supply.

**EARC Classification: R (Rhetorical — truth-anchor + false-rider construction)**

The analytical gap is rhetorical: the epistemically dangerous property of this claim family is not that it is simply false (which is easy to debunk) but that it is substantially true while incorporating false riders in a structure that makes wholesale debunking incorrectly appear to dispute the true core. Fact-checking in this category requires precision surgery rather than blanket contradiction.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- The full scope and crop diversity of Gates' agricultural holdings and their specific supply relationships
- The water footprint of center-pivot irrigation operations at 100 Circle Farms relative to Columbia Basin aquifer sustainability
- Whether any portion of the farmland has adopted Innate or other approved GM potato varieties since 2024
- The terms of the supply agreement between 100 Circle Farms / Lamb Weston and McDonald's
