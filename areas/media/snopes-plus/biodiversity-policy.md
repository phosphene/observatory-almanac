# "Biodiversity" (As Used in Policy)


## 1. The Claim

**Policy deployment**: "Biodiversity loss" represents a unified, measurable conservation crisis requiring immediate policy intervention through biodiversity protection frameworks, metrics, and international agreements. The term functions simultaneously as a scientific concept, a policy metric, a funding mechanism, and a moral imperative—roles that sometimes conflict with each other.

**Conservation rhetoric**: Global biodiversity is declining uniformly, measurably, and catastrophically, making biodiversity metrics reliable indicators of ecosystem health and conservation success. This framing underpins international agreements, national legislation, corporate sustainability reporting, and the rapidly growing market in biodiversity credits and offsets.

The claim's importance extends beyond conservation biology. "Biodiversity" has become one of the most consequential terms in environmental governance, directing billions in funding, shaping land-use policy, and increasingly entering corporate regulation and financial disclosure requirements. Whether the term adequately captures what it purports to measure has implications for whether this enormous policy apparatus is well-targeted.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Scientific origins and strategic intent**: The term's history is more revealing than most users realize. Thomas Lovejoy first used "biological diversity" in 1980, but it was Edward O. Wilson who deliberately compressed it to "biodiversity" for the 1986 National Forum on BioDiversity, later published as *BioDiversity* (1988). Wilson was explicit about his strategic intent: he designed the term to create political urgency around conservation by providing a unifying concept that policymakers and the public could understand and mobilize around. In this, he succeeded spectacularly—but the strategic compression that made the term politically effective also introduced the conceptual ambiguities that now plague its scientific use.

**The policy success**: Wilson's strategy worked beyond what any single academic coinage typically achieves. "Biodiversity" enabled the Convention on Biological Diversity (Rio, 1992), signed by 150 governments. It structured the UN Decade on Biodiversity (2011–2020), shaped the Aichi Biodiversity Targets, and culminated in the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022), which committed nations to protecting 30% of the planet's land and ocean areas by 2030 (the "30×30" target). The term mobilized hundreds of billions in conservation funding and policy commitments over three decades. Few scientific concepts have achieved comparable policy traction.

**The genuine conservation emergency**: The underlying crisis that Wilson sought to address is real and well-documented. The IPBES Global Assessment Report (2019)—the most comprehensive assessment of biodiversity ever undertaken, involving 145 expert authors from 50 countries—documented that approximately 1 million animal and plant species are threatened with extinction. Species extinction rates are estimated at 100–1,000 times higher than natural background rates (Ceballos et al. 2015, *Science Advances*). The Living Planet Report (WWF, 2022) documented an average 69% decline in monitored vertebrate wildlife populations between 1970 and 2018. These findings represent genuine ecological catastrophe requiring urgent response.


**The single-metric fallacy**: Using "biodiversity" as a unified conservation metric resembles using GDP as a unified economic health indicator. Both successfully mobilize policy attention and resource allocation. Both capture real and important trends in aggregate. And both systematically distort the phenomena they measure in ways that can produce counterproductive policy outcomes when optimized as targets rather than used as rough indicators.

Goodhart's Law—"When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure"—applies with particular force to biodiversity policy. Once "biodiversity" became the target metric for international agreements, corporate reporting, and offset markets, the incentive to optimize the metric diverged from the goal of protecting functional ecosystems.

**Policy coherence challenges**: Different biodiversity measures often conflict in practice. Areas with high species richness may have low genetic diversity (as in recently invaded ecosystems) or low ecosystem integrity (as in degraded habitats with high weed diversity). Conservation strategies optimized for one dimension may actively harm others. The "30×30" target—protecting 30% of land and ocean by 2030—does not specify which 30% or by which biodiversity measure, leaving enormous scope for strategic selection of areas that satisfy targets while providing minimal conservation benefit.

**What actually drives conservation success**: Habitat protection, pollution control, invasive species management, and climate change mitigation protect ecosystems more effectively than biodiversity maximization per se. Intact ecosystems typically maintain appropriate biodiversity naturally—it is the outcome of functional ecosystems, not the input that creates them. Watson et al. (2018) in *Nature* argued that the single most effective conservation strategy is protecting remaining intact ecosystems rather than attempting to restore or optimize biodiversity in degraded ones.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The measurement problem**: "Biodiversity" conflates multiple distinct ecological phenomena that do not necessarily correlate and respond differently to environmental pressures. At minimum, the term encompasses:

- **Species richness**: the number of species present in a given area
- **Genetic diversity**: variation within species' gene pools
- **Ecosystem diversity**: the variety of ecosystem types in a region
- **Functional diversity**: the range of ecological roles performed by organisms
- **Phylogenetic diversity**: the evolutionary distinctness of species assemblages

These dimensions can move in opposite directions simultaneously. An area can gain species richness (through invasive species colonization) while losing genetic diversity (through population fragmentation), functional diversity (through loss of specialist species), and phylogenetic diversity (through replacement of evolutionarily distinct endemic species with cosmopolitan generalists). A single "biodiversity" number cannot capture these divergent trends without making implicit value judgments about which dimensions matter most.

**Vellend's paradox**: Mark Vellend's work, particularly "Global Meta-Analysis Reveals No Net Change in Local-Scale Plant Biodiversity Over Time" (*Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 2013) and elaborated in *The Theory of Ecological Communities* (2016), revealed a finding that initially seems to contradict the biodiversity crisis narrative. Analyzing local plant biodiversity data from sites around the world, Vellend found that local species richness has NOT declined on average, even as global species extinctions accelerate. In some regions, local species counts have actually increased.

**The resolution—biotic homogenization**: Vellend's findings do not contradict the global extinction crisis, but they reveal something important and troubling: species are being reshuffled geographically rather than uniformly lost everywhere. Invasive species colonize new areas, often increasing local species counts while displacing native species that may be declining globally. McKinney and Lockwood (1999) termed this process "biotic homogenization"—the world's ecosystems are becoming more similar to each other as cosmopolitan species replace endemic ones. Local richness can increase even as global distinctiveness collapses. This is analogous to measuring the number of restaurants in a city and concluding that culinary diversity is thriving, while every independent restaurant is being replaced by the same five chain outlets.

Dornelas et al. (2014), in a complementary analysis published in *Science*, found that while local species richness showed no systematic decline, species composition was changing rapidly—communities were turning over their constituent species at accelerating rates. This "community turnover" represents genuine ecological disruption that species-count metrics alone cannot detect.

**Measurement gaming and perverse incentives**: Biodiversity metrics, once embedded in policy targets and funding mechanisms, can be gamed in ways that satisfy measurement requirements while undermining conservation goals. Planting multiple non-native species increases local species counts and can satisfy biodiversity targets while potentially harming native ecosystem functions. Tree plantations with five or six species of fast-growing non-native trees can score as "biodiversity improvement" compared to monoculture plantations while creating ecological deserts compared to the native grasslands or forests they replace.

The conversion of species-rich native grasslands to tree plantations has been documented as a particularly perverse outcome of biodiversity and carbon offset policies. Veldman et al. (2015) in *BioScience* warned that afforestation programs targeting grassland and savanna ecosystems—justified partly on biodiversity and carbon grounds—were destroying some of the most biodiverse and carbon-rich ecosystems on Earth by misclassifying them as "degraded" land suitable for tree planting.


## 4. Verdict

**EARC Mode: Contrast (C)**

**CONTRAST**: "Biodiversity" successfully mobilized conservation policy and accurately identifies a genuine global conservation emergency, but functions as a policy metric more effectively than as a scientific measurement tool. The term's political success and its scientific limitations are both real, and the tension between them has practical consequences for conservation outcomes.

**What's scientifically documented**: Global extinction rates are accelerating at 100–1,000 times background rates. Habitat destruction continues at unprecedented scales. Biotic homogenization is replacing distinctive regional ecosystems with globally uniform assemblages of cosmopolitan species. Many ecosystem functions are degrading. These constitute a genuine conservation crisis requiring urgent policy response, and "biodiversity" played a crucial historical role in making that crisis visible to policymakers and the public.

**What's measurement-problematic**: Aggregating species richness, genetic diversity, ecosystem diversity, functional diversity, and phylogenetic diversity into a single "biodiversity" metric that can be measured, targeted, and optimized. Using local species counts as proxies for ecosystem health when biotic homogenization can increase local counts while degrading ecological integrity. Treating biodiversity as uniformly declining everywhere when the spatial and taxonomic patterns are complex, geographically heterogeneous, and sometimes counterintuitive.

**The gaming problem**: Biodiversity targets embedded in international agreements, corporate reporting requirements, and offset markets can be satisfied through interventions (non-native plantings, species introductions, strategic area selection) that increase metric scores while degrading ecosystem function. This reveals the fundamental gap between optimizing a metric and achieving the ecological outcomes the metric was designed to represent.

**The mobilization success**: "Biodiversity" achieved Wilson's original policy goal—it created international conservation frameworks, funding mechanisms, and policy commitments that would not have existed without a unifying concept. The Convention on Biological Diversity, the Kunming-Montreal Framework, and billions in conservation funding are real policy achievements enabled by the term's rhetorical power. This success should not be dismissed even as the term's scientific limitations are acknowledged.

**Scientific alternatives**: Ecosystem integrity assessments, habitat connectivity measures, functional diversity indices, phylogenetic diversity metrics, Essential Biodiversity Variables, and species-specific population monitoring all provide more precise conservation guidance than aggregated biodiversity metrics. The challenge is translating this scientific precision into policy frameworks that retain the political traction that "biodiversity" achieved through its very imprecision.

**Bottom line**: "Biodiversity" works politically while being scientifically imprecise—it mobilizes conservation action through a simplified concept that captures real environmental problems while obscuring the ecological complexity required for effective ecosystem management. The term is a successful piece of strategic scientific communication whose policy infrastructure has now outgrown its measurement foundations. The conservation crisis is real; the question is whether the conceptual tools we've built to address it are adequate to the task, or whether their very success has created institutional momentum that resists the more nuanced approaches the science now demands.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**The Aichi failure and its lessons**: The Convention on Biological Diversity's Aichi Targets (2011–2020) largely failed. The Global Biodiversity Outlook 5 (2020) concluded that none of the 20 Aichi Targets were fully met, and only six were partially achieved. The failure was partly attributable to insufficient political will and funding, but measurement inconsistencies played a significant role. When targets are defined in terms of a concept that different parties measure differently, declaring success or failure becomes a political exercise rather than a scientific one.

**Corporate biodiversity markets**: Companies now purchase "biodiversity credits" similar to carbon offsets, and mandatory biodiversity disclosure requirements are expanding globally. The EU's Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), the Taskforce on Nature-related Financial Disclosures (TNFD), and various national regulations are creating demand for standardized biodiversity metrics that the science cannot yet reliably provide. The biodiversity credit market faces even greater measurement and verification challenges than carbon offsetting—itself a market plagued by inflated claims and weak verification. If a carbon offset's effectiveness is difficult to verify (one molecule of CO₂ is identical to another), biodiversity offsets face the additional challenge that ecosystems are not interchangeable: destroying a thousand-year-old peat bog cannot be offset by planting a tree plantation, regardless of how the species counts compare.

**The local vs. global paradox**: Conservation policy must simultaneously address local ecosystem integrity and global species preservation, but these sometimes require different and even opposing strategies. Protecting native grasslands may require removing non-native species that increase local species counts. Preserving a globally threatened species may require ecosystem management that reduces local species richness. The "biodiversity" framing obscures these tensions by implying that all conservation goals are aligned and can be captured by a single directional metric.

**Alternative frameworks emerging**: Conservation biology is increasingly moving beyond species-count biodiversity toward more sophisticated assessment approaches. Ecosystem integrity assessments evaluate the functional completeness and self-sustaining capacity of ecosystems rather than simply counting species. Habitat connectivity measures assess whether landscapes maintain the linkages that allow species to move, adapt, and maintain viable populations. The "One Health" framework integrates human, animal, and environmental health rather than treating biodiversity as a domain separate from human welfare.

The Essential Biodiversity Variables (EBV) framework, developed through GEO BON (Group on Earth Observations Biodiversity Observation Network), represents an attempt to standardize biodiversity monitoring by defining a core set of measurable variables across genetic, species, and ecosystem dimensions. This approach acknowledges the multi-dimensional nature of biodiversity rather than collapsing it into a single metric, but its implementation remains patchy and its policy integration limited.

**Economic valuation and its discontents**: Costanza et al.'s "The Value of the World's Ecosystem Services and Natural Capital" (*Nature*, 1997) estimated global ecosystem service value at $33 trillion annually (later updated to $125 trillion in Costanza et al. 2014). The TEEB (The Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity) initiative extended this approach. But monetizing biodiversity for policy purposes faces the same measurement problems as direct biodiversity metrics, compounded by the ethical and philosophical challenges of assigning dollar values to ecological systems. As Norgaard (2010) argued in *BioScience*, the ecosystem services framework risks reducing conservation to a cost-benefit calculation that inherently favors economic development over ecological preservation whenever the numbers can be made to work.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes status**: Not systematically addressed. The scientific basis for biodiversity metrics has not been fact-checked, nor have the measurement challenges and policy deployment been analyzed for coherence with underlying ecological science. This gap is unsurprising—"biodiversity" operates at a level of abstraction that resists the binary true/false framework of traditional fact-checking. The term is not a claim that can be verified or debunked but rather a conceptual framework whose relationship to the phenomena it describes requires careful epistemological analysis.

The absence of fact-checking attention to "biodiversity" as a concept reflects a broader pattern: policy terms that encode genuine scientific concerns but whose measurement and deployment introduce systematic distortions rarely receive scrutiny proportional to their influence. The infrastructure built around "biodiversity" as a metric now has its own institutional momentum, creating incentives to preserve the term's utility even when its scientific limitations are acknowledged.
