# Do Birds Have Funerals for Their Dead?


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: Widely shared social media posts in late April and early May 2021 circulated video footage showing what appeared to be a group of dark-colored birds — possibly black vultures or another species — gathered around the body of a dead bird on a road, many with wings spread in what was described as a ritual posture. The accompanying framing asked whether this constituted a "funeral." More broadly, the claim being examined is whether birds, as a class of animals, engage in behaviors around dead members of their species that meaningfully parallel the human practice of holding funerals for the dead — involving gathering, ritual display, apparent grief, and communal acknowledgment of mortality.

**The cultural framing**: The word "funeral" is doing considerable work in this claim. Human funerals are complex cultural artifacts involving: acknowledgment of death; gathering of conspecifics; often ritual or ceremonial behavior; sometimes emotional expression; and frequently a social function (reinforcing community bonds, communicating social status, distributing resources or roles previously held by the deceased). For a non-human animal behavior to constitute a "funeral," some subset of these elements must be present — but which subset, and at what level of correspondence, is immediately contested. The claim therefore has anthropological, ethological, and philosophical dimensions that the simple True/False rubric of fact-checking cannot fully resolve.

**The specific video in question**: The video shared in the Reddit post examined by Snopes showed what appeared to be dark-colored birds gathered around a dead conspecific on a road surface. Several birds had their wings extended, head down in what appeared to be a ritualized posture. The post itself asked: "Is this a funeral?" — which is simultaneously an invitation to anthropomorphic interpretation and an acknowledgment of uncertainty. The video's provenance and species identification were not definitively established in the Snopes inquiry.

**The broader category of claims**: Snopes makes clear that the claim about bird funerals is not only about the specific video but about a broader popular assertion that has circulated for years: that crows, magpies, ravens, and other corvids conduct "funerals" for their dead. This assertion draws on a genuine scientific literature — published peer-reviewed studies documenting specific behavioral responses of corvid species to dead conspecifics — and on a rich tradition of naturalist anecdote.


## 2. What's Actually True

**What's True**: Birds — particularly corvids — do exhibit behavioral responses to dead conspecifics that have been formally described as "funereal" in peer-reviewed scientific literature. These responses include gathering in large numbers (aggregations of up to 40 magpies have been observed around a single corpse), vocalizing loudly in what are characterized as "alarm calls," maintaining apparent vigil for minutes to hours, and in some accounts touching or pecking at the corpse. A minority of accounts, particularly involving domesticated or semi-captive birds with strong pair bonds, describe behaviors more consistent with individual grief: prolonged proximity to the dead partner, apparent lethargy or appetite loss, and behaviors interpreted as vigil-keeping.

**What's False**: The claim that all instances of birds gathering around a dead bird constitute a "funeral" in the emotional and ritual sense is not well-supported. Research on corvids specifically indicates that the primary adaptive function of these gatherings is information transmission and threat assessment rather than grief expression. When a crow or scrub-jay encounters a dead conspecific, the gathering serves to (a) alert other group members to the presence of the corpse and its associated danger, (b) allow the group to assess the nature of the threat that caused the death, and (c) potentially update individual behavioral strategies based on the perceived risk in that location.

**The definitive UC Davis scrub-jay study**: A landmark study by Iglesias, McElreath & Patricelli (2012, *Animal Behaviour*) — "Western scrub-jay funerals: cacophonous aggregations in response to dead conspecifics" — provided the most rigorous experimental examination of this behavior. They found that western scrub-jays (*Aphelocoma californica*) responded to dead conspecifics by gathering and producing loud alarm calls ("cacophonous aggregations") and temporarily avoided the area where the dead bird was found. Lead author Teresa Iglesias clarified to NPR that the use of "funeral" in the article's title was deliberate but limited: "only to the extent that it is an animal paying attention to another dead animal." She explicitly disclaimed any intent to imply an emotional or cognitive response.

**Crow behavior**: Similar aggregation behavior around dead crows has been documented and studied. Research by Swift & Marzluff (2015, *Animal Behaviour*) found that crows (*Corvus brachyrhynchos*) responded to dead crows by gathering, alarm calling, and subsequently showing increased vigilance behavior in the area — consistent with an interpretation that the gathering functions to assess and broadcast information about a source of danger rather than to mourn.

**Magpie vigil behavior — Marc Bekoff's account**: Evolutionary biologist and ecologist Marc Bekoff (University of Colorado, Boulder) documented in personal observations published in *Psychology Today* a sequence of magpie behavior around a dead individual that he characterized in explicitly emotional terms: the surviving birds approached the corpse, appeared to touch it gently, brought grass and placed it beside the body, and held apparent vigil before departing. Bekoff noted that this sequence was followed by the birds tipping their heads slightly before flying off. The Cornell Lab of Ornithology's All About Birds resource independently notes that Black-billed Magpies engage in "one of the most notable" behaviors in which the first bird to discover a dead magpie begins loud calling, attracting up to 40 other birds that maintain the gathering for 10–15 minutes before departing silently.

**What's Undetermined**: Whether birds experience anything that subjectively resembles grief — whether there is something it is like, from the bird's perspective, to participate in these aggregations — is genuinely undetermined. The behavioral evidence is consistent with multiple hypotheses: threat-assessment learning, social cohesion reinforcement, grief, and simple conspecific recognition. None of these hypotheses is definitively excluded by available evidence, and definitively testing subjective experience in non-human animals faces irresolvable methodological challenges.

**Evidence from pair-bonded individuals**: Several naturalistic accounts of apparent grief in pair-bonded birds go beyond the corvid aggregation phenomenon. The account described by Audubon Society reporting involved two domestic mulard ducks at a New York sanctuary — birds with a documented strong pair bond — in which the surviving duck placed his head and neck on his dead partner's body repeatedly over an extended period before himself dying two months later. Osprey parents filmed experiencing the loss of hatchlings showed prolonged, apparently distressed attendance at the nest. These accounts involve individual attachment loss rather than species-level aggregation behavior, and the emotional interpretation is correspondingly less contested — though still ultimately inference from behavior.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What This Case Reveals Beyond the Verdict

The Mixture verdict for this claim is epistemically richer than it might appear. The fact that the answer is "it depends on what you mean by funeral" is not a cop-out but a genuine philosophical finding that connects empirical animal behavior science to deep questions about mind, emotion, and the categories through which we interpret non-human animal life.

**The definitional problem is not trivially resolvable**: Consider what is minimally necessary for the word "funeral" to apply. Option A (minimal): any distinctive behavioral response by conspecifics in the presence of a dead individual. Under this definition, many bird species — and many non-bird species — hold "funerals." Option B (intermediate): a collective gathering behavior that involves social signals specific to the death context, as opposed to general predator-alarm calls. Under this definition, corvids clearly qualify, and a small number of other bird species may. Option C (strong): grief-informed ritual in which the participants have some understanding that the dead individual is dead and will not return, and in which the behavioral response is driven partly by emotional states analogous to human grief. Under this definition, the evidence is weak and the philosophical obstacles to verification are severe. The fact-check properly identifies this definitional spectrum without resolving it, which is the most honest possible response.

**The scientific concept of animal thanatology**: The academic field of comparative thanatology — the study of how non-human animals respond to death — has expanded substantially in the early twenty-first century. A comprehensive review by Anderson (2016, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*) identified responses to conspecific death across a broad range of taxa, including cetaceans, primates, elephants, corvids, and other birds. The review established a tentative hierarchy of response complexity: from simple avoidance of corpses (very widespread) through extended examination and attendance (documented in great apes, corvids, elephants, and cetaceans) to behaviors interpretable as grief and mourning (most strongly documented in primates and elephants). Birds — particularly corvids — sit at the intermediate level of this hierarchy.

**The challenge of the "emotional" criterion**: Even if we accept that corvid aggregation behavior involves something more than rote predator-alarm responses — even if we grant that there is something about the specifically social-failure dimension of a conspecific's death that triggers these responses — the question of whether there is an emotional substrate remains open. Behavioral indicators of emotion in non-mammalian species are particularly difficult to interpret because the phylogenetic distance from mammals introduces uncertainty about whether homologous neural structures (e.g., limbic system analogs) are present and functioning. Avian cognition research has advanced the understanding of bird emotional processing substantially — demonstrating, for example, that ravens possess pessimistic cognitive biases as indicators of negative affective states (Scheid & Bugnyar, 2008, *Animal Cognition*) — but extrapolating from such findings to the specific question of grief when observing a dead conspecific is several steps beyond what the evidence directly supports.

**Anthropomorphism as a scientific risk and a scientific resource**: The history of comparative psychology contains examples of both errors: premature attribution of complex human-like mental states to non-human animals (anthropomorphism), and reflexive denial of any analogue of human experience in non-human animals on philosophical or ideological grounds (anthropodenial, a term introduced by Frans de Waal). The birds-and-funerals question sits in the contested territory between these errors. Bekoff's description of magpie vigil behavior, with its explicitly emotional framing, risks anthropomorphism. The hyper-functionalist reading that reduces corvid aggregation behavior to pure adaptive threat-assessment, with no possible emotional component, risks anthropodenial. The most epistemically defensible position acknowledges genuine behavioral evidence of death-recognition and potentially emotional responses while maintaining appropriate uncertainty about the nature of the inner experience involved.

**Implications for the "funerals" word choice**: The choice to use the word "funeral" in both scientific papers (Iglesias et al., 2012) and popular media to describe corvid death-response behavior is itself a form of anthropomorphic framing with scientific and communicative consequences. When a researcher uses "funeral" in a title, knowing it will be interpreted by a lay audience in fully human terms, they are making a communication choice that will shape public understanding of animal cognition in ways that may or may not be warranted. Iglesias's clarification to NPR — "only to the extent that it is an animal paying attention to another dead animal" — is a corrective footnote that most readers of the headline will not encounter.


## 4. Evidence Assessment and Source Quality

**The Iglesias et al. (2012) study**: Published in *Animal Behaviour*, a peer-reviewed journal, using controlled experimental presentation of dead conspecifics and live conspecifics, with behavioral ethological analysis. This is the most methodologically rigorous study cited in the Snopes fact-check, and its findings form the empirical core of the "What's True" component: scrub-jays do respond to dead conspecifics with distinctive aggregation and alarm behavior.

**Swift & Marzluff (2015)**: Published in *Animal Behaviour*, examining crow responses to dead crows using controlled experimental stimuli. Methods included presenting taxidermied dead crows and taxidermied live-postured crows and comparing behavioral responses. Findings corroborated the aggregation and alarm-calling response and added data on subsequent avoidance behavior in the experimental area.

**Bekoff's observations**: These are naturalist observations published in a popular science venue (*Psychology Today*), not peer-reviewed experimental data. They carry the weight of expert observation — Bekoff is a credentialed evolutionary biologist — but not the experimental controls of the Iglesias or Swift studies. Their inclusion is appropriate as anecdotal evidence but should not bear the full weight of establishing the emotional-grief claim.

**Cornell Lab All About Birds**: This is a credible, expert-reviewed secondary source for species behavioral descriptions, drawing on the Cornell Lab's ornithological database. The magpie "funeral" description cited there is consistent with peer-reviewed literature.

**Marc Bekoff's broader scholarship**: His summarizing statement — "we need more data about how different animals grieve and mourn the loss of friends and family, but there is overwhelming evidence that individuals of many different species do" — reflects a position shared by many comparative psychologists but remains contested in how it handles the emotion vs. behavior distinction.

**Gap assigned**: **C (Conceptual Gap)** — The primary gap here is not empirical but definitional and philosophical: there is no scientific consensus on what minimum behavioral criteria would be necessary and sufficient to establish that a non-human animal "grieves" rather than merely exhibiting adaptive death-response behavior. Without a consensual operationalization, the question of whether birds hold funerals cannot be resolved by additional data alone. It requires a philosophical settlement about what "funeral" means in cross-species application.


## 5. Broader Context — Animal Thanatology, Corvid Intelligence, and the Boundaries of Mourning

The birds-and-funerals question is not an isolated curiosity. It is part of a broader scientific project to understand the cognitive and emotional complexity of non-human animals, and the degree to which states and behaviors we consider distinctively human — mourning, grief, ritual — have evolutionary precursors in other species.

Corvids — ravens, crows, magpies, and jays — are established as among the most cognitively sophisticated non-human animals on earth, with documented capacities for perspective-taking, future planning, causal reasoning, and deceptive behavior. Studies by Bugnyar, Reber & Buckner (2016, *Nature Communications*) demonstrated that ravens attribute visual perspectives to conspecifics, indicating a form of theory of mind. Clayton and colleagues at Cambridge have documented episodic-like memory in western scrub-jays (Clayton & Dickinson, 1998, *Nature*). These cognitive capacities make it entirely plausible — though not demonstrated — that corvids responding to dead conspecifics are doing something richer than executing a fixed predator-alarm subroutine.

The elephant literature provides an instructive comparison. Elephants have been documented performing behaviors around dead conspecifics that are among the most compelling non-human death-responses observed: returning repeatedly to carcasses over days and weeks, manipulating and covering bodies, apparently grieving infants and close social associates over extended periods (McComb, Baker & Moss, 2006, *Biology Letters*). The emotional and cognitive complexity attributed to elephant death responses is substantially less contested than the analogous attributions for birds, in part because elephants' mammalian neural architecture is more directly analogous to human neural architecture and in part because the behavioral evidence is more robust.

For birds, the comparative framework is complicated by the fact that avian neural architecture, while producing sophisticated cognition, does so through a substantially different structural organization than mammalian cortex. The "avian pallium" performs functions similar to mammalian cortex but through different cellular organization (Jarvis et al., 2005, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*). This means that efforts to infer emotional states in birds from behavioral observations must proceed without the direct neural homology that partially justifies analogical inference in mammalian thanatology.

The claim that birds hold funerals is ultimately a question that lies at the boundary of science and philosophy. The behavioral evidence is real. The adaptive function of at least some of the relevant behaviors can be inferred. Whether there is additional emotional or experiential content to these behaviors — whether the gathering corvid is doing something that in any meaningful sense resembles what a grieving human does — cannot be resolved by observation alone. It requires a theory of consciousness that extends to avian neural architectures, and such a theory does not exist.


## 6. Verdict Summary and Takeaways

**Snopes Verdict: Mixture**

Birds — particularly corvids — exhibit behavioral responses to dead conspecifics that share structural features with human funeral behavior, including gathering, vocalizing, apparent vigil, and in some cases physical interaction with the corpse. The primary documented adaptive function of these behaviors is threat assessment and information transmission rather than grief expression, though individual anecdotes — particularly from pair-bonded birds — suggest genuine emotional attachment loss in some cases. Whether these behaviors constitute "funerals" depends on definitional framing: under minimal definitions, yes; under stronger grief-informed ritual definitions, evidence is insufficient.

**Key takeaways**:

1. **The behavioral evidence is real**: Peer-reviewed studies confirm corvid aggregation behavior around dead conspecifics. This is not myth or anthropomorphic projection onto normal flock behavior — it is documented, experimentally verified behavioral response.

2. **The function is primarily adaptive, not primarily emotional**: The best-supported scientific interpretation of corvid "funeral" behavior is that it serves information transmission about danger sources. Emotional content, if present, is secondary and not independently verified.

3. **Species and individual variation matter**: Not all birds respond to death in the same way. Corvids show the most robust and well-studied responses. Pair-bonded birds of many species may show individual grief responses qualitatively different from corvid group aggregations.

4. **Definitional precision matters**: The word "funeral" imports human cultural meaning that obscures rather than illuminates the animal behavior under discussion. More precise ethological language — "death-response aggregation," "alarm aggregation to dead conspecific" — would better represent what is actually observed.

5. **Conceptual gap**: The fundamental gap is philosophical — the absence of a scientific operationalization of animal grief that would distinguish "adaptive behavior around death" from "grief-informed death response." This gap cannot be closed by more data alone; it requires conceptual settlement about the nature of emotion and experience in non-mammalian cognizers.
