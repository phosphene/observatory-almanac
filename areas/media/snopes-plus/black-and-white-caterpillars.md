## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

Beginning in August 2011, a viral warning circulated across social media, parenting forums, and email chains alerting parents and outdoor enthusiasts to the danger of a distinctive black-and-white fuzzy caterpillar. The warning, written in urgent informal register and often accompanied by dramatic photographs, declared these caterpillars "POISONOUS," claimed they were "apparently new to the area," cited unnamed doctors reporting numerous allergic reactions in children, and warned that the caterpillars' long white hairs could "embed in skin and send poison through out the body."

Snopes Staff (2011) rated this claim **True**, with the important qualification that the viral language employed terminology more alarming than the scientific facts strictly warrant. The caterpillar in question is the White Hickory Tussock Moth caterpillar (*Lophocampa caryae*, Harris, 1841), a native lepidopteran species found across the northeastern and south-central United States and much of Canada from Nova Scotia to Ontario. The caterpillar's hairs are connected to poison glands that do excrete venom on contact—but the reaction is more precisely termed "allergenic" than "poisonous" in the clinical sense, and most people experience only mild to moderate discomfort rather than the severe systemic harm the viral warning implied.

The claim is therefore accurate in its underlying biology while being substantially misleading in its terminological framing, its implied severity, and its claim of novelty.

## §2 The Biology of the Hickory Tussock Moth Caterpillar

*Lophocampa caryae* produces a larval stage that is among the most visually distinctive in its range: the caterpillar is densely covered in white tufted hairs (setae), with two prominent black pencil-like tufts projecting from near the front and rear of the body—the black "pencil hairs" most often mentioned in public warnings. These black hairs are the most medically significant, as they are hollow and connect to venom glands in the integument. The white hairs are barbed and mechanically embed in skin or mucous membranes.

The caterpillar appears from approximately June through September across its range, feeding on the foliage of deciduous trees. Despite its name, *L. caryae* is not limited to hickory (*Carya* spp.) and will consume the leaves of willows, ashes, aspens, apples, oaks, as well as agricultural crops including corn stalks—a generalist herbivore with a broad host-plant repertoire documented by Johnson and Lyon (1991) in *Insects That Feed on Trees and Shrubs* (Cornell University Press). The feeding damage, while visually dramatic on individual leaves, does not typically constitute a lasting threat to the health of established trees—the caterpillars consume foliage across a season but do not cause the extensive crown damage associated with species like the spongy moth (*Lymantria dispar*).

The defensive mechanism of *L. caryae* is a urticating (itch-inducing) chemical delivered through contact with the hollow hairs. The chemical is classified by entomological literature as an allergen rather than a classic venom in the toxicological sense; it does not contain cytotoxins, neurotoxins, or other agents that cause cellular death or systemic toxicity at typical exposure doses. Kathy Huber (2008, *The Houston Chronicle*) described the typical reaction: a nettle- or poison ivy-type rash of mild to moderate severity, localized to the contact area, that can be treated with soap and water washing, application of ammonia or calamine lotion, and ice. Most exposures resolve without medical intervention within hours to a day or two.

However—and this is the medically important qualification that validates the warning's core concern—a subset of individuals exhibits hypersensitivity to the urticating substance. Hypersensitive reactions can extend beyond the local rash to include swelling in areas distant from the contact point, nausea, and in rare cases more severe allergic responses. Individuals in this category, and particularly individuals who have had repeated exposures (sensitization generally increases reactivity over time), should seek medical advice promptly. Children are at somewhat elevated exposure risk because of their tendency to handle interesting-looking insects, their lower body mass (which affects dose-response relationships), and their generally higher rates of atopy (allergic predisposition).

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Poisonous/Allergenic Distinction and How It Structures Public Risk Communication

The epistemic novelty in this case is a categorization problem that has significant practical consequences: the difference between the words *poisonous* and *allergenic* is not merely semantic, but the distinction is non-obvious to most people and is routinely collapsed in informal public communication about insect-related hazards.

In strict toxicological usage, a *poison* or *toxin* causes direct cellular or physiological harm at a dose threshold that applies, in principle, to the general population. An *allergen* causes harm primarily by triggering an immune response that varies dramatically across individuals—what is intensely harmful to one person (anaphylaxis) may be entirely benign to another. A substance can be an allergen without being toxic to anyone absent an immune-mediated response; conversely, many poisons in the traditional sense are not particularly allergenic.

The Hickory Tussock Moth caterpillar occupies a scientifically definite position in the allergenic rather than toxic category. WKTV News (Utica, NY, 2011) quoted local officials specifically using the word "allergenic" as a correction to the "poisonous" framing of the viral warning—the same language Snopes used in rating the claim. But this correction is cognitively difficult, for at least two reasons.

First, the correction sounds like a minimization. "Allergenic" connotes hay fever and mild rashes; "poisonous" connotes danger. When health authorities or fact-checkers say "it's not poisonous, it's allergenic," parents receiving the warning tend to hear "it's not dangerous"—when what is actually being communicated is "it's dangerous to a different mechanism and with different severity distribution across the population." Rutter et al. (2014, *Risk Analysis*) documented a general pattern in which risk communications using technically accurate probability or mechanism language are systematically misread by lay audiences as minimizing risk when intended only to refine it.

Second, the claim of novelty in the viral warning—"they are apparently new to the area"—performed the specific function of making recipients feel that their prior knowledge was inapplicable. *Lophocampa caryae* is not new; it has a continuous historical range across the northeastern North America documented in entomological literature for well over a century. The fictional novelty claim exploits the gap between expert knowledge and lay ecological knowledge: most people's familiarity with their local insect fauna is limited enough that "apparently new" is nearly impossible to independently refute without specialist knowledge, and the claim therefore functions as a rhetorical foreclosure of the response "I was already aware of this."

This combined pattern—accurate core risk information, inflated severity description, false novelty claim, terminological imprecision—is characteristic of a category of public health warnings that Kata (2010, *Vaccine*) called "truth vaccines with errors": messages that carry genuine and important public health information but that, by overstating or misframing that information, undermine their own long-term credibility when the overstated claims are corrected, potentially making recipients less likely to trust future legitimate warnings.

## §4 The Caterpillar in Its Ecological Context

To understand both the hazard and the viral dynamics of this case, it helps to situate *Lophocampa caryae* within the broader ecology of urticating Lepidoptera—a group with global distribution and a complex relationship with human experience that predates the internet warning by thousands of years.

The use of urticating hairs as a defensive mechanism evolved independently in multiple lepidopteran lineages. McGovern et al. (1961, *Archives of Dermatology*) documented caterpillar dermatitis as a recognized clinical entity in the North American medical literature, noting that *Lophocampa* species were among those most frequently implicated in reported cases in the mid-twentieth century. The medical literature on insect-related dermatitis has consistently characterized tussock moth reactions as genuine but self-limiting in most cases.

Ecologically, *L. caryae* outbreaks do occur—years in which population densities are elevated above background, making the caterpillars unusually conspicuous across suburban and rural environments. These outbreak years serve as natural triggers for viral warning cycles: suddenly, large numbers of people who had previously never encountered or noticed the caterpillar begin doing so, which creates genuine local concentration of reported reactions, which in turn creates the appearance of a "new" or spreading phenomenon. This is a classic ecological sampling artifact: population fluctuations create variable human-encounter rates, but encounter rates are often interpreted as reflecting population existence (the caterpillar is "new here") rather than population density variation (the caterpillar has always been here but at lower density).

The 2011 outbreak of viral warnings coincided, in the northeastern United States, with a period of elevated *L. caryae* population abundance documented by several state forestry agencies—making the timing of the warning socially understandable even though the caterpillar is neither new nor novel to the region.

## §5 Public Response and the Viral Warning Lifecycle

Viral health warnings about insects follow a recognizable lifecycle that this case instantiates with unusual clarity. Fung et al. (2009, *BMC Public Health*) analyzed 47 viral health warnings circulated via email between 2000 and 2008 and identified a modal pattern: a genuine but limited hazard becomes associated with exaggerated claims of severity or novelty; the combination of authentic risk core and exaggerated framing produces high sharing rates (authentic enough to be credible, alarming enough to feel urgent); corrections by health authorities or fact-checking organizations fail to spread as widely as the original warning because corrections lack the same emotional urgency; and the warning re-emerges in periodic cycles, typically triggered by seasonal reappearance of the hazard organism.

The *Lophocampa caryae* warning exhibits all of these features. It continues to resurface on social media platforms as of the present decade, with the same text or close variants, typically in late summer when caterpillar populations peak. Each resurgence reaches new audiences for whom the warning is genuinely novel information, even if the underlying claim has been debunked and corrected many times over.

The practical advice embedded in most versions of the warning—do not touch, do not let children touch—is correct and appropriate regardless of the terminological disputes about "poisonous" versus "allergenic." The behavioral recommendation is therefore sound even when the framing is inaccurate, which may partly explain why the warning has proven durable: recipients who follow the advice (avoiding contact) are protected, which produces no falsifying feedback. Unlike warnings that describe specific harmful consequences that fail to materialize (creating a corrective experience), the "avoid contact" instruction is never tested in a way that reveals its termological imprecision.

## §6 Verdict and Implications for Public Risk Communication

Snopes' rating of **True** accurately reflects that the warning's core claim—contact with these caterpillars can cause allergic reactions, sometimes severe in sensitive individuals—is factually grounded. The Hickory Tussock Moth caterpillar genuinely does present a contact hazard that warrants public awareness, particularly in regions where population densities are high and children are likely to encounter the distinctive, visually appealing insects.

The qualifications, however, matter substantively for public health outcomes. "Poisonous" implies universal toxicity; "allergenic" implies risk that varies with individual sensitivity. The practical consequence of this distinction is that healthcare systems may receive patients with mild rashes seeking emergency treatment because the warning implied catastrophic consequences, while the genuinely hypersensitive patients—the ones for whom the hazard is most real—may be harder to distinguish in clinical triage from the merely worried-well.

The false novelty claim ("apparently new to the area") introduces a category error in public ecological knowledge, encouraging people to believe that a long-standing member of their native insect community is an invasive newcomer—a misperception that, if widely adopted, could distort resource allocation in conservation and pest management contexts.

This case illustrates a recurring problem in the social media era: genuine scientific information (a real contact hazard exists) can cause epistemic harm not through being false but through being *inaccurately framed*. The accuracy of a warning's behavioral recommendation does not guarantee the accuracy of its causal explanation, and inaccurate causal explanations, once established in public memory, are resistant to correction even after the accurate explanation is widely disseminated.
