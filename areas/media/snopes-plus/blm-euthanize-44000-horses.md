## §1 · The Advisory Board Vote and Its Misrepresentation

On 9 September 2016, the BLM's National Wild Horse and Burro Advisory Board convened in Nevada and voted by a margin of approximately 7–1 to recommend that the Bureau of Land Management euthanize the entire population of unadopted wild horses and burros then held in its short-term and long-term holding facilities — a population estimated at approximately 44,000 to 46,000 animals. The sole dissenting vote came from Ginger Kathrens of the Cloud Foundation, an advocacy organisation focused on humane treatment of wild horses. The advisory board's function is precisely what its name indicates: it advises. Its vote produced a recommendation forwarded to the BLM for consideration, not an enforceable decision or operational directive.

The social media and press coverage that followed the vote systematically flattened this institutional distinction. Facebook posts and republished versions of the initial news reports stated that "the Bureau of Land Management has voted to euthanize 44,000 wild horses." The error is not trivial. The distinction between an advisory board recommendation and an agency decision is the entire difference between a vote that initiates no action and a vote that commits a federal agency to an operational programme. The advisory board cannot instruct BLM staff to do anything; it can only signal to BLM leadership what a mixed committee of agency and non-agency representatives believes the bureau *should* do. Kathrens herself confirmed this institutional structure to Snopes.

The misattribution was compounded by a secondary mischaracterisation that appeared in some versions: the claim that the proposed euthanasia was intended to "make room for beef cattle." This framing, while politically resonant in the context of public land grazing rights disputes, does not accurately characterise the advisory board's stated rationale. The driving concern articulated by the board, by Humane Society Senior Vice President Holly Hazard, and by the National Academy of Sciences (NAS) 2013 report was financial and ecological sustainability — the holding facilities were budget-exhausted and ecologically straining free-range land — not a deliberate prioritisation of commercial cattle interests over horse welfare.

## §2 · The BLM's Actual Response

Three days after the advisory board vote, on 12 September 2016, the BLM issued a formal statement declining to accept the board's euthanasia recommendation and committing to explore alternative management options. This rapid rejection was itself newsworthy: it demonstrated that the advisory process had generated significant public pressure that the agency chose to respond to within a very short window, and that the recommendation had been received as a signal of crisis rather than as a directive requiring implementation. The agency's statement was accompanied by an acknowledgment that "the Wild Horse and Burro Program" faced serious financial and ecological sustainability challenges that required resolution — an implicit affirmation that the underlying problem the advisory board was responding to was real.

The rapid news cycle around the event — advisory board votes euthanasia (9 Sept), social media outrage spreads (9–11 Sept), BLM rejects recommendation (12 Sept) — means that a significant proportion of audiences encountered the claim at a point when the BLM had already rejected it. The Snopes classification of **Outdated** reflects this temporal structure: the claim was never precisely accurate (it misstated the actor and status), but within days even the closest approximation to accuracy — the advisory board's recommendation existing as a live institutional proposal — was negated by the BLM's response.

## §3 · Epistemic Novelty: The Outdated Classification and the Temporal Dimension of Misinformation

The Snopes rating of **Outdated** is itself analytically interesting and represents a calibrated verdict category that deserves examination. It sits between True and False on the epistemic spectrum in a specific temporal dimension rather than in the uncertainty dimension occupied by Unproven. The claim captured a real institutional event — a recommendation existed and was reported — but the informational context that gave the claim its alarming content (an imminent mass government-sanctioned killing of wild horses) was overtaken by events within seventy-two hours.

The relationship between misinformation and temporal obsolescence has been underanalysed in the fact-checking literature relative to attention devoted to spatially or evidentially false claims. Tambuscio et al. (2015, *EPJ Data Science*, 4(1), 1–13) modelled the propagation of rumours through social networks and found that correction velocity — how quickly accurate updates spread after an initial false claim — is systematically slower than initial claim propagation velocity, largely because the emotional salience that drives initial sharing does not attach to correction messages with equal force. In this case, the correction (BLM rejects recommendation) involved an institutional non-event — an agency declining to do something — which generates inherently lower narrative energy than the original claim's dramatic content (44,000 horses to be slaughtered).

The consequence is a persistent residue: social media posts asserting that "the BLM voted to kill 44,000 horses" continued circulating long after the BLM's rejection, in some cases years later, reappearing in cycles of animal welfare advocacy concern without the temporal context that would correct their implication of ongoing threat. This is a specific misinformation failure mode: accurate-but-outdated claims reprinted without timestamping or event resolution context. The claim's emotional charge is supplied by the event's original horrifying premise, not by the event's actual resolution, and that charge does not decay in the way that a merely false claim does once corrected.

The Outdated classification, applied rigorously, should function as an instruction to audiences: this claim refers to an event that occurred, was subsequently resolved, and is no longer accurately described by the claim as stated. Whether that function is achieved depends on whether audiences access the classification in context or encounter the bare claim in de-contextualised social media distribution.

## §4 · The Wild Horse Population Crisis: Structural Context

The advisory board's controversial recommendation is incomprehensible without understanding the structural crisis in wild horse population management that produced it. The Wild Free-Roaming Horses and Burros Act of 1971 (16 U.S.C. §§ 1331–1340) established federal protection for wild horses and burros on public lands as "living symbols of the historic and pioneer spirit of the West" and prohibited private capture, branding, harassment, or killing. The Act mandated BLM management in a way that aimed to maintain healthy herds at sustainable population levels in designated Herd Management Areas (HMAs). The legal framework is explicitly anti-slaughter, committing the federal government to the preservation and welfare of wild equid populations as a cultural and ecological resource.

The ecological arithmetic, however, is deeply problematic. Eastern grey horse populations in Western US rangelands, like all ungulate populations in the absence of effective predators, grow at approximately 15–20% per annum under favourable forage conditions — a growth rate confirmed by the National Academy of Sciences review. The specific figure comes from the NAS 2013 report (*Using Science to Improve the BLM Wild Horse and Burro Program*), which found that existing monitoring methods were insufficiently rigorous and that actual growth rates were poorly constrained but likely within this range. At 18% annual growth, a herd of 100 horses becomes approximately 520 horses in ten years without culling or fertility management. The total on-range wild horse population in 2016 was estimated by BLM at approximately 67,000 animals across 26.9 million acres of HMA territory, against an Agency-defined Appropriate Management Level (AML) of approximately 26,700 animals — meaning the free-range population was already approximately 2.5 times the level the BLM considered ecologically sustainable.

The capture and holding programme that accumulated the 44,000–46,000 animals in facilities was itself the consequence of a management strategy that prioritised removal over ecological management: the BLM conducted annual roundups ("gathers") intended to reduce on-range populations to AML, transferred removed horses to holding facilities, and attempted to place them for private adoption. Adoption demand has chronically underperformed removal rates. The holding programme costs, funded by congressional appropriations, escalated from approximately $50 million annually in 2012 to over $80 million in 2016, consuming the majority of the entire Wild Horse and Burro Programme budget. The 2013 NAS report specifically called out the budgetary unsustainability: "the current management approach is not sustainable" and "promising fertility-control methods are available to help limit population growth."

## §5 · Fertility Control, Porcine Zona Pellucida Vaccine, and the Ignored Alternative

One of the more significant failures documented in the advisory board controversy is the BLM's historically inadequate implementation of fertility control as an alternative to capture-and-hold. The immunocontraceptive Porcine Zona Pellucida (PZP) vaccine has been available and tested since the 1990s for use in wild horse populations and has been demonstrated in multiple field studies to effectively reduce birth rates when administered consistently to mares. Ransom, Hobbs, and Lipeles (2010, *Journal of Wildlife Management*, 74(8), 1827–1836) documented significant birth rate reductions in treated Assateague Island herd populations using PZP, with a two-injection treatment protocol achieving effective suppression over a two-to-three-year period.

Kathrens cited the BLM's failure to deploy fertility control at meaningful scale as the primary cause of the crisis that made the euthanasia recommendation politically intelligible to advisory board members who nonetheless found it morally repugnant. The NAS 2013 report reinforced this finding, calling PZP and related fertility management tools "promising" and urging their deployment as a primary strategy rather than as an adjunct to removal. The HSUS, in its response to the advisory board vote, similarly identified "alternatives to this proposal [that] have been ignored for over 20 years."

The institutional failure to implement PZP at scale is partly logistical — administering injections to free-roaming horses in remote terrain requires significant field capacity and repeated treatment over multiple years — and partly political. Opposition to fertility control in wild horse populations has come from both animal welfare advocates (who object to any level of population intervention) and from ranching interests (who prefer removal to on-range population management, as removals reduce the density of horses competing with cattle for forage). This cross-ideological opposition created a political environment in which the scientifically recommended management approach was chronically underfunded.

## §6 · Verdict and Implications

The **Outdated** verdict is the most precisely calibrated available for this case, capturing both the reality of the advisory board event and the irrelevance of the claim as typically repeated. The BLM — the actor named in the claim — did not vote on the matter and rejected the recommendation it was forwarded. The claim's alarm-generating content (imminent government-sanctioned mass horse death) was resolved against within three days by the agency's own statement.

The structural policy crisis that made the advisory board's recommendation intelligible, however, was not resolved and was in no sense outdated. The wild horse population on Western rangelands continued to grow at approximately 15–20% annually; the holding programme continued to expand and consume an increasing share of BLM budgets; congressional appropriations continued to prohibit euthanasia while failing to fund fertility control at the scale recommended by the NAS; and periodic proposals to permit commercial slaughter or further euthanasia continued to generate advocacy controversy in subsequent years.

The implication for information literacy is a lesson in institutional precision. Media literacy frameworks should equip audiences to distinguish between the actions of an agency and those of its advisory bodies, between recommendations and decisions, and between a proposal in circulation and a resolved policy event. The BLM advisory board vote produced significant outrage and drove a rapid institutional response — demonstrating that democratic accountability mechanisms worked as intended in this case — and the resulting policy remained non-euthanasia. That partial success should be held alongside the recognition that the underlying biological and budgetary crisis warranting some form of more active solution, most responsibly a substantially scaled fertility control programme, remained unaddressed. Fact-checkers and advocacy communicators both serve their audiences better by distinguishing between "the alarm was misattributed and premature" — which is true — and "the management ecosystem is functioning well" — which, by the NAS's own assessment, was not.
