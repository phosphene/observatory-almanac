# A simple blood test can detect all types of cancer

*Series: The Truth Vault — examining the gap between what circulates online and what the science actually says.*

---

## 1. The Claim

By the autumn of 2021, a striking claim had begun moving quickly across health-focused social media accounts, wellness newsletters, and patient advocacy spaces: a simple blood test existed — or was being quietly rolled out — that could detect any form of cancer, at any stage, with a single blood draw. Variants of the claim placed differing emphasis on the test's current availability (already here vs. coming soon), its comprehensiveness (all cancers vs. most cancers), and the reasons the public might not know about it (suppression vs. regulatory delay vs. media silence).

The claim was grounded in a legitimate and widely covered news event: in September 2021, England's National Health Service announced the world's largest clinical trial of the Galleri blood test, developed by the biotech company Grail, which had shown an ability to detect signals from more than 50 types of cancer before symptoms appear. The announcement generated genuine excitement in oncology and public health communities, but as the news was digested and shared, the caveats got steadily stripped away.

The simplified version that circulated — "there is now a blood test that can find any cancer" — is both more exciting and significantly less accurate than what was actually announced.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

The underlying facts are genuinely significant. The Galleri blood test, developed by the company Grail (which was originally spun out of the gene-sequencing firm Illumina), is a multi-cancer early detection (MCED) test. It works by analysing cell-free DNA (cfDNA) — fragments of genetic material that circulate in the bloodstream after being shed by cells, including tumour cells. By detecting specific chemical changes in these cfDNA fragments, and by using machine learning trained on large datasets of cancer patients and healthy controls, the test attempts to identify signals associated with cancer before a person develops symptoms.

In its published validation studies, Galleri has shown the ability to detect signals associated with more than 50 types of cancer. That figure, however, requires careful interpretation. The 50+ cancers span a wide range of types, some of which are extremely rare. Detection sensitivity — the probability that the test will correctly identify a positive case — varies substantially across cancer types. For some cancers, particularly those that are aggressive and often diagnosed at a late stage (pancreatic, head and neck, bowel, lung, oesophageal), early detection rates in validation studies were encouraging. For other types, sensitivity was lower, particularly at Stage I disease when tumour DNA shed into the bloodstream is minimal.

The UK trial announced in September 2021 — called the Galleri trial, run by the NHS in England in partnership with Cancer Research UK and King's College London — aimed to recruit 140,000 volunteers between the ages of 50 and 77. The randomised controlled design was intended to compare outcomes between those whose blood was screened immediately and those whose sample was stored for later testing. If the trial demonstrates that the test can identify cancers at an earlier, more treatable stage across the enrolled population, that would constitute robust clinical validation for use in routine screening.

Critically, as of the announcement date, Galleri had not been approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration. It had received a "breakthrough device designation" from the FDA in 2019 — a classification that expedites review for devices addressing serious conditions — but that designation is not the same as approval. In the United States, the test was available on a prescription-only basis at a cost of $949 per test, not covered by insurance. This is a commercially available but not officially approved tool in a regulatory category distinct from a standard diagnostic.

The test does not diagnose cancer. What it does, when it returns a positive result, is indicate the presence of a cancer signal in the blood and predict the likely anatomical origin of that signal. A positive Galleri result prompts further clinical investigation — scans, biopsies, specialist review — which may or may not confirm a cancer diagnosis. The test is designed to complement, not replace, existing screening methods such as mammography, colonoscopy, or PSA testing.

Early cancer detection offers demonstrated clinical benefits. Cancers identified at Stage I or II are typically smaller and less likely to have spread, which generally means more treatment options, less aggressive interventions, and substantially higher survival rates. For some cancers, particularly those like pancreatic cancer with almost no current early-detection tools, even a modestly sensitive blood-based signal could have dramatic effects at population scale. This is why the trial generated genuine optimism in oncology circles.

The qualified summary is: a real multi-cancer blood test exists, entered major trials in 2021, and shows real promise — but it is not available to everyone, it does not detect all cancers with uniform reliability, it is not a diagnostic (a diagnosis still requires follow-up investigation), and it was not being suppressed or hidden. The NHS made a formal announcement and held a media briefing.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The appeal of a simple, comprehensive cancer blood test is not hard to understand. Cancer screening as currently constituted is fragmented, invasive, and stressful. Colonoscopy preparation is deeply unpleasant. Mammography requires a dedicated appointment, a machine, and trained radiologists. Cervical smear tests require a clinician. Not all cancers have dedicated screening programmes at all — pancreatic cancer, one of the most lethal, has no standard screening test available to the general public.

Against this backdrop, a single blood draw that could check for more than 50 cancers at once sounds not just convenient but transformational. It sounds like something that would have obvious appeal to patients, insurers, public health systems, and governments. And that is exactly what it is — which is why the news attracted broad coverage in 2021 and genuine investment in the NHS trial.

What happens between legitimate news and viral claim is a process of simplification driven partly by the incentive structures of content sharing. A headline reading "NHS launches world's largest trial of promising multi-cancer blood test" is accurate but hedged. A post reading "There's a blood test that finds any cancer — here's why your doctor hasn't told you yet" is inaccurate but shareable. The inaccurate version introduces a conflict frame (why hasn't your doctor mentioned this?) that is more engaging and creates a sense of access to privileged information.

The emotional resonance of cancer is compounding. People who have watched family members die from cancers that were detected too late — from a missed pancreatic tumour, from a late-stage ovarian diagnosis with no current reliable early screening tool — have legitimate reason to be interested in any development that might have changed that outcome. That emotional urgency can compress the cognitive distance between "clinical trial underway" and "ready for deployment."

There is also a structural element specific to the US context: the test was technically available via prescription in America for $949, not covered by insurance, several years before any definitive proof of population-level benefit. This asymmetry — available if you can pay, not available to ordinary patients — fits neatly into established narratives about healthcare access and pharmaceutical pricing. Even where the explanation for that asymmetry is simply "regulatory process and insurance coverage awaiting clinical trial evidence," it can look from the outside like selective access or suppression.

---

## 4. Verdict

**The blood test is real. The claim that it detects "all" cancers or is being suppressed is false.**

The Galleri multi-cancer early detection blood test entered the world's largest clinical trial for this type of screening in September 2021. The claim attached to the news — that a simple blood test can now find any cancer — is a significant distortion. The test detects signals for more than 50 cancer types with varying sensitivity, does not detect all cancers reliably (particularly at the earliest stages), is not a diagnostic, and requires clinical follow-up for positive results. It was not in routine use in public healthcare systems at the time of the announcement; it was entering large-scale trial to determine whether routine use would be beneficial.

The suppression element of the claim is straightforwardly incorrect. The NHS made the trial announcement at a press conference covered by all major British news outlets. The CEO of NHS England described it as a potential "game-changer" and a potential "revolution in cancer detection." Cancer Research UK listed it on its website as an active trial. King's College London profiled its involvement. The information was never hidden; on the contrary, it was actively promoted.

The accurate verdict: promising technology in a large and legitimate trial, with real potential benefit to patients if results confirm effectiveness, and no evidence of suppression, concealment, or deliberate access restriction beyond the ordinary regulatory timeline.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Liquid biopsy — the blanket term for tests that detect cancer-related material circulating in the blood — is one of the most active areas of cancer research in the world. The Galleri test is the furthest-developed MCED (multi-cancer early detection) product in human trials, but it is far from the only effort in this space.

The scientific principle underlying all liquid biopsy approaches is that tumours shed material — DNA, RNA, proteins, whole cells — into the bloodstream. Different technologies are designed to detect different types of shed material. Cell-free DNA testing, exemplified by Galleri, looks for circulating tumour DNA (ctDNA) and identifies characteristic methylation patterns that distinguish tumour-derived fragments from normal cellular DNA. Circulating tumour cell tests capture whole cancer cells that have broken free from primary tumours. Exosome-based tests examine tiny vesicles shed by cells that carry protein and nucleic acid cargo. Proteomics-based tests, like the CancerSEEK test developed at Johns Hopkins, combine protein biomarkers with gene mutation detection.

Each approach has a different sensitivity/specificity trade-off. All of them face the same fundamental challenge: at very early stages (Stage I disease), the amount of tumour-derived material in the bloodstream is typically very small — below detection thresholds for most current tests. The result is that current MCED tests are most sensitive for advanced-stage disease, which is not where population-level screening value is highest. The NHS Galleri trial was specifically designed to investigate whether the test adds clinical value in a real-world screening context across all stages.

From a public health standpoint, the questions around MCED tests go beyond whether they work. Even if a test detects a cancer signal accurately, the downstream consequences matter: does detection lead to intervention that extends life and reduces suffering, or does it lead to overdiagnosis and overtreatment — interventions for slow-growing cancers that would never have become symptomatic, with real harm from treatment and no benefit to longevity? These are not rhetorical objections; they are the central questions of cancer screening science, and they apply to MCED tests just as they have long applied to PSA testing for prostate cancer.

The answer to these questions is precisely what the NHS Galleri trial and similar trials in the US and globally are designed to provide. Running controlled trials on hundreds of thousands of people is slow and expensive, but it is also the only way to know whether introducing a new screening tool into public health practice will, on balance, help people live longer and better. This is not suppression; it is due diligence.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published its fact-check of the blood cancer test claim in September 2021, immediately following the NHS announcement. Like the breast cancer vaccine entry, the assigned rating was **True** — again referring specifically to the factual claim being checked, which was narrowly whether the NHS had launched a world-first trial of a multi-cancer blood test in mid-September 2021. That factual claim was straightforwardly verifiable and accurate.

The Snopes analysis by Madison Dapcevich is notable for its careful layering of the positive and limiting facts. The piece describes what the Galleri test can detect, notes its FDA status (breakthrough device designation, not approved), explains its cost and insurance non-coverage in the United States, clarifies that a positive result does not constitute a diagnosis, and specifies the trial design and timeline. This is health fact-checking operating at its most constructive: not trying to dismiss emerging science, but assembling the context that allows the reader to understand it accurately.

The pattern this entry illustrates is important enough to name explicitly. Health misinformation about emerging diagnostics often operates in the gap between *trial announcement* and *clinical deployment* — a gap that can be years or even decades wide. When a trial is announced, the technology has already passed safety checks and shown promising early results. From a lay perspective, the fact that researchers are confident enough to trial it in tens of thousands of people can look indistinguishable from "it exists and is ready," especially when the news is framed optimistically by institutions keen to attract trial participants.

Fact-checkers in this space have developed vocabulary for this problem: "not yet ready for clinical use," "not approved for routine screening," "promising but unproven at population scale." These are signals that the underlying science is real but the claim of deployment or availability is premature. Recognising those signals is part of the media literacy work that effective health fact-checking tries to teach, one annotated claim at a time.
