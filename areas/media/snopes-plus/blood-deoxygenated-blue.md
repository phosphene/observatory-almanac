## The Claim

The blood traveling through your veins is blue because it has been depleted of oxygen. When blood picks up oxygen in the lungs it becomes red, which is why arterial blood is red. When that oxygen is consumed by tissues, the blood becomes deoxygenated and turns blue. This is why, if you look at your wrist, you can see blue veins beneath your skin.

This belief is remarkably pervasive. It is repeated in textbooks, by teachers, in science programs on television, and by adults who are otherwise well-informed about biology. It has the satisfying quality of an explanation that seems to confirm everyday observation — you can see the veins in your wrist, they look blue, so of course the blood in them is blue.

## What's Actually True

Blood is never blue. Under any oxygenation condition found in a living human body, blood is red. The shade of red varies — bright scarlet arterial blood, dark maroon venous blood — but the color always falls within the red spectrum. The color derives from hemoglobin, the iron-containing protein inside red blood cells that carries oxygen through the body.

**The biochemistry of blood color**

Hemoglobin is a protein made up of four subunits, each containing an iron atom embedded in a porphyrin ring called a "heme group." When oxygen binds to the iron, it changes the geometry and electronic configuration of the molecule in a way that alters which wavelengths of light it absorbs and reflects. Oxygenated hemoglobin (oxyhemoglobin) absorbs light in the blue-green range and reflects red and infrared wavelengths strongly, producing the bright red appearance of arterial blood and fingertips during a pulse oximetry check. Deoxygenated hemoglobin absorbs light across a broader range, including more red wavelengths, and by comparison the blood appears darker — a deep, purplish-crimson red, like the color of dry red wine or dark cherry juice. But it is still unmistakably red.

You can verify this yourself, as medical and nursing students do: when blood is drawn from a vein — deoxygenated blood — it comes out dark red, not blue. Surgical or trauma patients, who may lose significant blood, never produce blue-colored bleeding. If you have ever had blood drawn, you observed exactly this: the blood in the collection tube is a deep, rich red.

For blood to actually appear blue, hemoglobin would need a completely different chemistry. Some animals, notably most mollusks and crustaceans — octopuses, horseshoe crabs, lobsters — do have blue blood. In their case, the oxygen-carrying molecule is not hemoglobin but hemocyanin, which contains copper rather than iron. When hemocyanin binds oxygen it turns blue; when deoxygenated, it is colorless or faintly gray. These creatures genuinely do have blue blood. Humans categorically do not.

**Why veins look blue through skin**

If blood is consistently red, why do veins appear distinctly blue or blue-green when viewed through pale skin? The answer lies in the physics of light interacting with biological tissue, and it is both subtle and fascinating.

Skin and the tissue beneath it do not transmit all wavelengths of light equally. When light hits the skin:

1. Red and near-infrared wavelengths penetrate relatively deeply into tissue — several millimetres — because blood and tissue chromophores absorb them more weakly at those wavelengths.
2. Blue and green wavelengths are absorbed much more rapidly near the surface. They scatter back from shallow layers rather than penetrating deeply.

This means that when you look at a vein beneath the skin, the red light that would normally make blood look red has often been absorbed by the surrounding tissue before it reaches your eye. The blue and green light, meanwhile, scatters back more readily from the surface layers, giving the overlying skin a blueish tinge.

The vein itself also scatters some light. When light reaches the vessel, deoxygenated blood absorbs somewhat more red light than oxygenated blood does. The net result, as observed at the skin surface, is a blue or blue-green appearance — even though the underlying blood is dark red.

A 2005 paper by Alwin Kienle and colleagues in *Applied Optics* modelled this phenomenon rigorously using Monte Carlo simulations of photon transport in tissue, demonstrating mathematically why veins appear blue or green beneath skin even though the blood they contain is red. The effect is partly depth-dependent: superficial veins appear more blue-green; deeper vessels, when visible at all, often appear greenish or disappear into background skin tone.

**Pulse oximetry and light absorption diagnostics**

The wavelength-dependent absorption of oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin is not merely a curiosity — it is clinically useful. Pulse oximeters, the devices clipped to fingertips in hospitals and used widely during the COVID-19 pandemic, work by shining two wavelengths of light (typically around 660nm red and 940nm near-infrared) through the tissue and measuring how much of each wavelength is absorbed. Because oxyhemoglobin and deoxyhemoglobin absorb these wavelengths at different ratios, the device can calculate the proportion of hemoglobin that is oxygenated (the SpO₂ percentage). This diagnostic technology both confirms the red nature of blood under all conditions and demonstrates that the meaningful perceptual difference between oxygenated and deoxygenated blood is a shade difference within the red spectrum, not a transition from red to blue.

**The one context where "blue blood" is sometimes seen**

In extreme laboratory conditions — specifically, very high-pressure oxygen environments — carbon monoxide poisoning produces a compound (carboxyhemoglobin) that gives blood an unusually bright cherry-red color. Some variants of hemoglobin abnormalities can affect blood color slightly. But no normal physiological state produces blue human blood. The closest clinical exception is methemoglobinemia, a rare condition in which iron in hemoglobin oxidizes to the +3 state (methemoglobin), giving blood a chocolate-brown color — still not blue.

## Why People Believe This

This misconception is a case study in how a simplified, incorrect model can become embedded in educational infrastructure to the point where it is difficult to dislodge even when the evidence against it is readily accessible.

**Educational simplification gone wrong**

The motive for the oversimplification is understandable. Biology education uses diagrams to represent the circulatory system, and diagrams require color-coding to distinguish arteries from veins. The convention — red for arteries carrying oxygenated blood, blue for veins carrying deoxygenated blood — is a representational shorthand meant to make the diagram legible and to communicate the functional difference between the two types of vessels. It is a map convention, like how political maps often color countries in bright, distinct hues that bear no relationship to how the terrain actually looks.

Somewhere in the chain of simplified teaching, the map became confused with the territory. Color-coding a vein as blue on a diagram for visual clarity became teaching students that venous blood *is* blue — an inference that the diagram itself does not necessarily require, but that students and sometimes teachers drew anyway.

**Everyday visual experience seems to confirm it**

The most powerful reinforcer of the myth is the observation it appears to explain. You can look at your wrist right now and see blue lines under the skin. If that's where blood is, and it looks blue, the inference that the blood is blue is natural, even if it's wrong. Without understanding the optical physics of tissue light absorption, there's no obvious reason to suspect that the appearance and the reality would diverge so dramatically.

This is a classic example of naive perceptual realism — the assumption that what we observe directly corresponds to the underlying physical reality, when in fact perception is an active construction that involves complex optical and neural processing.

**No strong correction mechanism in mainstream culture**

Many myths are eventually corrected by encounter with disconfirming evidence — people see or experience something that contradicts the myth. In this case, the most obvious disconfirming evidence is blood draws: venous blood is visibly dark red when drawn. But people who have had blood drawn typically do not connect that observation to the myth about blue blood. The contexts don't obviously link. You see blue veins on your wrist, you have a blood draw, the blood is red, but the two perceptions don't trigger a "wait, those two facts contradict" response because you're not explicitly comparing them.

**The authoritative source problem**

Unlike rumors and conspiracy theories that can be corrected by pointing to credentialed experts, this myth frequently *originates from* credentialed sources — textbooks, teachers, educational videos. This reduces the motivation to fact-check it: why would you look for an authoritative source to contradict your authoritative source? The myth thus benefits from exactly the epistemic shortcuts we rely on to function in a world where we can't verify everything personally.

**The "just a simplification" defense**

When informed of the error, some educators respond that calling veins blue is "just a simplification for teaching purposes." This defense has some merit — educators routinely simplify, and perfect accuracy can sometimes impede clear teaching — but it fails here because the simplification produces a false belief about a concrete physical property. Saying the atom looks like a miniature solar system is a simplification; saying electrons are in fixed orbits is a claim that needs to be revised. Color-coding on a diagram is fine; stating that venous blood is blue as a biological fact crosses into misinformation.

## Verdict

**False.** Human blood is always red — oxygenated blood is bright red, deoxygenated blood is dark red. The blue-green appearance of veins through skin is an optical effect caused by the wavelength-dependent penetration and scattering of light through tissue, not the color of the blood inside.

The claim has genuine observational grounding: you can see blue-looking veins on your body. But the causal inference — that veins look blue because the blood in them is blue — is incorrect. The color you see at skin surface is a perceptual artifact of optical physics, not a transmission of blood color.

EARC verdict: **C — Mostly false on the key factual claim.** The observation (veins look blue through skin) is accurate; the explanation (blood is blue because it's deoxygenated) is false.

## The Wider Picture

The blue blood myth is arguably the most durable piece of misinformation in biology education. It illustrates several structural problems that make science education vulnerable to producing well-intentioned but inaccurate beliefs.

**The diagram-as-reality problem**

Visual representations in science education are necessarily simplified — and necessarily use conventions. Diagrams of the solar system aren't to scale. Models of atoms don't represent electron cloud probability distributions. Cell illustrations can't show the dense molecular chaos of real cytoplasm. These simplifications are pedagogically necessary but carry a cost when students don't learn that the diagrams *are* simplifications, with specific features chosen for legibility rather than accuracy.

The circulatory system diagram with red arteries and blue veins does exactly what it's meant to do — it makes the functional difference between the two structures instantly legible. The problem arises when the representational convention is verbally reinforced with a false factual claim rather than an explicit note that "we're using blue for veins, even though blood is always red."

**What this reveals about the persistence of school myths**

Research on conceptual change in science education documents that misconceptions acquired in formal schooling are often more resistant to correction than misconceptions from informal culture, precisely because students trust the authoritative source. A child who learns from a trusted teacher that blood is blue may resist correction as an adult not because they've thought carefully about it and believe the evidence supports the blue-blood position, but because the belief is part of their identity as a "person who learned this in school" and challenging it requires implicitly challenging their trust in their entire educational history.

**Implications for blood-related medicine and health literacy**

While the blue blood myth rarely causes direct harm, it is part of a broader pattern of health literacy gaps that can matter clinically. People who are genuinely confused about basic physiology may be less likely to engage productively with medical information — if basic facts they learned in school are wrong, what else might be? Building public trust in science communication partly depends on getting school-level teaching right.

**Animal blood and the genuine diversity of respiratory chemistry**

The existence of the myth also obscures something genuinely fascinating about biology: the remarkable diversity of oxygen-carrying molecules evolved across the animal kingdom. Hemocyanin, the copper-based Blue protein in mollusks and many crustaceans, is a convergently evolved solution to the same problem hemoglobin solves. Hemoglobin itself, with its iron porphyrin system, evolved very early in the history of life and appears in organisms as diverse as bacteria, yeast, plants, and vertebrates. Some Antarctic icefish have lost hemoglobin entirely and rely on oxygen dissolved directly in unusually voluminous blood, which is indeed pale and colorless. Brachiopods have green blood from a protein called chlorocruorin. The rich chemical variety of life's oxygen-carrying systems is genuinely worth knowing — and the false story about human blood being blue actually displaces this genuine wonder with a mundane error.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The blue blood claim has been addressed by various science communication platforms, with somewhat variable quality.

**Where corrections succeed**

The most effective corrections combine the three-part explanation: (1) blood is always red, (2) here's what hemoglobin chemistry shows, (3) here's the optical explanation for why veins look blue. Addressing only the first point — "blood is red, not blue" — often fails to satisfy, because it doesn't explain the visible observation. People's mental model of blue veins needs to be replaced with a better model of why skin and tissue change how blood looks, not just contradicted.

**Common partial corrections**

Many science communication pieces correctly state that blood is always red but leave the question of why veins look blue under-explained or unexplained, which can leave people with a corrected fact but no framework that actually connects to their experience. A correction that can't account for the observation it's overturning tends not to stick.

**The classroom context**

Interestingly, this is one myth where schoolteachers themselves are often the original transmitters, which creates an awkward dynamic for science communicators: you are implicitly saying that what teachers taught was wrong. Good science communication navigates this by framing it as an understandable simplification that got miscommunicated along the way, rather than blaming individual educators. The myth is systemic, embedded in the way textbooks were illustrated and the way those illustrations were verbally described, and fixing it requires systemic correction — better textbook language, better teacher training, clearer conventions — not just individual-level correction campaigns.

**Snopes gap**

This claim falls into a category that standard fact-checking platforms rarely address: myths of schoolroom origin that are incorrect, widely believed, and traceable to established educational materials rather than hoax chains or political misinformation. Such claims are poorly served by the viral-rumor-oriented model of Snopes-style checking, which is part of the purpose of the Observatory Truth Vault.
