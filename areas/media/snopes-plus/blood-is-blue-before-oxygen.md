## The Claim

At some point in their education — usually in a biology class, sometimes in a casual conversation about how the body works — most people encounter the claim that blood changes colour depending on whether it carries oxygen. The standard version runs approximately like this: *blood in your veins is blue because it has given up its oxygen; when it contacts the oxygen in the air, it turns red*. Some formulations are more specific: *deoxygenated blood is actually blue or bluish-purple until oxygen binds to it*. Some people have refined this into a claim about vein colour: *the reason you can see blue veins through your skin is that the blood inside them is blue*.

This is one of the more persistent anatomical myths in circulation. It is the kind of "fact" that many people believe they were actually taught in school — and in some cases they were, because the myth has infiltrated even educational contexts. The claim has a satisfying internal logic: we look at our wrists and see blue-ish vessels, and it seems intuitive that the colour we see through the skin corresponds to the colour of the blood inside. There is also a convenient story: oxygen turns blood red, so blood without oxygen must be a different colour. The explanation feels complete, mechanistically plausible, and consistent with observation.

It is, nonetheless, false — and the truth is considerably more interesting.

## What Is Actually True

Human blood is always red. The only variation is in the *shade* of red: bright cherry red when it is carrying abundant oxygen, and a darker, deeper red — often described as maroon or dark crimson — when it is oxygen-depleted. At no point in the human circulatory system does blood become blue, blue-purple, or any colour other than some shade of red.

**The chemistry of haemoglobin.** Blood's colour is determined almost entirely by haemoglobin, the iron-containing protein in red blood cells that performs the critical function of carrying oxygen from the lungs to the tissues. Each haemoglobin molecule contains four haem groups — a porphyrin ring structure wrapped around a central iron atom. It is this haem group that binds oxygen, and it is this haem group that determines blood's colour.

The colour of any chemical compound depends on which wavelengths of light it absorbs and which it reflects. Haemoglobin bound to oxygen (oxyhaemoglobin) absorbs light primarily in the blue-green portion of the spectrum, reflecting the red-orange wavelengths back — which is why oxygenated arterial blood appears bright cherry red. Haemoglobin without oxygen (deoxyhaemoglobin) absorbs a slightly different pattern of wavelengths, shifting the absorption somewhat toward the blue-green end, and reflects a different combination of long wavelengths — producing the darker, more bluish-red or maroon colour of venous blood.

The word "bluish" in that last sentence warrants careful interpretation: it means the red is shifted toward the blue direction of the spectrum, producing a darker, less saturated red. Deoxygenated blood is not blue. It does not reflect blue light predominantly. It absorbs most of the blue-green spectrum, just as oxygenated blood does, with slightly different peak absorption. When you draw venous blood and observe it directly — as phlebotomists and medical professionals do routinely — it is visibly dark red, not blue. This is not a fine factual point that requires sophisticated measurement; it is something that anyone who has had blood drawn in significant quantity, cut a vein, or worked in a medical setting can observe directly.

**The optics of skin and subcutaneous tissue.** So why do veins look blue or bluish-green through the skin? The answer lies in the optical properties of human tissue, not in the colour of the blood.

Human skin and the subcutaneous tissue beneath it have complex optical properties. Light that hits the skin is scattered, reflected, and absorbed by multiple tissue components — melanin, water, fat, connective tissue, and blood itself. The critical point is that different wavelengths of light penetrate to different depths. Red and infrared light penetrate relatively deeply into tissue — often several centimetres — before being absorbed. Blue and violet light, however, penetrate much less deeply; they are scattered and absorbed near the surface.

This differential penetration depth has a striking consequence for vein visibility. When you look at a vein near the skin surface, both red and blue wavelengths are present in the light reaching the vein. The vein contains dark red (deoxygenated) blood, which absorbs wavelengths across the spectrum. The reflected light pattern that reaches your eye is influenced by how much of each wavelength was absorbed in the tissue above the vein versus the vein itself. Because red wavelengths penetrate deeply, they are substantially absorbed by the blood and tissue throughout the depth; because blue wavelengths do not penetrate as deeply, a relatively higher proportion of blue light is reflected back before reaching the vein or in the tissue layers above it. Your eye therefore sees proportionally more blue than red reflected from the region of the vein — even though the blood itself is dark red.

This optical phenomenon was described rigorously in a 1996 paper by Kienle and colleagues published in *Applied Optics*, which modelled the differential absorption and scattering of light in layered tissue structures. The physics is specific: at typical vein depths in superficial anatomy, the skin's preferential near-surface reflection of blue light combined with the greater depth penetration of red light creates exactly the colour contrast that makes veins look blue through pale skin. The darker the overlying skin, the less pronounced the blue vein appearance — which is why vein visibility varies substantially with skin tone.

**The confounding role of medical diagrams.** There is a second major source of the myth: the almost universal convention in medical and anatomical diagrams of colouring arteries red and veins blue. This convention is pedagogically useful — it provides instant visual distinguishability between the two vascular systems, which carry blood in opposite directions and serve opposite physiological functions. The convention predates modern understanding of blood chemistry and developed primarily as a schematic convenience, not a biological description.

However, generations of students have encountered these diagrams in textbooks from primary school through university, have seen blue consistently associated with veins and venous blood, and have made a natural inferential error: if the diagram shows venous blood as blue, perhaps venous blood *is* blue. The diagram is a representation; it is not a photograph. Its blue colour is a convention, like the use of specific colours to represent countries on a political map — China is not literally the shade of tan it appears in one atlas. But the human visual system is not well calibrated to make this distinction when the same diagram is used throughout a decade of science education.

**A note on non-human blood.** The mythological resonance of "blue blood" — royal privilege, aristocratic lineage — and the actual biological reality of blue blood in some animal species may have played a background cultural role in making the myth feel plausible. It is genuinely true that some animals have blue blood: horseshoe crabs, many squids and octopuses, some snails, and other invertebrates use haemocyanin rather than haemoglobin as their oxygen-transport molecule. Haemocyanin contains copper rather than iron, and copper-oxygen complexes reflect blue light. These animals genuinely do have blue blood. But they are not mammals, and their biochemistry has essentially nothing to do with human physiology. The existence of blue blood in the animal kingdom may lend the myth a residual plausibility — "well, some blood is blue" — that it would not otherwise have.

## Why People Believe This

The blood-is-blue myth is a case study in how a false belief can gain remarkable cultural traction through the convergence of three mutually reinforcing factors: a genuine perceptual phenomenon, a pedagogical convention misgeneralised, and a narratively satisfying explanation.

**The visual evidence seems clear and compelling.** Look at your wrist in good light. The vessels visible beneath the skin appear blue or blue-green. This is not an obscure anatomical region that requires special conditions to observe — it is a part of your body visible every day. The visual experience of blue veins is vivid, consistent, and available to nearly everyone. It provides what feels like direct observational evidence for the claim. Challenging this belief means asking people to distrust something they can see with their own eyes — which is a significantly harder epistemic task than correcting a belief based on hearsay or misremembered facts.

**The school-diagram pipeline.** Many people have explicit memories of being taught the red-artery/blue-vein diagram in school, and some have explicit memories of being told that venous blood is blue (though in many cases this reflects an inferential step the student made independently rather than something the teacher actually stated). When a belief appears to have been taught by an authority figure in an explicitly educational context, it becomes substantially more resistant to correction. The cognitive status of a "thing I learned at school" is different from the cognitive status of "something I heard somewhere" — the former feels more like established knowledge and less like a rumour.

**The explanation is mechanistically attractive.** The story told — oxygen turns blood red, so blood without oxygen must not be red — has the structure of a satisfying causal account. It fits within a broader understanding of chemistry (oxygen causes colour changes; rust is red and includes oxygen; blood changes colour when oxygen binds) and it predicts the observation (veins look blue). An explanation that accounts for both mechanism and observation, in a way that feels coherent, is much harder to displace than one that lacks this internal architecture. Correcting it requires not just asserting "you're wrong" but providing a better causal account — the optics story — which is more complex and counterintuitive than the oxygen-colour story.

**The myth is socially circulated.** Because the claim is confidently stated by people who believe they learned it in school, it circulates as settled fact in everyday conversation. When someone sees blue veins and mentions them, a nearby person may confirm: "Yes, that blood doesn't have oxygen yet." The social circulation amplifies the apparent credibility of the claim — if everyone believes it, and people have believed it since they were taught it in school, it must be right.

**Absence of corrective experience.** Unlike some myths that can be directly tested by personal experience, the blood-colour myth evades personal disconfirmation for most people. To observe that venous blood is dark red rather than blue requires drawing blood in visible quantity — something that happens only during medical procedures. Most people's entire lived experience with visible blood involves either small cuts (in which the blood is predominantly arterial, thus bright red) or major injuries (in which the shock and distress make careful colour observation unlikely). Phlebotomists and surgeons know from direct observation that venous blood is dark red. Most people never have that direct observation available to them.

## Verdict

**False, cleanly and without significant nuance.**

Blood is never blue. Oxygenated blood is bright red. Deoxygenated blood is dark red (maroon). The blue appearance of veins through skin is an optical phenomenon arising from differential light penetration of subcutaneous tissue — not a reflection of the blood's actual colour. Medical diagrams use blue to represent veins as a convention for visual clarity, not as a biological description.

The EARC mode is **R** (Refuted): this claim is straightforwardly false, contradicted by direct physical observation available to any clinician drawing blood and by well-established biochemistry and optical physics. The gap category is **phenomenologically-real**: the experience motivating the belief — blue-appearing veins visible through skin — is genuine, but it has been misinterpreted.

Snopes categorises this as false and references the same optical physics explanation described here.

## The Wider Picture

At first glance, the blood-colour myth might seem like a harmless piece of anatomical folklore — wrong, but not dangerous. And in many respects it is. But the case opens onto several more significant observations about science education, visual inference, and the anatomy of persistent myths.

**Science education and the diagram problem.** The widespread use of blue for veins in anatomical diagrams is a genuine pedagogical problem that has received increasing attention from science educators. When the same visual convention is used throughout primary and secondary school education without explicit statements that this is a convention and not a colour description, a substantial fraction of students will exit the educational system believing venous blood is literally blue. Some studies have found that a significant majority of adults hold this belief, and that it persists even among healthcare workers who have not received explicit correction. Corrective strategies include adding visible legends ("convention only — deoxygenated blood is dark red") to diagrams and explicitly addressing the common misinterpretation in classroom settings.

**Visual inference and its limits.** The blood myth is a case study in the limits of visual inference from indirect observation. We do not see the blood directly; we see light that has passed through skin and tissue and been differentially filtered. The colour signal we receive is mediated by optical physics that is non-trivial and non-obvious. This is a general feature of perception: most of what we see is not direct information about the world but a reconstruction based on the information that survived the journey from object to retina. The blue-vein example shows how dramatically this reconstruction can deviate from the underlying physical reality.

**The arterial-venous distinction in clinical context.** In clinical medicine, distinguishing arterial from venous blood has direct practical importance during procedures — arterial blood is bright red and comes under pressure; venous blood is dark red and flows more slowly. Clinicians performing procedures that risk arterial puncture (central line placement, arterial blood gas sampling) rely on these colour and flow characteristics. The myth does no direct harm in this domain — it does not cause clinicians to miscategorise blood, since direct observation corrects the belief — but it illustrates a broader point about the value of direct observational experience in medical training versus textbook representations.

**Carbon monoxide and colour variation.** Beyond the oxygenated-deoxygenated dichotomy, the Snopes article referenced draws attention to a striking variation: carbon monoxide poisoning. Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin with approximately 200 times the affinity of oxygen, and the resulting carboxyhaemoglobin complex produces blood that is a vivid, bright cherry red — even brighter than fully oxygenated blood. This means victims of carbon monoxide poisoning sometimes appear deceptively rosy-cheeked and healthy-looking even when severely poisoned, which has practical consequences for emergency assessment. The blood-colour story is more complex than the simple oxygen/no-oxygen binary suggests.

**A note on epistemics.** The blood-is-blue myth provides a particularly clean illustration of a phenomenon central to science education: the gap between things that seem obviously true from direct experience and things that are actually true given careful measurement and physical understanding. Veins look blue — that is a direct observation. Blood is not blue — that is a conclusion derived from biochemistry and physics that contradicts the naive visual inference. Navigating this gap is not a trivial cognitive achievement; it requires some trust in the inferential chain that leads from chemical spectroscopy to colour prediction to the acknowledgment that what you see through your skin is mediated by tissue optics rather than directly representing the blood's intrinsic colour.

This kind of epistemological gap — between naive observation and careful scientific inference — is at the heart of many science communication challenges. The most effective corrections acknowledge the validity of the observation ("yes, veins do look blue") while providing a compelling, correct account of why this observation does not mean what it seems to mean ("because of how skin and tissue absorb different wavelengths of light, the blue light is reflected back to your eye more efficiently than the red light the dark red blood reflects").

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published a detailed explainer on this myth in August 2019, drawing on content from The Conversation (authored by science educators) that walks through the chemistry of haemoglobin, the optics of skin and subcutaneous tissue, and the history of the anatomical diagramming convention. The Snopes treatment is notable for being genuinely educational rather than merely dismissive — it does not simply state "false" and move on, but works through the underlying physics and biochemistry in accessible language.

The fact-check approach here is different from that required for more politically or emotionally charged myths. The blood-colour claim does not arise from motivated reasoning or ideological commitment. It arises from genuinely misleading perceptual experience combined with pedagogical convention. Correcting it therefore requires education rather than debunking in the usual sense — it is more a case of "here is a fuller explanation of a confusing observation" than "here is why this alarming claim is wrong."

Fact-checkers dealing with this myth often face the challenge that people who hold the belief are not readily embarrassed or destabilised by learning it is wrong — because the original source of the belief was authoritative (schoolteachers, textbooks) and the supporting observation is genuinely real (veins do look blue). The correct approach is to add information rather than remove it: the visual phenomenon is real, the perceptual experience is valid, and the correct explanation — optical physics of tissue layers — is simply more detailed and accurate than the incorrect oxygen-colour story.

One instructive detail from the Snopes article: the piece notes that some animals do have genuinely blue blood (horseshoe crabs, squid, octopuses), which uses haemocyanin rather than haemoglobin. This information does something useful: it acknowledges the category of the claim as biologically possible in principle while clarifying that human blood is not in this category. Rather than leaving the reader to wonder "but then why do some animals have blue blood?", the fact-check uses that curiosity as an opportunity to explain copper-based versus iron-based oxygen transport — a teaching moment that enlarges the reader's biological understanding rather than simply taking something away.

For science communicators, this case offers a model for handling myths whose persistence arises from genuinely misleading experience rather than bad-faith information sharing: validate the experience, explain the mechanism, and provide a richer story that makes the world more interesting rather than merely correcting an error. The blood-is-not-blue story — involving differential light penetration of tissue, the iron chemistry of haemoglobin, the pedagogical history of anatomical diagrams, and the existence of genuinely different blood chemistries elsewhere in the animal kingdom — is substantially more interesting than the myth it replaces.
