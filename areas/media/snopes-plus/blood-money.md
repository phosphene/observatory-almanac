# Blood money — myths about selling blood, plasma, and human tissue for profit

## 1. The Claim

The cluster of claims surrounding the sale of blood, plasma, and human tissue for money is unusually heterogeneous. Unlike most entries in this series, this topic does not have a single false claim at its center — it has a constellation of related claims at varying distances from truth, held together by a common emotional subtext: that the human body is a tradeable biological commodity, that poor people are systematically being harvested for profit, and that the systems governing biological commerce are either more dangerous or more lucrative than officially acknowledged.

The specific claims include: *You can sell your blood for cash at blood banks.* This is false in the United States for whole blood but partially true for other countries and partially true domestically for plasma. *Plasma donation centers are front operations for pharmaceutical companies that exploit poor donors.* This is partially true in a way worth examining carefully. *You can sell your organs legally.* This is false for most organs in most countries, though Iran is a notable exception for kidneys. *You can earn a significant monthly income from plasma donations.* This is true at the entry level but significantly overstated by most popular renderings. *Frequent plasma donation is medically dangerous and leaves you malnourished and sick.* This is contested — the answer is complex and depends on frequency, individual health, and center practices. *Hospitals sell your blood after surgeries.* This misunderstands how blood banking works but is not entirely groundless. *Hair, eggs, sperm, breast milk, and fecal transplant material can all be "sold."* This is partially true in each case but with important regulatory and semantic nuances.

The aggregate of these claims constitutes a narrative: the human body, particularly the bodies of poor people, is a biological resource being commercially extracted under conditions of economic coercion, with neither the regulatory protection nor the transparency that the affected individuals deserve. That narrative has some legitimate foundations in documented fact, which makes it significantly more difficult to address than a purely fabricated claim.

## 2. What's Actually True

Parsing the truth in this domain requires precision about which biological product is being discussed, which country's law governs it, and which part of the claim is under scrutiny.

**Whole blood cannot be sold for cash in the United States.** The American Red Cross and most hospital-affiliated blood banks accept only volunteer donations. The FDA does not prohibit the sale of whole blood, but no major blood collection system purchases it, and the FDA requires that blood purchased for transfusion be labeled as "paid donor blood," which hospitals avoid due to documented historical associations with higher rates of transfusion-transmitted infection in paid donor populations. If you walk into a Red Cross donation center expecting payment, you will leave disappointed.

**Plasma can be sold for cash, and is, at significant industrial scale.** The United States is one of the very few countries in the world that permits the commercial purchase of blood plasma from donors. Approximately forty percent of the world's plasma-derived medications are sourced from the United States' paid plasma donor network. Compensation at plasma donation centers — operated by companies including Grifols, BioLife (Takeda), CSL Plasma, and Octapheresis — typically ranges from $50 to $150 per session for new or return donors in promotional windows, with standard sessions paying considerably less. Donors can give up to twice per week under FDA regulations. First-month promotional rates can reach $800 to $1,000 at some centers; ongoing compensation for established donors averages significantly lower. The plasma is fractionated and sold to pharmaceutical manufacturers who produce immunoglobulins, clotting factors, and albumin products, which are sold back into the healthcare system at substantial markups.

**The economics of plasma extraction create real ethical tensions.** Plasma centers are disproportionately located in low-income neighborhoods and near college campuses — areas with concentrations of people who need supplemental income. Research published in *Health Affairs*, *JAMA*, and other peer-reviewed journals has documented that plasma donors in the United States are disproportionately food-insecure, uninsured, and economically marginal. (See, e.g., Ochoa, A., Shaefer, H.L., & Grogan-Kaylor, A. (2021). The interlinkage between blood plasma donation and poverty in the United States. *Journal of Sociology & Social Welfare*, 48(2), Article 4.) A 2022 FDIC working paper found that approximately seventy percent of U.S. plasma donors were primarily motivated by essential expenses including food, emergencies, and debt repayment, and documented a 7–10 percent increase in grocery store foot traffic following the opening of a new plasma center nearby. (Gallagher, E. (2022). *Blood money: The financial implications of plasma sales for individuals and non-bank lenders*. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation Consumer Finance Research Paper.) This is not hidden — it is the economic structure the industry was built on — but it is rarely foregrounded in how plasma collection is publicly discussed.

**Donor frequency and health are genuinely contested.** The FDA allows plasma donation up to 104 times per year. European regulatory bodies cap donation at significantly lower rates — twenty-four to forty-eight times per year, depending on the country — partly because of concerns about protein depletion, immunoglobulin levels, and long-term physiological effects. The scientific literature on whether twice-weekly plasma donation at U.S.-permitted rates causes harm is genuinely mixed. Most major studies sponsored by the plasma industry, unsurprisingly, find minimal harm. Independent studies have raised more concerns about immunoglobulin depletion, particularly in frequent donors. A 2024 systematic review in *Transfusion Medicine Reviews* noted that the long-term health effects of high-frequency plasma donation remain incompletely studied and that existing studies are frequently industry-funded. (Van Remoortel, H., van den Hurk, K., Nilsson, J.T.E., De Krispe, E., Browett, P., Guéguen, M., Van Remoortel, P., & Erikstrup, C. (2024). Balancing donor health and plasma collection: A systematic review of the impact of plasmapheresis frequency. *Transfusion Medicine Reviews*, 38.) This is a legitimate scientific uncertainty, not a resolved question.

**Organ sale is illegal in nearly every jurisdiction.** The National Organ Transplant Act of 1984 prohibits the purchase or sale of human organs in the United States, a prohibition replicated in most other countries through the WHO's Guiding Principles on Human Cell, Tissue and Organ Transplantation. Iran is the well-documented exception: a government-regulated system allows the sale of kidneys by living donors, primarily to address a chronic shortage. In the United States and most other countries, organ sale is a black market activity associated with exploitation of impoverished donors in developing countries under conditions of minimal regulatory protection. This black market is real and documented, but it operates in violation of law, not as an above-board commercial option.

**Hair, eggs, sperm, and milk occupy a different regulatory category.** These materials are classified differently from whole blood and organs. Egg donation agencies in the United States offer compensation typically ranging from $8,000 to $15,000 per cycle, with premium rates at elite donor agencies significantly higher. Sperm banks pay considerably less — typically $50 to $200 per accepted sample. Breast milk marketplaces operate in a regulatory gray zone; the FDA recommends against selling breast milk informally due to screening and safety concerns but does not prohibit it. Egg donation is not regulated as a medical sale but as compensation for time and discomfort, a legal distinction that masks what is substantively a payment for biological material.

## 3. Why People Believe This (and Get It Wrong in Multiple Directions)

The mythology around biological commerce fails in two opposite directions simultaneously, which is unusual in misinformation analysis and worth noting explicitly.

**Direction one: overclaiming the opportunity.** Claims that you can easily make thousands of dollars a month from plasma donations, that selling blood and tissue is a reliable income supplement, or that the market for human biological materials is an untapped economic resource for ordinary people — these claims exaggerate the available income, ignore the health trade-offs, and obscure the conditions under which the compensation is offered. They circulate through informal personal finance communities, TikTok and YouTube content about side hustles, and clickbait financial advice. They tend to be believed by people who are economically desperate enough to find the premise attractive and motivated not to look critically at the fine print.

**Direction two: overclaiming the danger and sinisterness.** Claims that plasma centers are "harvesting" poor people, that they are front operations for covert organ harvesting, that donated plasma or blood is being sold for enormous profits to enrich pharmaceutical executives at the donor's expense, or that the entire system is an unregulated exploitation engine with no legitimate medical purpose — these claims overstate the danger, often invent illegal practices, and collapse a legitimate if ethically fraught industry into a conspiracy narrative. They circulate through activist and political content aimed at audiences predisposed to distrust pharmaceutical companies and regulatory agencies.

**Both failures emerge from the same root condition: the actual truth is economically uncomfortable.** The real situation — a legal, regulated industry that extracts a biological product from economically marginal people, pays them compensation that is real but modest, and sells the product at significant markup to pharmaceutical companies — is troubling enough on its terms that people in both directions feel the need to intensify it. The financial opportunity version makes the exploitation more bearable by making the donor's benefit seem larger. The conspiracy version makes the exploitation more narratable by making it villainous rather than structural.

**Economic anxiety makes the financial opportunity version maximally sticky.** In environments of widespread wage stagnation, medical debt, and housing insecurity, the claim that the body itself can be a source of income is powerfully appealing. The plasma center marketing industry has, in many cases, contributed to this appeal deliberately — advertising in ways designed to emphasize the income opportunity and minimize the medical reality. The belief that biological self-commodification is a reasonable financial strategy is partially manufactured by the industry that benefits from it.

**Disgust responses amplify the conspiracy version.** The idea that one's body is being extracted for commercial profit activates a powerful visceral response related to bodily integrity and autonomy. Claims that pharmacy companies are getting rich from poor people's plasma tap into legitimate disgust about economic inequality. The problem is that this disgust is applied to an industry that does have genuine medical justification — plasma-derived medications are clinically necessary for patients with primary immunodeficiency and hemophilia, among others — and that overlaying it with a pure exploitation or conspiracy narrative erases the legitimate medical dimension.

**The regulatory complexity creates a knowledge vacuum.** Most people do not know the difference between whole blood donation regulation and plasma donation regulation, between FDA donor deferral categories and payment policies, between the status of organs and the status of eggs in US law. This knowledge vacuum is filled by simplified narratives in both directions, because simple narratives are cognitively economical and emotionally satisfying in ways that accurate regulatory complexity is not.

## 4. Verdict

**MIXED — Distorted in Both Directions.**

The claim that you can straightforwardly sell blood for cash is **FALSE** for whole blood in the US. The claim that plasma can be sold for cash is **TRUE** and industrially significant. The claim that doing so can generate substantial ongoing income is **EXAGGERATED**. The claim that plasma centers are dangerous or covertly illegal is **DISTORTED** — there are legitimate documented concerns about donor health at high-frequency donation rates, and there are legitimate documented structural concerns about economic coercion, but most specific conspiracy claims lack evidentiary support. The claim that organ sale is legal and available is **FALSE**. The claim that eggs, sperm, and milk can generate income is **TRUE** with significant caveats about regulatory frameworks and health considerations.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The mythology of biological self-commodification is not merely a collection of individual false claims. It maps onto a set of much larger social and moral questions about bodily autonomy, economic justice, the ethics of paid biologics markets, and the structures of global medical inequality. These questions deserve direct engagement rather than dismissal as misinformation.

**The United States as global plasma source.** The US's status as the dominant global supplier of plasma is a policy choice, not a natural fact. Most high-income countries prohibit or strictly limit paid plasma collection because of ethical concerns about economic coercion. The US's permissive model exists in part because the alternative — sourcing from volunteer donation — has historically produced insufficient plasma supply for global pharmaceutical needs. The ethical architecture of the global immunoglobulin market therefore rests on a specific US regulatory decision that makes economically precarious American donors the backbone of medication supply for wealthy patients worldwide. This is documented and uncomfortable, and the discomfort is at the root of much of the mythology.

**Organ trafficking is a real and severe global problem.** While organ sale is illegal in most jurisdictions, organ trafficking — the coerced or deceptive extraction of organs from impoverished people in developing countries — is documented and ongoing. The WHO and multiple human rights organizations have published detailed accounts of organized networks in South and Southeast Asia, East Africa, and Eastern Europe that recruit economically desperate people under false pretenses, remove organs under inadequate medical conditions, and pay far less than promised. The existence of this real trafficking network lends credibility to conspiracy claims about legal paid biologics markets in the US, even though the two operate in entirely different legal and structural environments.

**The egg and sperm market raises distinct bioethical concerns.** The commercial market for eggs and sperm involves additional layers of concern beyond those raised by plasma donation. Egg donation involves significant medical risk — hormonal hyperstimulation, surgical retrieval, long-term fertility effects that are not fully studied in donors — and the compensation structures, while substantial, are widely considered inadequate relative to the medical burden. Reproductive medicine scholar Diane Tober and others have documented that egg donors frequently report inadequate informed consent about risks and outcomes. (Tober, D., et al. (2020). Alignment between expectations and experiences of egg donors: What does it mean to be informed? *Reproductive Biomedicine & Society Online*, 11, 48–56.) Sperm banks impose selection criteria — height, education, appearance — that some bioethicists describe as a commercialized eugenics. Neither of these markets is well-represented in popular mythology, which tends toward either "easy side income" or "conspiracy," neither of which captures the actual ethics.

**The informal breast milk market illustrates regulatory gaps.** Online breast milk marketplaces — where lactating mothers sell to parents of infants who cannot access formula or to body-builders who believe in its performance benefits — operate in a space the FDA has not formally regulated as a sale of biological material. The FDA recommends against purchasing informal breast milk due to disease transmission risk (HIV, CMV, hepatitis), potential adulteration, and storage quality concerns. Studies have found that a substantial proportion of informally sold breast milk contains bacterial contamination or does not match its claimed composition. (Keim, S.A., et al. (2013). Microbial contamination of human milk purchased via the internet. *Pediatrics*, 132(5), e1227–e1235; Keim, S.A., et al. (2015). Cow's milk contamination of human milk purchased via the internet. *Pediatrics*, 135(5), e1157–e1162.) This is a clear case where the absence of popular myth (most people do not have strong views about the breast milk market) leaves a genuine and documented public health concern unaddressed.

**The political economy of biologics is a legitimate public concern.** Behind the myths about biological commerce lies a real story about a global industry that extracts biological products from economically vulnerable people in wealthy countries and sells the processed results at enormous markup. The valuations of plasma fractionation companies — Grifols, CSL Behring, Takeda — reflect the margin structure of this extraction. A liter of plasma purchased from a donor for approximately $50 to $75 worth of compensation generates, after processing, pharmaceutical products with wholesale values in the hundreds to thousands of dollars. This margin structure is not hidden; it is simply rarely discussed in public discourse about plasma donation. The myths that grow up around it — in the direction of dangerous conspiracy or easy income — often reflect a correct intuition about economic asymmetry that has been distorted into a false narrative because the true narrative is underreported.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The biological commerce mythology cluster presents fact-checkers with an unusual structural challenge: it requires not simply confirming or denying a claim, but accurately calibrating a complex reality across multiple dimensions simultaneously. Established fact-checking methodologies have mixed track records with this kind of claim.

**The binary verdict problem.** Standard fact-checking formats — true, false, misleading, partly true — are poorly suited to claims like "selling plasma is dangerous." The claim is simultaneously true (at donation frequencies the FDA permits that European regulators would not), false (plasma donation at normal frequencies is safe for most healthy adults), misleading (the framing ignores the industry's economic coercion dimension), and incomplete (it leaves out the genuine medical necessity of plasma-derived therapies). A simple verdict obscures more than it reveals. Fact-checkers at PolitiFact and FactCheck.org have struggled with this genre and have received criticism both for being too dismissive of legitimate concerns and for lending credibility to overclaims.

**The regulatory expertise gap.** Evaluating claims about FDA plasma donation policy, the comparative regulatory frameworks of different countries, the medical evidence on high-frequency donation effects, and the economics of plasma fractionation requires specialized expertise that general-purpose fact-checkers often lack. Coverage of this topic by non-specialist fact-checkers has frequently been either superficial (confirming only the most obviously false claims) or inadvertently misleading (treating industry-funded research as settled science).

**The what-counts-as-a-sale problem.** Fact-checkers must navigate the consistent industry euphemism that donors are being "compensated" rather than paid, that plasma centers are facilitating "donation" rather than purchase. These linguistic conventions have real legal significance — they allow plasma centers to operate under rules that would not apply to straightforward purchase — but they muddy popular understanding. A fact-check that accepts the industry vocabulary at face value will consistently understate the commercial nature of the transaction. A fact-check that rejects it entirely will overclaim regulatory illegality.

**The scientific uncertainty acknowledgment standard.** Responsible fact-checking for this topic requires explicitly acknowledging scientific uncertainty where it exists — particularly on the question of whether twice-weekly long-term plasma donation causes meaningful immunological harm. The emerging standard in evidence-based medicine, endorsed by organizations including the Cochrane Collaboration, is to characterize evidence quality transparently: the evidence on long-term high-frequency donation effects in paid donors is insufficient, industry-funded studies show minimal harm, and independent reviews raise unresolved questions. A fact-checker who says "plasma donation is safe" without this qualification is presenting false certainty on a genuinely contested question.

**The call to structural honesty.** The most rigorous coverage of biological commerce mythology does not stop at individual claim adjudication. It situates each claim in the structural realities of the industry and the economic conditions of donors. Publications including Vox, The Guardian, and *The Atlantic* have produced investigative coverage of the plasma industry that goes beyond fact-check verdicts to describe the documented relationship between plasma center geography and poverty rates, the documented inadequacy of informed consent practices at some centers, and the documented international policy consensus that paid plasma donation is ethically fraught in ways the US regulatory framework underacknowledges. This kind of structural journalism sits adjacent to fact-checking but performs a different epistemic function — not just correcting individual claims but mapping the real terrain they distort.

For readers who encounter claims in this cluster, the key heuristic is to ask which specific biological product the claim is about, what the regulatory framework is for that specific product in the relevant jurisdiction, and whether the source of any medical safety claims is independent of the industry being discussed. The body is not a vending machine, but it is not a simple charitable resource either. The truth that lives between those poles is complicated, documented, and worth understanding accurately.
