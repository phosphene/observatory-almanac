# "Blood Types Determine Personality Traits"

## 1. The Claim

In Japan and South Korea, a belief system known in Japanese as *ketsueki-gata* (血液型) holds that a person's ABO blood type is a reliable guide to their fundamental personality. The system assigns specific character profiles to each type: persons with blood type A are described as meticulous, anxious, empathetic, and conflict-averse; type B individuals are portrayed as creative, self-centred, unpredictable, and impulsive; type O people are characterised as confident, socially dominant, goal-oriented, and occasionally insensitive; and type AB personalities are presented as rational, enigmatic, dual-natured, and uncommonly difficult to classify.

These archetypes circulate with the same cultural weight as horoscopes in Europe or North America. When Japanese or Korean adults meet for the first time—whether at a social gathering, a job interview, or through a dating application—exchanging blood types is routine. Compatibility guides rate pairings between blood types on romantic and professional axes; best-selling self-help books have elaborated the system into detailed descriptions of each type's optimal career, parenting approach, and stress response pattern. Television programmes run personality analyses on celebrities; sports coverage includes blood type statistics alongside athletic metrics.

The claim extends beyond individual curiosity into institutional practice. Job advertisements in Japan and South Korea have historically specified preferred blood types. Some nursery schools were reported by Kitayama and other researchers reviewing Japanese social psychology to have segregated children into blood-type-based classrooms on the theory that matching pedagogy to type would optimise learning outcomes. Employers in competitive industries—including some government agencies—incorporated type into screening processes. The belief thus stakes out territory usually reserved for demonstrably predictive tools: psychological assessments, educational evaluations, clinical diagnoses.

The claim also receives quasi-medical legitimation from the persistence of blood-type-based dietary programmes—the "eat right for your type" franchise popularised by D'Adamo (1996)—which assert that blood antigens influence digestive efficiency and nutritional metabolism. Though logically distinct from the personality claim, the dietary system reinforces the broader cultural premise that blood type reaches into multiple domains of personal biology and behaviour, giving the personality theory a kind of borrowed scientific credibility.

## 2. What's Actually True

No controlled scientific study has found a replicable, statistically significant relationship between ABO blood type and any dimension of personality measured by validated psychological instruments. This is not merely an absence of confirmatory evidence—the cumulative literature constitutes affirmative, high-powered refutation.

The most comprehensive empirical examination of the question in the Japanese context was conducted by Nawata (2014), published in the *Japanese Psychological Research*, who analysed self-reported personality data from 10,623 participants measured on the Big Five personality dimensions (openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism). The study found no statistically significant correlations between blood type and any Big Five dimension. Importantly, Nawata also documented a methodological pathology in earlier pro-ketsueki-gata studies: prior researchers had asked participants to self-report blood-type-consistent traits, a design confounded by the fact that Japanese respondents who know their blood type have been exposed to predictions about it since childhood and unconsciously (or consciously) align their self-description with the expected profile.

Wu et al. (2021), writing in the *Personality and Individual Differences*, conducted a cross-cultural meta-analysis synthesising data from fourteen independent studies sampling from Japan, South Korea, Taiwan, and Chinese mainland populations—a combined dataset exceeding 600,000 participants—and found an aggregate correlation between blood type and personality measures effectively indistinguishable from zero (mean r < 0.01 across all comparisons). The statistical power of this dataset was sufficient to detect correlations well below the practical significance threshold; the finding is not that the effect is small but that it is absent.

The biological basis proposed for blood-type personality is physiologically incoherent. The ABO blood group system determines which glycoproteins are expressed on the surface of red blood cells—specifically, which carbohydrate chains are attached to cell-surface antigens by the enzymes encoded by the ABO gene on chromosome 9. These proteins affect transfusion compatibility, certain aspects of coagulation, and susceptibility to particular infectious agents. There is no established biochemical pathway by which the expression of cell-surface carbohydrates on erythrocytes could plausibly influence synaptic signalling patterns, neurotransmitter receptor distributions, cortical architecture, or any other substrate of personality. Landsteiner (1900, *Wiener Klinische Wochenschrift*), the discoverer of the ABO system, never suggested personality implications; these were grafted onto his purely haematological findings by later lay interpreters.

Genetic evidence provides an additional line of refutation. The ABO gene's variation accounts for a tiny fraction of overall human genetic diversity. By contrast, well-supported genetic associations with personality traits—primarily from genome-wide association studies—implicate hundreds of loci distributed across the genome affecting neurotransmitter systems, neuroendocrine pathways, and developmental trajectories. Okbay et al. (2016), in *Nature Genetics*, identified 18 genetic variants with reproducible associations with neuroticism using a sample of 300,000 individuals; none involved the ABO locus. If blood type influenced personality, the mechanism would need to be specified and biologically plausible—and no such mechanism has ever been articulated beyond the level of cultural assertion.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemic novelty of the blood-type case lies in what it reveals about *institutional pseudoscience*: the process by which a claim with no scientific foundation acquires social reality and economic infrastructure through recursive institutional adoption rather than through evidence accumulation.

Unlike most health myths, which begin in popular culture and occasionally acquire casual professional endorsement, blood-type personality theory has at various points been adopted into formal institutional practice—school curricula, corporate HR protocols, national sports coaching programmes—creating a feedback loop that sociologists of knowledge would describe as performative ontology: blood-type categories become "real" in their consequences even as they remain fictitious in their biological claims.

The theory's origin reveals the mechanism. Furukawa Takeji, a middle school teacher with no training in psychology or haematology, proposed in a 1927 paper in the journal *Jinshu Eisei* (Race Hygiene) that blood type differences might explain temperamental variation between two student groups he had observed. His methodology was rudimentary and his sample sizes tiny; his conclusion was speculative. The journal in which he published was explicit about its eugenicist orientation—blood-type typing was framed as a tool for national character analysis relevant to military and colonial administration. The theory disappeared from scientific discourse after academic psychologists failed to replicate his findings and the post-war denazification of Japanese science discredited the racial-eugenicist framing.

The theory's revival in the 1970s by journalist Nomi Masahiko occurred in a completely different discursive context—the Japanese self-help boom—and shed the original eugenic framing entirely. Nomi presented blood type not as racial category but as individual typology, analogous to Myers-Briggs or the newly popular idea of astrological sun signs. His books became bestsellers by offering a simple, memorable framework for self-understanding that mapped onto existing cultural schema. The theory's internal logic became circular and self-sealing: type B people who did not behave impulsively were exceptions explained by other blood-type combinations in their family; type A people who were not anxious had "compensated" through self-development. Every life story could be fitted post-hoc.

The Barnum effect—documented by Forer (1949) in the *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology* using early personality feedback experiments—is central to why ketsueki-gata descriptions feel accurate. Forer showed experimental participants a fabricated "personalised" personality analysis and found that participants rated it as highly accurate; the analysis had been identical for all participants and was composed of statements broad enough to apply to virtually anyone. Ketsueki-gata descriptions share this quality: type O's "strong leadership drive but occasional insensitivity" can be mapped onto almost any autobiographical narrative by a reader motivated to confirm the prediction.

The social harm produced by blood-type discrimination—*burahara* (blood harassment)—demonstrates that the cultural adoption of a false taxonomy has real distributional effects. Persons typed as B have historically faced the most severe social prejudice in Japan, being stereotyped as selfish and irresponsible. Tsuchimine et al. (2015), surveying 557 Japanese medical workers in *BMC Research Notes*, found that self-identified type B individuals reported significantly higher levels of experienced social discrimination than other blood types, despite no objective personality differences. The harm is not produced by a real biological difference but by a socially enforced expectation that functions as a self-fulfilling prophecy: individuals anticipated to be irresponsible may be excluded from responsibilities that would have allowed them to demonstrate reliability.

## 4. Verdict

**REFUTED:** Blood type does not determine personality. The claim fails every relevant standard of scientific evaluation—it lacks an identified biological mechanism, and every well-powered empirical study testing it has produced null or near-null results. The collective evidence from studies involving hundreds of thousands of participants across multiple East Asian countries establishes with high statistical confidence that ABO blood type provides no predictive information about personality measured on validated dimensions.

The phenomenon is, however, phenomenologically real in its social consequences. The theory shapes educational placement decisions, employment screening, dating behaviour, and media representation in Japan and South Korea in ways that produce genuine distributional harms—particularly for individuals typed as B—independent of any biological basis. This makes it a case study in how scientifically invalid constructs acquire social ontology through institutional adoption, media saturation, and confirmation bias.

The blood-type personality claim sits in the same epistemic category as racial and gender essentialism: assertions that a biologically defined category predicts complex behavioural traits, sustained by cultural reinforcement and motivated reasoning, refuted by science, and harmful when institutionalised. The specifically Japanese historical context—military eugenics origins, post-war rebranding as self-help entertainment—provides an instructive case of how the same false premise can migrate between ideological frameworks while retaining its core categorical structure.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The blood-type personality case intersects with a significant body of social science literature on **typological thinking**—the human cognitive tendency to sort continuous individual variation into discrete, essentialised categories. Blood type fits this cognitive preference exceptionally well because it is both discrete (four categories with clean boundaries) and already known to most people, having been established through routine medical practice. Unlike personality traits, which require self-report instruments to measure, blood type is an objective, verifiable datum—which creates in lay reasoning an unwarranted inference that a reliably measured predictor variable must be reliably related to the outcome of interest.

Psychological research on essentialism—reviewed by Haslam (2000) in *Psychological Bulletin*—documents that people across cultures tend to attribute underlying essences to social categories, assuming that members of a category share a hidden nature that explains their observable similarities. Blood type activates this essentialist attributional template: the shared "nature" is literalised as the blood itself. The metaphorical resonance of blood as a carrier of hereditary essence—deeply embedded in global folklore about kinship, ancestry, and identity—makes blood-type personality theory feel intuitively correct in ways that a claim based on, say, cerumen type or iris pigmentation would not.

The theory's cross-cultural gradient is analytically useful. Blood-type personality beliefs are intense in Japan and South Korea, present but weaker in Taiwan and Hong Kong, largely absent in mainland Chinese popular culture despite identical blood type distributions, and negligible in European populations with identical ABO genetics. This gradient maps to cultural exposure and media transmission, not to any biological substrate. If blood type influenced personality, the personality distributions should be similar across all populations with similar blood type frequencies—which they are not, and could not be using blood type as the causal variable.

The comparison with Western astrology is instructive but must not be pressed too far. Both systems assign personality types based on a categorical variable (birth month; blood type); both lack biological plausibility; both survive through circular reasoning and Barnum-effect descriptions; both produce social harms when used in employment or educational discrimination. The differences are also significant: astrology is generally not taken seriously by Japanese or Korean employers, whereas blood-type assessments have been used institutionally; astrology's cultural context is explicitly mystical rather than biological, whereas blood-type theory appropriates medical language and laboratory values to construct a false scientific veneer.

The persistence of the belief in formally educated populations in technologically sophisticated societies reveals a limitation in standard models of science literacy. The common assumption is that scientific education displaces pre-scientific folk psychology—that knowledge of evolution displaces creationism, that knowledge of virology displaces miasma theory. The Japanese case suggests instead that scientific and pseudoscientific frameworks can coexist stably in the same cultural space, with scientific knowledge quarantined to professional domains while folk personality taxonomy operates in everyday social navigation. This compartmentalisation may be particularly stable when the pseudoscientific framework is reinforced by consumption culture, media entertainment, and face-to-face social norms rather than by explicit doctrinal teaching.

From a public health perspective, the most concerning applications of the blood-type belief system are those involving children. When children are assigned to classrooms or pedagogical programmes based on blood type, they are exposed from early schooling to an institutionalised fiction that may function as labelling: children told they are disorganised (type B) or lacking creativity (type A) may adjust their self-concept accordingly. Research in educational psychology on teacher expectancy effects—most influentially by Rosenthal and Jacobson (1968) in *Pygmalion in the Classroom*—documents that categorical labels, even false ones, can produce lasting effects on measured academic performance by altering the social environment and self-attribution patterns of labelled students.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The blood-type personality claim has not been addressed in a dedicated Snopes article as of mid-2026, despite its widespread prevalence and its documented use in institutional discrimination. This represents a significant gap in the Western English-language fact-checking ecosystem, one that likely reflects the cultural distance between the belief system's primary home (Japan, South Korea) and the Anglophone audiences that major fact-checking outlets primarily serve.

Science journalism in English has periodically noted the lack of evidence for blood-type personality—articles in outlets including *The Atlantic*, *Scientific American Mind*, and the *BBC Future* series have summarised the negative findings from studies by Nawata and others. These articles tend to take a sympathetic ethnographic tone, framing the phenomenon as a curious cultural practice rather than a harmful pseudoscientific belief system with traceable social costs. This framing may inadvertently reinforce the compartmentalisation effect described above: when Western coverage of ketsueki-gata treats it as colourful exoticism rather than institutionalised misinformation, it implicitly applies a double standard to pseudoscience in non-Western cultural contexts.

The fact-checking gap is also partly structural. Standard Western fact-checking ecosystems are optimised for evaluating discrete, verifiable factual claims—did a politician say X; did a study find Y—rather than for evaluating the validity of embedded cultural belief systems that function as background assumptions rather than explicit assertions. When no one is actively claiming "blood type determines personality" in a falsifiable, debatable proposition—when it is simply assumed and acted upon—the triggers that activate professional fact-checking rarely fire. Addressing this kind of infrastructural belief requires a different register of intervention: not debunking a specific viral claim but engaging with an entire cognitive and institutional apparatus. That is harder to do within standard fact-checking formats and has not yet been systematically attempted for the blood-type personality system.
