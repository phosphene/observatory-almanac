# Can You Really Blow Bubbles from the Sap of a Plant? The Truth About *Jatropha curcas*

## 1. The Claim

In November 2020, a video posted to the Reddit community r/NatureIsFuckingLit by user @bravebroccoli showed a woman snapping the stem of a green, leafy plant, placing the broken end to her lips, and blowing a stream of translucent, soap-like bubbles into the tropical air. The clip accumulated over 106,000 views within days and prompted the predictable spectrum of internet reactions: delight, skepticism, and outright accusations of fakery. The implicit claim was simple — the stem of this plant contains a sap so naturally sudsy that it can substitute for commercial bubble solution straight out of the ground.

Snopes journalist Madison Dapcevich investigated the claim on November 21, 2020, and rated it **TRUE**. The plant in question is *Jatropha curcas* L. (family Euphorbiaceae), commonly called the Barbados Nut, a semi-evergreen shrub-to-tree species native to Mexico, Central America, and the Caribbean. Its latex-like sap is rich in saponins — naturally occurring glycosides that lower the surface tension of water in much the same way commercial soap does — and is, indeed, capable of forming bubbles when air is blown through a film of it.

The phenomenon was not new to 2020. Similar "bubble plant" videos had circulated online since at least 2016, and ethnobotanical records document the practice far earlier. What was new was the viral velocity: a single Reddit post transformed a centuries-old piece of folk knowledge into a piece of digital content that millions of people encountered without any accompanying botanical context. The Truth Vault exists to supply that context.

## 2. What's Actually True: The Sap, the Saponins, and *Jatropha curcas* Biology

### Taxonomy and Distribution

*Jatropha curcas* belongs to the family Euphorbiaceae, one of the largest and most economically important flowering-plant families on Earth. The Euphorbiaceae encompass more than 7,500 species across roughly 300 genera, including cassava (*Manihot esculenta*), the rubber tree (*Hevea brasiliensis*), and the castor-oil plant (*Ricinus communis*) (Webster, 1994, *Annals of the Missouri Botanical Garden*). The genus *Jatropha* itself contains more than 175 recognized species of shrubs and small trees distributed across tropical and subtropical regions worldwide (Dehgan & Webster, 1979, *University of California Publications in Botany*).

*Jatropha curcas* is native to tropical regions of Central America, Mexico, and the Caribbean, but centuries of human cultivation have carried it to Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, India, and beyond (Heller, 1996, *Promoting the Conservation and Use of Underutilized and Neglected Crops*, International Plant Genetic Resources Institute). It is a semi-evergreen, drought-deciduous shrub or small tree that can reach heights of up to 20 feet (approximately 6 meters) under favorable conditions, though specimens in marginal soils often remain smaller. It thrives on degraded land, tolerates poor soils, and establishes readily in waste areas of warm climates — characteristics that have made it a candidate for cultivation in areas unsuitable for food crops.

### The Sap

The plant produces a viscid, greenish-yellow latex that exudes freely when stems or leaves are broken. This sap is a complex biological fluid containing lipids, proteins, terpenes, alkaloids, and — crucially for the bubble phenomenon — high concentrations of saponins (Devappa, Makkar, & Becker, 2011, *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*). Emily Magnaghi, botany collections manager at the California Academy of Sciences in San Francisco, confirmed the mechanism to Snopes: "It seems to be full of saponins which are the sudsy part of soap. I knew the plant for its biofuel uses. The seeds are high in oil content and can be processed into biofuel and it grows easily in waste areas of warm climates."

The Botanical Dermatology Database, a digitized version of a comprehensive 1979 reference work on plants and skin (Mitchell & Rook, 1979, *Botanical Dermatology: Plants and Plant Products Injurious to the Skin*), provides one of the earliest explicit cross-cultural references to the bubble-blowing practice: "The greenish, viscid juice and pounded leaves are rubefacient and styptic and can cause inflammation of the eyes. The sap can cause dermatitis but is used by children in Malaysia and in Africa for blowing bubbles." This single sentence encapsulates the dual nature of *Jatropha curcas*: a plant whose chemistry is simultaneously a source of childhood play and a genuine dermatological hazard.

### Morphology and Growth Habit

The plant bears alternate, palmately lobed leaves typically 10–15 cm in diameter, and produces small greenish-yellow flowers that develop into trilocular ellipsoidal fruits containing two or three large, dark seeds. The bark is smooth and pale, and the branching pattern is sympodial, giving the plant a somewhat irregular, spreading canopy form (Openshaw, 2000, *Biomass and Bioenergy*). The latex canals run throughout the cortex and phloem, meaning that breaking almost any green stem or young branch will produce the characteristic saponin-rich exudate. The ease of sap extraction is precisely what makes the bubble trick so reproducible: no special preparation, fermentation, or processing is required. You snap a stem, you blow.

## 3. The Sense of Wonder and Its Epistemology

### Why We Don't Believe Plants Can Be This Interesting

The most telling feature of the 2020 Reddit thread was not the video itself but the comment section beneath it. A substantial proportion of respondents expressed disbelief or demanded evidence of staging. The intuition animating these responses was something like: *bubbles are a human artifact — they come from factories, from bottles of soapy water, from plastic wands. Nature doesn't do this.* The skepticism was, in its own way, reasonable. It reflected a heuristic that serves us well in an information environment saturated with manipulated content: if something looks too much like a trick, it probably is.

But this heuristic, which we might call the **artificiality bias**, fails systematically when applied to the plant kingdom. Plants have had hundreds of millions of years of evolution to produce chemical novelty that routinely exceeds what intuition prepares us for. The carnivorous trapping mechanisms of *Dionaea muscipula* (the Venus flytrap), the explosive seed dispersal of *Impatiens* species, the bioluminescent root fungi symbioses of certain forest floors — all of these seem, on first encounter, more like engineered devices than evolved organisms. The philosopher of biology Daniel Dennett described this phenomenon as "competence without comprehension" — the capacity of evolutionary processes to generate functional complexity that mimics, and often surpasses, deliberate design (Dennett, 2017, *From Bacteria to Bach and Back*, W. W. Norton).

The epistemological problem is compounded by what cognitive scientists call **plant blindness** (Wandersee & Schussler, 1999, *Plant Science Bulletin*). Humans are perceptually and cognitively biased toward noticing animals over plants. We process animal motion, animal faces, and animal behavior with dedicated neural circuitry that has no plant equivalent. Plants, being sessile, photosynthetic, and mostly green, recede into the background of our perceptual field. The consequence is that even well-educated adults often hold impoverished mental models of plant capabilities. When a plant does something visually striking — like producing soap — the event collides with an implicit expectation that plants are passive, inert backdrops to the animal drama of the world.

### The Credibility Gap Between Viral Content and Botanical Reality

There is a structural tension between how botanical knowledge has historically been transmitted and how internet virality works. Ethnobotanical knowledge — the accumulated wisdom of indigenous and local communities about plant uses — is typically transmitted slowly, orally, within networks of trust built over generations (Balick & Cox, 1996, *Plants, People, and Culture: The Science of Ethnobotany*, Scientific American Library). A Malaysian child learns to blow bubbles from *Jatropha* sap because her grandmother showed her, who was shown by her grandmother, and so on across a chain of embodied, place-specific knowledge.

Viral content, by contrast, arrives without provenance. A 15-second video clip provides no taxonomic identification, no explanation of the underlying chemistry, no warning about potential dermatitis, and no cultural context. The viewer is left to adjudicate the claim using only visual evidence and prior expectations — and for most viewers, those prior expectations do not include "plants can make soap." The result is a credibility gap: the claim is true, but the medium through which it arrives strips away precisely the contextual information that would make it believable.

This dynamic is not unique to bubble-blowing plants. It recurs wherever genuinely remarkable natural phenomena enter the viral content ecosystem: the "watermelon snow" caused by *Chlamydomonas nivalis* algae, the "rainbow eucalyptus" (*Eucalyptus deglupta*) whose bark peels in technicolor strips, the "living stones" of the genus *Lithops* that mimic pebbles so perfectly that they are invisible until they flower. In each case, the phenomenon is real, well-documented in the botanical literature, and known to local communities for generations — yet it reliably elicits disbelief when encountered as a decontextualized internet image or video. The epistemological lesson is that our skepticism calibration for natural phenomena is systematically miscalibrated. We are too credulous toward claims about technology ("there's an app for that" rarely surprises anyone) and too skeptical toward claims about biology ("there's a plant for that" almost always does).

### Recalibrating Wonder

The philosopher and naturalist Rachel Carson argued in *The Sense of Wonder* (1965, Harper & Row) that the capacity to be astonished by nature is not a childish naïveté to be outgrown but an epistemic virtue to be cultivated. The *Jatropha* bubble is a small but vivid case study in this argument. The fact that a tropical shrub produces a latex so rich in natural surfactants that children on three continents have independently discovered they can blow bubbles with it is not a parlor trick. It is a window into the staggering chemical diversity of the plant kingdom — a diversity that has, among other things, given us aspirin (from *Salix* bark), quinine (from *Cinchona* bark), and rubber (from *Hevea* latex). The bubble is not the anomaly. Our surprise at it is.

## 4. The Chemistry Behind the Bubbles

### Saponins: Nature's Surfactants

The bubble-forming capacity of *Jatropha curcas* sap derives primarily from its saponin content. Saponins are a large and structurally diverse class of plant secondary metabolites characterized by a hydrophobic aglycone (either a triterpenoid or a steroidal nucleus) linked to one or more hydrophilic sugar chains (Vincken, Heng, de Groot, & Gruppen, 2007, *Phytochemistry*). This amphiphilic molecular architecture — one end water-loving, the other water-repelling — is the defining feature of all surfactants, and it is precisely the property that allows saponins to lower the surface tension of aqueous solutions and stabilize thin films of liquid.

When you dip a plastic wand into commercial bubble solution and blow, you are exploiting a synthetic surfactant (usually sodium lauryl sulfate or a related compound) to create a thin aqueous film whose surface tension is low enough to stretch into a sphere without immediately collapsing. When a child in Malaysia blows through a broken *Jatropha* stem, the same physics is at work, but the surfactant is endogenous — produced by the plant's own secondary metabolism and delivered in a matrix of latex, water, and assorted organic compounds (Oskoueian et al., 2011, *International Journal of Molecular Sciences*).

### Saponin Diversity in the Euphorbiaceae

The Euphorbiaceae are particularly prolific producers of secondary metabolites, including terpenes, alkaloids, phenolics, and saponins. *Jatropha curcas* produces several classes of saponins, with the triterpenoid saponins being most relevant to the bubble phenomenon. Makkar, Becker, Sporer, and Wink (1997, *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*) identified phorbol esters and saponins as the principal bioactive compounds in *Jatropha* seeds and latex, noting that saponin concentrations in the plant can vary considerably depending on genotype, growing conditions, and tissue type.

The functional role of saponins in the plant is primarily defensive. Their bitter taste and hemolytic activity deter herbivory, and their antifungal properties provide protection against soil-borne pathogens (Augustin, Kuzina, Andersen, & Bak, 2011, *Phytochemistry*). The bubble-forming property is, from the plant's perspective, an incidental consequence of a chemical defense strategy — an evolutionary spandrel, to borrow Stephen Jay Gould's architectural metaphor (Gould & Lewontin, 1979, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*). The plant did not evolve saponins so that children could blow bubbles. It evolved saponins to avoid being eaten, and the bubbles are a delightful accident of molecular geometry.

### Surface Tension, Film Stability, and Bubble Physics

For a bubble to form and persist, three conditions must be met: the surface tension of the liquid must be substantially lower than that of pure water (approximately 72 mN/m at 25°C); the film must be elastic enough to stretch without rupturing; and evaporation must be slow enough to allow the bubble to persist for at least a few seconds (Isenberg, 1992, *The Science of Soap Films and Soap Bubbles*, Dover Publications). Saponin solutions typically reduce surface tension to approximately 30–40 mN/m, comparable to commercial soap solutions (Stanimirova et al., 2011, *Langmuir*). The additional organic compounds in *Jatropha* latex — including proteins and polysaccharides — likely contribute to film viscosity and elasticity, improving bubble stability beyond what a pure saponin-water solution would achieve.

## 5. Biofuel, Toxicity, and the Full Profile of *Jatropha curcas*

### The Biofuel Promise

*Jatropha curcas* attracted intense global attention in the first decade of the 21st century as a potential biofuel feedstock. The seeds contain 30–40% oil by weight, and this oil can be transesterified into biodiesel or processed into bio-jet fuel with properties comparable to petroleum-derived diesel (Achten et al., 2008, *Biomass and Bioenergy*). The plant's tolerance for marginal land, its drought resistance, and its inedibility to livestock (reducing competition with food crops) made it a seemingly ideal candidate for sustainable fuel production in developing countries.

India, Mozambique, Tanzania, and several Southeast Asian nations launched large-scale *Jatropha* plantation programs in the mid-2000s, often with significant governmental and international development support (Kant & Wu, 2011, *Applied Energy*). The results were mixed. Yields on degraded land proved lower than projected, requiring more water and nutrient inputs than initial optimistic models suggested. The economics of *Jatropha* biodiesel remained challenging without subsidies, and many plantation projects were abandoned or scaled back by 2015 (Openshaw, 2000, *Biomass and Bioenergy*; Achten et al., 2010, *Biofuels, Bioproducts and Biorefining*). Nevertheless, research into improved cultivars, optimized agronomic practices, and integrated land-use systems continues, and *Jatropha* remains a plant of considerable economic and environmental interest.

### Toxicity: The Darker Side of the Latex

The same chemical richness that makes *Jatropha curcas* a source of bubble-blowing sap also makes it genuinely dangerous. The seeds contain curcin, a ribosome-inactivating protein structurally related to ricin (from *Ricinus communis*, another Euphorbiaceae member), as well as phorbol esters — potent tumor-promoting diterpenes that cause severe gastrointestinal distress and can be fatal if ingested in sufficient quantities (Devappa, Makkar, & Becker, 2010, *Food and Chemical Toxicology*). Cases of *Jatropha* seed poisoning, particularly among children who mistake the seeds for edible nuts, have been documented in India, Mozambique, and Central America (Abdu-Aguye, Alhassan, Bashir, & Kela, 1986, *Human Toxicology*).

The sap itself, while not as acutely dangerous as the seeds, is a documented dermatological irritant and contact allergen. Mitchell and Rook (1979) classified the latex as rubefacient (causing skin reddening) and styptic (capable of stopping bleeding), noting its capacity to cause contact dermatitis and eye inflammation. Toxicity varies from tree to tree, influenced by genotype, environmental conditions, and the specific tissue from which the sap is extracted. This variability means that the same practice — blowing bubbles from a broken stem — may be harmless with one tree and irritating with another, a nuance that no 15-second video conveys.

The dual-use nature of *Jatropha* chemistry exemplifies a broader principle in ethnobotany: the most useful plants are often the most dangerous ones. The therapeutic window between "folk remedy" and "poison" is frequently narrow, and traditional knowledge systems encode not only which plants to use but which parts, at what dose, at what season, and with what preparation — a granularity of knowledge that is lost when a practice migrates from embodied folk tradition to viral internet content (Balick & Cox, 1996).

### Other Traditional Uses

Beyond bubbles and biofuel, *Jatropha curcas* has a long ethnobotanical resume. The latex has been used traditionally as a hemostatic agent for wounds, a treatment for skin infections, and a purgative medicine — though the last application is dangerous given the seed toxicity (Heller, 1996). The oil has been used for soap-making (contributing to the common name "physic nut" and its association with cleanliness), lamp fuel, and lubrication. The press-cake remaining after oil extraction is rich in nitrogen and has been investigated as an organic fertilizer, provided the toxic phorbol esters are first deactivated (Makkar, Becker, Sporer, & Wink, 1997). In some regions, the plant is used as a living fence or boundary marker, exploiting its rapid growth and the reluctance of livestock to browse its toxic foliage.

## 6. Verdict and Significance

**Verdict: TRUE.** The stem of *Jatropha curcas* contains a saponin-rich sap that can indeed be used to blow bubbles, exactly as depicted in the viral November 2020 Reddit video. The phenomenon is well-documented in both the ethnobotanical literature and the phytochemical research record. Children in Malaysia, Africa, Central America, and elsewhere have been blowing bubbles from this plant's latex for generations — long before the internet existed to be astonished by it.

### What the Claim Tells Us

The *Jatropha* bubble story is, at one level, trivially small — a fun fact, a curiosity, a piece of nature content optimized for a few seconds of scrolling engagement. At another level, it is a case study in several larger phenomena that the Truth Vault is designed to illuminate.

**First**, it demonstrates the depth and resilience of ethnobotanical knowledge. The bubble-blowing practice appears in ethnographic records spanning at least three continents and was documented in a dermatological reference text as early as 1979. This knowledge did not originate with Reddit, and it will not disappear when the algorithm moves on.

**Second**, it illustrates the chemical sophistication of the plant kingdom. The same saponins that enable a child's game also serve as the plant's defense against herbivores, and the same seeds that produce bubble-forming latex also contain compounds lethal enough to hospitalize or kill. Reducing *Jatropha curcas* to either "bubble plant" or "poison tree" is a category error; it is both, and its full profile resists simplification.

**Third**, it exposes the calibration failures in our collective epistemology of nature. We live in an era of sophisticated digital manipulation, and our skepticism toward viral content is often well-placed. But that skepticism must be tempered by a recognition that the natural world routinely produces phenomena that feel implausible precisely because our baseline expectations of biological capability are too low. Plants synthesize their own soap. They manufacture their own insecticides, their own sunscreen, their own antifreeze. The error is not in believing the bubble video. The error is in having a prior model of plants so impoverished that the video seems unbelievable.

The *Jatropha* bubble is true. And the truth, in this case, is more interesting than the skepticism it provoked.
