# 'Boy Pets Creature' Ad Misleads. Here's the Original Video


## 1. The Claim

In early January 2022, a series of online advertisements began circulating with variations of the headline: "Boy Pets Creature Before Realizing What It Is," "Boy Pets Creature, Then Suddenly Realizes What He's Touching," and "You'll Never Guess The Sea Creature This Young Boy Befriended." Each advertisement featured the same image: a photograph of a young boy crouching at the edge of what appeared to be a harbour wall or dock, his hand extended toward a large flat creature in the water below.

The advertisements were distributed across multiple platforms through standard display advertising networks, appearing adjacent to news content, social media feeds, and entertainment websites. They generated sufficient viewer curiosity — and sufficient social media response, including apparent Reddit threads asking for explanation — to register as a notable viral phenomenon. Users who clicked through were taken to an eighty-page slideshow article whose headline read: "Boy Pets Something Unknown That Shocked Everyone."

The article claimed the boy was on vacation, had not initially realized what he was touching, named a specific resort where the family was supposedly staying, and elaborated a dramatic narrative of discovery and shock across its eighty pages. Readers who sat through the full eighty pages — each page generating advertising revenue for the publisher — received this fabricated narrative wrapped around a genuine underlying image.

Snopes rated the claim "Mixture" — the image was real, but the narrative promoted by the ads and the slideshow article was essentially fabricated. This is an accurate verdict, and Jordan Liles' investigation produced the original source material that definitively traced the image to its actual context. However, the story is far richer as a case study in advertising fraud, image decontextualisation, and manufactured viral content than the "Mixture" verdict alone conveys.


## 2. What's Actually True

The photograph displayed in the advertisement is genuine. It is a screenshot from a YouTube video uploaded in 2013 by Henner Riemenschneider of the production company Bilderwerkstatt Gekko-Vision La Gomera. The video, titled "Manta, Manta y Niños 2013 - La Gomera," was filmed on August 8, 2013, in the port of Vueltas, in the Valle Gran Rey area of the island of La Gomera, one of the eight main islands of Spain's Canary Islands archipelago. As of January 2022, the video had been viewed approximately 59 million times on YouTube, indicating it was far from obscure — yet its viral history apparently failed to prevent its image from being stripped of context and repurposed in misleading advertisements years later.

The video's German-language caption, translated via Google, provides detailed context. The port of Vueltas is an old traditional fishing harbour where boats still operate and fish scraps are regularly deposited. The waters at the harbour entrance attract rays that have learned to position themselves near the landing ladders where fish are processed. A boy named Joel and his friends regularly visited the harbour and fed the rays, which apparently had become habituated to human presence at this specific location. The video captured what the caption called "a small morning show" — Joel feeding what the video caption describes as a large stingray while the animal was also described by Riemenschneider as possibly a manta ray, eagle ray, or butterfly ray (*Gymnura* species).

The identification of the ray species is worth briefly addressing. The video caption mentions stingrays, eagle rays, and butterfly rays among the species that visit the harbour. Stingrays and butterfly rays (*Gymnura*) are members of the order Myliobatiformes and are found in subtropical Atlantic waters including around the Canary Islands. Eagle rays (*Myliobatidae*) are also present in Canarian waters. Without a definitive close-up photograph with visible diagnostic features, the precise species identification is uncertain, though the large flat profile visible in the screenshot is consistent with a large *Dasyatis* or *Gymnura* species. None of these species are considered dangerous to humans under normal interaction conditions; larger stingray species (*Dasyatis pastinaca*, the common stingray of European Atlantic and Mediterranean waters) are capable of inflicting a painful sting with their tail-mounted barb if threatened or accidentally stepped on, but the rays in Vueltas harbour, habituated to human contact, were clearly behaving in a non-defensive manner.

The advertising narrative — the fabricated overlay — is false in virtually every particular: there was no "vacation," no "resort," no moment of sudden shock, no one who "didn't realize what they were touching." Joel knew exactly what the animal was and had, by the video caption's account, been feeding it regularly. The specificity of the narrative fabrication (naming a resort, describing the family's plans for the day) is notable because it appears calculated to generate the kind of immersive, semi-plausible story that tricks readers into continued engagement. The eighty-page slideshow format is designed specifically to monetise this engagement across as many page loads as possible.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

Snopes' investigation successfully identified the original source of the image and delivered the core correction: the picture is real, the narrative is fabricated. This is genuinely valuable. But the piece does not deeply analyse the mechanism by which the deception was constructed or why it works effectively on its intended audience. Understanding that mechanism has broader significance for media literacy.

**The image decontextualisation pipeline.** The sequence here follows a pattern that recurs across many viral image scams. An authentic image or video accumulates genuine organic views and social sharing based on its actual content. Years later, an operator in the advertising arbitrage ecosystem detects the image's proven engagement history (high view count, repeat shares) and concludes it has reliable attention-generation potential. The image is extracted from its original context and paired with a fabricated narrative tailored to the format (slideshow with advertising-supported pages) that maximises monetisable engagement. The fabricated narrative is calibrated to exploit the specific ambiguities in the image: the image shows a boy near an unidentified creature, which makes "he didn't know what it was" a plausible-sounding claim even though it is false.

This pipeline exploits what might be called **epistemic overcrediting of photographs**: the widespread assumption that photographs are uniquely reliable as evidence, such that an authentic photograph serves as implicit validation of any narrative associated with it. The advertisement does not fabricate a photograph — it appropriates a genuine one — and then parasitically borrows the authenticity of the image to lend credibility to a completely fictional story. This is technically more sophisticated than pure fabrication because it is harder to debunk; the fact-checker who verifies "the photograph is real" has only done half the necessary work.

**Advertising arbitrage as an information ecology problem.** The economic engine driving the "boy pets creature" ad is advertising arbitrage (sometimes called "clickbait arbitrage" or "traffic arbitrage"). The operator pays a relatively low cost-per-click to display the advertisement on ad networks (often lower-quality inventory on content sites and social platforms), then generates ad revenue from the eighty pages of the slideshow article that the click leads to. The economic logic is: (revenue from 80 page loads × CPM for the slideshow's ad inventory) > (cost of the initial advertisement click). When this arithmetic works out, the operator profits. The eighty-page format is not arbitrary — it is mathematically calibrated to the revenue differential between premium display advertising (which funds news sites that place the initial ad) and low-quality remnant inventory (which monetises the slideshow).

Academic analysis of the clickbait arbitrage ecosystem has documented its structural features: content is systematically optimised not for informational value but for cost-effective engagement generation, which rewards emotional arousal, mystery framing, and artificial length (Amazeen & Wojdynski, 2018, "Reducing Native Advertising Deception: Revisiting the Interplay of Persuasion Knowledge and Exemplification as a Dual Process Theory," *Mass Communication and Society*, 21(2), 166–191). The "world's biggest" and "what is this creature?" framings are canonical exemplars of the genre because they exploit the human uncertainty aversion — the discomfort of not knowing the answer — that drives completion of slideshows even when each individual page is unsatisfying.

**The Canary Islands as context.** The specific location — the port of Vueltas in Valle Gran Rey, La Gomera — is not merely a geographic detail. La Gomera is part of the Canary Islands, an autonomous community of Spain located approximately 100 kilometres off the northwest coast of Africa. The islands are positioned in the subtropical northeast Atlantic, in the warm waters of the Canary Current, and the marine environment supports a rich elasmobranch fauna including multiple ray species. The habituation of rays to the fishing harbour in Vueltas is a documented local phenomenon that has been filmed multiple times; the "boy pets creature" video was not an isolated event but part of an ongoing documented pattern of rays congregating at the harbour entrance (Riemenschneider, 2013, video caption, YouTube). This is ecologically coherent: ray habituation to provisioning sites at fishing harbours is known from multiple locations globally, including the famous Stingray City in the Cayman Islands where southern stingrays have been provisioned and touched by tourists for decades (Semeniuk & Rothley, 2008, "Costs of Group-Living for a Normally Solitary Forager: Effects of Provisioning Tourism on Southern Stingrays *Dasyatis americana*," *Journal of Applied Ecology*, 45(2), 544–553).


## 4. The Eighty-Page Slideshow Format and Reader Psychology

The eighty-page slideshow format deserves analysis as a deliberate design choice with specific psychological targets. Standard long-form journalism creates reader investment through narrative arc, emotional engagement, and informational development over a sustained reading experience. The arbitrage slideshow mimics the surface appearance of journalism (headline, introductory paragraph, narrative progression) while hollowing out the informational content and replacing it with artificial cliffhangers at the bottom of each page.

Research on successive approximation and variable reward schedules in digital behaviour suggests that the "click to next page" action in slideshow formats functions analogously to slot machine mechanics: each click promises a resolution that is deferred to the next page, creating a low-intensity compulsion to continue (Fogg, 2003, *Persuasive Technology: Using Computers to Change What We Think and Do*, Morgan Kaufmann). The resolution never quite arrives, because the operator's commercial interest lies in maximising page count rather than delivering informational closure.

The eighty-page format is unusually extreme — most clickbait slideshows run 20–40 pages. This implies either that the revenue per click was high enough to justify such length, or that testing showed eighty pages generated more total revenue than shorter formats despite higher abandonment rates at the extreme length. Either interpretation reveals the degree to which these articles are engineered commercial products whose design parameters are economically optimised rather than editorially determined.

Users who complete the full eighty pages receive, at the end, essentially the information that Snopes was able to deliver in three paragraphs: the picture shows a boy feeding a ray at a harbour in the Canary Islands. The elaborate fabricated narrative — the resort, the family's plans, the "shock" — disappears across the journey as the article gradually reveals the real context, each revelation deferred just long enough to justify another page load. The article is architectural deception: not technically lying, but precisely managing the release of truthful information to maximise commercial value.


## 5. Internet Archaeology and Source Recovery

Snopes' investigation is a valuable example of what might be called **internet archaeology** — the practice of tracing viral content back through layers of secondary and tertiary sharing to identify the original source. The "boy pets creature" case involved a five-year lag between the original 2013 video (which had organically accumulated 59 million views over those years) and the 2022 advertisement campaign. This lag meant the image had become sufficiently common in social memory to be recognizable, but sufficiently detached from its original context that new viewers encountering it through the advertisement had no immediate access to the original source.

The investigation required identifying the original YouTube video, reading its German-language caption, verifying the geographical location through cross-referencing with images of the harbour in Vueltas, Valle Gran Rey, and confirming that the photographic credit (Henner Riemenschneider / Bilderwerkstatt Gekko-Vision La Gomera) was consistent with the video metadata. This is methodologically straightforward once the source video is found, but finding it requires reverse image search capability and familiarity with the viral-image tracking tools that professional fact-checkers use regularly.

What the investigation confirms is important for media literacy: most viral images do not emerge from nowhere. They have documentable origins and original contexts. The time and methodological competence required to perform source recovery — to trace an image from its decontextualised viral state back to its original setting — is non-trivial for an ordinary internet user, but is within the capabilities of anyone who has access to reverse image search tools (TinEye, Google Images, Yandex Images) and the patience to follow the trail. The Snopes investigation demonstrates what this kind of work produces when done systematically.

The broader implication is that internet users encountering dramatic "you'll never guess" image stories bear more investigative responsibility than the framing of those stories implies. The advertisements are designed to present content as if the fictional narrative is the origin story, but the existence of 59 million views on the authentic original video demonstrates the actual story was available all along.


## 6. Conclusions and Epistemic Assessment

Snopes' "Mixture" verdict accurately captures the dual-register nature of the claim: the image is genuine, the narrative is fabricated. Jordan Liles' investigation successfully identified the original 2013 video, verified its location in La Gomera, and confirmed the basic facts that the fabricated advertisement narrative was designed to replace for commercial purposes.

The deeper analysis shows that the "boy pets creature" case is a canonical instance of advertising arbitrage deception: an authentic image stripped of its context is paired with a fabricated narrative optimised for slideshow engagement, and the authenticity of the image is borrowed to lend credibility to the false story. The economic mechanism (cost-of-click < revenue-from-slide-views) drives the production of such content at scale, and the psychological mechanism (uncertainty aversion, variable reward scheduling) drives its consumption.

What the Snopes rating does not fully capture is that the mechanisms described here are not marginal or rare phenomena on the contemporary internet. Advertising arbitrage content is a large and systematic part of the lower-quality display advertising ecosystem. The "boy pets creature" campaign is one documented instance of a continuous, large-scale production of similar content. The fact-check treats it as a specific incident to be resolved; it is more accurately understood as a structural feature of the current information economy that individual fact-checks can document but cannot dismantle.
