# The Brain Works Like a Computer

## 1. The Claim

The brain is essentially a biological computer. It takes in inputs, processes them, stores the results as memories, and retrieves them when needed. There is a central executive — analogous to a CPU — that coordinates processing. Information is encoded in neural "circuits" the way data is encoded in silicon. When we remember something, we are retrieving a stored file. When we learn, we are writing new data. When we sleep, we are consolidating memory, the way a computer might defragment its drive. The language is natural: we "process" grief, "store" experiences, "retrieve" facts, "buffer" distractions.

This metaphor is not merely popular — it is foundational to how neuroscience, cognitive psychology, and artificial intelligence research have been conducted for decades. It shapes how researchers design experiments, how clinicians describe brain function to patients, how educators talk about learning, and how technologists build systems they describe as intelligent. It is among the most successful scientific metaphors of the 20th century.

It is also, in important ways, wrong — and the wrongness has costs.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

In 2016, psychologist Robert Epstein published an essay in the journal *Aeon* titled "The Empty Brain" that crystallized a critique that had been building in cognitive science and philosophy of mind for decades. Epstein's argument, stated sharply: the brain does not process information, does not retrieve memories, does not operate on rules, and does not store representations. These are properties of computers. They are not properties of brains. The computer metaphor is not a useful approximation — it is a category error that has been masquerading as a scientific finding.

The argument is worth unpacking carefully, because it is counterintuitive even to people who know it well.

**There is no CPU.** The brain does not have a central processor that coordinates all activity. It has massive parallel processing across interconnected regions. The frontal cortex does not "manage" the amygdala the way an operating system manages processes. Different regions interact through dynamic, reciprocal, context-sensitive connections that have no clean computational analogue. The "executive function" metaphor applied to the prefrontal cortex borrows from corporate hierarchy in the same way it borrows from computing — it is an organizing story, not a description of mechanism.

**There is no RAM.** Working memory — the ability to hold information in mind for short periods — is not analogous to RAM in any mechanistically meaningful way. RAM stores discrete, stable bit-patterns that can be exactly retrieved. Working memory is a dynamic, reconstructive, easily distorted process. It degrades with distraction not because a buffer fills up but because the neural patterns sustaining it dissipate. The contents of "working memory" are not stored — they are actively maintained through ongoing neural activity, and the maintenance is always imperfect.

**There is no stored data.** This is the sharpest and most contested of Epstein's claims. The computational metaphor assumes that memories are encoded as stable representations — patterns written into synaptic connections that can be read back out. But memory research over the past 30 years has repeatedly demonstrated that memories are not stored like files; they are reconstructed each time they are accessed. Retrieval is not playback. It is confabulation — the brain rebuilds an approximation of the past using current context, prior reconstructions, emotional state, and motivated cognition. This is why eyewitness memory is so unreliable, why false memories can be implanted, why the same event is remembered differently by the same person at different times. The storage metaphor predicts stable retrieval. Memory research finds dynamic reconstruction.

**Processing is parallel, analog, and embodied.** Computers are (traditionally) serial, digital, and disembodied. The brain is none of these things. Neural processing is massively parallel — billions of neurons active simultaneously in overlapping, interacting patterns. It is analog — the relevant signal is not binary but graded, noisy, and probabilistic. And it is embodied — brain function is not separable from the body it inhabits, the hormonal environment in which it operates, or the physical and social environment in which behavior unfolds. The brain does not process information about the world from outside it. It is continuously embedded in the world, shaped by it, and acting back on it.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The history here is essential, and it is the history of metaphor.

Every era maps the brain onto its most impressive technology. This is not a coincidence or an intellectual failure — it is how analogy works in science. Novel, complex, poorly understood systems get illuminated by comparison to well-understood systems. The comparison is productive. It generates hypotheses, focuses experiments, and organizes intuitions. The problem comes when the metaphor stops being a tool and becomes a model — when the comparison is taken to be a literal description rather than a heuristic.

Before computers, the dominant brain metaphors were hydraulic. Descartes described the nervous system as a system of pipes and fluids; "animal spirits" flowed through the nerves like hydraulic fluid through tubes. This was not ignorance — Descartes was engaging with the best engineering his era could produce. The hydraulic nervous system generated real science. But it was wrong as a literal account, and it eventually constrained as much as it illuminated.

In the 19th century, the telephone exchange became the dominant metaphor. The brain was a switchboard, connecting inputs to outputs through fixed circuits. The Bell System was the most impressive communication infrastructure anyone had seen, and it seemed to explain how sensory signals got routed to responses. This metaphor generated the reflex arc model, which was extraordinarily productive for early neuroscience. It was also insufficient and, in its strong form, wrong.

The computer metaphor emerged with force in the mid-20th century, with Norbert Wiener's cybernetics, Shannon's information theory, and the first digital computers. It was spectacularly generative. The information-processing model of cognition produced cognitive psychology as a discipline, enabled artificial intelligence research, and generated the entire research program that underlies modern cognitive neuroscience. We owe enormous amounts to it.

But it is still a metaphor. And it smuggles assumptions. When we describe the brain as processing information, we import the assumption that there is a medium-independent content that can be separated from its physical substrate — the same information could be instantiated in silicon or carbon. When we describe memories as stored, we assume discrete encoding and stable retrieval. When we describe attention as executive control, we import hierarchical management structures. These assumptions have shaped which experiments get run, which findings get privileged, and which phenomena get explained away as noise.

## 4. Verdict

**The brain-as-computer metaphor is a powerful and productive heuristic that has done immense scientific work — and it is epistemologically false as a literal model of brain function.**

The brain does not have a CPU, RAM, or stored data in any mechanistically accurate sense. Memory is reconstructive, not playback. Processing is parallel, embodied, and context-dependent in ways that no existing computer architecture replicates. The metaphor has been so successful that it is easy to mistake success for accuracy — but a map that helps you navigate is not the same thing as the territory.

This does not mean the metaphor must be abandoned. Metaphors in science are rarely simply true or false — they are more or less useful for particular purposes. The information-processing metaphor is useful for designing experiments about attention, working memory capacity, and decision-making. It becomes misleading when applied to questions about the nature of memory, the embodied basis of cognition, or the relationship between neural activity and conscious experience.

The appropriate position is: use the metaphor deliberately, know its limits, and resist the temptation to treat its vocabulary as a literal description of mechanism.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The history of the brain-computer metaphor is a case study in how scientific language shapes scientific inquiry in ways that are difficult to see from inside the paradigm.

Researchers who design experiments using the information-processing framework tend to find results that are interpretable in that framework. This is not fraud — it is the normal functioning of paradigm-governed science, as Thomas Kuhn described it. The paradigm focuses attention, generates productive hypotheses, and provides interpretive frameworks. It also creates blind spots: phenomena that don't fit the paradigm get explained away, minimized, or simply not studied.

One significant blind spot has been **embodied cognition**. Decades of research in cognitive science and neuroscience have demonstrated that cognition is not a process happening in a brain-as-computer that happens to be housed in a body. Bodily states affect thinking. Emotion is not a noise signal that can be stripped from rational processing — it is constitutively involved in decision-making, memory, and reasoning (Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis is the most influential version of this). The motor system is not merely an output device; it is involved in language processing, conceptual representation, and social cognition (mirror neuron research, however contested some of it has become, opened these questions). None of this fits the information-processing model cleanly.

Another blind spot has been **neural dynamics**. The brain is not in a particular computational state waiting for inputs. It is continuously active, generating patterns that interact with incoming stimuli rather than passively receiving and processing them. Predictive processing and active inference frameworks (Karl Friston's work is the most developed version) propose that the brain is fundamentally a prediction machine — generating models of expected sensory input and updating them based on prediction error — rather than a passive processor. This is a radically different computational architecture from the input-process-output model, and it fits the neural data better in several domains.

The metaphor also has stakes in **artificial intelligence**. AI systems designed to mimic brain function using the computer metaphor — traditional symbolic AI, for instance — ran into walls. The successes of deep learning in the past decade came partly from moving away from explicit symbolic representation and toward statistical pattern matching in high-dimensional spaces. Whether deep learning is more "brain-like" is itself a contested question, but the failures of classical AI were partly failures of the brain-as-computer model applied to machine intelligence.

For the general public, the consequences are subtler but real. People who understand their own minds through the computer metaphor — "I just need to process this," "I'm buffering," "my hard drive is full" — are applying a model that may systematically mislead them about how memory, emotion, and cognition actually work. The expectation that memories are stable recordings is at odds with the reality of reconstructive memory, with consequences for how people evaluate their own reliability as witnesses to their own lives.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The brain-as-computer is not a claim that circulates as a discrete viral falsehood — it is a background assumption, a piece of the furniture of modern thought. Traditional fact-checking infrastructure, which is designed to evaluate specific falsifiable claims, is poorly suited to auditing background assumptions.

Epstein's "The Empty Brain" essay generated significant public and professional discussion when it appeared, and some responses pushed back — cognitive scientists who argued that the information-processing model was being too quickly dismissed, that stripped of its literal excesses it remained the best framework available. This is a legitimate counter-argument. Science is not served by replacing one overconfident framework with another. The appropriate response to the limits of the computer metaphor is not to claim that we have a better literal account of what the brain is — it is to hold the metaphor more lightly, use it where it works, and resist it where it doesn't.

What makes this epistemologically loaded, in the sense the Truth Vault uses that term, is that the claim is not simply true or false — it is a question about the relationship between scientific models and the phenomena they describe. The brain-as-computer is a useful model that has been mistaken for a description. Distinguishing those two things requires conceptual work, not just evidence-gathering. It is a philosophical question as much as an empirical one, and fact-checking tools developed for empirical questions don't map onto it cleanly.

Science journalists and science communicators who cover neuroscience have, in recent years, become more careful about this. "Brain scans show..." stories that imply clean input-output processing are less common than they were in the peak fMRI hype era of the early 2000s. The framing has shifted somewhat toward acknowledging complexity and uncertainty. But the baseline assumption — that the brain is, fundamentally, doing something like computing — remains the default in popular neuroscience coverage. It shapes the stories that get told, the analogies that get used, and the research that gets funded. Shifting it requires not just individual corrections but a broader renegotiation of the metaphors through which a culture understands its own minds.
