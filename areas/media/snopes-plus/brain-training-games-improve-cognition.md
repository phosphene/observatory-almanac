# Brain Training Games Like Lumosity Improve General Cognitive Function

## 1. The Claim

The advertisements are bold and ubiquitous. Lumosity, once described as "brain training for everyone," promised users they could "train their memory and attention" and achieve results grounded in neuroscience. Similar apps — CogniFit, Elevate, Peak, BrainHQ — have collectively attracted hundreds of millions of downloads on the promise that daily digital exercises will sharpen the mind, stave off cognitive decline, and improve performance in school, work, and everyday life.

The claim, stated plainly, is this: spending time each day playing games specifically designed to challenge memory, attention, processing speed, and problem-solving will produce measurable improvements in those same cognitive faculties in real-world situations. Not just better scores on the game itself — better thinking, broadly.

This belief is remarkably widespread. Surveys suggest a majority of adults in wealthy countries believe mental exercise can prevent or delay dementia. Brain training apps sit at the intersection of several deeply appealing ideas: that the brain is a muscle that can be strengthened through use, that cognitive decline is not inevitable, and that a relatively painless daily habit — ten minutes on your phone — can meaningfully change your mental trajectory.

The industry built on this belief is enormous. Lumosity alone reported over 100 million registered users at its peak. The global brain training market has been valued in the billions of dollars, with aggressive growth projections into the 2030s. It is one of the most commercially successful wellness categories in the history of digital health. The question, which the science has been answering with increasing clarity since at least 2014, is whether the core promise holds up.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

The science of brain training distinguishes carefully between two types of cognitive improvement: **near transfer** and **far transfer**.

Near transfer describes what definitely happens when you train on a specific cognitive task: you get better at that task, and at tasks closely resembling it. Play Lumosity's memory game for thirty days and your scores on that game, and similar memory games, will almost certainly improve. This is not a trivial finding — it demonstrates that brains remain plastic and trainable across the lifespan. But it is also not a surprise. Practice improves performance. This is true of playing the violin, solving Sudoku puzzles, and swinging a golf club.

Far transfer is the commercially valuable claim: that practice on digital games generalises to meaningful improvements in untrained cognitive domains — memory in daily life, attention at work, fluid intelligence, academic performance, or resistance to cognitive ageing. This is what the advertisements imply. And this is where the evidence falls apart.

The turning point in public scientific discourse came in 2014, when a group of more than 75 leading cognitive scientists and neuroscientists signed an open letter warning of "a growing gap between the [scientific] evidence and the claims made by companies." The letter was direct: the scientific literature did not support the conclusion that brain training transfers to real-world cognitive improvements.

A comprehensive meta-analysis published in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* (Simons et al., 2016) examined the evidence in exhaustive detail. The authors — a distinguished group including several researchers with no financial interest in either confirming or refuting the claims — concluded that the evidence for far transfer in brain training was weak, inconsistent, and frequently marred by methodological problems. Studies with positive results often used inadequate control conditions (passive controls who did nothing, rather than active controls doing some other stimulating activity), had small sample sizes, and relied on outcome measures that bore suspicious resemblance to the trained tasks themselves.

The U.S. Federal Trade Commission went further than peer review. In January 2016, the FTC charged Lumosity with deceptive advertising, finding that the company had made false claims about its ability to reduce or delay cognitive impairment associated with ageing and neurological conditions including Alzheimer's disease. Lumosity settled the charges for $2 million — reduced from a potential $50 million due to the company's financial limitations. The settlement required Lumosity to notify customers and offer refunds to those who had signed up for lifetime subscriptions.

To be fair, some brain training researchers dispute the bleakness of this picture. The ACTIVE trial (Advanced Cognitive Training for Independent and Vital Elderly), one of the largest and most rigorous studies of cognitive training in older adults, found that certain training protocols — particularly those targeting processing speed — produced modest improvements that persisted for years and were associated with reduced rates of dementia in follow-up analyses. Michael Merzenich, a neuroscientist at the University of California San Francisco and founder of BrainHQ's parent company Posit Science, has long argued that the evidence base for his platform is more robust than critics acknowledge.

These positive findings are real and should not be dismissed. But they exist alongside a much larger body of null or weakly positive results. The consensus position of the scientific community, as reflected in review papers, meta-analyses, and regulatory findings, is that the evidence for meaningful general cognitive improvement from commercial brain training games is weak. The gap between what the science can actually show and what the advertisements promise remains substantial.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The brain-as-muscle metaphor is among the most intuitive and durable in popular science. We are accustomed to the idea that physical exercise builds physical capacity — that running makes your cardiovascular system stronger, that lifting weights builds muscle. The notion that "mental exercise" might do the same for the brain feels natural, almost axiomatic.

This intuition is not entirely wrong. The science of neuroplasticity — the brain's demonstrated capacity to reorganise and adapt in response to experience — is real and well-established. Learning new skills, acquiring new languages, and sustained intellectual engagement throughout life are all associated with some degree of cognitive resilience. The leap, the one that billions of dollars of marketing have encouraged consumers to make, is from "the brain adapts to experience" to "this particular app will make your brain sharper."

Brain training companies have been sophisticated in their use of scientific language. Lumosity's early marketing leaned heavily on the vocabulary of neuroscience — plasticity, processing speed, working memory capacity — and cited research, some of it internally funded, to give the claims scientific texture. Users who saw modest improvements in game scores interpreted this as evidence that the broader promise was being fulfilled. The feedback loop of the games themselves — progress bars, level unlocks, personal best notifications — is engineered to generate feelings of improvement and mastery.

There is also a powerful emotional driver: fear of cognitive decline. As populations age and awareness of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias increases, the appeal of a simple, affordable preventive intervention becomes enormous. Brain training apps offer the user something deeply valuable — a sense of agency over their own cognitive future. The desire for this to be true is understandable, even moving. It helps explain why customers have spent billions of dollars on products whose core benefits remain scientifically unproven.

Anecdotal evidence and confirmation bias do the rest. When a user who has been doing daily Lumosity exercises manages to recall a name they might otherwise have forgotten, or scores well on a workplace assessment, the mental training gets credit. When the game scores improve, users assume the improvement is general. The mind is not well-equipped to run controlled experiments on itself.

## 4. Verdict

**Exaggerated.** Brain training games improve performance on the specific tasks they train and on closely related activities (near transfer). The claim that this improvement generalises to broader cognitive abilities, academic performance, workplace effectiveness, or protection against cognitive ageing (far transfer) is not supported by the weight of peer-reviewed scientific evidence. The FTC's 2016 action against Lumosity represents the regulatory enforcement endpoint of this assessment.

The claim contains a kernel of truth — the brain does remain plastic, practice does improve performance, and some specific training protocols may have modest real-world benefits for specific populations. But as a general, commercially marketed proposition — "use our app for ten minutes a day and become cognitively sharper" — it is an exaggeration of what the science actually shows.

The gap_category is **distorted-but-grounded**: the underlying neuroscience of plasticity and the reality of near-transfer improvement provide genuine factual foundations, but the commercial extrapolation into general cognitive enhancement is unsupported by the best available evidence.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The brain training debate sits at the intersection of several larger scientific and social questions.

**The replication crisis in psychology** has complicated the picture. Several early studies showing promising transfer effects have not replicated reliably. The field has grappled with publication bias — positive results are more likely to be published than null results — meaning the literature likely overstates the efficacy of cognitive training. Large, pre-registered, well-controlled studies tend to show smaller effects than smaller, less rigorous investigations.

**The opportunity cost question** is underexamined. If someone spends thirty minutes a day on brain training apps instead of taking a walk, learning a musical instrument, or reading widely, they may actually be choosing a less effective cognitive intervention. The evidence base for physical exercise — aerobic exercise in particular — as a support for brain health is substantially stronger than for digital brain training. Cardiovascular fitness is associated with larger hippocampal volume and better memory performance in controlled studies. The brain training market may, in a real sense, be displacing more effective alternatives.

**Workplaces and schools** have been affected by these claims. Some organisations have purchased brain training subscriptions for employees on the premise that it will improve performance. Some educational programmes have incorporated brain training games as interventions for students with attention difficulties or learning challenges. The evidence base for these applications is particularly thin.

**The dementia prevention framing** deserves special scrutiny. Some brain training marketing has implied that using these products reduces the risk of developing dementia or delays its onset. This is among the most consequential health claims a consumer product can make, and it is among the least supported. The Lancet Commission on dementia prevention has identified lifestyle factors — physical inactivity, hypertension, obesity, depression, social isolation, smoking, hearing loss, limited education, air pollution — that together account for approximately 40% of dementia cases. Commercial brain training games are not on that list.

The ACTIVE trial follow-up data — which did suggest some association between processing speed training and reduced dementia risk — is intriguing and warrants further investigation. But one study, even a large and long-running one, cannot carry the weight of a clinical recommendation, particularly when its dementia-related findings were not the primary pre-registered endpoint.

There is also a meaningful distinction between different types of cognitive training. The structured, adaptive, clinician-supervised cognitive rehabilitation used with brain injury patients is a genuinely evidence-based field. The leap to consumer apps as casual self-administered cognitive enhancement is not supported by the transplantation of that evidence base.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Brain training claims have attracted attention from science journalists and fact-checkers rather than traditional political fact-checking organisations like Snopes or PolitiFact, because the claims derive from corporate advertising rather than partisan political speech.

**Science-based fact-checking outlets** — including Science-Based Medicine, the science sections of *The Skeptical Inquirer*, and health journalism outlets like STAT News — have examined brain training claims in depth and consistently reached conclusions aligned with the scientific consensus: near transfer is real, far transfer is not reliably established.

**Regulatory enforcement** has functioned as a form of institutional fact-checking. The FTC settlement with Lumosity established a legal finding that the advertising claims were deceptive. The settlement required Lumosity to notify subscribers, offer refunds, and refrain from making unsubstantiated health claims going forward. This is a rarer but more consequential form of claim adjudication than journalistic fact-checking.

**The open letter from cognitive scientists** (2014) is frequently cited as a form of scientific community self-correction — researchers making their collective assessment of the evidence publicly available in a format accessible to consumers and journalists.

**Wikipedia's handling** of the Lumosity article is instructive: it gives prominent placement to the FTC settlement and includes sections on the scientific controversies, reflecting the community's general practice of giving weight to regulatory findings and scientific consensus statements.

**Consumer advocacy organisations** in several countries have advised caution about brain training marketing claims, noting the gap between advertising language and peer-reviewed evidence.

The primary challenge for fact-checkers in this domain is the gradient between genuine scientific uncertainty and misleading corporate claims. The science of cognitive training is not settled — there are legitimate researchers who believe the evidence for some forms of training is stronger than the consensus view allows. This genuine scientific debate creates cover for advertising language that extrapolates well beyond what even the most optimistic researchers would endorse.

For readers evaluating such claims, the key diagnostic questions are: Does the evidence come from independent researchers without financial ties to the company? Were control groups active (doing something comparably stimulating) rather than passive? Were the outcome measures genuinely different from the trained tasks? Did the study measure real-world outcomes — grades, job performance, quality of life — rather than laboratory cognitive tests? For most commercial brain training, honest answers to these questions support considerable scepticism.
