# Who Threw That Ham?


## 1. The Claim

The legend presents itself as a true anecdote: a person concealing a large ham (or occasionally a chicken or other meat item) under their outer garment while shoplifting is caught when the concealed item falls to the floor. Rather than acknowledging the theft, the person responds to the dropped item by loudly demanding to know who threw it at them — deploying audacity and implausible denial in the face of obvious evidence. The punchline turns on the thief's brazenness: the claim that someone *threw* the ham at them is transparently absurd, yet the thief performs it with apparent conviction.

The claim enters as a "true story" heard secondhand from a "friend of a friend" (FOAF in folklorist terminology) — the classic distancing formula of urban legend transmission. It is positioned as reportage rather than fiction, asked to be believed rather than merely enjoyed, and presented as revealing something true about the world even as its internal details resist any single moment of first-person verification.

The narrative circulates in multiple versions that preserve the core structure (shoplifter + dropped meat + audacious denial) while varying the setting (Winn-Dixie, inner-city Detroit supermarket, a meatpacking plant), the relationship of the narrator to the event (eyewitness, coworker, reader of a newspaper), the specific meat item (ham in most versions, chicken in the oldest recoverable variant), and the racial coding of the protagonist (African American in most versions, rendered through dialect spelling and explicit description).

The story appeared in *Reader's Digest* in September 2008, a publication whose editorial conventions typically require some attestation of factual basis for submitted "true stories." This is a significant datum for evaluating the legend's circulation history: either Reader's Digest failed to verify the story adequately, or a real incident resembling the legend occurred and was reported as such. The distinction matters for the legend's epistemological status, though Snopes correctly notes the broader tradition that precedes any specific verified incident.

The legend also connects to a recognized family of shoplifting narratives — including the "Meathead" legend mentioned in Snopes (a thief who conceals a frozen chicken under a hat), the broader category of "thief caught by improbable circumstances," and international parallels in British and Australian shoplifting lore — that suggests a structural rather than purely circumstantial origin.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rates this as a LEGEND, which is the appropriate designation for a narrative that has folkloric structure and transmission patterns consistent with urban legend status but cannot be verified as a specific historical event. The designation does not mean the story is necessarily false — real incidents *could* have occurred that resemble the legend, and the Reader's Digest publication suggests at least one individual claimed the story was true — but the narrative has circulated as legend long enough and widely enough that it cannot be treated as straightforward reportage.

The claim to *factual* status is undermined by multiple features of the narrative's transmission:

*The FOAF structure:* Every version of the story filters through at least one layer of distance from any alleged first-person witness. "A friend of mine tells of being in a supermarket" is the paradigmatic formulation. This distancing structure is characteristic of urban legend transmission and signals that the story is traveling as legend rather than as documented reporting.

*The multiple simultaneous variants:* No urban legend in authentic reportage simultaneously exists with a Chicago setting, a Detroit setting, a Winn-Dixie setting, and a meatpacking plant setting with different narrators all claiming first-person proximity. The morphological diversity of the versions is evidence of folkloric evolution, not independent documentation of multiple similar events.

*The historically attested antiquity:* Snopes notes an oral tradition dating to the 1950s involving a chicken rather than a ham and set on a military ship. If the story is truly from the 1950s in a different form, its current ham-focused, racially-coded versions are plausibly subsequent adaptations rather than independent reportage of real events.

*The structural perfection:* The narrative achieves a perfect punchline structure — the preposterous claim made with straight-faced conviction — that is the fingerprint of polished legend rather than the rough edges of remembered reality. Real incidents, as reported to police or in court documents, rarely achieve this kind of narrative economy.


## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes, in Barbara Mikkelson's July 2, 2002 article, correctly classifies the story as a legend and makes two observations that are analytically important: (1) the story has folkloric antecedents dating to at least the 1950s; and (2) the story contains "barely suppressed racist elements" embedded in the dialect rendering ("Who trew dat ham at me?") and in the consistent association of the shoplifter with Black identity and with ham as a foodstuff associated with Southern African American cuisine. These are precisely correct observations that the Snopes article does not develop fully.

The epistemic novelty begins with the structural analysis that Snopes gestures toward but does not pursue to its logical conclusion. The Snopes piece identifies the racism but does not place it within the formal analysis of how racial coding functions in American urban legend tradition.

*First*, the specific choice of ham rather than other stolen foodstuffs is not incidental. Mikkelson correctly notes that the legend invariably involves ham rather than pot roast or steak, and attributes this to racial association with "Southern cooking." This observation invites a deeper inquiry into what folklorists call *code objects* in legend transmission — specific, culturally loaded items whose inclusion signals the racial, ethnic, or class identity of the legend's protagonist without requiring explicit statement. Brunvand (2001), in *Too Good to Be True: The Colossal Book of Urban Legends* (Norton), analyzes how code objects operate in contemporary American legends to carry racial messaging with plausible deniability: the legend can be told as a "funny story about a shoplifter" while simultaneously activating racial stereotypes through the choice of items, dialect, and setting.

*Second*, Snopes identifies the legend as belonging to the "audacity of thieves" tradition without developing what this tradition means as a cultural phenomenon. The legend type is well-established in folklore scholarship under the general category of what Jan Harold Brunvand termed "legends of criminal boldness." These narratives serve a specific social function: they simultaneously condemn theft (the thief is caught, at least implicitly) while celebrating a certain transgressive audacity that carries its own cultural appeal. The "Who threw that ham at me?" story works as entertainment precisely because the thief's response is *so* preposterous — it represents an extreme form of brazen denial that the audience finds funny while also finding it morally instructive.

*Third*, the dialect phonetic spelling ("Who trew dat ham at me?") is itself a significant textual feature that Snopes mentions without analyzing. The use of eye dialect — phonetic respelling that represents non-standard pronunciation through orthographic distortion — is a well-documented rhetorical technique in American racial caricature with a long history in minstrelsy, racist humor, and regional stereotype literature. Rickford and Rickford (2000), in *Spoken Soul: The Story of Black English* (Wiley), document how eye dialect has functioned historically as a marker of racial othering in American text, reducing speakers to caricature through the visual performance of non-standard speech. The consistent appearance of this dialect spelling across multiple versions of the "ham" legend is a significant feature of its ideological content, not merely an incidental detail of oral transmission.

*Fourth*, the gender dimension is underexplored. Most versions of the legend describe the shoplifter as a woman — specifically, as a large or heavyset woman concealing the ham under a coat or dress. This gendered framing places the legend in connection with a broader tradition of narratives about large, threatening, criminal, deviant Black women that has deep roots in American racial and gender stereotype literature. Gilman (1985), "Black Bodies, White Bodies: Toward an Iconography of Female Sexuality in Late Nineteenth-Century Art, Medicine, and Literature" (*Critical Inquiry*, 12(1), 204–242), traces the origins of this iconographic cluster, which shapes contemporary humor at a level that operates below conscious awareness. The Snopes article identifies the racial element but not the gendered one.


## 4. The Broader Evidence Base

The documentary evidence for the legend's existence as a legend is robust: the Snopes article itself provides multiple independently collected versions from different dates and settings, which constitutes primary evidence of folkloric circulation. The Reader's Digest 2008 publication (Schoen, Roger. "Our 50 Funniest (True!) Stories." *Reader's Digest*, September 2008, p. 87) represents the legend's penetration into mainstream editorial media in a context that asks for true stories — evidence of both the legend's durability and of ordinary editorial verification failures in popular media.

The structural comparison with the "Meathead" legend (concealed frozen chicken under a hat produces unconsciousness from cold exposure) illustrates the morphological range of the meat-concealment shoplifting legend family: the shared elements are meat as contraband, a body-concealment strategy, and a comic failure of concealment. This structural cluster constitutes a recognizable legend type with multiple documented variants.

The 1950s naval origin story — involving a chicken rather than ham, and set on a ship rather than a grocery store — if reliably documented, represents the earliest attested version and suggests a pre-racial-coding origin that was subsequently given racial specificity in its diffusion through American urban culture. This trajectory (origin in non-racialized context → subsequent racial coding) is a documented pattern in American urban legend development, where racially specific versions of legends tend to be later adaptations of structurally prior forms.

Comparative international folklore provides a useful evidential frame. British and Australian shoplifting legends with similar structures have been documented by folklore researchers, notably: the work of Paul Smith and Gareth Williams, documented in *The Archeology of Urban Legends* (1983, International Society for Contemporary Legend Research), demonstrates that the concealment-and-denial narrative structure appears across multiple national contexts, suggesting that the structural template is cross-culturally available even as specific racial and cultural coding is locally imposed.


## 5. Epistemic Status and Confidence

**Confidence in the "LEGEND" verdict:** Very high (95%+). The FOAF transmission structure, multiple variant versions, historical antiquity, and structural perfection of the punchline all confirm folkloric rather than reportorial status.

**Could a real incident resembling this legend have occurred?** Yes, and quite plausibly. Shoplifting incidents with absurd or brazen outcomes do occur; instances of dropped concealed goods and denial responses are not impossible. But the documented narrative is a legend in the structural folkloric sense regardless of whether any real incident inspired it.

**Is the racial coding incidental or structural?** Structural. The consistent appearance of African American identity markers (ham, dialect, "inner city" setting) across independently collected versions strongly suggests that the racial coding is a stable structural feature of this legend's performance, not an occasional variation. This does not mean the legend is necessarily more racist than its tellers intend, but it does mean the racial content is intrinsic to how the legend circulates.

**Does the Reader's Digest publication change the verdict?** No. The publication documents that at least one person told the story to Reader's Digest as true, but the existence of a reported instance does not change the archeological evidence showing the story circulated as a legend for decades. The Reader's Digest instance may be: (a) a real incident that happened to closely resemble the legend; (b) a misremembered or embellished account that conformed to the legend structure; or (c) the legend itself told with first-person framing as a joke or fabulai.


## 6. EARC Assessment and Gap Analysis

**EARC Mode: C** — The Snopes verdict is correct, and the article makes analytically important observations about racial coding. But the legend type and its racist content deserve fuller structural analysis than a verdict-plus-brief-commentary affords.

**Gap Category: folklore-structural-analysis** — The legend has recognizable structural features that connect it to a well-documented tradition in urban legend scholarship. Placing it within that tradition, while also analyzing the specific racial encoding mechanisms at work, constitutes the primary epistemic gap.

**What Snopes does not cover:**
- Structural analysis of the legend's place within the "criminal brazenness" legend family
- The cultural function of code objects (ham specifically) in racially coded American legends
- The eye-dialect tradition and its historical roots in minstrelsy and racial caricature
- The gender coding (large woman under coat) and its connection to racist/sexist iconographic traditions
- International comparative data showing the structural template is cross-national, even as racial coding is locally imposed
- The *Reader's Digest* publication as an instance of legend penetration into mainstream editorial media

**Research gap:** A systematic study of how racial coding becomes attached to originally non-coded legend structures in American oral tradition — specifically examining the transformation trajectory from the 1950s naval chicken variant to the ham-focused Black-identity-coded versions — would be a significant contribution to American folklore studies.

**Recommended for:** Readers interested in folklore methodology, the sociology of racial coding in humor and narrative, urban legend studies, and the cultural history of theft narratives in American popular culture.
