## The Claim

In October 2024, images appearing to show a dramatic medical condition circulated on Reddit and X (Twitter). The posts described an MRI image of a breakdancer who had developed a so-called "headspin hole" after years of intensive practice. One post on X accumulated over 114,000 views. Reddit communities including r/BeAmazed hosted versions of the content, with captions describing a man who had "developed a headspin hole after years of breakdancing." The implied visual claim was specific: that an MRI image showed a deformation of the top of a human skull caused by repetitive head-spinning. The secondary claim was that this condition is real and medically documented.

Both claims are true. Snopes reporter Madison Dapcevich confirmed the images' authenticity and traced them to their source: a peer-reviewed clinical case report published in the British Medical Journal (BMJ) Case Reports journal. The case report described surgical treatment of the condition and included MRI imaging, photographs of the excised tissue, and extensive description of the patient's experience.

## What's Actually True

The images are authentic clinical photographs and MRI scans published in a peer-reviewed medical journal. Skotting and Søndergaard published the case report titled "'Headspin Hole': An Overuse Injury among Breakdancers" in BMJ Case Reports, Volume 17, Issue 9, in September 2024 (Skotting & Søndergaard, 2024, *BMJ Case Reports*). The full article is available via open access at press.psprings.co.uk.

The case described in the report involved a man in his early 30s who had been breakdancing for 19 years. He had developed a "noticeable scalp protuberance" over approximately five years of intensive head-spinning practice. His practice regimen was substantial: he danced five times per week for approximately ninety minutes per session, applying direct pressure to his scalp for between two and seven minutes per session during headspin drills. Although the bump had grown in size and tenderness over the five-year period of its development and was "aesthetically displeasing" to the patient, he had not stopped dancing.

MRI imaging of the patient's head showed thickening of the subcutis — the deepest skin layer, lying closest to the muscle beneath — forming a distinctive "cone-head" shape at the apex of the cranium. The case report's co-author Søndergaard, a researcher at the Department of Brain and Nerve Surgery at Copenhagen University Hospitals, clarified to Snopes's Dapcevich that the term "headspin hole" specifically refers not to an actual perforation or depression but to the hair loss that often accompanies the condition, creating a visible bald spot — understood colloquially as a "hole" in the hair rather than in the skull.

The fibrous mass was surgically removed. The excised tissue was benign — a fibrous tumour mass, histologically consistent with reactive fibrosis produced by chronic mechanical trauma. The surgery was successful, and the patient's subjective account of his experience, quoted in the published case report, was detailed and positive: he described well-organised care, professional and skilled surgeons, significant improvement in his appearance, and satisfaction with the outcome. He noted that his head still had "very thick skin" with a slight residual elevation, but he chose not to seek further referral to a plastic surgeon.

Report co-author Christian Baastrup Søndergaard is affiliated with the Department of Brain and Nerve Surgery at Copenhagen University Hospitals (Rigshospitalet), one of Denmark's primary neurosurgical centres.

## The Medical Science of the Headspin Hole: Overuse, Fibrosis, and the Biomechanics of Headspins

The epistemic novelty here is not primarily in the question of authenticity — the images are unambiguously from a peer-reviewed journal — but in what the condition reveals about the biology of chronic mechanical stress and about the emergence of novel sports injuries from emerging athletic disciplines.

Breakdancing — now officially termed "breaking" and included as a competitive sport in the 2024 Paris Olympics — involves a range of athletic movements executed at or near the interface between the body and the floor. Toprock (upright rhythmic footwork), downrock (floor-level footwork such as the 6-step), freezes (static balance positions), and power moves (dynamic spinning elements) constitute the core vocabulary of the form. Power moves include windmills, flares, airflares, and headspins — the latter requiring the dancer to balance inverted on the crown of the skull and spin horizontally, generating substantial rotational momentum through body positioning and arm movements.

A headspin exerts several distinct types of mechanical stress on the scalp. Compressive force from the body weight transmitted through the crown of the head is amplified during the inverted position and during the initial loading phase of spin initiation. Rotational friction generates shear stress across the skin surface at the contact point. Repetition over years of practice results in chronic mechanical trauma to the subcutaneous tissue at the pressure zone. Chronic compression and friction of soft tissue produces a well-understood pathological response: initially, inflammatory responses cause local oedema and tenderness; over time, repeated injury-and-repair cycles drive fibroblastic proliferation — the same cellular process underlying all fibrous scar tissue formation — producing a progressively thickening mass of dense connective tissue at the site.

This pathological mechanism — reactive fibrosis from repetitive mechanical loading — is well-documented in occupational and sports medicine across a wide range of anatomical sites. Callous formation in hands (Moody & Sheringham, 2018, *Journal of Hand Surgery*), iliotibial band syndrome in runners (Fairclough et al., 2006, *Journal of Anatomy*), bursitis in rowers and cyclists, and similar soft-tissue thickening phenomena all represent variants of the same fundamental biology. The headspin hole is, in mechanistic terms, an occupational callous of the scalp — unusual only because the anatomical site is not commonly subjected to this type of mechanical loading in any other athletic or occupational context.

The earliest medical description of the headspin hole was published in German by Kauther in 2009 (*Sportverletzung, Sportschaden*, vol. 23, pp. 52–53), which constitutes the first medical description of the condition and documented that more than 60% of breakdancers experienced overuse injuries to the scalp, with approximately one-third experiencing hair loss and approximately one-quarter developing painless bumps on the head. This 2009 study established both the prevalence of the condition and its characterisation as an overuse injury long before the condition attracted broader media attention.

A second case report published in 2020 described a 38-year-old man with a headspin hole who had experienced dizziness and nosebleeds for several years (Korczynski et al., 2020, *Radiology Case Reports*). That man had been breakdancing since age 15, spinning on his head primarily with a baseball cap or skull cap as minimal padding. Korczynski et al. named the MRI finding the "cone-head sign," referring to the distinctive shape visible on cross-sectional imaging. They recommended that radiologists and clinicians be aware of the clinical presentation to avoid "unnecessary biopsy and inappropriate work" — recognising that the appearance of a soft tissue mass on a head MRI could prompt aggressive investigation if its benign mechanical aetiology were not recognised.

The epistemically significant point is that the headspin hole represents a genuinely novel sports medicine presentation — one that did not exist (or could not exist) before breakdancing became a sustained practice pursued at intensity over years to decades. The expansion of the condition's documentation in the medical literature, from a single German-language paper in 2009 to English-language radiological and surgical case reports in 2020 and 2024, reflects the maturation of breakdancing as an athletic discipline with practitioners who now have decades of training behind them.

## Why People Found This Hard to Believe

The scepticism the circulating images provoked was several-layered, and worth examining in some detail.

**The anatomical location seems implausible.** Most people do not have a working model of the amount of mechanical stress a headspin exerts on the scalp. The activity — balancing the body's weight on the top of the skull while executing horizontal rotations — is biomechanically unusual and not intuitively obvious to non-practitioners. The idea that a person could develop a medically significant scalp deformation from this activity required, for most viewers, both acknowledgement that headspins exist as a practice and accurate modelling of what the biomechanical consequences of years of daily practice would plausibly be. Neither of these was available to most viewers without prompting.

**The term "headspin hole" sounds informal.** The colloquial name — which refers to hair loss rather than to a literal perforation — reads as slang invented for social media. The medical alternative, "breakdance bulge" or "cone-head sign," is more descriptive but still unusual. The absence of a formally imposing Latin term (as would be expected for a condition with long clinical history) may have contributed to a sense that the condition was invented or exaggerated.

**MRI images look inherently artificial.** Magnetic resonance images are generated by radio frequency pulses and gradient magnetic fields, and produce cross-sectional grayscale representations of internal tissue that bear no resemblance to visual experience. For audiences without clinical training, MRI images are often visually indistinguishable from other forms of computer-generated imagery. The image of the cone-shaped subcutaneous thickening at the crown of the skull could look, to an untrained eye, like a digitally generated rendering rather than a clinical diagnostic image.

**The connection to the Olympics added novelty framing.** The 2024 Paris Olympics formally included breaking as a competitive sport — the first time in Olympic history. The October 2024 headspin hole coverage arrived in a media environment still processing this novelty, in which breakdancing had just received significant international attention as a formal athletic discipline. Some of the skepticism toward the "headspin hole" reporting may have reflected residual cultural uncertainty about whether breakdancing should be taken seriously as an athletic practice capable of producing genuine overuse injuries.

## Sports Medicine, Emerging Disciplines, and the Documentation Gap

The evolution of headspin hole documentation in the medical literature illustrates a general pattern in sports medicine: new athletic disciplines generate novel injury patterns that require years to decades to accumulate sufficient case numbers for systematic documentation. Before practitioners of an emerging discipline have been training long enough to develop overuse injuries, those injuries cannot be documented; by the time practitioners have trained long enough, the events are sporadic and may not reach the medical establishment's attention if practitioners are young, uninsured, or manage symptoms without professional care.

Breakdancing originated in the Bronx, New York, in the early 1970s, and spread to communities across the United States and eventually globally through the 1980s. By the time the first medical description of the headspin hole appeared in 2009, the pioneers of the discipline had been training for approximately 30–35 years. The 2024 case report's subject had been dancing for 19 years — long enough to have developed a significant fibrous response despite beginning well after the discipline's initial flourishing.

The underreporting of headspin holes is explicitly noted in both the 2020 and 2024 case reports. Korczynski et al. (2020) recommended clinical awareness specifically to prevent over-investigation; Skotting and Søndergaard (2024) emphasised the limited number of prior descriptions and the importance of recognising the condition. The fact that the condition is underreported does not mean it is rare — the 2009 Kauther study found the condition in approximately one-quarter of surveyed breakdancers — but that it is rarely brought to clinical attention.

This is a pattern common to sports injuries in disciplines that developed outside mainstream sports medicine infrastructure: high-impact parkour practitioners, artistic skateboarding athletes, aerial acrobats in circus arts, and advanced capoeira practitioners all have distinctive injury profiles that are underrepresented in sports medicine literature relative to prevalence in the practising community. As these disciplines become increasingly formalised — and breaking's Olympic inclusion is the apotheosis of this formalisation process — their injury epidemiology will become better characterised.

## Verdict

**True.** The MRI images and surgical photographs of a breakdancer's "headspin hole" that circulated on social media in October 2024 are authentic clinical images published in a peer-reviewed study in BMJ Case Reports. They depict a benign fibrous mass — reactive fibrosis produced by years of repetitive mechanical loading at the scalp's contact point during headspins — that was surgically removed with a successful outcome. The condition is real, documented in the medical literature since 2009, and represents a genuinely novel overuse injury that could only emerge from the specific biomechanical demands of sustained, intensive breakdance practice.

The images are not fabricated, not digitally generated, and do not show a skull hole or brain exposure — the "hole" in the colloquial term refers to associated hair loss, not to any perforation. The medical documentation is robust, the surgical treatment was effective, and the patient's subjective account of his care and outcome is included in the published case report. The viral reception of these images reflected appropriate wonder at a striking and previously unfamiliar medical presentation, and the scepticism was, in this case, unfounded.
