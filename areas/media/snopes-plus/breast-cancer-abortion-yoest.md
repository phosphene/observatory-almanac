# Abortion Increases the Risk of Breast Cancer


## 1. The Claim

The claim asserts that having an induced abortion meaningfully and causally increases a woman's risk of developing breast cancer — a framing that has circulated in anti-abortion political organizing, crisis pregnancy center materials, state-mandated counseling scripts, and conservative policy advocacy for several decades. The claim is most prominently associated with Charmaine Yoest, former president and CEO of Americans United for Life and former Assistant Secretary for Public Affairs at the United States Department of Health and Human Services (2017–2018), who stated in a 2017 New York Times interview: "I think the research is still very much out there" regarding an abortion-breast cancer link.

The hypothesis has been operationalized in state legislation. As of the mid-2020s, multiple U.S. states have required or have proposed requiring abortion providers to inform patients of an alleged increased breast cancer risk as part of informed consent procedures. Organizations including the Coalition on Abortion/Breast Cancer have produced pamphlets, websites, and advocacy materials presenting the link as established medical fact. Crisis pregnancy centers — facilities that provide counseling oriented toward discouraging abortion — have incorporated the claim into their standard client information packages.

The specific biological mechanism proposed varies by source. The most common version holds that a full-term pregnancy causes breast cells to differentiate into mature, more cancer-resistant forms, while an interrupted pregnancy allegedly leaves breast cells in a transitional, more susceptible state. This mechanism is sometimes presented as the "ABC link" (abortion-breast cancer) in advocacy literature, with its proponents citing a subset of older epidemiological studies as support.

The political stakes are substantial. If the ABC link were real and causal, it would constitute a legitimate medical ground for breast cancer risk warnings in abortion informed consent communications. The sustained advocacy effort to establish the link as settled science reflects the material consequences for abortion regulation that a confirmed causal relationship would produce. This is not a claim advanced merely for intellectual interest — it serves a concrete political function in the ongoing policy debate over abortion access, informed consent requirements, and the regulatory framework governing abortion providers.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The scientific consensus is clear: induced abortion does not increase breast cancer risk.** This conclusion is not the position of a single study or a single organization but reflects the convergent findings of multiple independent large-scale investigations examined under different methodological frameworks across multiple countries and demographic populations.

The National Cancer Institute (NCI) convened a workshop of over 100 of the world's leading experts on this topic in February 2003. The workshop reviewed population-based, clinical, and animal studies examining the relationship between pregnancy outcomes and breast cancer risk. The panel's conclusion, published in the *Journal of the National Cancer Institute* and maintained on the NCI website as standing guidance: "Induced abortion is not associated with an increase in breast cancer risk."

The American Cancer Society states: "The scientific evidence does not support a link between induced abortion and breast cancer." The World Health Organization, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists in the United Kingdom have reached identical conclusions through independent institutional review processes.

The evidentiary base supporting these conclusions is substantial. A 2004 collaborative reanalysis published in *The Lancet*, led by Valerie Beral and colleagues at Oxford University, pooled and re-examined individual-level data from 53 epidemiological studies covering 83,000 women with breast cancer in 16 countries. This represents one of the largest collaborative recalibrations of epidemiological evidence on any topic in the field. The reanalysis found no significant association between induced abortion and breast cancer incidence after controlling for established risk factors.

The methodological critique that undermined earlier studies pointing toward an ABC link is well-established in the epidemiological literature. Many early studies were conducted using case-control designs that introduce "response bias" (also called "recall bias" or "reporting bias"): women who develop breast cancer are more likely to accurately and fully report their full reproductive history, including previous abortions, because they are searching for explanations for their illness. Women in the control group — who have not developed cancer — are more likely to underreport induced abortions due to stigma, privacy concerns, or the social desirability of presenting a simpler reproductive history. This systematic asymmetry in reporting produces a spurious statistical association that disappears when the analysis is conducted using prospectively collected data or registries not subject to differential reporting.

Large-scale prospective studies, which collect reproductive history data from participants before any cancer diagnosis, consistently find no ABC link. These include the Nurses' Health Study II (based at Harvard), the CARE study, and multiple Nordic registry studies that leverage Scandinavia's comprehensive health record infrastructure to examine cancer outcomes against population-level abortion records without relying on participant self-report.

The biological mechanism proposed by ABC advocates — that interrupted pregnancy leaves breast cells more cancer-susceptible — is also not supported by the available experimental and cellular biology evidence. Breast cell differentiation occurs throughout a full-term pregnancy in a complex hormonal cascade; there is no documented mechanism by which an early induced abortion captures cells in a uniquely higher-risk state that persists long-term.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of the abortion-breast cancer claim despite scientific consensus against it demands explanation that goes beyond simple ignorance. The claim's survival reflects several intersecting dynamics, some epistemological and some political.

**The hypothesis had a genuine scientific moment.** The ABC link was not invented whole cloth. Beginning in the 1980s, a series of epidemiological studies — operating before the methodological critique of response bias was fully developed and widely appreciated — did find statistical associations between induced abortion and breast cancer incidence. A 1994 study by Janet Daling and colleagues in the *Journal of the National Cancer Institute*, now extensively criticized on methodological grounds, received significant media attention as an apparent confirmation of the link. That early scientific attention gave the ABC claim a foundation of apparent legitimacy that subsequent corrections have struggled to fully dislodge from public and political memory.

The history of science includes many examples of hypotheses that had initial empirical support, attracted significant attention, and were later revised or rejected — but whose earlier versions persist in public memory and advocacy contexts even after the scientific community has moved on. This is a predictable consequence of how scientific findings propagate through public discourse: initial confirmatory findings travel further and faster than subsequent corrections.

**Charmaine Yoest and the political infrastructure of manufactured uncertainty.** Yoest's comment in the 2017 New York Times interview — suggesting "the research is still very much out there" — is a more sophisticated framing than claiming the ABC link is proven. It deploys epistemic doubt: framing settled science as unsettled, suggesting ongoing legitimate scientific debate where none exists, and implying that further research might vindicate the link. This rhetorical strategy mirrors documented patterns in tobacco industry communications about lung cancer risk, fossil fuel industry communications about climate science, and other instances where political actors have incentive to manufacture the appearance of scientific uncertainty.

The "manufactured uncertainty" playbook has been extensively documented by scholars including Naomi Oreskes and Erik Conway in *Merchants of Doubt* (2010). The pattern involves emphasizing outlier studies, challenging methodological details of mainstream research, demanding ever-higher evidentiary standards, and asserting that "the jury is still out" in the face of overwhelming consensus. Applied to the ABC link, this strategy keeps the claim politically viable even though the scientific community treats the question as resolved.

**Crisis pregnancy centers as an institutional distribution mechanism.** The United States has an estimated 2,500–3,000 crisis pregnancy centers (CPCs) — facilities that provide pregnancy testing, ultrasound, and counseling services but are typically operated by anti-abortion religious organizations and do not provide abortion services or contraception referrals. Research on CPC practices, including congressional staff investigations and journalistic reviews of informational materials, has documented widespread distribution of ABC link claims as established medical fact. For women who receive their primary pregnancy-related health information from CPCs — a population that may be larger in rural and low-income communities where alternatives are limited — the ABC link may be presented as medical consensus, creating sincere belief in an authoritative-seeming claim.

**The breast cancer context makes the claim emotionally resonant.** Breast cancer is among the most feared and culturally prominent disease categories in American health consciousness. Susan G. Komen foundation campaigns, celebrity diagnoses, awareness ribbons, and decades of public health messaging have created deep public concern about breast cancer risk factors. In this context, a claim that a specific behavior raises breast cancer risk carries inherent persuasive weight that the identical claim about a less prominent disease would lack. The emotional register of breast cancer risk activates precautionary intuitions that can override demand for rigorous evidentiary standards.


## 4. Verdict

**False.** The claim that induced abortion increases the risk of breast cancer is not supported by the scientific evidence and contradicts the established conclusions of the world's leading cancer research and medical institutions. The National Cancer Institute, the American Cancer Society, the World Health Organization, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and the World Health Organization have each independently reviewed the available evidence and concluded that no credible causal link exists.

The hypothesis originated in a real scientific debate that was resolved by more rigorous methodological research over the past two decades. The persistence of the claim in political and advocacy contexts reflects its utility in abortion policy debates rather than its scientific credibility. The careful use of epistemic doubt language — "the research is still out there," "we don't fully know" — by prominent advocates like Charmaine Yoest is not a legitimate expression of scientific uncertainty but a documented rhetorical strategy for sustaining politically motivated claims against scientific consensus.

State policies requiring abortion providers to inform patients of an increased breast cancer risk as part of informed consent communications force physicians to communicate claims that contradict their training, their professional organizations' guidance, and the weight of available evidence. Medical organizations including ACOG have consistently opposed such mandates as violations of evidence-based medical practice standards.

Women making decisions about reproductive healthcare deserve accurate medical information. The ABC link claim, presented as established or contested medical fact in informed consent requirements or crisis pregnancy center materials, constitutes a documented form of medical misinformation with real consequences for healthcare decision-making.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The abortion-breast cancer claim sits within a substantial and well-documented tradition of using the rhetoric and architecture of scientific concern — risk assessment, statistical association, precautionary principle — to advance policy positions that are not themselves grounded in scientific evidence. Understanding this tradition requires examining both the specific claim and the institutional infrastructure that sustains it.

The relationship between politically motivated actors and scientific uncertainty is asymmetric in a structurally important way. Establishing scientific consensus requires extensive, rigorous, replicated research conducted across multiple independent institutions and subject to peer review and methodological critique. Disrupting the public perception of that consensus requires only the amplification of doubt — the selective citation of outlier studies, the assertion of methodological shortcomings, and the insistence that the question remains open. The cost of doubt is lower than the cost of consensus, which creates an inherent asymmetry in political-scientific disputes that favors those with incentive to manufacture uncertainty.

The ABC link claim also illuminates the role of state-level regulatory architecture in maintaining discredited scientific claims. When states mandate that abortion providers read specific risk language — including alleged breast cancer risk — as part of informed consent procedures, they transform a scientific controversy into an official state communication. Patients who receive this information from a licensed medical provider in a clinical setting, as part of a statutory informed consent process, have reasonable grounds for treating it as medically credible. The state imprimatur that attaches to mandatory disclosure language creates a credibility signal that market-based or advocacy-organization claims lack. This mechanism means that the institutional preservation of the ABC link in state law produces ongoing belief in the claim even as the scientific community's consensus strengthens against it.

The history of Charmaine Yoest's career trajectory is instructive. As president of Americans United for Life, she was among the most prominent advocates for state-level abortion restrictions including informed consent mandates. Her appointment as Assistant Secretary for Public Affairs at HHS in 2017 represented an instance of a political advocate for a contested scientific claim being placed in an institutional position with authority over public health communications. During her tenure, the HHS website's language on various reproductive health topics was reviewed and revised. This pattern — in which political advocates for manufactured scientific uncertainty gain institutional positions that allow them to influence official scientific communication — is a mechanism worth mapping carefully for consumers seeking to understand how discredited health claims maintain policy traction.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The abortion-breast cancer claim has been directly addressed by major fact-checking organizations and receives among the clearest verdicts in reproductive health misinformation coverage.

PolitiFact has rated versions of the ABC link claim "False" on multiple occasions, citing the NCI workshop consensus, the Lancet collaborative reanalysis, and ACOG's position. The organization's coverage specifically addresses Charmaine Yoest's epistemic-doubt framing, noting that characterizing the ABC link as an area of ongoing scientific uncertainty misrepresents the state of the evidence. Snopes has similarly addressed the claim in the context of crisis pregnancy center information, rating it as not supported by evidence and providing methodological context for why earlier studies produced misleading results.

FactCheck.org addressed the claim in the context of state informed consent laws, examining the specific evidence cited by proponents and finding that the cited studies have been superseded by higher-quality prospective research. The Washington Post Fact Checker has addressed versions of the claim in the context of congressional testimony and policy advocacy.

The methodological focus that separates good fact-checking on this claim from superficial coverage involves explaining *why* earlier studies found the association that subsequent research rejected — specifically, the response bias problem in retrospective case-control studies. Fact-checking that merely cites institutional consensus ("the NCI says there's no link") without explaining the methodological evolution of the evidence is less durable than coverage that explains the epistemological dynamics: why reasonable-seeming early evidence was flawed, how the scientific community revised its understanding, and why that revision process took the specific form it did.

For healthcare providers and medical educators, the ABC link functions as a case study in communicating settled science against a background of organized political opposition. Medical students and residents trained after the 2003 NCI workshop may encounter patients who have received ABC link claims from crisis pregnancy centers, legislative informed consent scripts, or advocacy websites, and who bring those claims into clinical discussions of reproductive healthcare. Effective patient communication requires not just familiarity with the NCI consensus but understanding of the specific historical and political context of the ABC claim — how it emerged, why it persists, and why the institutional sources patients may have encountered appear credible.

This entry's classification as "politically-manufactured uncertainty" reflects the documented pattern established in the scholarly literature on science communication and political interference: the ABC link is not a case of genuine ongoing scientific debate but of a settled scientific question being represented as unsettled for identifiable political-strategic purposes.
