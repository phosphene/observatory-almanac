## The Claim

In late March 2021, a photograph of an infant covered in a severe, hives-like rash began circulating across Facebook groups, parenting forums, and anti-vaccine communities. The post accompanying the image told a harrowing story: a woman named "Lyndsi" had received her second dose of a COVID-19 vaccine on a Wednesday. By the following Thursday, her breastfed baby was reportedly covered in a head-to-toe rash. By that night, the infant was inconsolable and declining rapidly, prompting a trip to the emergency room. According to the post, the baby was then diagnosed with thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) — a rare and life-threatening blood disorder — alongside dangerously elevated liver enzymes. The infant was hospitalized, received various treatments, and, the post claimed, died.

The story spread virally in a chain-letter format, copied and pasted from user to user, group to group. It appeared on children's health defense pages, personal Facebook timelines, and in communities already skeptical of the newly authorized COVID-19 vaccines. The implicit — and often explicit — suggestion was devastating: that COVID-19 vaccines could pass through breastmilk and kill infants. The claim arrived at a particularly fraught moment. Vaccine rollout was accelerating across the United States, and questions about safety for breastfeeding parents were already swirling without adequate clinical data to settle them. Into this information vacuum, a grieving story reached millions of people. A Facebook user identifying as a registered nurse, "Caitlyn RN," whose timeline featured numerous anti-vaccine posts, amplified the story with additional details and emotional urgency.

At its core, the claim asked people to accept that the mRNA technology in COVID-19 vaccines, administered by injection to mothers, could reach breastmilk in a form potent enough to cause a catastrophic immune and blood-clotting cascade in a nursing infant — ultimately killing him. It was a claim that combined genuine parental anxiety, biological complexity, and zero verifiable facts into a story with enormous emotional velocity.

## What's Actually True

Snopes investigated the claim in late March 2021 and rated it **"Unproven"** — a verdict that reveals as much about what was *not* present in the claim as what was. Crucially, the original post contained no surname for "Lyndsi," no geographic location, no hospital name, no death certificate, and no other identifying information that investigators could use to trace the reported events. The story had been copied and re-shared so many times that any chain of custody leading back to an identifiable source was thoroughly obscured. Fact-checkers could neither confirm the death occurred nor rule it out based on the evidence available.

What investigators *could* examine was the photograph itself and the proposed biological mechanism. On the photograph: the image appeared to show a real rash on a real child's torso. However, viral rashes in infants are exceedingly common, caused by a range of infections including chickenpox, measles, rubella, mononucleosis, roseola, and various herpes simplex strains. Elevated liver enzymes and TTP can both, in rare cases, accompany severe viral infections. The rash shown was consistent with any number of these causes. Without clinical context, no one could determine what caused it.

On the proposed biological mechanism: the claim that mRNA COVID-19 vaccines could harm a breastfed infant requires accepting a chain of events that medical science found implausible. The COVID-19 vaccines authorized at that time — Pfizer-BioNtech and Moderna — use lipid nanoparticles to encapsulate mRNA sequences coding for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. These are injected into muscle tissue. The muscle cells absorb the nanoparticles and briefly produce spike proteins, which the immune system learns to recognize and combat. The mRNA itself degrades within days. It does not enter the bloodstream in any meaningful quantity, let alone reach mammary gland tissue and transfer intact into breastmilk.

The Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine acknowledged, in a formal statement, that the Pfizer-BioNtech trials had excluded lactating individuals, meaning there were no direct clinical data on vaccine safety for nursing mothers at that time. However, the Academy also stated explicitly: "There is little biological plausibility that the vaccine will cause harm." Indeed, early studies that did examine COVID-19 vaccine components in breastmilk found antibodies — the immune-protective proteins the vaccine was designed to generate — transferring to infants through milk. This was considered a potential benefit, offering passive immunity to infants too young to be vaccinated themselves.

TTP — the condition claimed to have killed the infant — is a severe autoimmune blood disorder that causes clotting throughout small blood vessels. While TTP has been associated with a small number of adult COVID-19 vaccine recipients in case reports (primarily related to the adenoviral vector vaccines, not mRNA vaccines), its occurrence in breastfed infants secondary to maternal mRNA vaccination represented a mechanism with no biological basis in known science.

The CDC and major health organizations consistently recommended COVID-19 vaccination for breastfeeding people, noting both the lack of evidence of harm and the active evidence of benefit through antibody transfer. The official guidance did not change in response to this circulating claim, as it provided no verifiable clinical data to evaluate.

In summary: the story cannot be confirmed, the biology it implies is highly improbable, and the photograph provides no specific etiological evidence. The claim is unproven — and unverifiable as stated.

## Why People Believe This

Human beings are story-processing machines. When a mother's name — even just a first name — anchors a tragedy, when a photograph makes the suffering visible, and when the emotional stakes are as high as losing an infant, the story bypasses the evaluative circuits that might otherwise demand evidence. The breastfeeding-vaccine story was custom-built for viral spread, whether intentionally or not.

Several converging factors made this claim especially compelling to its target audience in March 2021.

**The timing.** COVID-19 vaccines had been authorized for emergency use only a few months earlier. Data from clinical trials was still being evaluated in real-world settings. Breastfeeding individuals had been excluded from initial trials — a legitimate gap in the evidence base that created genuine uncertainty. Into that uncertainty, a frightening anecdote arrives with the authority of apparent firsthand witness.

**The absence of data as permission.** When science says "we don't know yet" or "the trials didn't include that population," it is heard by many people as "anything is possible." The Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine's honest acknowledgment of limited data was weaponized: if the experts admit they don't have clinical data for breastfeeding mothers, how can they say it's safe? That question sounds reasonable. But it confuses the absence of direct evidence with the presence of evidence for harm, which is a logical inverse the rumor exploited.

**The chain-letter format.** The post spread not as a news article with a byline, editor, and institutional accountability, but as copied-and-pasted personal testimony. This format signals grassroots authenticity and bypasses the skepticism many people bring to mainstream media. When someone in your mom group shares something directly into the group feed, it feels personal, not institutional — and therefore more trustworthy.

**Anti-vaccine community infrastructure.** The story entered an existing ecosystem of organized vaccine skepticism. Pages like Children's Health Defense had already built large, engaged audiences primed to receive and amplify exactly this kind of claim. The "nurse" figure — "Caitlyn RN" — added professional credibility to the story even though her public Facebook profile displayed an established pattern of anti-vaccine posts. The professional credential lent authority; the community infrastructure provided reach.

**The emotional weight of infant death.** Loss of a child is among the most devastating human experiences imaginable. Claims involving infant death activate deep protective instincts and bypass rational evaluation almost automatically. Grief, fear, and protective rage are not conducive to careful source evaluation. The story asked mothers to look at that photograph, imagine their own baby, and decide: is this a risk you want to take? That is not a question most people answer with "let me check the primary literature."

**Distrust in institutions.** In 2021, polling showed substantial skepticism about COVID-19 vaccines across significant portions of the American public, rooted in distrust of pharmaceutical companies, government health agencies, and media. For those communities, the default was to believe reports of harm over institutional assurances of safety — not because they were irrational, but because their prior experiences with institutions had not always rewarded trust. The story fit a pre-existing interpretive frame.

**The unfalsifiability trap.** The claim was presented in a form that could not be disproven. No last name. No hospital. No location. No way to find a death certificate or medical record. Fact-checkers could say "we cannot verify this," but to skeptical audiences, that sounded like evasion — "they can't disprove it either." An unverifiable claim that fits your worldview is immune to standard debunking.

## Verdict

**Unproven — and biologically implausible as stated.** The claim lacks the minimum verifiable information — a surname, a location, a hospital record, a death certificate — to confirm whether the described event occurred at all. The proposed mechanism, in which mRNA COVID-19 vaccine components transfer through breastmilk in sufficient quantities to cause fatal blood and liver disorders in a nursing infant, is not consistent with the known biology of mRNA vaccines, lipid nanoparticles, or breastmilk composition. Health authorities including the CDC, the Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine, and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists all recommended COVID-19 vaccination for breastfeeding individuals, and this consensus was not revised in light of the circulating claim. The story was emotionally powerful and structurally designed to resist verification. It should not be treated as evidence of vaccine harm.

## The Wider Picture

The breastfeeding-baby claim sits at the intersection of several durable patterns in health misinformation.

**Unfalsifiable anecdote as evidence.** Medical science advances through verifiable, reproducible evidence — controlled trials, peer-reviewed case reports with identifying clinical detail, systematic reviews. The viral anecdote inverts this epistemology: by stripping all identifying information, it becomes immune to investigation while retaining the emotional force of a first-person account. This is not unique to vaccine claims; it appears across contested health narratives from supplement testimonials to miracle drug stories. The pattern is: share an unverifiable story of dramatic harm or benefit, let it spread on emotional resonance, and use its unkillability as evidence of suppression.

**The excluded-population data gap.** Pregnant and breastfeeding individuals have historically been excluded from early clinical trials for liability and ethical reasons, creating an apparently significant but often scientifically less alarming gap than it appears. For the COVID-19 vaccines, this gap was real: breastfeeding individuals had no direct clinical trial data. But the gap was quickly filled by real-world evidence, observational studies, and mechanistic analysis that found no signal of harm and positive evidence of antibody benefit. The claim exploited the gap at its maximum width — before that evidence accumulated — and continued circulating long after it narrowed.

**Rash as proxy for vaccine injury.** Infant rashes are among the most common medical presentations in pediatric practice. They can be caused by dozens of viruses, bacteria, allergens, and autoimmune processes. Using a photograph of an infant rash as visual evidence of vaccine harm is an epistemically dishonest move that nonetheless works extremely well rhetorically. The image feels like proof. Without clinical context, it proves nothing.

**The TTP signal.** The mention of thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura in the claim may reflect some degree of familiarity with early reports of coagulation-related adverse events in COVID-19 vaccine recipients. Vaccine-induced immune thrombocytopenia and thrombosis (VITT) was identified as a rare side effect of adenoviral vector vaccines (AstraZeneca and Johnson & Johnson) in adults. This real — if rare — adult phenomenon may have been grafted onto the fabricated or unverifiable infant story to give it scientific texture. The conflation of VITT in adults receiving adenoviral vaccines with hypothetical TTP in breastfed infants of mRNA vaccine recipients is a category error that nonetheless leveraged real medical vocabulary to seem credible.

**The role of institutional trust deficits.** Countries and communities with higher levels of trust in public health institutions consistently had lower rates of COVID-19 vaccine hesitancy. Claims like the breastfeeding-baby story competed not just with the content of official guidance but with the credibility of the institutions offering it. In communities where that credibility had been eroded — through historical abuses, political polarization, or economic marginalization — unverifiable personal testimony could carry more weight than expert consensus. Addressing vaccine hesitancy requires addressing its upstream causes, not simply fact-checking individual claims.

**Ongoing relevance.** As newer vaccines move through development and additional populations — including breastfeeding individuals — continue to be enrolled in trials later rather than earlier, the epistemological gap this claim exploited will appear in future iterations. The pattern of "anecdote-with-photo + professional amplifier + chain-letter format" is replicable and will be redeployed. Media literacy education that focuses specifically on the difference between unverifiable personal testimonials and verifiable clinical evidence is a recurring need that this case illustrates sharply.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's approach to this claim illustrates several key methodological choices that apply broadly in health misinformation investigations.

**The "Unproven" rating over "False."** Snopes declined to rate the claim "False" despite significant scientific skepticism about the proposed mechanism. This was a defensible choice: rating something False implies investigators have evidence the thing did not occur. Here, they had no such evidence — only an unverifiable story. The "Unproven" rating accurately reflects the investigative limit: we cannot confirm this happened; we also cannot prove it did not. This epistemically honest framing is more defensible than overclaim in either direction and less susceptible to the charge of denying real events.

**Source-tracing the chain letter.** One of the most important investigative moves was attempting to trace the claim back to an identifiable original source. The finding that the story had been copied and pasted — laundered through multiple intermediaries with no link to an original post — is itself significant. It transforms the claim from eyewitness testimony to telephone game. Each copy-paste strips away context, adds emotional framing, and distances the story from any potential verification path.

**Evaluating the amplifier's credibility.** The "Caitlyn RN" figure, presented as a nurse, was evaluated in context: her public Facebook timeline showed a consistent pattern of anti-vaccine content, which is relevant to assessing whether she was updating on new evidence or amplifying material consistent with a pre-formed conclusion. This does not mean her story was false — nurses with anti-vaccine views can have real clinical experiences. But it does contextualize why she was selected as the trusted voice to relay the story, and it signals the community of origin.

**Consulting authoritative but honest sources.** The Academy of Breastfeeding Medicine's frank acknowledgment of limited data was cited rather than elided. Good fact-checking does not pretend scientific certainty where it does not exist. It contextualizes what is and is not known while explaining why the absence of direct data is not equivalent to evidence of harm. This approach is more durable than overclaiming in either direction.

**Image analysis.** The photograph was addressed directly — it appeared to show a real rash on a real infant — but investigators were careful not to overclaim. They could not identify the type of rash from the photograph alone, nor establish its cause. This kind of methodological restraint prevents fact-checkers from inadvertently confirming or denying the underlying claim through overconfident visual analysis.

**Explaining the mechanism clearly.** Rather than simply asserting the claim was implausible, Snopes explained *why* mRNA vaccines are unlikely to transfer harmful substances through breastmilk — describing how lipid nanoparticles work, how mRNA degrades, and what the biological pathway from maternal vaccine to infant harm would have to look like. This educational component is what separates useful fact-checking from mere verdict-issuing. Readers who understand the mechanism are better equipped to evaluate future claims, not just this one.

The epistemological takeaway from how fact-checkers handle this case: "Unproven" is sometimes the most accurate, most honest verdict available — and a better service to public understanding than forcing certainty in either direction. In a landscape where trust is fragile and motivated audiences will seek evidence of bias in every verdict, the disciplined acknowledgment of what we do not and cannot know is itself a form of epistemic integrity.
