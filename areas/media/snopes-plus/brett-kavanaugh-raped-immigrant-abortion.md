# Did Brett Kavanaugh Rule That a Raped Immigrant Teen Must Delay Her Abortion in Order to 'Think About It Maturely'?

## 1. The Claim

During the heated final weeks of Brett Kavanaugh's Supreme Court confirmation process in October 2018, a tweet by user @maydaymindy9 went viral with a devastating claim: "Brett Bart Kavanaugh was the Judge that told the 17 year old immigrant girl that was raped that she couldn't have an abortion until she really really thought about it maturely bc what you do at 17 follows you forever." The tweet was quickly turned into a meme by the liberal Facebook page "The Other 98%" and spread to millions of users at the peak of one of the most polarizing Supreme Court confirmation battles in American history.

The impact of the claim derived from its narrative structure and timing. The underlying argument was one of hypocrisy. Kavanaugh, who had just been accused of sexual assault by Dr. Christine Blasey Ford—an accusation that concerned events when Kavanaugh was 17 years old—was being portrayed as a judge who had lectured a 17-year-old sexual assault survivor about maturity and the permanent consequences of her choices. The implicit rhetorical question was clear: if "what you do at 17 follows you forever" was Kavanaugh's standard, why should it not apply to his own alleged conduct?

The case referenced in the tweet was real. Known as *Garza v. Hargan* (or *Jane Doe v. Hargan*), it was decided by the U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit in October 2017, a year before the confirmation fight. Kavanaugh was a member of the three-judge panel that heard the case, and he did vote to allow the government to delay the young woman's abortion. But the specific language attributed to him—the phrase "think about it maturely" and "what you do at 17 follows you forever"—were not his words. They were fabrications that made the story more narratively compelling at the cost of factual accuracy.

Snopes investigated the claim on October 10, 2018, with journalist Dan MacGuill rating it "Mixture."

## 2. What's Actually True

The core of the claim is that Judge Brett Kavanaugh ruled that a 17-year-old undocumented immigrant could be required to delay obtaining an abortion. This is true. In October 2017, Kavanaugh was on the D.C. Circuit panel that heard an emergency appeal in the case of Jane Doe, a 17-year-old from Central America who had entered the United States without a parent or guardian. As an unaccompanied alien child (UAC), she was placed in the custody of the Office of Refugee Resettlement (ORR). While in ORR custody, she learned she was pregnant and expressed a desire to terminate the pregnancy.

ORR officials refused to facilitate the abortion, instead insisting that Jane Doe undergo counseling, receive an ultrasound, and be given time to consider alternatives. The ACLU and Jane Doe's court-appointed guardian, Rochelle Garza, filed suit on October 13, 2017, arguing that the government was violating Jane Doe's constitutional right to an abortion as recognized in *Roe v. Wade* and that the obstacles constituted an "undue burden" under *Planned Parenthood v. Casey*.

The D.C. Circuit panel initially issued a per curiam order directing that Jane Doe be allowed to obtain an abortion. The government requested a rehearing, and a divided panel then issued a revised order. Kavanaugh dissented from the portion of the order that allowed the abortion to proceed immediately. In his dissent, Kavanaugh argued that the government should be permitted to transfer Jane Doe to an immigration sponsor, rather than facilitating her abortion while she remained in federal custody. He reasoned that an 11-day delay to locate a sponsor was reasonable and would not impose an undue burden on her right to seek an abortion.

Kavanaugh's actual dissent used measured, legalistic language. It did not contain the phrase "think about it maturely" or "what you do at 17 follows you forever." These phrases were added by the tweet's author and the subsequent meme. They were not quotations from the judge, nor paraphrases of his reasoning. They were rhetorical inventions designed to intensify the emotional impact of the claim.

The claim that Jane Doe had been raped is also unsupported by the court record. Her court-appointed guardian testified that Jane became pregnant through a consensual relationship, though some media reports suggested ambiguity about this. In any case, the formal legal proceedings and Garza's written testimony to the Senate Judiciary Committee did not assert that Jane Doe had been raped. The rape claim appears to have been an assumption or invention by those who created and spread the meme, likely because it made the case more morally unambiguous.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's "Mixture" rating was accurate and appropriate for a claim that mixed verified facts with fabricated details. The article correctly identified the real case, the actual content of Kavanaugh's dissent, and the specific embellishments added by the meme. The article also correctly noted that the rape claim was unsupported by available documentation and that Kavanaugh's language was not nearly as dramatic or paternalistic as the meme suggested.

The article's sourcing was strong for a fact-check piece. Snopes relied on the actual court documents, including the complaint filed by Garza and the ACLU, the panel's orders and dissents, and Garza's subsequent Senate testimony. These primary sources provided a reliable basis for establishing what actually happened and what was said.

However, the article could have benefited from greater depth in several areas. First, the legal reasoning in Kavanaugh's dissent was presented accurately but without sufficient analysis of its substance. Kavanaugh's argument that an 11-day delay to locate a sponsor was not an undue burden was based on a specific legal framework that distinguished between government action that actively blocks an abortion and government inaction that fails to facilitate one. This distinction, while legalistic, is important for understanding his reasoning. The article mentioned the "undue burden" standard but did not explore how Kavanaugh applied it.

Second, the article could have explored the broader legal and policy context. The case raised novel questions about the obligations of federal custodians of undocumented minors. The ORR's policy at the time was not to facilitate abortions for minors in its custody, a policy that the Trump administration was actively defending. The case was not simply about Kavanaugh's personal views on abortion but about competing interpretations of the government's obligations under federal law and the Constitution.

Third, the article did not address the rhetorical strategy of the meme itself. The fabrication of the "think about it maturely" language was not accidental; it was a deliberate narrative choice designed to make Kavanaugh appear hypocritical in light of the Blasey Ford allegations. Understanding this strategy helps readers recognize similar tactics in future political disinformation. The article could have served as a case study in how emotionally resonant false details are added to otherwise accurate stories to increase their persuasive power.

Fourth, the article could have discussed the unusual procedural posture of the case and the fact that the D.C. Circuit panel was divided. The full court ultimately vacated the panel's order and granted the government's request for a stay, meaning that Kavanaugh's view did not ultimately prevail. A more complete procedural history would have given readers a better understanding of where Kavanaugh's position stood in relation to the broader judicial consensus.

Finally, the article did not examine the significance of the case being decided before *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* (2022), which overturned *Roe v. Wade*. At the time of *Garza v. Hargan*, the constitutional right to abortion was still the law of the land, and Kavanaugh's position was framed within that legal framework. In a post-Dobbs world, the case takes on additional significance as an early indicator of how Kavanaugh might approach abortion-related cases, which the article could have noted.

## 4. Why This Matters

The Kavanaugh abortion meme case matters because it exemplifies a particularly dangerous form of political disinformation: the false embellishment of an otherwise true story for maximum emotional impact. Unlike entirely fabricated claims, which can sometimes be dismissed as absurd, the Kavanaugh meme contained enough truth to be plausible. A real case, a real judge, a real dissent, and a real vulnerable teenager—these facts were then supplemented with fabricated language and an unsupported rape claim to create a narrative that was devastating, memorable, and false in its specific details.

This "truth-plus-fabrication" pattern is difficult for fact-checkers to counter. A simple "True" or "False" rating cannot capture the complexity: parts of the story are true, parts are false, and the combination creates an overall impression that is misleading. The "Mixture" rating that Snopes used is appropriate but limited; it requires readers to engage with the nuance, and many will not. The meme, by contrast, is simple, memorable, and emotionally satisfying. The correction requires work; the lie does not.

The timing of the meme's spread is also significant. It peaked during one of the most emotionally charged and high-stakes confirmation battles in the Supreme Court's history, with the Kavanaugh-Ford hearings having dominated national headlines just weeks earlier. The audience for the meme was primed to believe negative information about Kavanaugh, and the meme delivered confirmation of their worst suspicions. This is a classic pattern in political disinformation: the most effective lies are those that confirm what the audience already believes about the target.

The case also raises important questions about how judges are evaluated and confirmed. The Jane Doe case was the most frequently cited evidence of Kavanaugh's judicial philosophy during his confirmation battle, and the meme's distortions threatened to replace the actual record with a caricature. This is a risk in any high-stakes confirmation process: the complexity of a judicial record—spanning hundreds of opinions and thousands of pages—can be reduced to a single, emotionally charged story that may or may not be accurately represented.

## 5. Research Notes

The court documents in *Garza v. Hargan* are publicly available and provide a complete procedural history. The case was initially assigned to Judge Ketanji Brown Jackson (now a Supreme Court Justice herself), who ordered ORR to allow Jane Doe to obtain an abortion. The government appealed, and the case went to the D.C. Circuit panel consisting of Kavanaugh, Judge A. Raymond Randolph, and Senior Judge David B. Sentelle, all Republican appointees. The panel initially affirmed Judge Jackson's order in a per curiam ruling, but after the government requested a rehearing, a divided panel issued a revised order that both sides claimed partially as a victory.

Kavanaugh's dissent argued that the government should be given a reasonable amount of time—specifically 11 days—to place Jane Doe with a sponsor before facilitating the abortion. He wrote: "The Government has important interests in facilitating the placement of an unaccompanied alien child with a sponsor and in ensuring the alien child's wellbeing while in its custody." He argued that this brief delay did not constitute an undue burden under existing Supreme Court precedent. The full D.C. Circuit then granted rehearing en banc and vacated the panel's order, meaning the case was ultimately resolved by the full court rather than by Kavanaugh's panel.

The claim that Jane Doe had been raped appears to have originated outside the court record. In a letter to then-Senator Kamala Harris, Roe v. Wade co-counsel Sarah Weddington claimed that Jane Doe had been raped, but this was not part of the official record. The ACLU and Garza characterized the pregnancy differently in various filings. In Garza's written testimony to the Senate Judiciary Committee, she described Jane Doe as a "sexual assault survivor," a characterization that some media outlets treated as confirmation of rape. The distinction between "sexual assault" and "rape" in this context is meaningful, and the ambiguity was exploited by the meme's creators.

The language "what you do at 17 follows you forever" appears to have no connection to any statement by Kavanaugh or any document in the case. Its closest analogue in Kavanaugh's public statements is from his defense against the Blasey Ford allegations, in which he spoke about the importance of not letting accusations from high school define a person's adult life. The meme's creators appear to have inverted this sentiment and attributed the opposite view to Kavanaugh, creating the hypocrisy that was the meme's central rhetorical weapon.

The case was ultimately mooted when Jane Doe obtained an abortion through private arrangements, reportedly with the assistance of the Jane's Due Process organization. The legal questions raised by the case—about the obligations of federal custodians toward unaccompanied minors seeking abortion care—remain unresolved in many jurisdictions and have become more significant in the post-Dobbs legal landscape.

## 6. Verdict

**Snopes Rating: Mixture — Upheld.**

The claim that Brett Kavanaugh ruled that a 17-year-old undocumented immigrant must delay her abortion is partially true: he did vote to allow an 11-day delay while the government attempted to place her with an immigration sponsor. The claim that he used the language "think about it maturely" or told her that "what you do at 17 follows you forever" is false. And the claim that Jane Doe had been raped is not supported by the court record, though there is some ambiguity in secondary sources.

Snopes correctly classified this claim as "Mixture" and provided accurate documentation of the actual case and Kavanaugh's actual dissent. The article could have been strengthened by deeper analysis of Kavanaugh's legal reasoning, the procedural history of the case, and the rhetorical strategy underlying the meme's fabrication of specific language.

The Truth Vault affirms the Snopes rating while noting that this case exemplifies the most pernicious form of political disinformation: embellishment of a true story with emotionally resonant false details. The actual facts of the case—a judge voting to delay a teenager's abortion while she remained in federal custody—are serious enough to warrant discussion without the addition of fabricated language. The addition of the "think about it maturely" and rape claims may have made the story more powerful, but it also made it false, giving those who wished to defend Kavanaugh a legitimate basis for dismissing the criticism entirely. In the end, the embellishment weakened rather than strengthened the critique of Kavanaugh's judicial philosophy.
