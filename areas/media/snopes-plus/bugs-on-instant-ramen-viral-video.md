## §1 — Claim and Verdict

A TikTok video, originally published on January 28, 2024, by a creator known as "Micro Zoom Guy," purported to show tiny bugs — later identified as mites — crawling on uncooked instant ramen noodles when examined under magnification. The video accumulated over 83 million views and was subsequently shared across TikTok, X (formerly Twitter), YouTube, and Facebook. Multiple Snopes readers submitted the video for fact-checking.

Snopes fact-checker Jack Izzo investigated the claim and rated it **"Research In Progress" (Unproven)** in May 2024. The verdict does not rate the claim False; rather, it acknowledges genuine investigative uncertainty. The investigators found sufficient circumstantial evidence to raise doubts about the video's authenticity — particularly regarding the creator's use of non-sterile work gloves, the suspicious localisation of the mite cluster, and the absence of broader infestation evidence — but could not conclusively prove the video was staged.

This entry provides an extended analysis of the video's evidentiary chain, the biology of mite contamination in dry foods, the forensics of video authenticity claims, and the broader social context of microscopy content on social media.

## §2 — Viral Spread and Origin

The original video was posted by TikTok creator "Micro Zoom Guy" (handle: @microzoomguy) on January 28, 2024. The video shows a packet of uncooked instant ramen placed under a microscope, with the camera zooming in on what appears to be small, eight-legged organisms moving on the noodle surface. The scale of magnification made the creatures invisible to the naked eye.

The video's viral trajectory was exceptional: 83 million views on TikTok within four months. It was reshared extensively on X, prompting multiple investigative responses. Crucially, Snopes investigators discovered a second, very similar video by a different creator — the account @mir_glazami_microscopa (Russian for "world through the eyes of a microscope"), operated by Roman Kamburov — which had accumulated over 120 million views. This video predated widespread comparison but showed what appeared to be similar mite organisms on ramen noodles.

The Kamburov connection is significant for two reasons. First, Snopes had previously fact-checked a 2022 video by the same creator — allegedly showing a hot dog under a microscope — and determined it was fake (Evon, D., "A Hot Dog Under a Microscope?" *Snopes*, April 21, 2022). Second, Kamburov himself had admitted to Snopes that approximately 10% of his microscopy content was deliberately faked for entertainment purposes. When contacted for the 2024 ramen investigation, Kamburov confirmed his video was real, attributing the mites to dust mites (order Sarcoptiformes, family Pyroglyphidae) that had accessed the ramen while it was left unattended.

A third video, produced by TikTok user @oneminmicro (Walt), was created as an independent verification attempt. Walt examined his own package of instant ramen under a microscope and found no evidence of bugs. This counterexample adds weight to the sceptical interpretation but cannot be conclusively generalisable — his packet being clean does not mean Micro Zoom Guy's was.

## §3 — Evidence and Epistemic Analysis

### The Three Evidentiary Issues

Snopes' investigation focused on three specific observations that collectively cast doubt on the Micro Zoom Guy video's authenticity:

**1. The Ramen Handling Chain of Custody**

Micro Zoom Guy does not show the ramen being removed from its sealed packaging on camera before it is placed under the microscope. This is a critical forensic gap. The contamination — whether involving mites, detritus, or other materials — could have occurred: (a) before filming began, through pre-contamination of the sample; (b) during handling, through transfer from the creator's hands or gloves. Without continuous on-camera chain of custody from sealed package to microscope, the video cannot establish factory-origin contamination.

This chain-of-custody problem is standard in forensic video analysis. In the absence of documentation of each handling step, alternative explanations for observed phenomena remain live possibilities. Snopes correctly identifies this as a methodological limitation of the claim.

**2. The Gloves**

Micro Zoom Guy wore reusable black-and-red work gloves — as opposed to single-use latex, nitrile, or vinyl gloves worn by both Kamburov and Walt. These reusable work gloves, which appear in all of Micro Zoom Guy's microscopy videos, would accumulate environmental detritus, hair, mites, and other organisms over time. The physical handling of the ramen while wearing these gloves constitutes a plausible contamination pathway.

Dust mites (*Dermatophagoides pteronyssinus* and *D. farinae*) are among the most common components of household dust. Research by Platts-Mills, T. A. E., & Chapman, M. D. (1987). Dust mites: Immunology, allergic disease, and environmental control. *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*, 80(6), 755–775 — established that dust mite populations of tens to hundreds of thousands per gram of household dust are common in temperate climates. The transfer of one or several mites from contaminated gloves to ramen noodles is entirely plausible within an ordinary indoor filming environment.

**3. The Mite Cluster Distribution**

Snopes investigators noted that the mites in the Micro Zoom Guy video appeared in a single, localised cluster surrounded by a significant quantity of general detritus — rather than distributed across the ramen surface as would be typical of an infestation that developed within a manufacturing or storage environment. By contrast, both the Kamburov and Walt videos showed comparatively cleaner ramen noodle surfaces.

If mites had infested ramen during the manufacturing process or in long-term storage, the distribution pattern would likely be more diffuse, and evidence of mite biological activity (frass, cast skins, egg cases) would be visible across a larger surface area. The compact cluster, combined with the surrounding detritus, is more consistent with a recent surface transfer than with an established infestation.

### Arachnological Identification: The Eight-Legged Question

The Snopes investigation included an important taxonomic observation: the organisms in both videos had eight legs, not six. This definitively classified them as **arachnids** (class Arachnida), not insects (class Insecta). More specifically, the eight-legged, microscopic profile indicates a mite — most likely from the superorder Acariformes, which encompasses grain mites, dust mites, mold mites, and flour mites, among thousands of other species.

Snopes attempted to identify the specific species and found probable candidates in three groups:

- **Grain mite** (*Acarus siro* and related species): Common in stored grain products; described in extension resources from Penn State University.
- **Mold mite** (*Tyrophagus putrescentiae*): Associated with mold growth in high-moisture environments; described by Family Handyman (DeBella, T., 2023).
- **Dust mite** (*Dermatophagoides* spp.): Ubiquitous household arachnids; described in NIEHS resources.

The superorder Acariformes alone contains over 45,000 described species (Walter, D. E., & Proctor, H. C., 1999, *Mites: Ecology, Evolution and Behaviour*, CABI Publishing), with potentially many more undescribed. Definitive species identification from a consumer-grade video without a scale bar or substrate reference is not possible.

### Epistemic Novelty: Viral Microscopy Videos and Authenticity Forensics

The Snopes investigation of this video represents an unusual genre of fact-checking: scrutinising the authenticity of a first-person video that purports to show a real-time empirical observation. Most misinformation fact-checking deals with decontextualised photographs, fabricated quotes, or false statistical claims. Video microscopy content introduces a specific authenticity challenge: the technical apparatus (a microscope) confers scientific legitimacy through institutional association, even when the footage itself may be staged.

The phenomenon of **parasocial scientific authority** — where science-adjacent production aesthetics (lab equipment, magnification, white lighting) increase viewer trust in content accuracy regardless of actual methodological rigour — is a documented problem in public understanding of science. Research by Scheufele, D. A., & Krause, N. M. (2019). Science audiences, misinformation, and fake news. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 116(16), 7662–7669 — discusses how visual credibility cues in video content can override sceptical evaluation of the underlying claim.

The Snopes investigation adds an important methodological contribution: explicit identification of the chain-of-custody gap in microscopy videos. This framework — asking whether the sample's journey from source to slide is continuous and uncontaminated — is applicable to any viral microscopy content and constitutes a reusable analytical tool for science communicators and media literacy educators.

## §4 — Food Safety Context

### Mite Contamination in Dry Foods: Are They Common?

The question of whether mite contamination in dry goods like instant noodles is genuinely common has food safety implications beyond the specific video. Regulatory frameworks in the United States permit trace amounts of "natural or unavoidable defects" in food products under the FDA's Defect Levels Handbook, which acknowledges that perfect separation of food from its biological environment during processing and storage is not achievable.

For dry grain-based products, the primary arthropod food safety concerns are:

- **Grain weevils** (*Sitophilus granarius*, *S. oryzae*): Visible to the naked eye; infest stored wheat, rice, and corn.
- **Flour beetles** (*Tribolium confusum*, *T. castaneum*): Small beetles; larvae and adults contaminate wheat flour.
- **Grain mites** (*Acarus siro*): Microscopic; require high humidity to establish populations; responsible for "grocer's itch" dermatitis in grain handlers.

The key point for instant ramen is that the dehydrated, sealed nature of the product constitutes a significant barrier to mite infestation. Mites require moisture levels above approximately 65% relative humidity to thrive (Cunnington, A. M., 1976, "Physical limits for complete development of the grain mite *Acarus siro* L. in relation to its distribution and abundance," *Acarologia*, 17(4), 655–676). Sealed instant ramen packaging, with its moisture-absorbing flavour sachets, creates an environment hostile to mite populations.

Post-purchase, opened or improperly stored ramen could become susceptible to contamination, particularly in humid environments. The most parsimonious explanation for mite presence in Kamburov's video, if genuine, involves post-purchase contamination during storage in a mite-rich environment.

### Instant Ramen Manufacturing and Quality Control

Instant ramen production is conducted at industrial scale with food safety management systems (HACCP — Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points) required under FDA regulations. The claim that mites would be present in sealed, factory-fresh ramen at sufficient density to be immediately visible under low-power magnification is inconsistent with modern HACCP protocols.

Nissin Foods, the original inventor of instant ramen (Momofuku Ando, 1958), and major manufacturers including Maruchan, Nongshim, and Samyang all operate under HACCP compliance and CGMP (Current Good Manufacturing Practice) regulations. Visible mite infestations would constitute a reportable food safety incident.

## §5 — Expert Voices and Institutional Record

The Snopes investigation drew on the following source voices:

1. **Roman Kamburov** (@mir_glazami_microscopa): Confirmed his video was real, attributed the mites to dust mites, and noted the food safety implication: food should be stored in airtight packaging.

2. **Snopes prior fact-check (2022)**: The same creator's hot dog microscopy video had previously been found fake. Kamburov's own disclosure that 10% of his content is staged was both an admission and a context-setter.

3. **Walt** (@oneminmicro): Independent verification attempt. Found no mites in his own ramen, adding weight to the sceptical hypothesis — though his clean sample cannot generalise to other packets.

The investigation did not include testimony from: a food safety microbiologist, an acarologist (mite specialist), or a manufacturer representative. This expert gap is the primary limitation of the Snopes investigation, and represents a researchable extension of the work.

## §6 — EARC Classification, Research Gap, and Implications

### EARC Classification

- **Empirical (E): YES** — The claim is empirically testable: mite contamination in sealed ramen can be investigated through systematic sampling and microscopy by food safety researchers.
- **Amplified (A): YES** — The video's 83-million-view TikTok performance and cross-platform distribution via algorithmic recommendation represent maximal social media amplification. The gross-out factor of bugs on food is a known amplification driver.
- **Recurring (R): NO** — This claim is tightly coupled to specific video content published in 2024 and does not have the multi-decade recurrence pattern of other Truth Vault entries. It may recur if similar videos are produced.
- **Compound (C): YES** — The claim involves multiple nested questions: Is the video authentic? If so, are the mites from the factory or introduced by the creator? What species are they? Are they a food safety concern? Each question carries distinct evidentiary requirements.

### Research Gap

The central gap is the absence of peer-reviewed food safety literature on mite contamination rates in sealed instant noodle products. A systematic sampling study — examining factory-sealed ramen packets from multiple manufacturers and geographies under controlled magnification — would definitively resolve the core question raised by the video.

A secondary gap is the absence of a generalised framework for assessing viral microscopy video authenticity. The chain-of-custody analysis developed implicitly by the Snopes investigation should be formalised and published as a media literacy tool.

A tertiary gap is the under-theorised relationship between science-adjacent production aesthetics and audience credibility attribution. Understanding how microscope footage generates parasocial scientific authority would improve both platform moderation policy and science communication practice.

### Implications

The Snopes verdict of "Research In Progress" is epistemically honest, appropriate, and itself valuable as a model of fact-checking under genuine uncertainty. The investigation demonstrates that not all viral claims admit clean True/False resolution; some require the infrastructure of ongoing research. However, the intermediate verdict makes it difficult to broadly communicate: audiences are less equipped to share "unproven" verdicts than "False" verdicts, and the nuanced analysis may be lost in cross-platform retransmission of the fact-check itself.

For food consumers: the practical safety implication is that instant ramen in sealed packaging is produced under food safety protocols inconsistent with factory-origin mite infestation; post-purchase storage in humid, uncontrolled environments is the more plausible contamination pathway if any is real. The advice to store food in airtight containers remains good practice regardless of the video's authenticity.
