# Bulls are enraged by the color red

## 1. The Claim

Few images are as culturally entrenched as the charging bull and the red cape. In cartoons, advertisements, tourist imagery, and everyday metaphor, red functions as the universal trigger of bovine fury. The sequence is assumed to be causal: matador flourishes red cloth, bull sees red, bull charges. The redness is the mechanism.

This belief is not limited to casual cultural transmission. It appears in textbooks, in the narration accompanying bullfights, in the design of safety warnings around agricultural settings ("wear bright colors, not red"). The claim implies something specific about bovine visual cognition: that bulls process the color red as a threat or irritant, and that this processing reliably triggers aggression. It is not merely that bulls are aggressive — it is that red specifically activates that aggression.

The popularity of phrases like "seeing red" (meaning becoming enraged) reflects and reinforces the cultural assumption that red and rage are naturally connected, and the charging bull serves as the animal illustration of this connection.

The claim is wrong in its core mechanism, though it contains a grain of truth about the actual trigger — just not the one people think.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

Bulls are **dichromatic**, meaning they have two types of cone photoreceptors in their retinas rather than the three possessed by humans. Human trichromatic vision allows us to distinguish red, green, and blue wavelengths with fine resolution. Dichromatic vision, the form found in most non-primate mammals, does not permit this kind of color discrimination. Studies of bovine retinal structure confirm that cattle lack the long-wavelength cone type that enables red-green discrimination in humans.

This means bulls cannot meaningfully distinguish red from many other colors. To a bull, a red cape and a green cape of similar luminance are likely to appear similar — they register as a stimulus object of a certain shape, brightness, and movement pattern, not as specifically "the red object."

The evidence for this goes beyond anatomical inference. In 2007, the American television program *MythBusters* conducted a direct test: they presented bulls with flags of multiple colors — red, blue, and white — in a rodeo arena. The bulls charged all the flags with no apparent preference for the red flag over the others. What determined charging behavior was not the color of the flag but its movement. A stationary red flag was largely ignored; a moving flag of any color attracted attention and, under the conditions of a challenging situation, triggered charging behavior.

The actual trigger in bullfighting — and in cattle charging behavior more generally — is **movement**. The *muleta*, the red cape used in the final stage of the traditional bullfight, functions as a movement cue. When the matador swings it, the movement stimulates the bull's attention and aggression. The bull tracks the moving object and charges toward it. The color is incidental to this process.

Research in animal behavior supports this account. Cattle are naturally responsive to sudden movements in their visual field — this is consistent with prey-animal threat-detection systems, which prioritize motion detection over color discrimination. A predator's approach in grass would be detected by movement well before color became relevant.

**Why is the cape red, then?** The red *muleta* is traditional in the final third (*tercio de muerte*) of the Spanish corrida. The traditional explanation — widely repeated and almost certainly accurate — is that the red color serves the spectators rather than the bull. Red hides bloodstains. Both the bull and the matador bleed during the fight, and a red cloth shows less evidence of this to the audience. A white or pale cape would become visibly stained, creating a more viscerally graphic spectacle. The red cape maintains a visual cleanliness that audience sensibility, at least historically, found preferable.

A secondary traditional explanation is that the red and gold of the matador's costume and the red cape create vivid visual drama for the human audience. Bullfighting is theater as much as sport, and the visual palette is designed for human aesthetic experience.

The larger *capote* — the pink-and-yellow cape used in the earlier stages of the fight — is not red at all. If bulls were specifically triggered by red, one might expect the earlier stage to use red as well, but tradition has settled on magenta/pink and yellow. The bull charges the *capote* with the same readiness it charges the *muleta*, consistent with the movement-trigger hypothesis and inconsistent with the color-trigger hypothesis.

It should also be noted that bulls used in bullfighting have typically been selected and raised in ways that maximize their general aggression and responsiveness. They are in an unfamiliar, stressful environment, surrounded by noise and humans, subjected to physical provocation from picadors and banderilleros before the matador appears. By the time the *muleta* is produced, the bull is already physiologically aroused. It would charge a blue cape, a yellow cape, or a white cape with equal readiness.

## 3. Why People Believe This

Several factors combine to make this myth exceptionally persistent.

**Visual narrative coherence:** The image of the red cape and the charging bull is visually compact and narratively complete. It offers a clean cause-and-effect story — specific stimulus produces specific response — that is more intellectually satisfying than the messier truth (bull is stressed, exhausted, and provoked by movement, any movement, in a context of general arousal). Simple stories with clear triggers are cognitively preferred over complex stories with multivariate causes.

**Cultural saturation:** The bull-and-red-cape image has been reproduced so many times in so many contexts — bullfighting imagery, cartoons, safety signage, idiom — that it no longer needs active argument to maintain itself. It is a cultural default, maintained by sheer repetition. When a belief is reproduced enough times, the reproductions themselves become evidence for people who have not examined the claim.

**The color-emotion connection:** The association between red and strong emotion — danger, passion, anger — is deeply embedded in human culture and possibly has some basis in human physiology (red may be associated with arousal in some contexts). Extending this association to non-human animals makes intuitive sense even if it lacks empirical support for dichromatic species. The myth slots neatly into a pre-existing cultural schema about red's psychological potency.

**Confirmation bias in observation:** People watching bullfighting see a red cape and a charging bull. The causal connection seems self-evident. It requires no additional reasoning: the red is there, the charge happens, therefore the red caused the charge. Alternative hypotheses — the movement caused the charge, the physical stimulus from the banderillas caused the charge, the general arousal of the arena environment caused the charge — are less salient to a naive observer than the vivid red color of the cloth.

**The myth is not costly to believe:** Unlike some myths that cause direct harm when acted upon, the bull-red myth has relatively low practical stakes for most people. People who do not work around cattle do not need to adjust their behavior based on this belief. The myth spreads because there is no immediate feedback mechanism that corrects it — you believe it, you pass it on, nothing bad happens. Myths with low correction costs tend to have long lifespans.

**Traditional narratives within bullfighting:** The bullfighting tradition itself has not historically been invested in correcting this belief. For audiences who are watching the spectacle, the narrative that the matador is controlling the bull's rage through the red cloth adds to the drama. The actual mechanism — movement control — is less romantically compelling. Traditions are maintained partly by their narratives, and the red-trigger narrative serves the tradition.

---

## 4. Verdict

**False in its core mechanism, grounded in real bull behavior.** Bulls cannot distinguish red from other colors and are not specifically triggered by red. What triggers charging in bullfighting is movement, combined with a context of general physiological arousal from stress, fatigue, and direct physical provocation. The red cape is red for the benefit of human spectators — it hides bloodstains and provides visual drama — not because it affects the bull's behavior. The *MythBusters* test and the underlying bovine visual biology both confirm this. The claim is distorted-but-grounded in the sense that bulls really do charge in bullfighting and the cape really is red, but the causal mechanism is wrong.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The bull-and-red-cape myth, while minor in isolation, connects to a broader set of questions about animal cognition, visual perception, and how humans project their own sensory experience onto other species.

**Dichromacy across mammals:** Most non-primate mammals, including dogs, cats, horses, and cattle, are dichromatic. They have two types of cones rather than three, and their color discrimination is substantially reduced compared to humans. This is not a deficiency from the animal's perspective — it reflects evolutionary tradeoffs between different visual capacities. Dichromatic mammals typically have superior motion detection and low-light vision compared to trichromatic species. Cattle, as prey animals, benefit from broad peripheral vision and motion sensitivity; fine color discrimination offers fewer survival advantages in their ecological context.

**Anthropomorphic projection:** The bull-red myth is a case study in anthropomorphic projection — the attribution of human perceptual and psychological processes to non-human animals. Humans experience red as a vivid, emotionally charged color; we project that experience onto the bull. This projection is natural and often useful (it allows us to empathize with animals, which motivates their welfare) but can systematically mislead us about what animals actually perceive and respond to.

Understanding that cattle respond to movement, not color, has practical agricultural implications. Cattle handling — moving animals through chutes, into trailers, or through processing facilities — is more efficient and less stressful when it accounts for what actually triggers bovine alertness and panic. Temple Grandin's influential work on cattle handling design is built on actual bovine sensory biology: cattle are distracted by sudden movements, high-contrast visual transitions, and shadows, but not by specific colors. Low-stress handling designs that account for bovine perception reduce injury rates to both animals and handlers.

**The bullfighting context:** The myth's persistence within bullfighting culture raises questions about the relationship between tradition and factual accuracy. Bullfighting as a practice is in declining attendance in Spain and Latin America, partly due to changing cultural attitudes toward animal welfare. In this context, the factual question — what actually triggers the bull's behavior — intersects with ethical and political questions about the practice. Correcting the color myth does not resolve those debates, but it does contribute to a more accurate understanding of what the animals experience.

**Red in human visual culture:** The cultural association of red with danger and urgency has genuine utility — red traffic lights, red warning labels, red emergency stop buttons. These applications leverage human color perception effectively. The problem arises when the same cultural associations are misapplied to species whose color vision works differently. Red-based warning systems for dichromatic animals, or assumptions about animal behavior based on human color associations, reflect a failure to account for interspecies perceptual differences.

**Related myths about animal vision:** The bull-red myth belongs to a family of myths about animal color perception and behavior: that bees see flowers as humans see them (they see ultraviolet, invisible to humans), that sharks are attracted to bright colors (evidence is mixed and species-dependent), that goldfish can only remember things for a few seconds (false; goldfish have memory capacities lasting months). In each case, the myth reflects human assumptions about what animals perceive rather than actual investigation of animal sensory biology.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The bull-and-red-cape claim is one of the more tractable myths for fact-checking purposes because the core empirical question — can bulls distinguish red from other colors? — has a clear answer grounded in well-established visual biology, and the movement-trigger hypothesis has been directly tested in informal but illustrative experiments.

**Snopes** and similar major fact-checking platforms have addressed this claim, consistently rating it as false or mostly false. The standard correction notes bovine dichromacy and the movement trigger. These treatments are generally accurate and accessible.

**The MythBusters test** (Season 3, Episode 1, 2007) is often cited in popular fact-checking treatments. Its methodology was informal — it was a television production, not a peer-reviewed experiment — but its results were clear and consistent with the underlying biology. Fact-checkers should cite it for illustrative value while noting that the scientific case is grounded in retinal anatomy, not solely in the MythBusters result.

**The primary scientific basis** is in the animal vision literature. Research on bovine retinal structure, cone photoreceptor distribution, and comparative vertebrate vision provides the foundational evidence. Fact-checkers should note that this is not an area of active scientific controversy — the dichromacy of cattle is established and not contested.

**The nuance to preserve:** While the color trigger is clearly false, the claim is "distorted but grounded" because bull aggression in bullfighting is real. The distortion is in the mechanism: real bull behavior (charging, aggression) + wrong trigger (red color) rather than + right trigger (movement + arousal context). Fact-checking treatments should acknowledge the reality of the behavior while correcting the mechanism.

**Misapplication risk:** Fact-checkers should note that correcting this myth does not make bullfighting less dangerous or the bull less aggressive. People who work around cattle should not conclude from this correction that cattle are safe — they should conclude that cattle are responsive to movement and approach angle, not to the color of clothing. The practical implications of the correction point toward safer cattle handling, not toward reduced alertness around bulls.

**The broader pattern of animal cognition myths:** The bull-and-red-cape case belongs to a pattern of myths about animal cognition and perception that consistently turns out, on investigation, to reflect human projection onto animal experience. The corrective is not to conclude that animals have simpler inner lives than we imagined, but to conclude that their inner lives are *different* from ours in ways shaped by their specific evolutionary histories and ecological contexts. A bull perceives a world organized around motion, contrast, and spatial threat rather than the color-coded world of human experience. Understanding the actual perceptual world of the animals we interact with — whether we are working with cattle, training horses, or managing wildlife — is both more accurate and more practically useful than assuming they experience the world as we do.

**Snopes and comparable platforms** have addressed this claim directly. The corrective treatments are accurate and well-sourced, typically citing the visual biology of cattle, the movement-trigger mechanism, and the MythBusters experiment. Fact-checkers beginning with the Snopes treatment will find a sound starting point; verification against primary visual biology literature (particularly studies of bovine retinal structure and the comparative vertebrate vision literature) will confirm the core claims. Researchers in this space include Ron Douglas and colleagues, whose work on comparative vertebrate color vision provides detailed anatomical and functional data on dichromacy across mammalian species.

**On the MythBusters methodology:** Television experiments like the MythBusters bull test have limited scientific standing — they are not peer-reviewed, cannot control all relevant variables, and may be edited for dramatic effect. Fact-checkers should cite them for illustrative value only, always grounding the claim in the underlying biological evidence. In this case, the visual biology is conclusive enough that the MythBusters result is corroborative rather than foundational. The retinal anatomy of cattle is a settled question in comparative vertebrate neuroscience; it does not depend on what any individual bull did or did not charge in a 2007 television segment.
