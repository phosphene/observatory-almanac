# Can You Get a Free Bus Ticket by Doing 20 Squats in Romania?


## 1. The Claim

In mid-2022, social media users across multiple platforms began sharing images of a woman performing squats in what appeared to be a public transit kiosk. The accompanying text claimed, in various formulations, that residents of Romania could pay for a bus ticket by performing 20 squats, measured by a device that issued a ticket upon completion. Posts appeared on Facebook, Twitter/X, Reddit's r/interestingasfuck, Imgur, and other platforms, reaching substantial audiences on each. A frequently shared Twitter post from a user with a history of sharing unusual global innovations attributed the scheme to a program in "Cluj-Napoca, Romania," where "a device measures the squats and issues a bus ticket valid for a trip in the CTP Cluj Napoca network."

The claim belongs to a recurring genre of viral social media content centred on "clever solutions from other countries" — stories that serve the double function of presenting an engaging novelty and implicitly critiquing the comparatively stodgy public policy of the reader's home country. Such stories are not inherently false; they frequently document real innovations. But they are structurally vulnerable to exaggeration of scope (a single pilot becomes "Romania does this") and to temporal flattening (a limited-duration program is presented as a standing policy).

Snopes fact-checker Aleksandra Wrona investigated the claim in June 2023, rating it "Mostly True" while noting that the program was limited to a single city and ran for defined periods rather than being a permanent national or city-wide policy.


## 2. What's Actually True

The "health ticket" program is genuine, documented, and well-sourced across multiple primary and secondary records.

**Program origination and structure.** The initiative was created by the Sports Festival organisation — a cultural and sporting events promoter based in Cluj-Napoca — in collaboration with the Cluj-Napoca City Hall and the local council. The first edition launched in August 2020 and ran through December 2020, conceived as part of the European Week of Sport. The European Week of Sport is an annual initiative supported by the European Commission's Directorate-General for Education, Youth, Sport and Culture, designed to promote physical activity and healthy lifestyles across EU member states.

The mechanism was as described in viral posts: a specialised kiosk installed on Memorandumului Street in central Cluj-Napoca used a built-in camera system to track and count bodily movements. Participants performing 20 squats within a two-minute window received a single ticket valid for one journey on the CTP Cluj Napoca urban public transport network. Disabled and elderly residents could ride free without any exercise requirement.

**Multiple programme editions.** The scheme proved popular enough domestically and attracted sufficient international press attention that Sports Festival implemented subsequent editions. The second edition ran from August 2021 through January 2022. In both editions, the program accumulated substantial participation: Sports Festival reported that citizens performed over 2.3 million squats across the first two phases, a figure cited in programme promotional materials and covered in international press.

**The 2022 evolution.** By the third iteration in 2022, Sports Festival modified the concept. Rather than squats — a compound strength exercise with significant variation in form and accessibility across different age groups and fitness levels — the updated "health ticket 2.0" required participants to complete a 500-metre bicycle ride in under three minutes. The bicycle kiosk was located at the station in front of the Central store in Cluj-Napoca. Organisers explicitly cited accessibility as the rationale: cycling was deemed a movement available to "everyone, regardless of age and physical condition," making it more inclusive than the squat format that had defined the first versions.

**International recognition.** The program received coverage in European civic and urban innovation media, including TheMayor.EU, 3SeasEurope, and international general interest outlets. The Cluj-Napoca City Hall formalised its support through official council resolution — Resolution 255/2022 — providing legal backing for the 2022 edition.


## 3. The Epistemic Gap

The "Mostly True" rating with the qualifier that the program was city-specific and time-limited reflects a real but minor epistemic gap. The viral posts characterised a bounded municipal pilot as if it were a general feature of Romanian public transport, which elides important distinctions. But the story also raises more interesting and underexplored questions about what the health ticket program actually demonstrates.

**Scope flattening: the city-to-country elision.** The majority of viral posts attributed the scheme simply to "Romania" rather than to Cluj-Napoca specifically. This is a common pattern in feel-good innovation stories: a local initiative — conceived, funded, and managed by a single city's administration and an independent sporting organisation — becomes synecdochically attributed to the entire national infrastructure. Romania as a country did not adopt any such policy; no national law or national transport authority created squat-for-transit provisions. The CTP Cluj Napoca network is Cluj-Napoca's urban transport system, not Romania's national transport system. The program's geographic footprint was a single kiosk on a single street in one city of roughly 300,000 inhabitants.

**Temporal presentism.** As of the Snopes check in June 2023, the status of the program was uncertain. The documented editions ran through early 2022; no subsequent edition had been announced or confirmed. Social media posts continued to circulate through 2023 and beyond, presenting the scheme as a current feature of Cluj-Napoca public transport, which may not have been accurate at the time of any given resharing.

**The design novelty is genuinely understudied.** Beyond the scope corrections, the health ticket program raises substantive questions about gamified public health intervention design that the viral discourse entirely bypasses. The scheme embeds a behavioural economics mechanism — immediate, tangible reward for a single instance of exercise — that differs meaningfully from both traditional public health messaging (informational) and financial incentive programmes (cash or discounts).

The psychological mechanism at work is what behavioural economists describe as "immediacy" in reward structuring (Thaler and Sunstein, 2008, *Nudge*, Yale University Press). Traditional exercise promotion frequently emphasises long-term health benefits, which are temporally distant and probabilistic. The health ticket collapsed the temporal gap: the reward was instantaneous and certain — a bus ticket, issued within seconds of completing the squats. Research on immediate reward structures for health behaviours suggests they can be effective for initiating behaviour even if their long-term behavioural change effects are modest (Volpp et al., 2008, *JAMA*, vol. 300, no. 22, pp. 2631–2637, on financial incentives for smoking cessation as a comparison point for immediacy in health incentive design).

**The 2.3 million squats figure deserves scrutiny as a metric.** Sports Festival's headline claim that participants performed over 2.3 million squats across the program's first two editions is an impressive aggregate but must be contextualised. If we assume the program ran for approximately 10 months across its first two editions (August 2020–December 2020 and August 2021–January 2022), 2.3 million squats at 20 squats per ticket represents approximately 115,000 tickets issued, or roughly 575 tickets per working day. For a city of 300,000 with a transit system serving a much larger metropolitan area, this is a real but not transformative ridership contribution. The programme's significance is primarily symbolic and cultural rather than quantitatively measurable as a public health intervention.

**The cross-platform virality tells its own story about information desire.** The health ticket narrative recirculated globally across dozens of countries whose transit systems, public health frameworks, and municipal governance structures it had no direct relevance to. Its appeal was aspirational rather than informational: audiences valued it as evidence that different approaches to urban life were possible, as an implicit criticism of their own cities' failure to make similar investments in creative policy design. This aspirational framing drove engagement independent of the story's factual accuracy, which is why the city/country elision and the temporal status uncertainty were not corrected in most reshares — the emotional payoff was in the concept, not in the administrative specifics.


## 4. Who Spread It and Why

The health ticket story's viral mechanics reflect the intersection of several distinct appeal structures.

**Sports Festival's own promotional strategy.** The originating organisation, Sports Festival, is a commercial events promoter whose annual festival is one of Romania's largest music and arts events. The health ticket program served as a particularly effective piece of branded public interest programming — the kind of civic engagement initiative that generates favourable coverage for the associated brand at minimal financial cost. Sports Festival's YouTube videos documenting the program accumulated substantial view counts; the organisation's documentation of the 2.3 million squat milestone reads as a promotional success metric as much as a public health report.

**EU institutional framing.** The program's origin within the European Week of Sport gave it institutional credibility and EU political backing. This framing positioned it within a recognised pro-active public health policy tradition, making it more likely to be picked up by urban innovation media outlets that curate examples of successful policy experimentation. The EU imprimatur distinguished it from a simple commercial stunt.

**The "other countries do it better" narrative.** As noted above, the story functions as a form of comparative civic criticism — a demonstration that other political cultures are capable of creative, playful, human-centred policy innovations. This narrative has particular appeal among audiences who engage critically with their own countries' policy failures. The story circulated heavily in English-language social media primarily oriented toward audiences in the United States, United Kingdom, and Australia — countries where public transit culture and public health policy share different contours than in Cluj-Napoca's context.


## 5. What Matters for Evidence Literacy

The health ticket story is unusually useful as a case study in the specific information distortion induced by scope flattening and temporal ambiguity.

**Scope flattening is one of the most common distortions in feel-good innovation virality.** Local pilots, experimental programs, and one-city initiatives are routinely attributed to entire countries because the country-level attribution makes a better story and is harder to fact-check. The corrective discipline — asking "where exactly, operated by whom, at what scale, during what period?" — is always worth applying to innovation stories, because the answers frequently reveal that the innovation's actual implementation is more modest than its viral framing suggests.

**Temporal ambiguity in viral content is a structural problem.** Social media posts rarely timestamp their claims about current-status programs. A photograph taken in 2021 of a Cluj-Napoca squat kiosk, reshared in 2023 or 2024, carries no intrinsic signal of whether the program is currently operational. This is rarely malicious; it is an artefact of how content resharing strips context. The responsible consumer of such content should ask: when was this current, and is it still?

**"Mostly True" is a verdict worth taking seriously.** The claim as fact-checked is substantially accurate — this program existed, it worked as described, it was genuinely popular. The qualifications are real but they do not undermine the core claim. The story is a legitimate example of creative civic programming, even if framed with more generality than it merits.


## 6. Verdict and Confidence

**Snopes rating: Mostly True.** The core claim — that a program exists in Romania where doing 20 squats earned a free bus ticket — is accurate. The program was real, technologically implemented, well-documented, and ran across multiple editions. The key qualifications are that it was implemented in one city (Cluj-Napoca) rather than nationally, ran for defined periods rather than as a permanent policy, and had evolved in format by 2022.

**EARC assessment:** The primary gap is in *Context* — the omission of Cluj-Napoca's city-specific scope in favour of national-level attribution. The evidence itself is well-documented; no claim elements are substantially false.

**Gap category: accurate-but-incomplete.** The core claim is accurate. The incompleteness consists of scale and temporal framing rather than factual error.

**Practical upshot:** This story functions well as an example of civic gamification design. Its lessons for public health policy design — immediacy of reward, playfulness, social visibility of participation — are genuinely interesting and transferable. Its direct applicability to other cities' transit systems requires significant adaptation to local governance, funding, and cultural context.
