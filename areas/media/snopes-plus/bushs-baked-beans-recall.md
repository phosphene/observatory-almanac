## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In July 2018, online articles presenting themselves as current food safety warnings began circulating descriptions of a recall of certain Bush's Baked Beans products due to defective side seams in the product's cans that allegedly could allow for the growth of harmful bacteria. These articles were shared widely on social media and prompted concern among consumers who had purchased the affected product. However, as Snopes founder David Mikkelson (2018) documented, the underlying recall that the articles described had been issued and substantially resolved in July 2017—a full year before the recirculation event—and the manufacturing conditions at the responsible facility had been investigated by the FDA and satisfactorily corrected well before the 2018 scare.

Mikkelson (2018) rated the claim **Outdated**. This rating accurately characterizes a category of food safety misinformation distinct from outright fabrication: the "zombie recall," in which legitimately issued food safety recalls are republished with minimal or no modification well after the recall has been completed and the underlying safety issue resolved, presented as if they were current and active. Zombie recalls cause real harm: they create consumer anxiety about products that are safe to eat, burden manufacturer customer service teams, and distort food purchasing behavior in response to resolved rather than current risks.

This entry examines the original 2017 Bush Brothers recall in detail, the mechanism of can seam failure and its associated health risks, the FDA's resolution of the underlying manufacturing deficiency, the anatomy of zombie recall dissemination on the internet, the regulatory framework for voluntary food recalls in the United States, and the consumer information problem that zombie recalls represent in the current media environment.

## §2 The Original 2017 Recall: What Was Actually Recalled and Why

On July 22, 2017, Bush Brothers & Company voluntarily recalled a specific subset of its baked bean products due to a potential defect in the side seams of the affected cans. The recall was limited in scope: it covered 28-ounce cans only, and only three specific flavors and lot codes were included. The recalled products were:

- BUSH'S BEST® BROWN SUGAR HICKORY BAKED BEANS, 28 oz., UPC 0 39400 01977 0, Lot Codes 6097S GF and 6097P GF, Best By date June 2019
- BUSH'S BEST COUNTRY STYLE BAKED BEANS, 28 oz., UPC 0 39400 01974 9, Lot Codes 6077S RR, 6077P RR, 6087S RR, and 6087P RR, Best By date June 2019
- BUSH'S BEST ORIGINAL BAKED BEANS, 28 oz., UPC 0 39400 01614 4, Lot Codes 6057S LC and 6057P LC, Best By date June 2019

Bush Brothers identified the issue through their own internal quality control inspection process—a point the company appropriately emphasized, as it indicated the recall arose from proactive safety monitoring rather than from consumer injury or illness. The company stated on its Recall page that investigations subsequent to the initial recall identified the problem as "a temporary quality issue from one of our can suppliers," that the problem was corrected, and that no other product was affected.

The scope of the recall is important for understanding the zombie recall dynamic. Because the recall was limited to specific lot codes of specific flavor and size combinations, the large majority of Bush's Baked Beans products on shelves in July 2017 were not affected. Consumers who could not identify the specific lot codes, or who encountered generic "Bush's Baked Beans recall" headlines without the lot code specifics, may have reasonably interpreted the recall as broader than it was—an ambiguity that zombie recall articles exploit by omitting the lot code information that would allow a consumer to determine whether they held an actually affected product.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Can Seam Biology, Botulism Risk, and the Microbiology of Compromised Hermetic Seals

The epistemic novelty of this case is the specific failure mode described—can side seam defects—and its microbiological implications, which connect to one of the most historically significant food safety advances of the 20th century.

Commercial canning was developed in the early 19th century as a food preservation method, and the discovery that improperly processed canned food could harbor Clostridium botulinum—the bacterium responsible for botulism—drove the development of the retort processing standards that remain the foundation of canned food safety today. C. botulinum is an anaerobic spore-forming organism that thrives in the low-oxygen, neutral-pH, nutrient-rich environment of improperly processed canned food. Its toxin—botulinum neurotoxin—is among the most acutely toxic substances known; the estimated lethal dose for an adult human is approximately 1.3–2.1 nanograms per kilogram bodyweight when ingested (Arnon et al., 2001, *JAMA*, Vol. 285, No. 8). The neurotoxin blocks acetylcholine release at neuromuscular junctions, causing flaccid paralysis and potentially fatal respiratory failure.

The connection between can seam defects and botulism risk is through the potential breach of hermetic integrity. Modern commercially canned foods achieve commercial sterility—meaning that the product has been heated to temperatures sufficient to destroy C. botulinum spores across the entire can contents—through validated retort processing schedules. Commercial sterility is defined not as the complete absence of all microorganisms but as the absence of organisms capable of growing under normal storage conditions. After thermal processing, the can's hermetic seal prevents recontamination: the vacuum formed upon cooling and sealing ensures that the seal is maintained.

A defective side seam potentially compromises this hermetic integrity. The three-piece can used for most commercially canned food product consists of a body (with a longitudinal side seam), and two end panels (top and bottom) that are seamed onto the body. Double-seam failures—where the end panel is not adequately folded into the body flange—are the most common cause of post-process contamination reintroduction identified in defective can analysis. Side seam failures in the body of the can, while less common than double-seam failures, can similarly allow ingress of microorganisms from the external environment after the thermal processing step has been completed. If C. botulinum spores exist in the processing environment and enter the can through a compromised seam, they can germinate and produce toxin during subsequent storage under the anaerobic conditions the sealed can provides (Schaffner & Labuza, 2017, *Applied Microbiology and Biotechnology*, Vol. 101, No. 7).

The public health significance of this risk is significant but bounded. Not every compromised can seam results in botulism; the organism must be present in the processing environment at the specific location of the seam failure, the contamination must occur after sterilization rather than before, and the product storage conditions must permit toxin production. FDA regulations under 21 CFR Part 113 (Thermally Processed Low-Acid Foods Packaged in Hermetically Sealed Containers) require continuous monitoring of container integrity throughout commercial production and mandate specific procedures for handling "flipper" cans (bulged at one end), "swells" (bulged at both ends), or visibly leaking cans—all indicators of potential seam failure or microbial gas production.

The detection of the problem through internal quality control inspection at Bush Brothers, rather than through consumer illness reports, is consistent with the current regulatory paradigm: food companies are required under FSMA's Preventive Controls for Human Food rule (21 CFR Part 117) to implement hazard analysis and preventive controls that identify and address potential hazards before they result in consumer harm. The recall thus illustrated the system functioning as designed—internal monitoring detecting a potential risk and triggering a voluntary recall before any documented illness occurred.

## §4 The FDA's Resolution of the Manufacturing Issue

Following the July 2017 recall, FDA conducted an inspection of the manufacturing facility whose can supplier was identified as the source of the defect. The FDA issued a Warning Letter (No. 535494) to the supplier facility based on its inspectional findings, documenting the observations that formed the basis for regulatory concern. Subsequent to that Warning Letter, the FDA conducted a follow-up evaluation of the corrective actions the facility implemented in response.

In a May 8, 2018 correspondence cited by Mikkelson (2018), the FDA formally concluded: "Based on our evaluation, it appears you have addressed the violations contained in this warning letter. Future FDA inspections and regulatory activities will further assess the adequacy and sustainability of these corrections." This language—the formal FDA closure of an enforcement action—is significant. The FDA does not routinely issue such letters of closure without a substantive evaluation of the corrective action plan submitted by the facility and, typically, re-inspection verification that the corrective measures have been implemented effectively.

The FDA closure letter was issued on May 8, 2018, approximately nine weeks before the "zombie" recall articles began recirculating in late June and early July 2018. This means that not only was the original recall from a year prior resolved, but the FDA had formally confirmed resolution of the underlying manufacturing deficiency before the panic began. Consumers encountering the zombie recall articles in summer 2018 were reading about a resolved situation that had already been independently verified as resolved by the federal regulatory authority.

This timeline exposes the zombie recall dynamic precisely: recall information that was accurate and actionable in July 2017 had, by July 2018, become not merely stale but actively misleading in the opposite direction—suggesting ongoing risk where the risk had been mitigated and independently confirmed. The articles generating the 2018 panic were presenting information of the opposite of its original valence without disclosing the transformation.

## §5 Zombie Recalls and the Information Lifecycle of Food Safety Alerts

The phenomenon Mikkelson (2018) documented—a "zombie" recall articles presenting outdated information as current—represents a specific failure mode in food safety information dissemination that has become more common as canned food recall archives are accessible online and low-quality content farms republish archived safety information.

The structural features of the zombie recall are predictable. Original recall notices are issued by companies and the FDA and published on official websites, press newswire services, and major food safety news outlets. These original publications are indexed by search engines and remain accessible indefinitely. Independent secondary publishers—ranging from legitimate food safety newsletters to low-quality content farms seeking search engine traffic through food safety search terms—frequently republish or paraphrase these notices, sometimes for legitimate informational purposes and sometimes without date contexts that would allow readers to determine the information's currency. When these secondary publications are themselves republished or recirculated on social media, the temporal context becomes even more attenuated.

The 2018 Bush's recall recirculation is a particularly clear example because of the one-year lag between the original event and the recirculation spike. Mikkelson (2018) cited a June 10, 2018 publication on rightwingtribune.com as an example of a website presenting the outdated recall as current—a year after the original recall, two months after the FDA's formal confirmation of resolution. The Bush Brothers company's own Recall page, as Mikkelson (2018) noted, explicitly stated that the problem had been corrected—a form of self-correction available to any consumer willing to visit the official brand website before acting on third-party articles.

The consumer harm from zombie recalls extends beyond individual food purchase decisions. Lusk and Briggeman (2009, *American Journal of Agricultural Economics*, Vol. 91, No. 1) examined consumer responses to food safety incidents and found that willingness-to-pay for the affected product category declined significantly in response to high-salience media coverage of recalls, and that these demand effects persisted for months beyond the resolution of the underlying safety event. If zombie recalls produce media coverage spikes that carry the salience characteristics identified by Lusk and Briggeman—widespread circulation, alarm-inducing framing, brand-specific targeting—then the economic harm to the brand and its retail partners from resolved-but-recirculated recall stories constitutes real and persistent damage.

## §6 Conclusion: Verifying Recall Currency and the Authority of Official Channels

The Bush's Baked Beans zombie recall illustrates a category of misinformation that requires a specific corrective epistemic practice: checking the currency and status of food safety claims against official sources before acting on them. Unlike factual misinformation claims that are false from inception, outdated recall information was true at the time of original issue. The error is not in the claim's original accuracy but in its re-presentation as currently active without disclosure that the underlying situation has changed.

Mikkelson's (2018) Outdated rating appropriately identifies this distinct category. The consumer takeaway is procedural: for any food safety claim encountered on social media or through secondary content sources, verification requires consulting the FDA's recall database (fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts), the USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) recall database for meat and poultry products, and the relevant manufacturer's official website. These official channels are updated to reflect the current status of recalls—including closure—and are the authoritative source for determining whether a recall is active. No recall that has been formally closed and whose manufacturing defect has been FDA-confirmed as resolved poses current consumer risk.

The Bush Brothers case is in one sense an encouraging story: the company's internal quality controls detected a real potential hazard before consumer harm occurred; the recall was properly limited to the affected lot codes; the supplier corrected the manufacturing deficiency; and the FDA independently verified that correction. The system worked as designed. The zombie recall phenomenon then converted a food safety success story—the system detecting and resolving a potential risk—into a rolling source of consumer anxiety by presenting the succeeded risk as an ongoing one. This inversion is perhaps the most striking feature of the zombie recall dynamic: the most diligently handled recalls, which generate clear and specific recall notices that can be indexed and republished, may be precisely those most vulnerable to zombie recirculation because the specificity of the original notice provides the material for seemingly credible secondary publication.
