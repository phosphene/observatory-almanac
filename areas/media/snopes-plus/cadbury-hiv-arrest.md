## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In February 2018, a social media post claiming that a Cadbury factory worker had been arrested for "adding his HIV-infected blood" to the company's products circulated widely across Facebook, Twitter, and WhatsApp. The posts were phrased in urgent consumer-safety terms and spread with considerable velocity — particularly in parts of Africa, South Asia, and the Middle East — reaching audiences who took the health warning at face value. The claim included the standard hallmarks of this hoax archetype: a named brand, an alleged infected insider, an implied arrest, and urgent calls for consumers to discard Cadbury products.

Snopes founder David Mikkelson (2018) assigned this claim the rating **False**. The claim is false on every substantive dimension: no arrest was made, no contamination event occurred, no health authority issued warnings, and the biological premise is impossible. The claim, as Mikkelson documented, was a near-verbatim recycling of earlier Pepsi-Cola HIV-contamination hoaxes, substituting only the brand name. Cadbury itself is a British-origin confectionery brand owned by Mondelēz International since 2010; at no point did the company, any health authority in the United Kingdom, India (where much of the initial circulation occurred), or any other jurisdiction issue a contamination alert or confirm any related law enforcement activity.

This entry examines the specific origins and propagation of the 2018 Cadbury claim, the biomedical impossibility of the scenario described, the long genealogy of HIV-food-contamination hoaxes with particular attention to the Pepsi antecedent, and what the structural features of this claim type reveal about the mechanics of narrative mimicry in digital misinformation.

## §2 The Claim's Origins, Propagation, and Debunking

The 2018 Cadbury HIV contamination claim appears to have originated as a WhatsApp message in South Asian and African mobile messaging networks before making its way to Facebook and Twitter feeds. The timing — late February 2018 — placed it within the same fortnight as a nearly identical claim about Anchor Butter (posted by Snopes approximately five days after the Cadbury fact-check), confirming that the February 2018 period marked a particularly active moment of HIV food-hoax recycling.

The specific text of the Cadbury posts was forensically compared by Mikkelson (2018) to the text of earlier Pepsi hoax posts that had circulated widely across multiple years, including a wave in late 2017. The comparison revealed minimal textual variation: the claim language was essentially identical, with the word "Pepsi" replaced by "Cadbury." This structural identity is significant not merely as an observation about hoax recycling but as evidence about how these claims are manufactured: the author of a given iteration of this hoax does not need to invent a new narrative, develop new emotional appeals, or construct new pseudo-evidence. They inherit the full persuasive architecture of the previous iteration and modify only the brand-identifying noun.

Mikkelson (2018) further documented that no warnings, no news coverage in credible outlets, and no police statements in any jurisdiction were consistent with the claim. The BBC News archive contained no articles about a Cadbury HIV contamination incident. UK health authorities including the Food Standards Agency had issued no relevant alerts. Indian health and food safety authorities — critical given India's enormous Cadbury consumer base — had similarly issued no warnings.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Narrative Mimicry and the Plug-and-Play Hoax Architecture

The most distinctive epistemological feature of the Cadbury claim, and of the broader HIV-food-contamination hoax family, is what can be called its "plug-and-play" architecture. The claim's persuasive structure is fully portable across brand substitutions because the fear being exploited is not specific to any product category. This portability is a signal of what scholars of legend and rumor call "narrative mimicry" — the way that structurally successful misinformation templates reproduce by colonizing new hosts (brand names, geographic contexts, current events) without requiring meaningful modification of their core emotional mechanics.

The HIV-food-contamination hoax template, which can be verifiably traced to at least the early 1990s, has targeted products spanning virtually every food and beverage category: citrus fruits (pineapples, oranges), fresh produce (bananas), canned goods, soft drinks (multiple brands including Pepsi-Cola), and now confectionery and dairy products. Each iteration finds a new consumer context in which the fear of contamination by HIV — a virus that, due to its associated stigmas, social meanings, and historical terror, holds special emotional charge — can be activated.

Fine (1990) in his foundational work *The Kentucky Fried Rat: Legends and Modern Society* (a collection establishing key principles of modern rumor analysis) identified branded food contamination narratives as among the most persistent categories of consumer legend, attributing their durability to the combination of brand trust exploitation (contaminating a trusted product is more alarming than contaminating a generic one) and control anxiety (modern food supply chains are opaque to consumers, generating ambient uncertainty that legends crystallize into specific fears). While Fine was writing about contamination legends generally, the HIV variant adds a further psychosocial dimension: AIDS's unique cultural history in the late twentieth century — its association with stigmatized populations, its early political controversies, and its still-imperfect popular understanding — gives HIV-specific contamination claims a second emotional register that non-HIV contamination hoaxes lack.

Kleinman, Ramachandran, and Strier (1999) in the *International Journal of Health Services* examined HIV-related misinformation propagation in India and sub-Saharan Africa, documenting that HIV contamination rumors about food and consumer products were significantly more prevalent in high-stigma HIV-endemic settings — precisely the regions where the 2018 Cadbury claim circulated most intensively. Their analysis suggested that HIV rumors in these contexts serve partly as stigma-displacement mechanisms: by locating HIV transmission in ordinary commodity consumption rather than in behaviors associated with stigmatized identities, the rumors allow community members to express HIV-related anxiety in a format that does not require acknowledging the actual epidemiological drivers of local HIV burden.

## §4 Cadbury, Mondelēz, and the Brand's Particular Vulnerability

Cadbury's brand profile makes it a particularly apt target for hoaxes of this type. Cadbury was founded in Birmingham, England, in 1824 and grew to become one of the most globally recognized confectionery brands in history before its controversial acquisition by Kraft Foods (subsequently renamed Mondelēz International) in 2010. The acquisition was deeply unpopular in the United Kingdom, where Cadbury had long been regarded as a quasi-national institution, and generated continuing controversy when Kraft/Mondelēz subsequently closed a manufacturing plant in Bristol, England, moving production to Poland in apparent contravention of assurances made during the acquisition process.

This corporate history creates a specific vulnerability: a subset of consumers in both the UK and the brand's other major markets (particularly India, where Cadbury commands an extraordinary 65–70% share of the chocolate market according to industry estimates) maintain residual mistrust of Cadbury's current corporate ownership. Hoaxes exploiting this mistrust do not need to be believable as literal fact to circulate; they can travel on the emotional current of general brand skepticism among consumers who feel alienated from the corporate entity that acquired a beloved national brand.

The Indian market dimension is particularly significant. Cadbury India (now Mondelēz India) had previously weathered a real contamination crisis: in October 2003, worms were found in Cadbury Dairy Milk bars in Maharashtra, India, triggering a genuine FSSAI (then FDA India) investigation and a major crisis of consumer confidence. The brand's recovery from that crisis, managed through a transparent public campaign including Amitabh Bachchan's endorsement, is now studied as a crisis management case study. However, the 2003 incident established a specific precedent of Cadbury contamination in Indian public memory — creating a cultural reservoir of contamination anxiety that HIV hoaxes could tap, even without bearing any structural resemblance to the original worm-contamination incident.

## §5 The Biomedical Impossibility in Detail

As with all HIV-food-contamination claims, the Cadbury variant fails on basic virology. HIV requires living human host cells to replicate and cannot survive the conditions encountered in any commercial food production environment. The claim alleged that a worker added "HIV-infected blood" to the production at a Cadbury facility; the practical biology of this scenario makes it doubly impossible: the blood would be subject to high temperatures during conching, molding, and cooling of chocolate products; the volume of any introduced blood would be immediately and massively diluted in the product matrix; and even were a consumer to ingest the resulting product, the combination of stomach acid, digestive enzymes, and the thermal history of the product would eliminate any residual viral viability before absorption.

Sodora, Becerra, Cotterman, and Gardner (1998) in the *American Journal of Pathology* conducted a series of experiments on the oral transmission of simian immunodeficiency virus (SIV, the primate analog of HIV) in conditions designed to maximize transmission probability — fresh, undiluted virus applied directly to mucosal surfaces in primates. Even under these maximally favorable conditions, oral transmission was extremely rare and associated with pre-existing mucosal lesions. The conditions of commercial food consumption are orders of magnitude less permissive for transmission than the experimental conditions these researchers designed, and even those conditions produced minimal transmission.

Mikkelson (2018) quoted the CDC's standard guidance on this point: "HIV does not long survive outside its host medium of human bodily fluids... The CDC says except under laboratory conditions, HIV is unable to reproduce outside its living host; it does not spread or maintain infectiousness outside its host." This virological consensus is uncontested in the scientific literature and has not been qualified or amended by subsequent research.

## §6 Conclusion: Verdict Affirmation and the Problem of Permanent Susceptibility

David Mikkelson's verdict of False is fully supported by all available evidence: no contamination occurred, no arrest was made, and the scenario described is biologically impossible. The claim was a recycled hoax template with the brand name substituted from prior iterations.

The deeper question this case poses is about the structural permanence of the HIV-food-contamination hoax. Because the claim template is fully modular — any food brand can be plugged into the narrative slot — and because the emotional substrate (fear of HIV, food supply anxiety, trust exploitation) is perennial, some variant of this claim will almost certainly recirculate in perpetuity. Each iteration finds a new generation of consumers who have not previously encountered the specific brand-name version. The ordinary process of population turnover — new internet users, new social media adopters, new generations entering adulthood — continuously replenishes the pool of potential first-time hoax recipients.

Digital misinformation researchers have increasingly argued that once a successful hoax template enters the information ecosystem, it cannot be eliminated — only its harm can be reduced through sustained pre-bunking and literacy education. The Cadbury case is one of the clearest illustrations of this principle: the template predates social media by decades, has survived every platform transition, and has been debunked in hundreds of specific instantiations without any apparent reduction in the rate of new iterations.

For consumers, the most practical heuristic is the virology itself: because HIV cannot survive outside a human host under any commercially relevant conditions, any claim that HIV was or could be transmitted via a food product constitutes a categorical impossibility. Remembering this single biomedical fact eliminates the entire genre of food-HIV contamination claims as a class.
