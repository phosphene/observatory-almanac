## §1 — Claim Anatomy and Snopes Verdict

In early March 2006, an email warning circulated across Canadian inboxes and beyond, alerting consumers not to eat certain Cadbury chocolate eggs because they might contain pieces of hard plastic. The email reproduced what appeared to be text from a news report about an official health hazard alert. At the time of widespread circulation, the claim was sufficiently unusual — foreign body contamination events in major confectionery brands are notable — to generate active fact-checking demand.

Snopes rates this claim **True**. The underlying event was real, officially documented, and confirmed by three independent sources: a Canadian Food Inspection Agency (CFIA) health hazard alert issued on March 1, 2006; a voluntary recall announcement from Cadbury Adams Canada Inc.; and contemporaneous news coverage in major Canadian print outlets, including *The Toronto Star*.

The affected products were specific Caramilk chocolate eggs in three flavors — Caramilk, Caramilk Maple, and Caramilk Café — in 39-gram sizes, sold individually and in multipacks of 3, 6, and 12 eggs. The recall was national in scope, encompassing an estimated one million eggs, representing approximately 10 percent of Cadbury's total Easter egg chocolate product line in Canada. At least one verified injury was associated with consumption of the contaminated product: a consumer was cut on the inside of the mouth by a piece of plastic.

This entry provides a detailed account of the product safety event, the regulatory response, the mechanism of contamination, and the broader context of physical contamination recalls in the food and confectionery industry.

## §2 — Evidentiary Record: Official Documents and Contemporaneous Reporting

The primary evidentiary base for this fact-check is unusually clean compared to many Snopes investigations: the claim originated not from informal rumor but from an official government health advisory that circulated digitally. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency published its Health Hazard Alert on March 1, 2006, four days before the Snopes entry was authored.

The CFIA alert named the affected products with full specificity:

| Product | Size | UPC |
|---|---|---|
| Caramilk chocolate egg | 39 g | 0 615712 4 |
| Caramilk Maple chocolate egg | 39 g | 0 615882 2 |
| Caramilk Café chocolate egg | 39 g | 0 615892 9 |
| 3 Eggs Caramilk multipack | 3 × 39 g | 0 61200 05431 6 |
| 6 Eggs Multipack | 6 × 39 g | 0 61200 00581 3 |
| 12 Eggs Multipack | 12 × 39 g | 0 61200 21134 4 |

The CFIA coordinated with Cadbury Adams Canada Inc. through its standard voluntary recall monitoring framework, confirming the manufacturer's own recall initiation. Cadbury Adams Canada opened a dedicated consumer hotline (1-866-782-3267) and the CFIA maintained its standard public inquiry line. The recall was monitored for effectiveness by the CFIA under its standard post-announcement compliance assessment protocol.

Luisa Girotto, identified in contemporaneous reporting as Cadbury Adams Canada's spokesperson, stated that the contamination had been traced to a specific production line at the company's Hamilton, Ontario facility. A small piece of plastic used in the egg-moulding process had chipped and entered the chocolate product stream. This type of equipment-derived physical contamination is classified under the FDA (United States) and CFIA (Canada) frameworks as a "physical hazard" — a non-chemical, non-biological contaminant that poses risk of injury.

Curtis Rush's report in *The Toronto Star* (March 3, 2006) confirmed the contamination mechanism and injury count and provided the scale figure of approximately one million recalled eggs. The reach of the recall — nationally distributed through Canadian retail channels — was consistent with the centralized production at a single Hamilton facility feeding national distribution networks.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Anatomy of a Legitimate Recall Warning Email

The epistemic novelty of this case lies less in the underlying product safety event, which is relatively straightforward, and more in what it reveals about fact-checking methodology when applied to officially-sourced digital warnings versus organic rumor.

Most food-related chain emails or digital warnings that circulate widely represent transformed, exaggerated, or fabricated information. The Cadbury recall email is atypical in being almost entirely accurate. The email reproduced what appears to be the CFIA's own alert language, or closely derivative coverage, with minimal distortion. This places the Cadbury recall in the category of "legitimate warning propagated through informal channels" — a category that is epistemically distinct from "fabricated or exaggerated risk attributed to a known brand."

This distinction matters for media literacy pedagogy. Automatic skepticism toward chain email warnings about food products, while generally productive as a heuristic, fails when applied mechanically to accurate official-document-reproduction chains. The appropriate epistemic practice is not "disregard all chain warnings" but "verify the documentary chain: does an official source confirm the underlying claim?" In this case, CFIA documentation does confirm it.

A secondary epistemic observation concerns the structural features that made the Cadbury recall email more credible and accurate than typical food scare chains. The email reproduced specific, verifiable detail: named product variants, precise UPC codes, the CFIA's specific official phone numbers, and a named institutional origin (Cadbury Adams Canada). This level of specificity makes fabrication detectable — false product details or phone numbers would be promptly identified. This is precisely why legitimate official alerts contain such specificity, and why email reproductions that preserve that specificity tend to retain accuracy.

The recall also illustrates an important asymmetry in physical contamination risk communication. Unlike chemical or microbiological contamination, physical contamination from an identifiable equipment-specific event tends to be batch-localizable and temporally bounded. The risk window ended when the affected production batch was cleared and steps were taken to address the moulding equipment chipping problem. This self-limiting nature distinguishes physical contamination recalls from, for example, microbiological contamination events involving ongoing production practices.

## §4 — Physical Contamination Hazards in Confectionery Production

Physical contamination of food products is one of the three major categories of food safety hazard recognized by regulatory bodies worldwide (biological, chemical, and physical). Physical hazards are distinguished by their mode of injury — primarily laceration, choking, or dental damage — rather than toxicological or infectious mechanisms.

In confectionery production specifically, hollow chocolate products such as Easter eggs involve complex moulding processes in which liquid chocolate is deposited into precision moulds, allowed to set, and then demoulded. The mould materials used in commercial confectionery production include polycarbonate, which is dimensionally stable under normal operating conditions but subject to chipping when subjected to mechanical stress, thermal cycling, or normal fatigue after sufficient production runs.

When mould materials chip or fracture, the resulting foreign body can enter the product stream at the point of demoulding. Detection mechanisms in modern confectionery production typically include visual inspection checkpoints, X-ray inspection systems, and metal detector arrays. Plastic, however, presents a significant detection challenge: it is typically X-ray transparent (being of similar density to food components) and is not detected by metal detectors. This makes plastic mould fragments among the more difficult physical contaminants to detect automatically. The recall of one million eggs suggests that automated detection systems did not catch the contamination before distribution, and the consumer injury report was the effective trigger for investigation and recall.

Tellingly, the CFIA classified this event as a health hazard rather than a "may be unsafe from an advisory standpoint" alert — indicating that consumer injury had been confirmed. Health hazard classification in Canadian food law triggers heightened recall monitoring obligations compared to precautionary recalls initiated before documented injury.

Tolonen and colleagues (2004) reviewing foreign body complaints in European food surveillance data noted that dental injuries and oral lacerations were the most common presentations associated with hard plastic foreign bodies in food products, consistent with the type of injury described in the Cadbury case (*Food Additives & Contaminants*, 21(9), 865–872). Children and elderly individuals with dental restorations face elevated injury risk from hard plastic fragments due to different biting force profiles and dental vulnerability.

## §5 — Recall Scope, Consumer Communication, and Regulatory Response

The one-million-egg figure and the 10-percent-of-Easter-product-line scope are contextually significant. Easter seasonal confectionery is a time-sensitive product: eggs not recalled and replaced before Easter 2006 would have been commercially valueless after the holiday period. The timing of the recall — announced March 1, the CFIA alert published the same day and Snopes entry authored March 5, while Easter 2006 fell on April 16 — meant Cadbury had six weeks to execute the recall and consumers had six weeks to check their purchases.

The CFIA's recall effectiveness monitoring protocol involves follow-up assessment of whether affected products are removed from retail inventory, and may include random sampling of retail locations post-recall. For a nationally distributed product with clear identifiers (specific UPC codes), the effectiveness monitoring task was tractable. The fact that only the Caramilk-flavored Easter eggs were affected — not Cadbury's broader product line — reduced the operational complexity of the consumer communication task.

The consumer communication approach used by Cadbury Adams — a dedicated toll-free hotline staffed during business hours — was standard practice for major product recalls of this era. The email channel, while unofficial, proved effective at amplifying consumer awareness beyond what CFIA and news media channels alone would have reached, illustrating a case where informal digital communication served legitimate public health purposes by extending the reach of official advisory information.

Health Canada and the CFIA's publicly released information about this recall remains accessible and constitutes the verifiable documentary backbone of the Snopes True rating. Unlike cases where Snopes must reason from incomplete evidence or absence of documentation, this case represents a model instance of official confirmation enabling unambiguous verdict assignment.

## §6 — Verdict Synthesis, EARC Assessment, and Knowledge Gaps

**Snopes Verdict**: True. The recall was real, officially documented, and the injury that triggered it was confirmed. The email claim accurately reproduced the substantive content of a CFIA health hazard alert.

**Confirmed true**: Cadbury Adams Canada Inc. voluntarily recalled approximately one million Caramilk chocolate eggs in March 2006 due to the presence of hard plastic fragments, following one confirmed mouth injury to a consumer. The Canadian Food Inspection Agency published an official health hazard alert and monitored recall effectiveness. The contamination source was identified as a chipped plastic mould on a production line in Hamilton, Ontario.

**EARC designation: E (Empirically Grounded)** — The claim is supported by documented official recall notices, consumer product identifiers, CFIA regulatory documentation, and corroborating news reporting.

**Epistemic gap — Low**: The core factual claim is well-documented. Minor gaps remain in: (1) the total confirmed injury count beyond the initial one reported case — follow-up consumer reports may have increased this number but are not publicly documented; (2) the production line corrective action taken by Cadbury Adams following the recall — public regulatory filings do not always include post-recall facility reinspection findings; and (3) the specific final disposition of recalled product — whether returned to manufacturer, destroyed, or otherwise handled.

The low epistemic gap rating reflects the high quality and specificity of the primary documentary evidence, and the relative simplicity of the factual claim being evaluated (a specific institutional event with verifiable official documentation) compared to the mechanistic or epidemiological complexity of other entries in this series.

**Cross-references**: This entry connects to the broader framework of food product recall communication through informal channels, the regulatory architecture of the Canadian Food Inspection Agency, and the physical hazard subcategory of food safety contamination events. It provides a useful contrast case against entries involving exaggerated or fabricated food safety warnings, illustrating that email chain food warnings are not uniformly inaccurate.
