## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

The claim at issue holds that certain California wines — particularly inexpensive mass-market bottles — contain "dangerously high levels of poisonous inorganic arsenic" that pose a health risk to consumers. The claim was introduced to public attention on March 19, 2015, through reporting on a class-action lawsuit filed in California Superior Court by Kevin Hicks, the CEO of BeverageGrades, a private testing and certification company. Hicks claimed to have tested more than 1,300 bottles of wine and found that approximately one in four exceeded the EPA maximum contaminant level (MCL) for arsenic in drinking water, which is 10 parts per billion (ppb). The highest-arsenic wines in his testing were said to contain four to five times that level.

Snopes investigator Kim LaCapria (2015) rated the claim **Unproven**. The Unproven rating reflects the contested nature of the core empirical and regulatory questions involved: it is not disputed that trace arsenic exists in wine (it exists in virtually all agricultural products), but whether the measured levels constitute a genuine health hazard depends critically on what benchmark is applied and what consumption assumptions are used. The lawsuit and its attendant media coverage applied the EPA's drinking-water MCL without adjustment for the dramatically different quantity of wine a person drinks compared to the quantity of water — a methodological choice that critics, including winemakers, scientists, and industry bodies, identified as generating a misleading risk portrait.

Several additional complications further justify the Unproven rating. Independent testing commissioned by CBS News found arsenic levels in the named wines that were substantially lower than BeverageGrades' results. BeverageGrades simultaneously filed the lawsuit and issued a press release offering arsenic certification services to wine retailers, creating an apparent commercial conflict of interest. And the existing international regulatory framework — specifically the European Union's established arsenic limit for wine of 100 ppb or higher, ten times the EPA's drinking-water standard — was found to be consistently met by the California wines in question.

## §2 Origin, Propagation, and Source Assay

The story's introduction point was distinctive: rather than a social media fabrication or folk rumor, the arsenic-in-California-wine claim was introduced through the combined mechanisms of a class-action lawsuit and a private company's press release, timed for simultaneous release. This gave it an institutional credibility that most consumer-product rumors lack — a lawsuit in a named court, a specific plaintiff, specific named products, specific testing data with quantitative results, and specific identified toxicological benchmarks. The media environment amplified it rapidly: CBS San Francisco, CBS This Morning, and numerous food and wine publications all covered the story on the day of the lawsuit's filing.

The conflict-of-interest dimension was identified almost immediately by skeptics, most prominently by wine industry critics and by journalists at *Wine Spectator* who noted that BeverageGrades had sent a press release to wine retailers on the same day as the lawsuit offering its testing and certification services. This timing creates a financial nexus: companies whose wines appear on the high-arsenic list would have an incentive to purchase BeverageGrades' certification to demonstrate compliance, and the lawsuit itself generates the concern that makes that certification commercially valuable. Legal analysts describe this pattern as potential "entrepreneurial litigation" — using the threat of litigation not primarily to compensate injured parties but to establish a market position for ancillary services.

The wine industry's organized response was rapid. The Wine Institute — the trade association representing California wine producers — issued a statement on March 19, 2015, noting the ubiquity of trace arsenic in agricultural products and asserting that California wines were routinely tested by foreign government agencies and found to be within established international limits. Individual wine companies contacted by *Wine Spectator* reported that Hicks had not reached out to them before filing the lawsuit, which they characterized as inconsistent with good-faith scientific communication and with legal norms.

## §3 Epistemic Analysis — The Knowledge Gap

**Epistemic novelty:** The California wine arsenic story is epistemically interesting because it sits at the intersection of three knowledge domains that most consumers operate in independently and without integration: **environmental chemistry** (how trace elements enter agricultural products), **toxicological risk communication** (how consumption quantities should shape safety thresholds), and **regulatory comparative analysis** (why different agencies set different limits and why they are not interchangeable). The confusion in public reception of this story resulted from bridging all three.

The central knowledge gap is what might be called **benchmark transferability error**: applying a regulatory limit designed for one exposure context (an adult drinking approximately two liters per day of water every day as a primary source of hydration) to a categorically different consumption context (an adult drinking wine occasionally or socially, in smaller quantities). The EPA's MCL for arsenic in drinking water (10 ppb) was set by EPA in 2001 (effective as of 2006) on the basis of lifetime cumulative exposure risk through a route of exposure that assumes ingestion of approximately 2 liters per day throughout a lifetime. The risk calculation underlying this MCL is documented in the EPA's Arsenic in Drinking Water Rule (Federal Register, January 22, 2001).

There is no analogous regulation in the United States for arsenic in wine because the regulatory framework — consisting of the FDA for food safety and the TTB (Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau) for alcohol beverages — has not established a maximum contaminant level for arsenic in wine based on wine-specific consumption patterns and exposure pathways. The EU, where wine-specific limits exist, has set the limit at 100 ppb for white wine and higher for red wine — figures that reflect wine-consumption quantities and patterns rather than water-consumption quantities. California wines exported to the EU are tested against these limits and comply.

The use of the water standard as a proxy in the absence of a wine-specific standard is not obviously unreasonable — it is the available benchmark — but its application needs to be accompanied by serious discussion of the exposure adjustment necessary to account for the difference in consumption volume. A person who drinks two glasses of wine per sitting several nights per week ingests perhaps 250–750 ml (0.25–0.75 liters) per occasion, compared to 2,000 ml of water daily. The arsenic dose from wine consumption at these levels is a small fraction of the dose that would result from drinking equivalent amounts of water at the same concentration. The *Wine Spectator* criticism quoted extensively in the Snopes entry makes precisely this point, noting that even the most contaminated wine in BeverageGrades' dataset would require daily consumption equivalent to serious alcoholism before arsenic exposure approached levels that would be concerning under the water-standard math.

The EARC classification is **C (Contested)**: the claim is neither clearly true nor clearly false; it depends on contested methodological choices about benchmarking and on conflicting laboratory results, with a commercial conflict of interest structuring the presentation of the primary data.

## §4 Scientific and Empirical Context

Arsenic is a naturally occurring element present in virtually all soil, water, and atmospheric sources. Agricultural products absorb it from soil and water; its concentration in any given crop or product is a function of the geology of the growing region, irrigation water quality, and agricultural practices. Rice is the most widely discussed dietary arsenic source in the epidemiological literature — Meharg et al. (2008) in *Environmental Science & Technology* documented systematically elevated arsenic in rice from arsenic-contaminated growing regions — and the FDA has issued draft guidance on inorganic arsenic in rice and rice-based infant cereals. Apple and pear juice are also documented sources of dietary arsenic at levels that, when expressed in ppb, often exceed the drinking-water MCL; the FDA has established an action level for apple juice arsenic of 10 ppb (equal to the drinking water standard) specifically for infant apple juice, but the broader juice category is governed by context-adjusted guidance rather than the drinking water MCL directly.

The distinction between organic and inorganic arsenic is toxicologically critical. **Inorganic arsenic** — arsenic as a free ion or in simple inorganic compounds — is a known human carcinogen (IARC Group 1), associated particularly with elevated risk of bladder, lung, and skin cancer from chronic exposure. **Organic arsenic** — arsenic incorporated into organic molecules as found in seafood — is substantially less toxic and is treated differently in risk assessments. BeverageGrades' testing and the lawsuit referred to inorganic arsenic, which is the appropriate focus for cancer risk calculation. However, whether the specific forms of arsenic present in California wine are predominantly inorganic arsenic at the concentrations stated was not independently verified in the public dispute.

Chromated copper arsenate (CCA), a wood preservative formerly widely used in vineyard infrastructure and since phased out, has been identified as a potential historical source of arsenic in vineyard soils in some regions. California's wine country geology includes areas with naturally elevated arsenic in soil and groundwater. Jacobs et al. (2004) in *Environmental Health Perspectives* documented elevated arsenic in California Central Coast groundwater associated with natural geological sources, providing a non-anthropogenic mechanism for arsenic uptake in grapes and resulting wine. The price-arsenic correlation observed by BeverageGrades — cheaper wines tending toward higher arsenic levels — could reflect regional sourcing differences rather than a quality-related processing variable.

The independent CBS News testing of four wines named in the lawsuit produced substantially lower arsenic levels than BeverageGrades reported. One wine, Flip Flop Pinot Grigio, fell within the drinking-water MCL entirely; the others exceeded it but at levels lower than BeverageGrades' figures. The methodological discrepancy between the two sets of test results raises questions about BeverageGrades' analytical methods, sample preparation, or calibration — questions that were not resolved publicly. Standard analytical methods for arsenic in liquid beverages include hydride generation atomic absorption spectroscopy (HG-AAS) and inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS); the latter is the preferred method for low-level arsenic detection in complex matrices. Whether both labs used methods with equivalent detection limits and matrix-matched calibration was never publicly established.

## §5 Regulatory Framework and Legal Context

The United States regulatory framework for arsenic in alcoholic beverages is structurally incomplete by design. The TTB regulates alcohol labeling, labeling of ingredients, and certain aspects of production and distribution, but does not establish maximum contaminant levels for trace elements in wine. The FDA's Center for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition (CFSAN) has general authority over food adulterants under 21 U.S.C. § 342 (the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act's adulteration provisions), which could theoretically apply to arsenic in wine at levels posing a health risk — but in the absence of an established action level or MCL, enforcement would require a case-by-case finding of adulteration. No such finding was made in the BeverageGrades case.

The Wine Institute's response noted that California wines are tested against international standards when exported to the EU, Japan, and other markets with established wine-arsenic limits, and that they comply with those limits. The EU's wine quality regulation — Commission Regulation (EC) No. 606/2009, laying down certain detailed rules for implementing Council Regulation (EC) No. 479/2008 as regards the categories of grapevine products, oenological practices and the applicable restrictions — sets arsenic limits considerably more generous than the US drinking-water standard, reflecting the EU's own assessment of wine-specific risk.

The class-action lawsuit's fate was not well publicized following the initial media burst. Cases of this type in California — filed under consumer protection statutes — frequently result in dismissal, settlement with modest consumer payouts, or protracted litigation without substantive resolution. The commercial dynamic surrounding BeverageGrades' simultaneous certification-marketing activity does not invalidate the underlying empirical claims, but it is a relevant factor in assessing the objectivity of the primary data source.

## §6 Broader Significance and Lessons

The California wine arsenic story is one of the Truth Vault's most instructive entries because it concerns a claim that is neither fabricated, satirical, nor obviously wrong — it is a case of real but contested evidence being presented through a framing that selectively applies a regulatory benchmark without adjustment, generating an alarm arguably disproportionate to the evidence.

This matters because public discourse about food and environmental safety is full of structurally similar claims. The pattern — "this product contains [substance X] at levels exceeding [standard Y] set for a different context" — is a recurring form of food-scare communication, and its persuasiveness rests on the borrowed authority of the standard cited even when that standard is not designed for the comparison being made. Examples include debates about BPA in canned foods compared to industrial-occupational exposure limits; debates about glyphosate residues in oat-based foods compared to occupational standards; and debates about mercury in fish compared to water-quality standards.

The analytical move needed in each case is identical: identify the consumption metric underlying the cited standard, calculate the equivalent exposure at typical consumption rates, and compare the result to the actual dose from the real-world scenario being evaluated. This is the core of **dose-based risk communication**, the branch of toxicological public health that insists on quantity, route, and duration before any safety claim can be evaluated. Nordberg et al. (2015) in *Metal Ions in Life Sciences* (Royal Society of Chemistry) provide a standard reference for arsenic toxicokinetics that enables exactly this kind of recalculation.

The conflict-of-interest awareness lesson is equally general: when a claim about product safety comes from an entity with a commercial stake in the alarm the claim generates, that commercial interest is not dispositive of the claim's truth or falsity — the BeverageGrades data might be accurate — but it is a signal requiring independent corroboration before the claim is treated as established. The CBS News independent testing was a correct journalistic response, and its divergent results appropriately sustained the Unproven rating rather than converting it to Confirmed.

Finally, the regulatory-gap lesson: the absence of a US wine-specific arsenic standard does not mean wine is unregulated or that arsenic in wine is harmless. It means that risk managers in the relevant agencies have determined that wine is a lower-priority food-safety concern compared to other arsenic exposure routes. Understanding why standards are set where they are — and what assumptions they embed — is a component of regulatory literacy that empowers more accurate interpretation of "you have exceeded the limit" claims.
