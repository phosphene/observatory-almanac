# Dead People's Phones Have Made Calls or Sent Texts After Their Death


## 1. The Claim

The claim appears in many forms, but the experiential core is consistent: a person receives a phone call, a voicemail, a text message, or a notification from the phone number, app account, or messaging profile of someone who recently died. In the most emotionally powerful versions, the communication arrives at a significant moment — a birthday, an anniversary, a moment of private grief — and its content appears meaningful, even though the deceased could not have authored it.

Several distinct varieties circulate online:

**The post-mortem phone call:** Someone picks up their phone and sees an incoming call from a deceased family member's number. In some accounts the call connects to silence; in others, to static, ambient noise, or what sounds like breathing or distant voices.

**The posthumous text:** A text message arrives from a deceased person's number days, weeks, or months after their death. The message may be a partial word, a cached greeting, an automated system message, or — in the most striking versions — a contextually appropriate phrase that seems addressed to the recipient.

**The social media ghost:** Automated platform-generated messages arrive that appear to be from the deceased person's account — birthday reminders, algorithmic memory posts, suggested connections, or check-in notifications.

**The scheduled message:** A message the deceased composed and scheduled before their death arrives after they have died, often to the surprise of the recipient who had no knowledge it was prepared.

**The cloud artifact:** A device still signed into a deceased person's account generates activity — typing indicators in messaging apps, location shares, read receipts on old messages — that appears on the recipient's screen as evidence of presence.

These experiences are often interpreted, particularly by bereaved individuals, as evidence of post-mortem communication — the deceased person's consciousness using technology as an instrument of contact from beyond death. The claim moves from reported experience to metaphysical assertion: the dead can reach us through our phones.


## 2. What's Actually True

Modern smartphones are complex, always-connected computing devices running dozens of background software processes, synchronized with remote cloud infrastructure, and subscribed to numerous automated notification systems. The overwhelming majority of apparent post-mortem phone activity has a technically mundane explanation when examined carefully.

**Delayed message delivery.** Cellular network messages — SMS, RCS — can be queued for delivery when a network connection fails or when a device is powered off. If a deceased person sent a text in the final moments before death that was queued because the recipient's phone was unavailable or the network was congested, that message may arrive hours later. End-to-end encrypted messaging apps (iMessage, WhatsApp, Signal) implement their own delivery queuing that can delay messages significantly. A message composed and sent by a living person can arrive to a recipient after that person has died.

**Pre-scheduled messages.** Several smartphone applications allow users to compose and schedule messages for future delivery — morning greetings, event reminders, birthday messages. If a person scheduled a recurring birthday message and then died, that schedule will continue to execute unless someone with access to the account cancels it. This is not rare or exotic behavior; scheduling features are built into mainstream apps including Google Messages and various third-party SMS schedulers. A recipient has no visible indication that a message was composed in advance.

**Pocket dials and accidental activations.** A phone continued to be kept by a deceased person's estate, left in a drawer, placed in a bag, or forgotten by surviving family members may pocket-dial contacts if its screen is not locked. Voice assistant activation — Siri, Google Assistant — can trigger calls through environmental sounds or accidental button presses. A phone lying face-up in sunlight can perceive the light as sufficient illumination to deactivate auto-lock in some configurations. The combination of motion-sensitive accelerometers, capacitive touch screens, and always-on voice assistant listening creates multiple vectors for unintentional calls.

**Carrier forwarding and ported numbers.** A deceased person's phone number, if not suspended promptly, may eventually be reassigned by the carrier to another subscriber — typically after a period of inactivity ranging from months to years. If a recipient calls or texts the original number, they may reach the new subscriber. More disorienting: if the contact is stored in a recipient's phone, their messaging app may automatically create a thread with the new subscriber under the deceased person's name, since caller ID matches the stored contact entry. The recipient perceives a communication from the deceased person's number without realizing the number has been reassigned.

**Cloud-based read receipts and typing indicators.** Messaging apps display read receipts and typing indicators based on real-time account activity. A phone that remains signed into a deceased person's account — perhaps it was left unlocked at their home and a family member inadvertently opened the messaging app while looking through photos — will display a read receipt on previously unread messages and show a typing indicator if the phone's notification system causes the app to update. The recipient sees "Read" on a message they sent to the deceased, or briefly sees a typing indicator, and interprets this as evidence of activity.

**Automated social media systems.** Facebook, Instagram, Google, and other platforms generate automated messages that appear to originate from user accounts: birthday reminders sent to the account owner's contacts (which arrive before the platform has been notified of death and memorialized), "memories" surfaced on anniversaries, "you haven't heard from [person] recently" prompts, automated year-in-review compilations, and event-reminder notifications from shared calendar subscriptions. These systems have no awareness of the account owner's vital status and will continue operating until either the bereaved family memorializes the account or the platform's death-notification process is completed — a process that can take weeks or months.

**iCloud/Google account activity.** If a deceased person's Apple ID or Google account remains active and logged into their device (which is left powered on at a family home), various cloud services continue generating activity: iCloud Photo Library completing background syncs, Google Contacts updating shared contact entries, location-sharing apps sending periodic location pings, health data syncing to a shared family account. Recipients' devices may display notifications from these background activities under the deceased person's name or account.

**WhatsApp and end-to-end encrypted messaging edge cases.** WhatsApp's architecture means that messages are held on the sender's device until delivery is confirmed; in some edge cases, messages from a now-deceased sender stored on the sending device may be transmitted if the device comes back online. Additionally, WhatsApp's double-checkmark read-receipt system can display a "read" confirmation from messages opened by a family member on the deceased person's phone without awareness of the implications for the recipient.

None of these explanations exhausts the full range of possible technical sources for apparent post-mortem phone activity. The broader principle is that a modern smartphone is not a passive device that goes silent when its owner dies — it is a node in a distributed digital infrastructure that continues executing its processes according to its programming.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Acute grief and apophenia.** Grief creates a heightened state of perceptual vigilance for any signal that the deceased person might still be present in some form. This is a normal, documented feature of acute bereavement, not a pathological state. Bereaved individuals commonly report sensing the presence of the deceased, hearing their voice, catching a familiar scent, or seeing them in crowds. These experiences are often comforting and are widely reported across cultures and religious backgrounds.

Within this state of heightened vigilance, ambiguous signals are more likely to be interpreted as meaningful. A phone displaying an unexpected notification is ordinarily filed as a software artifact; received during acute grief about the notification's associated contact, it becomes evidence of presence. This is a form of apophenia — the perception of meaning in random or unrelated phenomena — shaped by emotional context rather than cognition.

**The timing coincidence.** If a bereaved person receives an apparent post-mortem communication on a significant day — the anniversary of a death, the deceased person's birthday, a moment of private grief — the coincidence of timing enhances the interpretation of the event as meaningful. But significant dates recur cyclically, grief is not uniformly distributed across the year, and automated systems like social media birthday reminders are designed precisely to fire on significant dates. The confluence is not evidence of purposeful communication — it is a feature of automated birthday-reminder systems behaving exactly as designed.

**Confirmation bias in memory.** People well remember the occasions when unexpected phone activity from a deceased person's contact occurred at a meaningful moment. They do not comparably remember the dozens of occasions when the same device generated similar unexpected activity with no associated significance. This asymmetric recall creates a distorted sense of the signal's frequency and specificity.

**The desire for contact.** The wish to believe a deceased loved one has communicated from beyond death is not a form of irrationality — it is a deeply human response to loss. The desire for contact with the dead is documented in every culture and expressed through religious practice, memorialization, ritual, and private psychology across human history. Technology creates new forms through which that desire finds expression and, for some bereaved individuals, apparent satisfaction. The emotional reality of the experience is genuine even when its supernatural interpretation is not warranted.

**Social amplification and cultural narrative.** Stories of post-mortem phone calls circulate widely because they are emotionally powerful and confirm a narrative — that death does not entirely sever connection — that many people wish were true. They are shared in grief communities, on social media, in news articles. The sharing generates additional sharing; each account that receives wide circulation makes the claim more culturally legible and provides framework through which subsequent unusual experiences get interpreted. A bereaved person who has read multiple accounts of post-mortem phone calls is primed to interpret unexpected phone activity in those terms.

**The genuine strangeness of digital existence.** Normal assumptions about human communication presuppose that communications reflect real-time intent by a currently-existing sender. The infrastructure of modern digital communication violates these assumptions continuously: messages are queued, delayed, scheduled, and cached; accounts outlive their owners; automated systems generate communications that are formally authored by individuals regardless of those individuals' vital status; cloud-synced content from years past surfaces as if freshly created. The strangeness of post-mortem phone experiences is partially a feature of how genuinely strange our digital communication infrastructure is, not only a projection of grief onto noise.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE** — in the specific form of the claim that deceased people's consciousness or presence is using technology to make contact.

No verified case of apparent post-mortem phone activity has been documented that resists technical explanation. Every reported case examined with access to the device, account logs, carrier records, or app-level activity data has resolved to one or more of the technical mechanisms described above. The experiences are genuine; the interpretation is not supported.

This verdict requires care. The metaphysical claim — that the dead are using phones to communicate — is false (or at minimum, entirely unsupported by available evidence), and the specific technological explanations for apparent post-mortem activity are well-established. But the experiences underlying the claim are real, often occur during acute grief, and carry genuine emotional weight that dismissive debunking does not honor.

The correct frame is not "you imagined it" but rather "here is what actually produced what you received, and you were not wrong to notice something unusual." The unusual thing was real. The technical explanation, not the metaphysical one, describes what produced it.

**A subsidiary claim worth distinguishing.** A small category of reported post-mortem phone activity involves **deliberately scheduled messages** — text messages, video calls, recordings — that the deceased person composed and scheduled prior to death, sometimes as acts of deliberate posthumous communication. These cases are genuine communications from the deceased, prepared with the intention of reaching loved ones after death, and they are not unusual occurrences in an era of widespread scheduling app availability. A person who receives a pre-composed message from a deceased loved one has received a genuine communication from that person — just not a supernatural one. The experience is real; the timing is intentional on the deceased's part; what it is not is evidence of post-mortem consciousness accessing technology.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The digital afterlife industry.** Post-mortem phone activity claims exist within a broader and rapidly developing space of questions about what happens to digital identity when a person dies — questions that intersect with technology, law, ethics, grief, and an emerging commercial sector. Companies including Eternos, HereAfter AI, StoryFile, and LifeNaut offer services designed to create interactive digital representations of individuals that can continue "communicating" with loved ones after death — trained on social media posts, emails, recorded conversations, and personal documents. Chatbots trained on a person's communication history can generate contextually plausible message responses in the person's expressed style.

These services represent a form of post-mortem communication that is genuinely happening — not through supernatural mechanism, but through machine learning — and that is beginning to create complex questions about consent, grief processing, and the ethics of digital resurrection. A "conversation" with an AI trained on a deceased parent's text messages is a different phenomenon from a pocket dial, but both may produce experiences that feel like contact from beyond death.

**Legal frameworks for digital death.** Most jurisdictions have addressed digital estate management inconsistently or incompletely. In the United States, the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act (RUFADAA) has been adopted in most states and provides a framework under which fiduciaries (executors, trustees, powers of attorney) can access digital assets including email accounts, social media, and potentially devices. However, practical death notification procedures for major platforms remain inconsistent: Google's Inactive Account Manager can be used to pre-designate legacy contact options; Facebook allows memorialization requests from approved family members; Twitter/X and some other platforms require a formal death notification process and do not act on informal reports.

The gap between legal authorization to manage a digital estate and the technical ability to execute that management promptly means that many accounts continue generating automated activity for weeks or months after a person's death, during the period when bereaved family members are most vulnerable to grief-amplified interpretation of that activity.

**Carrier and number reassignment policies.** Number reassignment — the reuse of a deceased person's phone number for a new subscriber — creates specific vectors for confusion of particular emotional intensity. CTIA (the wireless industry association) and FCC have addressed some aspects of this through the TRACED Act and associated number authentication requirements, but dead number reassignment timelines remain relatively short (often 90 days for prepaid numbers) compared to the period during which bereaved individuals commonly still have the deceased person's number stored in their devices and continue to think of it as associated with that person. The combination of fast reassignment timelines and slow contact-list grief processing creates predictable conditions for distressing cases of new subscribers receiving calls from bereaved people or sending routine messages that arrive as apparent communications from the dead.

**Grief support and technology.**  The psychological impact of unexpected apparent post-mortem communications should not be trivialized. For some bereaved individuals, these experiences are comforting; for others, they are distressing — reopening grief or creating confusing uncertainty. Mental health professionals working with bereaved clients report that digital afterlife phenomena are increasingly part of the presenting experience of grief, and that psychoeducation about the technical mechanisms underlying apparent post-mortem communications can be therapeutic — allowing clients to understand the experience without either dismissing its emotional reality or maintaining metaphysical beliefs that may complicate grief processing.

The most useful framing for bereaved individuals who have had these experiences is technical honesty combined with emotional validation: the experience was real, it carried genuine emotional weight, the technical explanation is well-understood, and it is entirely normal to find these coincidences poignant regardless of their explanation.

**Pareidolia in the digital environment.** Scholars of belief and perception have extended the concept of pareidolia — the perception of faces in random patterns — to a broader category of meaning-detection in noise that is now being applied to digital environments. The human pattern-recognition system is exquisitely sensitive to signals associated with known people, particularly in states of heightened emotional arousal such as grief. This sensitivity was adaptive in evolutionary contexts where correctly identifying social signals was survival-relevant. In the contemporary digital environment, where hundreds of automated systems regularly generate activity associated with known people's accounts and identities, this sensitivity produces frequent false positives that carry the full emotional weight of genuine social signals.

**The regulatory gap.** No regulatory framework currently requires platforms or carriers to provide clear, accessible informational materials explaining why a deceased person's phone or accounts may appear to continue generating activity. Publication of such materials — integrated into death notification procedures or provided in consumer guides — would reduce the frequency of distressing misinterpretations without requiring any technical changes to existing systems. This is a meaningful public health opportunity in digital grief support that has not yet been broadly taken up by the telecommunications industry, major platform operators, or grief support organizations.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not addressed calls-from-beyond claims, despite their persistent circulation in grief communities and their periodic resurgence as viral social media content. The claims are well-suited to fact-check treatment: they have consistent technical explanations, they generate genuinely significant emotional impact on bereaved individuals, and the intersection of grief psychology with technology infrastructure creates sufficiently complex terrain that a careful, well-sourced analysis would provide genuine public value.

The challenge for fact-checkers is tonal. Standard fact-check framing — claim, verdict, explanation — maps awkwardly onto experiences that are emotionally raw and personally significant for the people who report them. A dismissive verdict ("FALSE: your dead mother's phone didn't text you; it was a network cache") fails its audience. Effective fact-check treatment of this claim requires acknowledging the experiential reality, the emotional weight, the genuine technical complexity of modern telecommunications, and the human need for meaning in grief — while clearly establishing that the supernatural interpretation is not supported by any verified evidence.

Adjacent coverage exists: technology journalists have periodically explained specific technical mechanisms (pocket dials, cached messages, number reassignment) in individual case analyses. Grief counseling literature has addressed memory and contact experiences in bereavement. But no comprehensive fact-check treatment drawing these threads together has been published by a major fact-checking organization, leaving the claim to circulate largely unchallenged in the spaces where it most commonly appears.

This Truth Vault entry establishes the evidentiary record and technical framework that a comprehensive fact-check would require, sitates the claim within both telecommunications infrastructure and grief psychology, and identifies the regulatory and communications opportunities through which the most distressing aspects of this phenomenon could be mitigated.
