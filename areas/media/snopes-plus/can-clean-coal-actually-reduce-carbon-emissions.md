# "Clean Coal" Technology Can Actually Reduce Carbon Emissions

## 1. The Claim

"Clean coal" entered the vocabulary of energy and climate policy in the 1990s and has since oscillated between earnest engineering description and highly effective political rhetoric. In its most common popular formulation, "clean coal" refers to a suite of technologies that allow coal to be burned for electricity generation while substantially reducing or eliminating the carbon dioxide and other pollutant emissions associated with conventional coal combustion. Proponents in the fossil fuel industry and in political circles, particularly in countries with significant coal-mining sectors, have at various times claimed that clean coal technologies can make coal economically competitive with natural gas and renewables while meeting modern emissions standards, allowing continued coal use without catastrophic climate impact.

The claim circulates in several distinct registers. In industry and government white papers, it refers specifically to technologies such as carbon capture and storage (CCS), integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC) systems, and ultra-supercritical pulverised coal combustion. In political speech, particularly in the United States, Australia, Poland, and India, it functions as a rhetorical device suggesting that environmental concerns need not constrain coal production — that technology can resolve the tension between fossil fuel interests and climate responsibility. In popular media, it often appears as a binary assertion: either "clean coal is a complete myth pushed by coal lobbies" or "clean coal works and we should fund it instead of renewables." Both poles misrepresent a technically complex landscape.

Understanding what "clean coal" actually encompasses, which claimed emissions reductions are real, which are overstated, and how the term functions as a political instrument requires disaggregating a bundle of technologies that are frequently discussed as though they were a single thing.

## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rates the clean coal claim as **Mixture** — one of the more nuanced verdicts in their category system — and identifies correctly that the term encompasses a range of technologies with genuinely different performance characteristics. Their analysis distinguishes between technologies that address particulate and sulphur dioxide emissions (where there are real improvements) and technologies that address carbon dioxide (where the picture is far more contested).

The Snopes analysis is correct on the key factual points. Coal combustion in plants equipped with scrubbers, selective catalytic reduction systems, and electrostatic precipitators does dramatically reduce emissions of sulphur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, mercury, and particulate matter. This is not rhetorical — over the past four decades, modern coal plants in OECD countries have reduced these pollutant emissions by 70–90% compared to 1970s baselines. The regulatory driver in the United States was the Clean Air Act and its successive amendments; comparable legislation has driven similar improvements in Europe.

On carbon dioxide — the emissions most relevant to climate change — the picture is fundamentally different. Combustion efficiency improvements in modern coal plants reduce the amount of coal burned per unit of electricity generated, which reduces CO₂ per kilowatt-hour. Ultra-supercritical steam technology, operating at pressures and temperatures above the critical point of water, achieves thermal efficiencies of approximately 42–46%, compared to 33–37% in subcritical plants dominant in the United States fleet (IEAGHG, 2016). This translates to roughly 10–20% lower CO₂ per unit of energy output — a real reduction, but one that still leaves coal generating approximately two to three times the lifecycle CO₂ of natural gas and five to twenty times that of wind or solar power (Schlömer et al., 2014, _IPCC WGIII, Annex III_).

Carbon capture and storage — the technology most commonly associated with "clean coal" in contemporary political discourse — is where the gap between claim and reality is widest. CCS involves capturing CO₂ from flue gases after combustion (post-combustion capture), compressing it, transporting it, and injecting it into geological formations for permanent storage. The technology works in laboratory and small pilot settings, and several demonstration projects have operated at commercial scale for other industries, most notably the Sleipner natural gas processing platform in Norway (Baklid et al., 1996, _Society of Petroleum Engineers_) and the Quest project in Canada. However, no commercially operating coal-fired power plant anywhere in the world currently captures and stores a majority of its CO₂ output with CCS technology.

The Boundary Dam project in Saskatchewan, Canada — the world's first commercial-scale CCS retrofit on a coal plant, operated by SaskPower — captured approximately 50–60% of design capacity on average during its early years and has faced persistent operational challenges that have kept actual capture rates below target. The Kemper County IGCC project in Mississippi, designed to gasify coal before combustion and capture CO₂, was mothballed in 2017 after costs ballooned from an initial estimate of $2.4 billion to over $7.5 billion without achieving commercially viable operation. These high-profile failures have substantially reduced investor confidence in utility-scale CCS for coal.

Snopes's Mixture verdict acknowledges this complexity without fully working through the technopolitical function of the term itself — which is where the most analytically interesting content lies.

## 3. The Epistemics — What the Fact-Check Misses

The epistemic novelty in the clean coal case is the most structurally unusual of the three entries in this batch: the claim is not primarily a scientific error but a **political-semantic manoeuvre**. The most important analytical insight is that "clean coal" is a contested term, and that the central epistemological challenge is not evaluating the empirical performance of named technologies but recognising how a strategic ambiguity in terminology allows advocates to migrate between empirical domains when challenged.

This is what might be called **definitional migration under challenge**: when a broad, rhetorically powerful term is subjected to critical scrutiny, proponents can shift the referent of the term to whichever sub-component is currently supportable, while implying the validity of the full package. When critics point out that CCS has not achieved commercial scale on coal plants, proponents shift to "but combustion efficiency has improved dramatically." When critics point out that efficiency improvements still leave coal far above renewables in carbon intensity, proponents shift back to "but carbon capture can achieve near-zero emissions once deployed." Each pivot involves accurate specific claims; the rhetorical work is done by the umbrella term "clean coal" that ties them together as a coherent pro-coal proposition.

This argumentative structure is epistemically distinct from ordinary scientific error. It is not that proponents of clean coal hold false beliefs about specific technologies — most industry spokespeople know very well that existing CCS-equipped coal plants have not achieved claimed performance targets. The rhetorical function is to maintain policy space for continued coal investment while technology development continues, through a term that sounds accomplishing more than any specific technical component actually accomplishes.

James Hansen, one of the world's most cited climate scientists, made this point explicitly in a 2009 letter, arguing that "clean coal" functions as a political holding action where the phrase implies a mature, deliverable technology that does not currently exist. This framing — that the political function of the term diverges from its accurate technical application — is more useful analytically than asking whether the technologies associated with the term do or do not reduce emissions.

The IPCC Fifth Assessment Report acknowledged that CCS could theoretically play a significant role in emissions mitigation scenarios consistent with 2°C warming, but noted that this depended on massive, rapid, and sustained cost reductions and deployment at a scale not demonstrated by any existing project (Bruckner et al., 2014, _IPCC WGIII, Chapter 7_). Integrated assessment models that feature CCS heavily in mitigation pathways are not making claims about current technology performance; they are specifying what would need to be true about future technology performance for certain cost-effective mitigation pathways to remain available. Treating these modelling assumptions as endorsements of current "clean coal" technology is a further significant inferential leap.

The comparison with renewables is the most empirically telling context for understanding why the political framing matters. Between 2010 and 2023, the levelised cost of a new utility-scale solar photovoltaic installation fell by approximately 90%, and wind power costs fell by over 60% (Lazard, 2023, _Levelised Cost of Energy Analysis_). During this same period, no utility-scale CCS-equipped coal plant achieved commercially viable operation at its design capacity. The energy economics that once made "clean coal" a credible R&D wager have shifted dramatically, and the term increasingly functions to advocate for a technology trajectory that has not materialised against alternatives that have.

The fact-check that evaluates clean coal as a technical performance question — does CCS work? — misses the question that has become more analytically urgent: given where we now are in the energy transition, under what epistemic conditions does continued investment in coal CCS represent rational resource allocation versus opportunity cost relative to proven renewable and storage technologies? This is not a question Snopes is designed to answer, but it is the question that matters for public understanding.

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Partially True (efficiency and pollution reduction) / False as commonly used (CCS commercial viability) / Analytically Misleading (term itself)
**Confidence:** High
**Epistemic Basis:** Engineering performance data; economic analysis; political rhetoric analysis
**Phenomenological Status:** Irrelevant
**Mechanistic Status:** Mechanisms are real; commercial deployment at claimed scale is not
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Low to Negative (term obstructs rather than informs)

"Clean coal" as a single unified claim admits no single verdict, which is itself the point. Technologies grouped under the label have very different emissions performance profiles:

1. **Particulate, sulphur and nitrogen pollution reduction:** Real and substantial. Modern scrubbing and catalytic systems dramatically reduce non-CO₂ pollutants from coal combustion. These reductions are not controversial and are well-documented. They do not address climate-relevant CO₂ emissions.

2. **Combustion efficiency improvements:** Real and documented. Ultra-supercritical coal plants emit meaningfully less CO₂ per unit of electricity than older subcritical plants. However, the improvement is insufficient to bring coal into the CO₂ range of natural gas, let alone renewable energy, at any scale of deployment.

3. **Carbon capture and storage at coal plants:** Technologically feasible in principle; economically non-viable and operationally unproven at commercial scale. Every major utility-scale CCS-equipped coal project has either failed (Kemper County), underperformed persistently (Boundary Dam), or not advanced beyond planning. The assertion that CCS makes coal "clean" describes a technology that does not exist in commercially operational form.

4. **The aggregate claim:** Evaluating clean coal as a unified category implies that the real achievements in points 1 and 2 validate the undelivered promise in point 3. This inference is the primary rhetorical function of the bundled term, and it is analytically unjustified.

The honest verdict: modest CO₂ benefits from efficiency, no demonstrated large-scale CO₂ capture from coal, and a political term that routinely implies more than any technologically honest description could support.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The "clean coal" episode is a case study in how technologically credible R&D programmes can be co-opted as political instruments, and in the dynamics of policy lock-in around legacy industries facing structural economic disruption.

**History of the Term and Its Political Deployment**: The phrase "clean coal" appears in technical literature from at least the 1980s, where it described primarily the desulphurisation and particulate-control technologies that were then being mandated by the Clean Air Act. The American Clean Coal Technology programme, a joint government-industry initiative launched in 1985, funded research into IGCC, fluidised bed combustion, and other efficiency-improving technologies. At this stage, the term had a reasonably well-defined technical referent.

The migration toward the more ambitious claim — that clean coal could substantially address CO₂ emissions — accelerated in the late 1990s and early 2000s, as climate science consensus consolidated and it became clear that the coal industry would face increasing regulatory pressure on greenhouse gas emissions. The coal lobby and several major electric utilities invested substantially in CCS research and in political communication framing CCS as the industry's climate solution. The 2005 George W. Bush administration's energy plan featured "clean coal" prominently, and the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 allocated approximately $3.4 billion for clean coal technology demonstration, including the FutureGen 2.0 project that was ultimately cancelled in 2015.

In international policy contexts, clean coal arguments have been most consequential in countries where coal plays a large economic and political role. In Poland, coal supplies approximately 70% of electricity generation and employs tens of thousands of workers; "clean coal" rhetoric provides political cover for continued coal investment that the EU emissions trading system would otherwise make increasingly uneconomic. In India and Indonesia, coal development has been defended on grounds that future CCS deployment will address emissions, a claim that is speculative but useful for short-term investment justification.

**The Economics of CCS: Why It Has Not Scaled**: The basic economic challenge of coal CCS is that capturing, compressing, transporting, and storing CO₂ from power plant flue gases consumes approximately 15–25% of the plant's own energy output — a "parasitic load" that reduces the effective efficiency of the plant and raises the cost of electricity production. This energy penalty combined with the capital costs of capture equipment, compression infrastructure, and storage wells means that CCS fundamentally increases the cost of coal electricity.

In the early 2000s, the assumption was that the carbon price signal from cap-and-trade or carbon taxation would create sufficient economic incentive for utilities to invest in CCS equipment despite this cost premium. This assumption has been repeatedly frustrated: carbon pricing mechanisms have been weaker than modelled, alternative low-carbon technologies have fallen in cost faster than expected, and the practical engineering challenges of scaling CCS have proved greater than pilot projects suggested.

The International Energy Agency's World Energy Outlook models (IEA, 2023) have progressively reduced the projected role of coal CCS in technically feasible low-carbon scenarios, as the cost curves of renewables have diverged from those of CCS-equipped coal. Scenario modelling that features large coal CCS deployment now typically relies on assumptions about future policy instruments (high carbon prices, direct CCS subsidies) rather than natural market dynamics.

**The Stranded Asset Problem**: The most consequential practical implication of the clean coal controversy is its relationship to stranded asset risk in the coal sector. Coal infrastructure has an economic life of 30–50 years. New coal power plants commissioned in the 2020s on the assumption that CCS will eventually be available at commercial scale may find themselves operating in an energy market — and a regulatory environment — that makes them economically unviable long before end of physical life.

The Carbon Tracker Initiative and academic researchers including Pfeiffer and colleagues (2016, _Nature Climate Change_) have quantified the "committed emissions" from currently operating and planned fossil fuel infrastructure — the CO₂ that would be emitted if all currently operating plants run to their expected economic lifetimes without early retirement. Their analysis found that meeting Paris Agreement targets would require retiring a substantial fraction of existing coal plants before end of life, implying trillions of dollars in stranded assets. "Clean coal" rhetoric complicates this analysis by suggesting that existing and planned coal plants could be retrofitted with CCS to achieve compliance, a possibility that has consistently failed to materialise economically.

**Integrated Gasification Combined Cycle: The Unfulfilled Promise**: IGCC was perhaps the most technologically compelling component of the clean coal portfolio. Rather than burning coal directly, IGCC converts coal into syngas (primarily hydrogen and carbon monoxide) through high-temperature partial combustion with steam and oxygen, then burns the syngas in a combined-cycle turbine system with higher thermal efficiency. The pre-combustion capture of CO₂ from syngas is theoretically easier and cheaper than post-combustion CCS from conventional boiler flue gas, because the CO₂ is at higher concentration and pressure.

Several IGCC demonstration plants operated in the United States and Europe in the 1990s–2010s, including the Wabash River and Tampa Electric Polk Power Station projects in the US and the Buggenum plant in the Netherlands. These demonstrated that IGCC was technologically viable, but the projects also revealed formidable operational challenges: lower availability rates than conventional coal plants, higher maintenance complexity, and capital costs substantially above simple-cycle coal. The Kemper County failure — the most ambitious attempt to commercialise IGCC with CCS — cost nearly three times its original budget and never achieved sustained gasification operation as designed.

**The Comparative Renewable Context**: The energy landscape into which CCS technology has been slow to deploy is not static. Between the major CCS programme launches of the mid-2000s and the mid-2020s, the global generation capacity of solar and wind expanded by over an order of magnitude, while costs fell faster than any credible modelling anticipated. The Bloomberg NEF New Energy Outlook annually tracks the trajectories, and by the early 2020s had established that new-build utility-scale solar was the cheapest new electricity generation source in most of the world without any CO₂ pricing — a condition that was essentially unimaginable when the major clean coal investment programmes were designed.

This comparative context does not make CCS technically impossible or worthless — it remains relevant for reducing emissions from industrial processes (steel, cement, chemicals) where direct electrification is more difficult, and in biomass energy with carbon capture (BECCS) as a potential negative-emissions technology. But it does mean that "clean coal" as a framing for continued coal investment competes against alternatives whose cost reductions have been dramatic and empirically verified, while CCS costs on coal plants have remained high and commercial performance has remained elusive.

**Genuinely Valid Operational Reasons for Transition Provisions**: The most honest version of the clean coal argument — made by some economists and energy security analysts rather than coal industry advocates — is not that CCS makes coal clean at current technology maturity, but that coal plants with CCS representing a longer transition timeline than immediate closure may be preferable in countries with severe energy poverty, grid reliability challenges, and limited renewable deployment capacity in the near term. This argument does not assert that current CCS technology achieves the claimed emissions performance; it argues for a phased transition in which coal plays a diminishing but stabilising role while renewable infrastructure is built out.

This is a legitimate policy argument, though one with strong objections from climate advocates who argue that the urgency of emissions reductions is incompatible with extended coal transitions. But it is a fundamentally different argument from the claim that "clean coal" currently works as advertised. The conflation of the transition management argument with the technical performance claim is another instance of definitional migration — deploying the technically accurate parts of the proposition to lend credibility to the poorly-evidenced parts.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes evaluates "clean coal" as **Mixture**, applying their intermediate verdict category that acknowledges the multi-part nature of the claim. Their analysis distinguishes between pollution-reduction technologies that have delivered real results and carbon capture technologies that have not achieved commercial scale, arriving at a nuanced but accurate bottom line.

The Snopes treatment is among the stronger examples of fact-checking a technically complex, multi-component claim. Their acknowledgement that the term is used in ways that are "sometimes accurate and sometimes not" reflects a genuine understanding of the definitional instability of the phrase. They cite the main high-profile CCS failures (Kemper County) and note that the term's political usage often exceeds what the current technology portfolio actually delivers.

The primary analytical gap is the treatment of "clean coal" as primarily a technical question rather than a political-communicative phenomenon. The most consequential aspect of the term is not which technologies it describes but how the bundled term functions rhetorically to preserve policy space for coal in climate debates — and how definitional migration under challenge allows advocates to shift between components when any specific piece is criticised. Understanding this function requires a political and rhetorical analysis that goes beyond technical performance evaluation.

The Snopes treatment also understates the comparative renewable context. Evaluating whether CCS works in isolation, without reference to the dramatic cost reductions in alternatives during the same period, leaves readers with an incomplete picture of what "keeping coal viable" actually implies as a resource allocation decision in 2026. The opportunity cost framing — what might have been deployed with the capital invested in failed clean coal projects — is crucial for assessing the claim's practical significance and is entirely absent from the fact-check.

Finally, the Snopes piece would benefit from explicitly naming the rhetorical strategy of definitional migration — explaining to readers not only which components have or have not delivered, but why the bundled term is persistently useful to coal advocates even as individual components fail to materialise. This explanatory layer is what would most improve a reader's ability to evaluate future iterations of the claim as the technology landscape continues to evolve.
