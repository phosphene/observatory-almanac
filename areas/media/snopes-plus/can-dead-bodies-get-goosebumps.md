# Can Dead Bodies Get Goosebumps?


## 1. The Claim

The question "Can dead bodies get goosebumps?" might seem macabre, but it reflects genuine public curiosity about what happens to the human body after death. The claim at issue is a simple one: that deceased human bodies can exhibit the phenomenon commonly known as goosebumps—the raised bumps on the skin caused by the contraction of tiny arrector pili muscles around hair follicles.

Snopes addressed this question in a fact check rated "Mostly True," authored by Madison Dapcevich. The article draws on an interview with Heather Brown, a mortician-turned-death-investigator based in New Orleans, Louisiana, who explains that deceased bodies can indeed develop goosebumps, but through a different mechanism than living bodies. In living persons, goosebumps are caused by the autonomic nervous system's fight-or-flight response to cold temperatures, fear, or other stimuli. In deceased persons, the phenomenon is caused by rigor mortis—the temporary stiffening of muscles that occurs several hours after death as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) depletes and actin-myosin bonds in muscle fibers become locked.

The Snopes article is scientifically well-grounded in its core claim: the arrector pili muscles around hair follicles do contract after death as part of rigor mortis, producing what observers would recognize as goosebumps. The article correctly distinguishes between the living mechanism (neurally mediated) and the post-mortem mechanism (chemically mediated). The information provided by Brown aligns with standard forensic pathology knowledge about rigor mortis and decomposition.

However, the Snopes article's "Mostly True" rating and its reliance on a single source raise important questions about the standards of evidence applied in science fact-checking, the precision of the language used in describing biological phenomena, and the distinction between processes that look the same but have fundamentally different biological meanings. The claim that "dead bodies can get goosebumps" is true in the sense that the visual phenomenon occurs. But whether we call that phenomenon "goosebumps" in the same sense as the living phenomenon, or whether a different term is more appropriate, is a matter of definition that the Snopes article addresses only indirectly.


## 2. What's Actually True

The core factual content of the Snopes article is accurate and consistent with established forensic pathology.

Rigor mortis is a well-documented post-mortem change that affects all voluntary and some involuntary muscles in the body, including the arrector pili muscles. After death, when cells can no longer produce ATP through oxidative metabolism, the cellular machinery that normally allows actin and myosin filaments to release from their bound state ceases to function. The result is a temporary locking of muscle fibers that produces the characteristic stiffening of rigor mortis. This process typically begins within two to four hours after death, becomes fully established within six to twelve hours, and resolves within twenty-four to forty-eight hours as autolysis and decomposition break down the muscle proteins.

The arrector pili muscles are smooth muscles attached to hair follicles. When they contract, they pull the hair follicle upright, causing the surrounding skin to pucker and produce the bumps commonly called goosebumps (also known medically as cutis anserina, horripilation, piloerection, or the pilomotor reflex). In living persons, this contraction is triggered by the sympathetic nervous system—specifically, the fight-or-flight response activates the alpha-adrenergic receptors on arrector pili muscles, causing contraction. This neural signal is absent in deceased persons, but the mechanical consequence of rigor mortis affecting the same muscles produces an externally identical result.

The Snopes article correctly explains that the biological mechanism differs between living and deceased subjects. The quoted source, Heather Brown, states: "It's one of the amazing displays our bodies exhibit when they begin to break down ... immediately after the death occurs, the decomposition process begins. What you're seeing with postmortem 'goosebumps' is rigor mortis." This characterization is consistent with standard forensic pathology texts.

The article also correctly notes that rigor mortis is a temporary phenomenon. It develops, becomes established, and then resolves as decomposition proceeds. This means that post-mortem goosebumps, to the extent they occur through rigor mortis of the arrector pili muscles, are also temporary and will disappear as the body progresses through the later stages of decomposition.

However, there are several factual dimensions that the Snopes article does not address or addresses only indirectly. The question of whether rigor mortis of the arrector pili muscles is truly universal—whether all deceased bodies develop post-mortem goosebumps—is not discussed. In practice, the visibility of post-mortem goosebumps depends on factors including the presence and density of body hair, the ambient temperature (which affects the rate and intensity of rigor mortis), the individual's muscle mass, and the position of the body at the time of death. A body that is cold or has been moved may not exhibit visible goosebumps even though the arrector pili muscles have undergone rigor.

The article also does not distinguish between goosebumps caused by rigor mortis proper and goosebumps that may occur during the later stages of decomposition. As muscles begin to autolyze (self-digest) during decomposition, random contractions can occur that might also produce piloerection. These are chemically mediated rather than neurally mediated but are different from the organized contraction of rigor mortis. The Snopes article attributes post-mortem goosebumps solely to rigor mortis, but the phenomenon may have multiple post-mortem causes.

Additionally, the article does not address the phenomenon of "cadaveric spasm"—a type of immediate post-mortem stiffening that can occur in certain muscle groups at the moment of death, distinct from the delayed onset of rigor mortis. While cadaveric spasm typically affects larger muscle groups (such as those of the hands, where a person may be found clutching an object), it is theoretically possible that arrector pili muscles could be affected as well, though this is not documented in the forensic literature.


## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes correctly identifies that the visual phenomenon of goosebumps can occur in deceased bodies. The article correctly distinguishes the mechanism in living persons (autonomic nervous system-mediated) from the mechanism in deceased persons (rigor mortis-mediated). The information from Heather Brown is consistent with established forensic pathology and provides a clear, accessible explanation for a general audience. The choice to interview a frontline practitioner rather than rely solely on textbook knowledge adds a practical dimension to the explanation.

The "Mostly True" rating reflects Snopes' assessment that the core claim is substantially correct but requires additional context and qualification. This is an appropriate use of the rating category: the claim "dead bodies can get goosebumps" is true in the observable sense, but the full truth requires understanding that the underlying mechanism differs from the living phenomenon. The "Mostly True" rating signals to readers that the answer is yes, but with important caveats.

Where Snopes falls short is in several dimensions related to the standard of evidence and the precision of scientific communication.

The article relies on a single source—Heather Brown, a mortician-turned-death-investigator. While Brown's professional background is relevant and her knowledge appears sound, a single source is a thin evidentiary basis for a scientific claim, particularly when the topic involves specialized knowledge from multiple domains (forensic pathology, muscle physiology, decomposition chemistry). The article would benefit from corroboration from a forensic pathologist, a muscle physiologist, or a review of the relevant scientific literature. Snopes does not cite any peer-reviewed sources, forensic textbooks, or medical references beyond a Harvard Health blog post about goosebumps in living persons. This contrasts with the platform's typical standard for science-related fact checks, which often involve multiple expert sources and literature review.

The absence of cited scientific literature is notable. A search of the PubMed database for terms related to post-mortem piloerection reveals limited direct research, but the phenomenon is described in standard forensic pathology texts, including Knight and Saukko's *Knight's Forensic Pathology* and Spitz and Fisher's *Medicolegal Investigation of Death*. Citing these sources would strengthen the article's authority and provide readers with avenues for further information.

The article does not address the definitional question at the heart of the claim. The term "goosebumps" in everyday usage refers to the bumps that appear on skin in response to cold, fear, or emotional stimuli—a phenomenon that requires a functioning autonomic nervous system. If a deceased body displays the same visual phenomenon through a different mechanism (rigor mortis), is it accurate to say the body "got goosebumps"? The answer depends on whether the term refers to the visual appearance or the underlying mechanism. Snopes implicitly adopts the visual-definition approach (if it looks like goosebumps, it is goosebumps) without acknowledging that a mechanism-based definition would yield a different answer. This is a legitimate scientific and definitional question that the article does not explore.

The article also does not address whether post-mortem goosebumps are visible to the naked eye as readily as living goosebumps. Rigor mortis of the arrector pili muscles occurs alongside rigor of other muscle groups, which may distort the skin surface in ways that make goosebumps less visible or differently visible than in living persons. The temperature of a deceased body, which is typically colder than a living body, might also affect the visual presentation. A mortuary worker or forensic examiner might report having seen "goosebumps" on deceased bodies, but whether lay observers would recognize the phenomenon as equivalent to living goosebumps is a separate question.

Finally, the Snopes article does not address the broader implications of the question for public understanding of death and decomposition. The question "can dead bodies get goosebumps?" may arise from genuine curiosity, but it may also reflect misconceptions about death—for example, the belief that deceased bodies retain some form of consciousness or responsiveness that could produce an emotional or physiological reaction. By answering the question without addressing the underlying concerns that might prompt it, the article misses an opportunity to advance public understanding of what death means biologically.


## 4. Why This Matters

The goosebumps question, while seemingly trivial, illustrates several important principles in science communication, public understanding of biology, and the practice of fact-checking.

The first principle concerns the relationship between everyday language and scientific precision. The term "goosebumps" is a folk concept, not a scientific one. It refers to a visual phenomenon that we recognize from shared experience. But when we ask whether deceased bodies "get goosebumps," we encounter a boundary case that tests the definition. If goosebumps are defined by mechanism (autonomic nervous system activation), the answer is no—dead bodies cannot get goosebumps. If defined by visual appearance (raised bumps on the skin from arrector pili contraction), the answer is yes—dead bodies can exhibit this appearance during rigor mortis. The Snopes article implicitly chooses the visual definition without acknowledging the choice. This matters because the ambiguity between mechanistic and phenomenological definitions is a recurring challenge across all domains of science communication, from medicine (is brain death "death"?) to physics (is "weight" the same as "mass"?).

The second principle concerns the role of expert sources in science fact-checking. Snopes relied on a single death investigator with practical experience. A single practitioner, however knowledgeable, may not represent the full range of professional opinion or have complete knowledge of the scientific literature. The forensic pathology community has guidelines for reporting findings, textbooks that describe post-mortem changes, and peer-reviewed literature on rigor mortis. Citing these sources would provide readers with verification pathways and would guard against the possibility that a single practitioner's view is idiosyncratic or incomplete. The standard in science fact-checking should be higher than in politics or entertainment fact-checking, where firsthand knowledge of an event may be sufficient.

The third principle concerns the difference between "can happen" and "always happens." The Snopes article states that dead bodies "can experience goosebumps" through rigor mortis. This framing is careful—it does not say all dead bodies do experience goosebumps. But readers may interpret "can" as "typically does," which would be an overgeneralization. The visibility and occurrence of post-mortem goosebumps depend on multiple variables, and not all bodies will exhibit the phenomenon visibly. A more precise article would have discussed these variables and their implications for understanding the claim.

The fourth principle concerns public engagement with death and decomposition. The question about goosebumps is one of many questions the public has about what happens to the body after death. These questions are driven by genuine curiosity, cultural taboos around discussing death, and the increasing distance between modern life and direct experience with death. Fact-checking platforms that answer such questions provide a public service by normalizing discussion of death and providing accurate information. But they also have a responsibility to answer in a way that does not inadvertently reinforce misconceptions. The goosebumps question, answered without context, might leave the impression that deceased bodies retain some measure of responsiveness or sensation, when the phenomenon is purely mechanical and chemical.

The fifth principle concerns the "Mostly True" rating as a genre. The rating category acknowledges that claims are rarely simply true or false—they require context, qualification, and explanation. But the "Mostly True" rating also creates an expectation that the claim is substantially correct, potentially discouraging readers from thinking critically about its boundaries. A reader who sees "Mostly True" might conclude "yes, dead bodies get goosebumps" and move on without absorbing the important distinction about mechanism that the article carefully explains. This is not a failure of the rating system so much as a limitation of any categorical approach to complex scientific questions.


## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article by Madison Dapcevich relies on an interview with Heather Brown, a mortician-turned-death-investigator based in New Orleans. Brown's characterization of post-mortem goosebumps as resulting from rigor mortis is consistent with standard forensic pathology knowledge. The article also cites a Harvard Health blog post by Dr. Robert Shmerling that explains the mechanism of goosebumps in living persons.

The article does not cite any forensic pathology textbooks, peer-reviewed journal articles, or professional guidelines. A search of the forensic pathology literature reveals limited direct research on post-mortem piloerection specifically, but the phenomenon is mentioned in several standard texts:

- Knight, B., & Saukko, P. (2016). *Knight's Forensic Pathology* (4th ed.). CRC Press. Section on rigor mortis discusses contraction of all muscle fibers, including smooth muscle associated with hair follicles.
- Spitz, W. U., & Spitz, D. J. (Eds.). (2006). *Spitz and Fisher's Medicolegal Investigation of Death* (4th ed.). Charles C Thomas.
- DiMaio, V. J., & DiMaio, D. (2001). *Forensic Pathology* (2nd ed.). CRC Press.

The phenomenon of rigor mortis was first systematically described by French physician Eugene Bouchut in the 19th century. The underlying biochemistry—ATP depletion leading to irreversible actin-myosin cross-linking—was elucidated in the mid-20th century by researchers studying muscle physiology. The application of this knowledge to forensic pathology is well established.

The prevalence of the question "can dead bodies get goosebumps?" in public search suggests genuine curiosity about post-mortem body changes. Online forums, Q&A sites, and social media platforms contain numerous instances of this question, often arising from personal experiences with recently deceased loved ones or from popular culture depictions of death. The question touches on broader public interest in death, decomposition, and the boundary between life and death.

Snopes's "Mostly True" rating for this claim is among the more generous assessments in its science fact-checking portfolio. By comparison, claims about dietary supplements, alternative medicine, and health interventions typically receive "False" or "Mostly False" ratings. The discrepancy may reflect the lower stakes of the goosebumps question compared to health-related claims, but it also reflects the difficulty of rating claims that depend on definitional choices rather than factual disputes.

The article was published as a stand-alone fact check without an associated news article or series, suggesting it was generated in response to reader inquiries or search trends rather than as part of a planned editorial project. This ad-hoc approach to topic selection, while responsive to audience interest, means that the article may not benefit from the extended research and multiple expert reviews that characterize more systematic science fact-checking efforts.


## 6. Verdict

**Classification**: **Distorted but Grounded — Mechanistically Qualified**

The claim that dead bodies can get goosebumps is true if "goosebumps" is defined by visual appearance (raised bumps on the skin caused by contraction of arrector pili muscles). The claim is false if "goosebumps" is defined by underlying mechanism (autonomic nervous system activation in response to cold or emotional stimuli). The Snopes article implicitly adopts the visual definition, producing a "Mostly True" rating that is defensible but masks the definitional ambiguity at the heart of the question.

**What Snopes gets right:**
- Deceased bodies can exhibit the visual phenomenon of goosebumps through rigor mortis
- The mechanism in deceased bodies (rigor mortis) differs from the mechanism in living bodies (autonomic nervous system activation)
- The phenomenon is temporary, corresponding to the duration of rigor mortis
- The information from the expert source is consistent with established forensic pathology

**What Snopes misses or underplays:**
- The definitional ambiguity between mechanism-based and appearance-based definitions of "goosebumps"
- The absence of corroborating sources beyond a single practitioner
- The variable visibility of post-mortem goosebumps depending on environmental and individual factors
- The potential for multiple post-mortem mechanisms to produce piloerection beyond rigor mortis alone
- The broader public concerns about death and responsiveness that may motivate the question
- The lack of cited forensic pathology literature to support the factual claims

The goosebumps question, despite its apparent simplicity, reveals fundamental challenges in science communication: the relationship between folk concepts and scientific categorization, the standards of evidence appropriate for different types of factual claims, and the difficulty of communicating scientific nuance in formats designed for categorical verdicts. The Snopes article performs adequately within the constraints of its format, but a more complete answer would acknowledge the definitional ambiguity, cite the scientific literature, and use the question as an opportunity to advance public understanding of post-mortem biology.
