# Can European Starlings Talk?


## 1. The Claim

In June 2021, a video posted to Reddit under the account name omgmypony — and subsequently shared widely across multiple platforms — showed a European starling named Jabber producing phrases that sounded unmistakably like human speech: "gonna give him a kiss," "who's my precious?", and fragmentary utterances that, while not syntactically structured, were clearly modeled on auditory templates from human language rather than from the starling's natural vocal repertoire. The video attracted millions of views and generated substantial skepticism: parrots were widely known as vocal mimics, but a common European songbird producing human speech seemed, to many viewers, implausible or digitally altered.

The claim assessed by Snopes, published June 7, 2021 and authored by Dan Evon, is that the video shows a genuine European starling mimicking the human voice. The rating assigned is **True**. The capability is real, the individual animal Jabber was documented over several years of videos by the same Reddit user, and the vocalization mechanisms available to starlings support mimicry well beyond what most viewers would expect from a common urban bird.

The broader claim embedded in the viral response — that starlings cannot speak or that the video was fabricated — is incorrect. European starlings have been documented as vocal mimics in scientific literature for well over a century, and the cognitive mechanisms underlying this ability have been a productive subject of neuroethological research since the late twentieth century.


## 2. What's Actually True

The European starling (*Sturnus vulgaris*) is a medium-sized passerine bird native to Europe, western Asia, and north Africa, now established as an invasive species across North America, southern Africa, Australia, and New Zealand. It is a member of the family Sturnidae, the starlings and mynahs — a family with a notable concentration of vocal mimics, of which the hill myna (*Gracula religiosa*) is perhaps the most celebrated for human voice reproduction.

Starlings are indeed exceptional vocal mimics. The vocal repertoire of a free-living male European starling has been documented to include components drawn from roughly 35 to 65 distinct species of other birds, plus an additional layer of environmental sounds including human speech, the calls of domestic animals, car horns, telephone rings, and mechanical equipment (Hindmarsh, 1986, *Biological Reviews*). The extent and compositional complexity of an individual's repertoire increases with age, as birds continue to add novel imitations throughout their lives in a pattern more consistent with open-ended learning than with the closed developmental windows characteristic of many other songbird species (Eens et al., 1992, *Animal Behaviour*).

The vocal apparatus of songbirds — the syrinx — differs from the mammalian larynx in both anatomy and control organization. The syrinx is located at the bifurcation of the trachea into the two bronchi, and in many songbird species is characterized by independent muscular control of the two sides of the organ, enabling the simultaneous production of two independent sound sources. This bilateral independence is one factor underlying the complexity of songbird vocalizations (Goller & Larsen, 1997, *Journal of Experimental Biology*). In the context of mimicry, the operational precision of the syrinx allows detailed reproduction of the frequency contours, amplitude modulations, and tonal qualities of target sounds, including the formant transitions characteristic of human vowel production.

Mimicry in starlings appears to serve multiple behavioral functions depending on context. Washington State University wildlife ecologist Rodney Sayler, who has studied starling vocal behavior, notes that the functions include territorial advertisement, mate attraction, identity signaling to flock members, and potentially deception — some mimicry targets, particularly the calls of raptors, may function to disrupt competitor behavior by triggering flight responses. Sayler's statement to Snopes summarized the multifunctionality of the repertoire: how and why starlings incorporate such elaborate vocal diversity remains a complex, multivariate question without a single explanatory framework.

The case of Jabber is well-documented through multiple videos spanning several years. The Reddit account omgmypony posted videos showing Jabber as a hatchling (documented from approximately 2017) through adulthood, with the vocabulary growing in successive posts. The 2021 widely-shared video was thus the tip of a longitudinal record, not an isolated performance. This evidentiary continuity substantially strengthens its authenticity: fabricating or manipulating a multi-year video record with internally consistent vocal and developmental progression would constitute an extraordinary and purposeless effort.

The human-speech mimicry demonstrated by Jabber is not unique among documented starlings. Historical examples include a starling owned by Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, which was documented in the composer's account book in May 1784 as having been purchased because it could sing a theme from the Piano Concerto No. 17 in G major, K. 453 — a theme that was not published until after the bird apparently reproduced it, suggesting either that Mozart composed the theme in part from the bird's song or that the bird had overheard Mozart working on the concerto (West & King, 1990, *American Scientist*). The musicological and ornithological intersection of this case has been the subject of sustained scholarly attention and represents one of the most unusual documented instances of cross-species musical influence.


## 3. Why People Doubt This

The skepticism with which the Jabber video was received encodes several layers of incorrect background knowledge that are worth unpacking precisely because they are widely shared and extend to domains beyond starling biology.

The dominant background model most viewers carry for "birds that talk" is organized around the parrot order Psittaciformes — African grey parrots, Amazon parrots, budgerigars, and macaws being the most familiar examples. This model is accurate in its identification of psittacines as capable vocal mimics but incorrect in its implication that mimicry is a psittacine-exclusive ability. The actual distribution of vocal learning in birds is much broader. Corvids (ravens, crows, jackdaws) are documented mimics; mockingbirds (*Mimus polyglottos*) are named for the ability; lyrebirds (*Menura* spp.) can reproduce chainsaws, camera shutters, and car alarms with phonographic accuracy; certain manakins, wrens, and bowerbirds have been documented reproducing human speech or environmental sounds in field conditions. The parrot-centric model is a cultural product of the centuries-long human practice of keeping psittacines as pets, which concentrates parrot vocal behavior in domestic settings where it is widely observed, while equivalent behavior in non-psittacines goes largely unwitnessed.

**Epistemic Novelty — The Cognitive Mechanism Problem and What Mimicry Actually Requires**: The deeper epistemically interesting question raised by starling vocal mimicry is not whether the behavior exists — it clearly does — but what cognitive architecture is required to produce it and what this tells us about vocal learning as a convergently evolved capacity.

Oscine songbirds (the suborder Passeri, which includes starlings) are among the small number of animal groups known to exhibit vocal learning — the capacity to acquire vocalizations through auditory experience and practice rather than through species-typical developmental programs. Vocal learning is taxonomically rare: besides oscine songbirds, it has been reliably documented in parrots, hummingbirds, cetaceans (whales and dolphins), pinnipeds, elephants, and humans. This distribution is strictly non-phylogenetic: the capacity has evolved independently at least five to seven times (Jarvis, 2004, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*). The neural substrates for vocal learning in oscine songbirds — the "song system," a set of interconnected forebrain nuclei involving area X, LMAN, RA, HVC, and the robust nucleus of the arcopallium — are functionally analogous to but structurally distinct from the corticospinal motor circuits that control human speech production (Doupe & Kuhl, 1999, *Annual Review of Neuroscience*).

This convergent architecture matters for how we interpret the cognitive significance of starling speech mimicry. Jabber reproducing the phrase "who's my precious?" is not evidence that Jabber understands the semantic content of that phrase or possesses a model of the human relationships it references. But it is evidence that the starling's vocal learning system — which includes auditory template acquisition, motor pattern learning, and self-monitoring feedback loops structurally similar to those operative in human language acquisition — is powerful enough to model human vocal tract output from auditory input alone, without any homologous vocal anatomy. This is a substantial computational achievement, and the ease with which audiences dismiss it by categorizing the bird as "just parroting" (the phrase itself encoding the dismissal in onomastic form) obscures the genuine complexity of the underlying mechanism.

The word "parrot" used as a verb — meaning to reproduce speech without comprehension — embeds a persistent and somewhat unfair cognitive hierarchy in everyday language. The full picture is more nuanced: Alex, the African grey parrot studied by Irene Pepperberg, demonstrated productive use of learned labels that exceeded simple sound mimicry, including apparent object categorization, numerical competence, and the spontaneous production of novel combinations (Pepperberg, 2006, *Brain and Language*). Whether starlings achieve equivalent semantic depth remains largely untested. The operational question is not whether they "understand" in a human sense but whether the neural mechanisms underlying their mimicry are continuous with or categorically distinct from those underlying comprehension-associated vocalization in other vocal learners.


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: TRUE**

European starlings are genuine and accomplished vocal mimics capable of reproducing human speech. The 2021 viral video of Jabber the pet starling producing recognizable human phrases on command is authentic, corroborated by a multi-year documentary record from the same owner, and consistent with documented starling vocal capabilities extending back through the primary ornithological literature. The claim that the video was fabricated or impossible is false.

**EARC Mode: E — Empirically Confirmed**

Starling vocal mimicry is documented in peer-reviewed behavioral ecology and neuroethology literature across decades. The specific video's authenticity is corroborated by longitudinal documentation. The neurobiological basis for the capacity is established through studies of the avian song system and its functional analogies with mammalian vocal learning circuits.

**Gap Category: Animal Cognition / Viral Video**

This is a case in which a surprising but well-documented animal capability generates viral skepticism specifically because the viewer's background model of which animals can display it is too narrow. The corrective function of verification here is primarily schema expansion rather than false-claim refutation.


## 5. The Wider Picture

European starlings present a remarkable case study in the intersection of cultural history, ecological invasion, and ornithological biology. The North American population of what is now the most numerically abundant bird species on the continent traces to a single deliberate introduction event in Central Park in 1890 and 1891, orchestrated by Eugene Schieffelin, a Bronx resident and member of the American Acclimatization Society who reportedly sought to populate the United States with every bird species mentioned in the works of William Shakespeare.

The Shakespeare connection is documented through the character Hotspur in Henry IV, Part 1, written around 1600. Forbidden to speak the name of Mortimer to the king, Hotspur resolves to train a starling to repeat the name incessantly in the king's ear — a plot point that assumed familiarity with the starling's vocal capacities among Shakespeare's audience. The passage implies that starling speech capability was sufficiently well-known in early modern England to feature as a device in a revenge plot without requiring dramatic explanation. This represents one of the earliest documented references to the behavioral characteristic in the popular literature, predating systematic scientific study by approximately three centuries.

The ecological consequences of Schieffelin's introduction have been thoroughly characterized in the subsequent literature and are uniformly negative from the perspective of native North American cavity-nesting birds. European starlings are obligate cavity nesters that compete aggressively for nest sites with native hole-nesting species including Eastern bluebirds (*Sialia sialis*), red-headed woodpeckers (*Melanerpes erythrocephalus*), and several species of cavity-dependent ducks and owls. Koenig documented significant negative correlations between starling population density and reproductive success in several native cavity-nesting competition experiments (Koenig, 2003, *Biological Conservation*). The North American starling population, estimated at over 200 million individuals, makes it one of the continent's most significant avian biomass components — a population derived entirely from a few hundred founding individuals released within living memory of individuals born in the late 19th century.

The starling's ecological success in North America illustrates a general pattern in biological invasions: the same characteristics that make a species cognitively flexible and behaviorally diverse — broad dietary tolerance, high individual learning capacity, social information transfer, and vocal flexibility — are also the characteristics that confer advantage in novel environments. The cognitive attributes that allow starlings to mimic human speech are not operationally separable from the cognitive attributes that allow them to exploit novel food sources, navigate urban environments, flock adaptively in response to changing predation pressures, and rapidly colonize habitat types absent from their evolutionary experience.

The aesthetic wonder of Jabber's vocal performances and the ecological damage caused by Jabber's hundreds of millions of conspecifics co-exist in the same organism. The public's selective engagement with the former while remaining largely unaware of the latter represents a characteristic feature of how charismatic behavioral traits in familiar invasive species operate to reduce rather than increase conservation-relevant public understanding. Viral videos of talking starlings tend not to link to the ornithological literature on their competitive exclusion of bluebirds.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes entry by Dan Evon handles the central verification competently, confirming video authenticity through longitudinal account documentation and providing the key ecological and historical context — the Shakespeare connection, the 1890 introduction, the continental spread — that transforms the individual viral moment into a durable explanatory frame. The fact-check is well-structured: it confirms the primary claim, identifies the specific video's provenance through the Reddit account history, provides expert behavioral context from Rodney Sayler's analysis, and then extends into the historically interesting backstory of how starlings came to be in North America at all.

The format limitation, common to Snopes fact-checks, is the absence of primary literature citation for behavioral claims. The assertion that starlings are "accomplished mimics" capable of reproducing "a variety of sounds" is accurate but would benefit from citation to the Hindmarsh (1986) *Biological Reviews* review or the Eens et al. (1992) *Animal Behaviour* studies on repertoire acquisition, which would ground the behavioral claim in the quantitative primary literature rather than in WSU press releases. This gap is not a factual error but a pedagogical limitation: audiences who want to trace claims to primary evidence cannot do so from the fact-check format alone.

The mention of the Mozart starling connection — not included in the original Snopes piece but deeply relevant to the historical depth of the phenomenon — represents the kind of contextual enrichment that separates a bare verification from a genuine understanding-expanding entry. West and King's 1990 *American Scientist* account of Mozart's starling (Bird, 1787 original account book entry) establishes a documented 18th-century case of human-starling vocal exchange that predates systematic behavioral science and locates the species' mimicry within the aesthetic and cultural history of European musical culture rather than solely in the viral video ecology of the 21st-century internet. The gap between what social media audiences know about starlings and what is recorded in the ornithological and cultural-historical literature is enormous, and each high-engagement viral moment offers a narrow window to transmit more of that recorded knowledge than audiences would otherwise encounter.
