## The Claim

The popular claim at the heart of this article isn't a viral hoax or a fabricated image — it is a question posed with earnest seriousness in the wake of a scientific loss. In July 2019, Sarah — described as "the world's smartest chimp" — died at the age of nearly sixty. She had spent most of her life as a subject of cognitive research, her behaviour studied and parsed for glimpses into the inner world of our nearest living relative. Her death prompted researchers, philosophers, and science writers to revisit a foundational question that decades of careful science had made no more settled: can we really know what animals are thinking?

The claim embedded in the common understanding is broadly optimistic: yes, we can know what animals think. After all, we can observe their behaviour, we can teach them rudimentary language, we can measure their brain activity, and we can note that many animals exhibit behaviour that, in humans, we would unhesitatingly label as thought, intention, grief, play, or curiosity. The popular press has for decades celebrated the cognitive achievements of great apes, dolphins, corvids, elephants, and many other creatures. The implicit message of this popular science tradition is that the barrier between human and animal minds is thinner than we once assumed and increasingly permeable to scientific inquiry.

The philosopher's counter-claim — articulated in the Snopes-hosted article from The Conversation — is more unsettling: despite all of this evidence, we face principled limitations on our ability to truly understand, and particularly to precisely characterise, what animals think. This is not merely a problem of insufficient data or immature technology. It is potentially a problem of structure — a problem about the very nature of thought, language, and translation.

## What's Actually True

The scientific consensus is clear on the preliminary question: animals think. The behavioural evidence is extensive, sophisticated, and very difficult to explain away. Chimpanzees demonstrate problem-solving, social cunning, rudimentary tool use, and what appear to be proto-cultural behaviours transmitted between generations. Corvids — ravens and crows — solve multi-step puzzles, delay gratification, and appear to understand causal relationships in ways that rival or exceed the capacities of human children at certain ages. Elephants recognise themselves in mirrors, demonstrate what looks like grief at the death of companions, and maintain complex multigenerational social networks. Dolphins communicate in ways that include individual-specific signals that may function as names.

The chimpanzee Sarah became particularly famous because of an elegant experimental design. In one study that became a touchstone in cognitive science, Sarah was shown a person struggling to reach some bananas. When offered a choice between a stick and a key, she consistently chose the stick — the item that would allow the person to reach the bananas. When shown a person in a locked cage, she chose the key. The researchers interpreted this as evidence of a "theory of mind" — Sarah understood that the human in the scenario had intentions and goals, and selected the correct tool to satisfy those goals. This is not trivial. Theory of mind — the capacity to attribute mental states to others — is considered a hallmark of higher cognitive function.

But here is where the certain ground becomes uncertain: immediately after this study was published, other researchers objected. Not that Sarah's performance was unimpressive — it clearly was impressive. The objection was to the language used to describe it. Were "intention," "belief," and "knowledge" really the right human concepts to apply to Sarah's mental states? The disagreement has never been fully resolved. Decades of subsequent research have refined our understanding of chimpanzee cognition enormously, but the question of how to accurately characterise what they are thinking — in human terms — remains genuinely open.

The philosopher's argument, drawing on the work of W.V.O. Quine, deepens this difficulty substantially. Quine is famous for his analysis of "radical translation" — the project of translating a completely foreign language with no shared lexical or grammatical heritage. His conclusion was that there would always be multiple, equally valid translations. Even in the best case, we could never precisely characterise what individual words mean in that foreign language. There would always be residual indeterminacy. This is already unsettling when applied to human languages with shared evolutionary origins. When the "language" in question is the internal representational system of an animal with a fundamentally different perceptual system, evolutionary history, and behavioural repertoire, the problem becomes potentially insurmountable.

The analogy offered in the source article is illuminating: trying to translate the Mona Lisa into sentences. You could write a description of the painting — "it depicts a woman smirking slyly" — but no description captures it precisely. The painting is composed of pigments arranged spatially; sentences are composed of words arranged syntactically. The two representational systems are incommensurable at the level of structure. You can make approximations. You can make coarser or finer descriptions. But there is no precise translation in the strict sense.

Animal thought, the argument goes, is structurally like the Mona Lisa. It is composed of something — neural representations, perceptual states, bodily orientations, motivational gradients — that is organised according to principles fundamentally unlike the principles of human language. Our attempts to describe what Sarah was thinking when she chose the stick are not wrong, exactly. They may be excellent approximations. But they are not precise characterisations of her mental states in the way that translating "Schnee ist weiss" into "Snow is white" is a characterisation of the German. Something is always lost, and we cannot fully account for what is lost because we don't have independent access to the animal's representational system to check our descriptions against.

Crucially, the argument is not that animals lack rich inner lives. The argument is that we face principled limits on our ability to accurately describe those inner lives in human language. The inner life may be more complex, more vivid, or more alien than our descriptions can capture.

## Why People Believe This

The question "can we know what animals are thinking?" is one that humans have implicitly answered yes to for as long as we have lived with other animals. This instinctive answer is not irrational — it is grounded in deep evolutionary continuity. Humans and other mammals share neural architecture, behavioural drives, hormonal systems, and evolutionary history. The signals of fear, pain, pleasure, curiosity, and social bonding that we observe in dogs, chimpanzees, and dolphins are not arbitrary — they map onto systems that we have analogues of in ourselves. Intuitions about animal minds are not merely projections; they are informed by genuine structural homology.

**Anthropomorphism as adaptive heuristic.** The tendency to attribute mental states to animals — and to objects, environments, and even weather systems — is deeply embedded in human cognition. It likely had adaptive value: treating a predator as a purposive agent with intentions and goals is more useful than treating it as a purely mechanical system, because it more accurately describes predator behaviour. The same pattern of attribution applied to social partners, competitors, and domestic animals. We are wired to read minds, and we apply this wiring indiscriminately because in most of the ancestral environments it was applied to, it was more useful than its absence.

**The cultural narrative of animal intelligence.** A genre of popular science — from books like "Animal Liberation" and "When Elephants Weep" to documentary series on animal cognition — has cultivated an increasingly enthusiastic public picture of animal minds. This body of work performs an important and largely justified corrective to the older Cartesian view of animals as mere automata. But it also creates an expectation that animal thought is more directly accessible and legible than it may be. When popular science says "elephants grieve" or "crows plan for the future," these are approximations being offered as settled descriptions.

**Language teaching as apparent breakthrough.** Several famous research programmes — teaching great apes American Sign Language or symbol systems — generated enormous excitement because they seemed to offer a direct window into animal thought. If Sarah learns words, surely she will tell us what she thinks? But the philosopher's observation is sharp here: giving an animal a language doesn't resolve the translation problem, it transforms it. We now face the question of what Sarah's words mean, which is no more easily answered than the question of what her pre-linguistic behaviour means. The apparent breakthrough turns out to be a step sideways rather than forward.

**The emotional investment in yes.** There is a powerful motivational current behind the popular belief that animals think in ways we can understand. Humans care about animals. They form deep attachments to them. They find meaning in perceiving themselves in relationship with non-human minds. The possibility that animal thought may be in some principled sense opaque to us is genuinely uncomfortable — not just intellectually, but emotionally. It does not diminish concern for animal welfare, as the source article makes clear, but it does complicate the simple project of "understanding" that drives much popular animal cognition discourse.

## Verdict

**The scientific question and the philosophical question are separate, and we should be careful not to collapse them.**

On the scientific question — do animals think? — the evidence is overwhelming. Animals think. The cognitive capacities of great apes, corvids, dolphins, elephants, and many other species are remarkable and real, and the literature documenting them is extensive and of high quality.

On the philosophical question — can we precisely know what animals are thinking, in the sense of providing accurate characterisations of their mental content in human language? — the honest answer is: not entirely, and perhaps not at all in any strict sense. This is not a failure of science. It is a consequence of the structural incommensurability between animal representational systems and human language. The best we can do is what we already do: build rich, careful, empirically grounded approximations, flag their limitations, and remain genuinely humble about what remains opaque.

Sarah the chimpanzee demonstrated something extraordinary in her decades of research participation. Exactly what she demonstrated — precisely what concepts she was deploying, what her inner experience was like, what she understood about the humans around her — is something we can approximate but cannot fully specify. That incompleteness is not a reason for despair. It is a reason for ongoing humility, continued inquiry, and a sustained refusal to treat either confident anthropomorphism or confident dismissal as acceptable epistemic stances.

## The Wider Picture

The question of animal minds sits at the intersection of philosophy of mind, cognitive science, comparative psychology, evolutionary biology, and ethics. It is among the most profound questions in the study of mind and consciousness, and its difficulty is not merely a matter of insufficient technology or data — it touches on fundamental features of how minds represent the world and how representations can be shared across radically different species.

**The hard problem, extended.** The philosopher David Chalmers famously articulated the "hard problem of consciousness" — the difficulty of explaining why physical processes give rise to subjective experience at all. The difficulty of knowing animal minds is continuous with this hard problem but adds a further layer: even if we assume that animals have subjective experiences, the question of whether those experiences can be meaningfully described in a language built for a different kind of mind is a separate and additional challenge. We face, in the study of animal consciousness, both the hard problem and what might be called the cross-species interpretation problem.

**Welfare implications.** This philosophical uncertainty has direct ethical implications. The debate about how to treat animals ethically has often turned on questions of cognitive complexity: do they suffer? do they anticipate? do they have preferences about their futures? If we cannot precisely characterise animal thought, some argue this weakens the case for strong animal welfare protections. But the argument can run the other way: epistemic uncertainty should counsel caution, not dismissal. If we cannot rule out that an animal is experiencing something morally significant, the precautionary principle applies to our treatment of it. The inability to fully know what animals think is not a reason to act as though they think nothing.

**The diversity of animal minds.** The discourse around animal cognition often implicitly centres on "animal" as a single category, with humans as the standard of comparison. But animal minds are not arrayed along a single dimension from primitive to human-like. A raven's cognitive abilities are different from an elephant's which are different from an octopus's — each is specialised for the evolutionary problems of its lineage, and none is simply a "less advanced" version of human cognition. This diversity matters tremendously for the interpretation problem: translating raven thought into human language is a different project from translating chimpanzee thought, because ravens and chimpanzees have different perceptual systems, social structures, and ecological niches that generate different representational priorities.

**AI and the interpretation problem.** There is an interesting parallel emerging in the study of artificial intelligence. Contemporary large-scale neural networks are demonstrably capable of sophisticated behaviour — producing coherent text, solving complex problems, interpreting images. Yet there is a vigorous and unresolved debate about what, if anything, these systems "think" or "understand" or "know." The interpretive tools available to study neural network cognition are in some ways more powerful than those available for biological brains — we can examine the weights, trace the activations, perturb inputs and examine changes. Yet the question of what a neural network "means" when it processes an input remains contested. The structural incommensurability problem appears here too: the representational structures of a transformer neural network are not built from human linguistic units, and mapping them onto human conceptual categories involves the same kind of translation difficulty Quine identified.

**The legacy of Sarah.** Sarah the chimpanzee represented more than a research subject. She represented a sustained attempt by humans to reach across the species boundary and genuinely understand another kind of mind. The decades of work she participated in produced genuine knowledge — about the capabilities of chimpanzees, about social cognition, about the relationship between language and thought. That knowledge is real and valuable. The philosopher's observation that this knowledge remains incomplete and approximate is not a diminishment of that effort. It is a recognition of how genuinely difficult the project is, and how much further it has to go.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This article from Snopes is not a standard fact-check. It was published under Snopes' news section as a republication of an article originally appearing in The Conversation, an academic publication that commissions explanatory articles from academic researchers. The author was a philosopher writing in the month following the death of Sarah the chimpanzee. The piece was not evaluating a specific false claim but was making a philosophical argument about the epistemic limits of cognitive science.

This is important context for how to read and use this kind of material. Snopes — and fact-checking operations generally — do more than simply adjudicate true/false claims. They also, at their best, provide contextual and analytical frameworks that help readers think more carefully about categories of claim. This article exemplifies that broader function.

**What this piece does well.** The article provides rigorous philosophical content in accessible language. The Quine reference and the radical translation argument are compressed but accurately rendered. The Mona Lisa analogy is genuinely illuminating rather than merely decorative. The article is careful to distinguish between the empirical question (animals think) and the interpretive question (we can precisely characterise what they think), rather than collapsing both into a single confident answer.

**What this piece does not do.** It does not engage in sustained detail with the empirical literature on animal cognition — it refers to Sarah's famous experiment and gestures at broader research, but does not review the field comprehensively. This is appropriate given its philosophical rather than empirical focus, but readers seeking a review of the cognitive science literature would need additional sources.

**The genre problem.** Standard fact-checking operates within a binary or near-binary framework: a claim is true, false, or somewhere on a spectrum between. This article instead presents a philosophical argument that is not itself truth-apt in the fact-checking sense. The "claim" being addressed — that we can know what animals are thinking — is not the kind of claim that can be adjudicated true or false through evidence-gathering in the standard way. It is a conceptual and philosophical claim about the structure of mind and language. This type of claim requires different tools: philosophical analysis, conceptual precision, attention to the structure of the argument.

**For readers encountering this territory.** The epistemological modesty the article recommends — holding confident anthropomorphism and confident dismissal equally at bay — is not merely philosophical caution. It reflects the genuine structure of the evidence. We know a great deal about what animals can do. We know considerably less about how to precisely characterise what they experience in doing it. Both of these things can be true simultaneously, and sitting with that complexity rather than resolving it prematurely into a simple narrative is the intellectually honest position.

The question of whether we can truly know what animals are thinking may never be fully resolved. But the tools for approaching it with rigour — empirical observation, philosophical analysis, epistemic humility, and the sustained willingness to revise our characterisations in light of evidence — are available. What we owe to the animals in our care and in the wild is not a simple answer to this question, but the serious effort to ask it well.
