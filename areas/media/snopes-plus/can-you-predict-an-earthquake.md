# Scientists can predict earthquakes days or weeks in advance


## 1. The Claim

The belief that scientists either can or are close to predicting earthquakes days or weeks in advance is widespread and deeply persistent across both popular culture and mainstream media. The claim takes a number of related forms: that seismologists are developing methods to detect precursor signals — radon gas emissions, electromagnetic anomalies, unusual animal behaviour, ionospheric disturbances — that will allow authorities to warn at-risk populations hours or even weeks before a major event; that in some cases successful predictions have already been made; that countries like China or Japan have cracked or nearly cracked the prediction problem; and that scientists are being overly cautious or are withholding information about promising precursor methods.

A variant holds that significant scientific progress toward short-term prediction has been made but ignored or suppressed by institutional seismologists who regard prediction as heretical within their professional culture. This variant is especially common in the aftermath of high-profile failed prediction episodes or in contexts where an unorthodox researcher argues that their precursor method deserves more attention than it receives. The Italian courts' 2012 conviction of scientists involved in the L'Aquila earthquake communication drew enormous international attention and reinforced public narratives that scientists either knew more than they disclosed or judged public communication less important than professional caution.

The practical stakes of the claim are high. If earthquake prediction were genuinely possible, the humanitarian outcomes would be extraordinary. The 2010 Haiti earthquake killed approximately 160,000 people; the 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake and tsunami killed more than 220,000 people across fourteen countries. Advance warning of even a few hours — enough time to evacuate at-risk buildings, pause surgeries, stage emergency services — could save enormous numbers of lives. This humanitarian dimension gives the claim a motivational urgency that makes people want to believe it and makes skepticism seem cold-blooded or obstructionist. The emotional logic runs: if it were possible to save so many lives, surely scientists would be working urgently on it; and if they are working on it, surely they must be making progress.

The claim persists in part because of genuine scientific activity in this space. Research into seismic precursors — signals that reliably precede earthquakes — has been ongoing for decades. Funding agencies have supported it. Journals have published it. Individual scientists have staked careers on it. The research is real even where the results are disappointing. For lay observers, the presence of active peer-reviewed research implies a state of knowledge that the actual results do not yet support, and this gap between "scientists are studying this" and "scientists can do this" is a persistent, recurring source of public confusion.

Additionally, earthquake early warning systems do exist and function. Japan's J-Alert system and the United States' ShakeAlert system provide notifications before strong shaking reaches a given location. These systems have become more sophisticated and reliable over time. Media coverage of these systems — which routinely describes them as earthquake "prediction" — compounds the confusion. Early warning is not prediction in the sense the claim implies, but the word "prediction" is regularly misapplied to it in public communication, and the existence of the systems is then treated as evidence that prediction is real.

The 1975 Haicheng earthquake in China, which was preceded by a successful partial evacuation based on an official warning, fed the impression that at least some countries have achieved workable prediction in the past and that the question is merely one of consistent application. The fact that this event is exceptional and that the far more deadly 1976 Tangshan earthquake was not predicted is typically omitted from the popular narrative.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Deterministic short-term prediction is not validated by science.** The scientific consensus in seismology, as of the most recent comprehensive assessments, is that short-term deterministic prediction — specifying approximate time, location, and magnitude of an earthquake within a window of days or weeks — is not currently possible and may be fundamentally constrained by the physics of fault systems. Geller et al. (1997, *Science*) published a landmark analysis arguing that earthquake prediction is not feasible given the inherently nonlinear and chaotic dynamics of fault rupture, which cause small variations in initial conditions to amplify into large differences in event outcomes in ways that are not amenable to forward prediction from precursor observations. This paper generated substantial controversy and a large number of published responses, including direct refutations from researchers who believed the conclusion was premature. However, the core finding — that no validated method of short-term deterministic prediction exists — has held up across subsequent decades of research.

**Probabilistic seismic hazard assessment is real and valuable, and is not the same as prediction.** Seismology does provide robust probabilistic forecasting, which is structurally different from prediction. The Uniform California Earthquake Rupture Forecast (UCERF3), produced by the US Geological Survey, the Southern California Earthquake Center, and the California Geological Survey (Field et al., 2014, *Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America*), provides estimates of the probability that a given fault will produce an earthquake above a specified magnitude over a defined time horizon — typically 30 years. These forecasts are scientifically rigorous, regularly updated, and extremely valuable for building codes, insurance calculations, land use planning, and emergency management. They are not predictions. They do not tell you that an earthquake will occur tomorrow or next week. They tell you that an earthquake of magnitude 6.7 or above has a 60% probability of occurring somewhere in the San Francisco Bay Area within the next 30 years. This is meaningful information of a fundamentally different kind from the claim under examination.

**Earthquake early warning gives seconds, not days.** Japan's Earthquake Early Warning system, the US ShakeAlert system, and equivalent systems in Mexico, Taiwan, Romania, and China detect the fast-moving P-waves from an earthquake that has already begun and issue alerts before the slower but more destructive S-waves arrive at distant locations. Allen & Melgar (2019, *Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences*) provide a comprehensive review of these systems, noting that useful warning times range from a few seconds to approximately a minute depending on distance from the epicenter. This is transformative for automated responses — slowing high-speed trains, pausing robotic surgeries, alerting nuclear control rooms, giving people a few seconds to take protective positions — but it is not prediction. The earthquake has already started when the alert is issued. The technology detects an event in progress; it does not anticipate an event that has not begun.

**Precursor research has largely not produced validated methods.** Decades of research into proposed precursor signals — radon gas emanations from stressed rock (Wakita et al., 1988, *Geophysical Research Letters*), ionospheric electron content anomalies measured by GPS satellite networks, unusual animal behaviour in days before earthquakes, electromagnetic emissions detected at the surface — have not produced validated, prospectively tested prediction methods. Retrospective analyses frequently identify patterns that appear to precede specific earthquakes, but prospective tests consistently show that these patterns also occur without earthquakes following, generating unacceptably high rates of false alarms relative to true positives. The fundamental methodological challenge is that many proposed precursors are common and variable phenomena that appear frequently in the noise of normal geophysical and biological activity; identifying them as reliable earthquake signals requires prospective demonstration that cannot rely on selecting the subset of cases where an earthquake did follow.

**Operational earthquake forecasting after mainshocks is a real, narrow capability.** Jordan et al. (2011, *Seismological Research Letters*) introduced the framework of "operational earthquake forecasting" precisely to separate what can be communicated usefully to the public — probabilistic short-term hazard updates after a mainshock, based on established aftershock scaling relationships (Omori's Law), or during a swarm of seismic activity — from the unvalidated prediction claims that have eroded public trust in seismology communications. After a major earthquake, the probability of strong aftershocks is elevated and can be communicated probabilistically with calibrated accuracy. This is a genuine, valuable capability, but it is not prediction of primary events and it relies on the fact that a preceding earthquake has already occurred.

**The Haicheng case and its limits.** The successful evacuation before the 1975 Haicheng earthquake is real: local officials issued warnings based on an unusually prominent foreshock sequence, and a partial precautionary evacuation occurred before the magnitude 7.3 mainshock. Wang et al. (2006, *Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America*) analysed this case in detail and found that the prediction relied on an atypically clear and escalating foreshock pattern that is not representative of most earthquake sequences. The following year the magnitude 7.8 Tangshan earthquake — which killed approximately 242,000 people — was not predicted. Foreshock-dependent warning cannot be generalized into a reliable method because most major earthquakes are not preceded by identifiable foreshock sequences that distinguish themselves clearly from routine seismicity in advance.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of earthquake prediction belief reflects something genuinely interesting about how people model scientific progress and how the structure of scientific communication creates predictable, systematic misunderstandings — not just in this domain but across a range of hazard science claims.

**The category collapse between forecasting and prediction.** In everyday English, "forecast" and "predict" are near-synonyms. A weather forecast is a prediction; a financial forecast is a prediction. When seismologists say they can "operationally forecast" earthquake probabilities, most non-specialists hear "predict." The terminological distinction that seismologists have carefully developed to separate probabilistic hazard assessment from deterministic event prediction is invisible to audiences who have no reason to treat these as different categories of claim. The same word gets used for fundamentally different epistemic activities, and the credibility of the probabilistic forecasting enterprise — which is real, mature, and scientifically substantial — bleeds into the prediction claim, which remains unvalidated. This is not primarily a failure of public intelligence; it is a failure of scientific communication to operationalize a distinction that carries enormous practical importance but has no natural resonance in non-specialist usage.

This confusion is structurally different from most misinformation problems. Most misinformation involves people believing claims that are simply false. The earthquake prediction case involves people believing a coherent-sounding scientific capability that is real (forecasting, early warning) is something it is not (days-ahead deterministic prediction). The error arises not from credulity about a fabricated claim but from legitimate inference about what science that is described as "predicting" must involve. Recognizing this distinction matters for how corrections should be designed: the goal is not to debunk a conspiracy or a lie but to clarify a structural ambiguity in how scientific capabilities are communicated.

**The early warning system naming problem.** Earthquake early warning systems are regularly described as "earthquake prediction" systems in media coverage and in some official communications. A technology that sends an alert seconds before shaking arrives genuinely feels like a prediction to a person receiving it — if you are in a building in San Francisco and your phone alerts you five seconds before you feel the ground move, the experiential gap between alert and shaking closely mimics the structure of advance prediction. The phenomenology of early warning is functionally indistinguishable from short-term prediction at the individual experience level, even though the epistemological situation is entirely different. The conflation is understandable and persistent because it is experientially reinforced.

**Post-disaster attribution pressure.** Every major earthquake that kills large numbers of people generates public pressure on scientists to explain what signals they missed and whether better monitoring would have provided warning. This creates recurring pressure within scientific communication to frame capability aspirationally and to describe precursor research more optimistically than the evidence warrants, so as to avoid the politically damaging appearance of having given up on protecting the public. The resulting communication environment makes it systematically difficult for scientists to maintain the careful distinction between "we are studying possible precursors" and "we have validated a prediction method."

**Social proof from historical cases.** The Haicheng evacuation story circulates uncorrected in the majority of popular science writing and educational materials that discuss earthquake prediction. When readers encounter it, they take it as evidence that prediction has been done and can in principle be repeated. The Tangshan failure the following year — which demonstrates that Haicheng was an exception, not a generalizable method — is rarely paired with the Haicheng success in the same popular accounts. This asymmetric availability of confirmatory versus disconfirmatory cases is a standard mechanism of belief persistence in domains where confirming instances are more emotionally resonant than disconfirming ones.


## 4. Verdict

**REFUTED as a current validated capability.** The claim that scientists can predict earthquakes days or weeks in advance is not supported by the seismological literature. Short-term deterministic prediction — specifying time, location, and magnitude within a narrow window sufficient for evacuation planning — has not been demonstrated in prospective validated tests and is believed by most working seismologists to be fundamentally constrained by the chaotic, nonlinear physics of fault rupture.

What does exist is genuinely valuable and should be clearly communicated: rigorous probabilistic seismic hazard assessment over long time horizons; operational short-term probability updates following mainshocks and during active sequences; and earthquake early warning systems that provide seconds to a minute of alert after an earthquake has begun. These capabilities are distinct from the claim and should not be described as "prediction" in public communication without careful qualification.

Anyone encountering a report of a breakthrough earthquake prediction method should ask: Was this a prospective test, made before the earthquake with specific parameters in a publicly documented form that could have been falsified if no earthquake occurred — or a retrospective pattern match applied after the fact? Was the false alarm rate accounted for in the validation? Has the method been independently replicated? Nearly all claimed earthquake predictions that receive media coverage fail at least one of these tests.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The earthquake prediction problem is one of the most instructive cases in the philosophy of science precisely because it combines genuine methodological effort, compelling humanitarian motivation, institutional culture controversy, and repeated cycles of claim and disappointment over more than half a century.

The debate over whether earthquake prediction is achievable in principle versus in practice has been a methodological flashpoint in geophysics since at least the 1970s. Geller et al. (1997, *Science*) were paired with counter-arguments from researchers who believed prediction achievable with the right precursor methods, and the subsequent literature reveals genuine disagreement not about disputed facts but about what evidentiary standard a prediction claim needs to meet. This bifurcation — a mainstream that moved toward operational forecasting and early warning as tractable and valuable, and a minority committed to precursor research that views the mainstream as having prematurely abandoned the prediction problem — resembles the structure of other controversial areas where the scientific community and a motivated subset disagree about evidentiary thresholds.

The L'Aquila earthquake affair of 2009 and 2012 had significant effects on the field. Six scientists and one government official were convicted of manslaughter in 2012 for providing "incomplete, imprecise, and contradictory" reassurances before the magnitude 6.3 earthquake that killed 309 people; the convictions were partially overturned on appeal in 2014. The affair generated widespread discussion in the seismological community about how to communicate probabilistic hazard assessments to the public and what obligations scientists have when uncertainty is high and stakes are extreme. The ICEF (International Commission on Earthquake Forecasting for Civil Protection) report (Jordan et al., 2011, *Annals of Geophysics*) came directly out of this process and articulated the operational earthquake forecasting framework specifically to provide better communication protocols distinguished clearly from prediction.

Building codes, land use planning, early warning investments, and insurance pricing all rest on probabilistic seismic hazard assessments of the type that seismology can reliably provide. The practical value of what seismology can do is enormous and has saved many lives through improved built environments in earthquake-prone regions. Confusing this with short-term prediction does not merely overstate scientific capability — it also risks creating public disillusionment when prediction fails to materialize, which may undermine confidence in the probabilistic hazard science that is genuinely reliable.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The earthquake prediction claim surfaces in media contexts at predictable moments: immediately after major earthquakes, when unorthodox researchers claim a breakthrough, when early warning technology receives new investment or wider deployment, and periodically when historical cases like Haicheng are discussed in popular science contexts.

Mainstream fact-checking platforms have addressed specific prediction claims — particularly after viral posts claim that someone "predicted" a specific earthquake using an unorthodox method — but the underlying epistemological confusion about the difference between prediction, probabilistic forecasting, and early warning rarely receives full treatment. The most common fact-check intervention is the post-hoc prediction debunk: a claim circulates that an individual predicted an earthquake, fact-checkers trace the original statement, find that it was a general hazard probability or a vague comment about elevated risk, and rate the retrospective claim as false or misleading. This addresses individual instances without treating the structural source of the confusion.

For readers encountering claims about earthquake prediction, the most productive diagnostic question is: Was this a prospective prediction made before the earthquake, with specific parameters (time window, location, magnitude range) in a publicly documented form, following a procedure that could be repeatedly tested? Or was it a retrospective identification of patterns that look predictive given knowledge of what happened? The distinction is foundational to evaluating whether any proposed prediction method is actually doing what is claimed. Retrospective pattern matching is not prediction. It is the identification of what might have been a precursor, in one case, with no prospective validation of how often such patterns appear without earthquakes following.

Science journalists who cover seismology well explain the early warning / probabilistic forecasting / deterministic prediction distinction as standard background. Journalists who cover it rarely tend to collapse these categories, most often by describing early warning as "prediction," which produces sustained public overestimation of what earthquake science can currently do.
