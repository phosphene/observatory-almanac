# Canada Legalized Psychedelics as a Treatment for Depression


## 1. The Claim

Canada legalized psychedelics — primarily psilocybin and MDMA — as medical treatments for depression, making it a pioneering jurisdiction in a global shift toward psychedelic-assisted therapy.

The claim circulates most prominently in the context of mental health advocacy and psychedelic reform movements, where Canada's regulatory actions from 2020 onward have been presented as proof-of-concept for mainstreaming psychedelic medicine. It appears in social media posts that describe Canada as having "legalized" or "approved" psilocybin therapy, in news articles that characterize ministerial exemptions as "legalization" or "approval," and in fundraising and advocacy materials for psychedelic research organizations that celebrate Canada's actions as a breakthrough.

The claim situates within a genuine broader trend: the renaissance in psychedelic medicine research since roughly 2010, the FDA's Breakthrough Therapy designations for psilocybin (for treatment-resistant depression) and MDMA (for PTSD) in the United States, publication of landmark clinical trials, and growing political and cultural momentum for reform. Against this backdrop, Canada's regulatory flexibility has been presented as a vanguard moment — a country formally opening the door to psychedelic medicine.

Specific trigger events for the claim include: the August 2020 decision by then-Health Minister Patty Hajdu to grant Section 56 exemptions to four terminally ill patients allowing them to use psilocybin; subsequent grants of individual exemptions to patients and authorized healthcare providers; Health Canada's January 2022 expansion of the Special Access Program (SAP) to include psilocybin and MDMA; and media coverage that described these changes using "legalization" and "approval" language.


## 2. What's Actually True

Canada has created expanded compassionate access pathways for psychedelic substances under specific, narrow conditions — and this is genuinely significant policy movement. But this is legally, procedurally, and practically different from legalization, and the specific framing "treatment for depression" mischaracterizes both who can access these substances and through what process.

**The legal framework:** In Canada, psilocybin and MDMA remain Schedule III controlled substances under the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act. They are not approved drugs; no psychedelic has received a drug approval (Notice of Compliance) from Health Canada. Schedule III classification means that trafficking, possession without authorization, and production remain criminal offenses. No structural change to this classification occurred through the actions reported as "legalization."

**Section 56 Exemptions:** The Minister of Health has the authority under Section 56 of the Controlled Drugs and Substances Act to grant exemptions to the provisions of the Act for medical or scientific purposes. Beginning in August 2020, Health Canada granted Section 56 exemptions to individual patients — initially terminally ill patients in palliative care — allowing them to access psilocybin for end-of-life distress, not specifically for depression. These exemptions are individual, ministerially granted permissions, not a general authorization for psilocybin therapy. Over time, Health Canada also granted exemptions to therapists in training, allowing limited personal-use experience with psilocybin as part of therapeutic training programs.

The Section 56 mechanism is explicitly labeled "compassionate use" and is modeled on longstanding approaches to other not-yet-approved substances or uses. It is not legalization; it is an exception to a prohibition for defined individuals under defined conditions.

**The Special Access Program:** Health Canada's Special Access Program (SAP) allows healthcare practitioners to request access to drugs not approved for sale in Canada for patients with serious or life-threatening conditions when conventional treatments are inadequate. In January 2022, Health Canada amended the SAP regulations to allow requests for drugs that are still in clinical trials — which opened the door to psilocybin and MDMA requests for individual patients. Importantly, the SAP is practitioner-initiated (a physician or nurse practitioner must apply on behalf of a specific patient), it requires a demonstrated serious or life-threatening condition, and it functions case by case. Access is not guaranteed; Health Canada reviews each request.

The SAP expansion did not specify depression as a target condition, and it did not create a pathway specifically for depression. The conditions eligible for SAP access include any serious or life-threatening conditions where conventional treatments have failed — which could include treatment-resistant depression, but also PTSD, end-of-life distress, severe OCD, and other conditions being studied in clinical trials. The claim that Canada specifically legalized psychedelics "for depression" is not accurate.

**What regulated therapy looks like elsewhere:** The claim is partly sustained by comparison with Oregon (Measure 109, passed 2020, implemented through licensed service centers), which created the first state-level regulated framework for psilocybin therapy in the United States. Australia announced in February 2023 that it would reclassify MDMA and psilocybin as recognized medicines for PTSD and treatment-resistant depression respectively, with prescribing authorized through Therapeutic Goods Administration-approved psychiatrists. The Australian reclassification — which took effect in July 2023 — is genuinely more substantial than anything Canada has done, and is closer to what the phrase "legalization as treatment" suggests; the Australian media coverage, which the Canadian coverage partly echoed, may have contributed to confusion.

**The state of the evidence:** The enthusiasm around psychedelic medicine rests on a genuine and growing body of clinical research. Carhart-Harris, Bolstridge, Rucker, Day, Erritzoe, Kaelen, Bloomfield, Rickard, Forbes, Feilding, Taylor, Pilling, Curran, and Nutt (2016, *The Lancet Psychiatry*) published an open-label feasibility study finding significant antidepressant effects of psilocybin in patients with treatment-resistant depression. Davis, Barrett, May, Cosimano, Sepeda, Johnson, Finan, and Griffiths (2021, *JAMA Psychiatry*) published a randomized controlled trial finding that psilocybin-assisted therapy produced significant reductions in major depressive disorder symptoms compared to a delayed-treatment control. Carhart-Harris, Giribaldi, Watts, Baker-Jones, Murphy-Beiner, Murphy, Martell, Blemings, Erritzoe, and Nutt (2021, *New England Journal of Medicine*) compared psilocybin to escitalopram (a standard antidepressant) in a randomized trial, finding comparable reductions in depression scores with psilocybin, and greater improvements in some secondary outcomes including emotional connectivity and well-being.

These are promising findings. However, clinical promise and regulatory approval are different things. The treatments studied in these trials involve carefully curated therapeutic settings, extensive preparation and integration sessions, screened populations, and controlled conditions that do not yet exist in any general health system — including Canada's. The gap between "promising clinical trial results" and "approved treatment available to patients" is substantial in any jurisdiction.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The claim that Canada legalized psychedelics for depression is supported by a specific mechanism that emerges from the interface between regulatory complexity and advocacy storytelling: **liminal-status inflation**, the tendency to describe borderline or preliminary regulatory access as full approval when the narrative serves a desired political conclusion.

This mechanism operates in several reinforcing ways. First, the vocabulary of "legalization" and "approval" is blunt and emotionally resonant, while the vocabulary of actual regulatory change — "Section 56 exemption," "Special Access Program," "compassionate access," "not-yet-approved substance" — is technical, hedged, and difficult to fit in a headline or social media post. Journalists covering the story face a genuine choice between accuracy and communicability, and many choose communicability. The result is coverage that implies more legal transformation than occurred.

Second, advocacy communities — psychedelic research organizations, mental health reform advocates, harm reduction groups — have genuine incentives to frame Canada's actions as breakthroughs. Funding, momentum, political will, and public opinion all benefit from narratives of forward progress. "Canada showed it can be done" is a more useful advocacy sentence than "Canada granted individual ministerial exemptions to a small number of patients under a compassionate-use framework that does not constitute approval or legalization." The advocacy framing is not dishonest — it is genuinely directionally true — but it compresses important distinctions.

Third, the psychedelic renaissance has produced a reliable cultural script: clinical trials show promise → FDA/Health Canada/TGA action follows → mainstream medicine changes. Events that fit any part of this script get read as confirmation of the whole arc. Canada's SAP expansion fits enough of the pattern to be assimilated into the script even though the specific claim — legalization for depression — is inaccurate on both the "legalization" and the "depression" components.

Fourth, the Australian reclassification in 2023 — which is genuinely more substantial — generated international coverage that blurred with earlier Canadian coverage in people's memories. Australia actually did reclassify MDMA and psilocybin as recognized medicines under TGA authority. Canada did not. But both stories ran in similar publication contexts, under similar headings, and for audiences tracking the same policy trend. Conflation is predictable.

Fifth, there is a legitimate underlying claim that the strict claim overstates: Canada's regulatory actions are a meaningful shift from total prohibition to supervised, case-by-case compassionate access. This is real progress relative to the prior state. The fact that it is not legalization does not mean it is insignificant. The distance between what is true (meaningful regulatory flexibility) and what the claim says (legalization) is large enough to matter but small enough that people with a genuine investment in the underlying direction find the fine-grained correction less compelling than the narrative.


## 4. The Evidence Landscape

The evidence for and against the claim distributes across regulatory history, clinical science, and comparative drug policy.

**Regulatory history:** The August 2020 Section 56 exemptions granted by Health Minister Hajdu represented the first time Health Canada authorized any patient access to psilocybin outside of clinical trials. TheraPsil, a Canadian psychedelic advocacy and access organization, drove the initial applications and provided legal and logistical support. Subsequent Section 56 grants expanded to cover therapist training and additional patient classes. The January 2022 SAP regulatory amendment (published in *Canada Gazette*, Part II) formally opened the SAP to drugs still in clinical trials, removing a prior barrier that had prevented psilocybin and MDMA from being requested through that channel. These are documented regulatory actions — they are not fabrications — but they do not constitute legalization or even drug approval.

**Clinical evidence for psilocybin in depression:** Beyond the landmark studies cited above, Goodwin, Aaronson, Alvarez, Arden, Baker, Bennett, Bird, Brockbank, Coello, Cousin, D'Ausilio, and colleagues (2022, *New England Journal of Medicine*) conducted a larger Phase 2b randomized dose-finding trial of psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression involving 233 participants, finding that a 25 mg dose produced sustained antidepressant effects over 12 weeks. These Phase 2 trial results are the basis for the FDA's Breakthrough Therapy designation for psilocybin for treatment-resistant depression (granted to COMPASS Pathways in 2018 and Usona Institute in 2019). Phase 3 trials are underway.

**MDMA for PTSD:** The regulatory path for MDMA-assisted therapy for PTSD has been distinct from psilocybin for depression, though the two are often collapsed in public discourse. Mitchell, Bogenschutz, Lilienstein, Harrison, Kleiman, Parker-Guilbert, Ot'alora, Garas, Paleos, Gorman, and colleagues (2021, *Nature Medicine*) published Phase 3 trial results showing a significant reduction in PTSD severity among participants receiving MDMA-assisted therapy. The FDA declined to approve MDMA-assisted therapy in August 2024, citing concerns about blinding limitations and requesting additional data — a setback that somewhat complicated the narrative of inevitable approval but did not invalidate the underlying clinical findings.

**Australian reclassification:** The Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) of Australia announced in February 2023 that MDMA would be reclassified as a Schedule 8 (controlled) medicine for PTSD treatment, and psilocybin as a Schedule 8 medicine for treatment-resistant depression, effective July 1, 2023. Authorized prescribers (psychiatrists who apply for specific authorization) may now prescribe these substances under TGA-approved protocols. This is a genuine reclassification — meaningfully closer to the "legalized for treatment" description — but it applies to Australia, not Canada.

**Oregon's regulated framework:** Measure 109, passed by Oregon voters in 2020, created a regulated service framework for psilocybin that does not involve prescribing or medical diagnosis. Facilitators licensed through the Oregon Health Authority administer psilocybin sessions in licensed service centers. Oregon's approach is distinct from Canada's and Australia's — it is not a medical model — but is regularly cited in the same coverage as evidence of psychedelic mainstreaming.


## 5. Verdict

**Technically false, directionally true.** Canada did not legalize psychedelics as a treatment for depression. It established mechanisms — Section 56 ministerial exemptions and Special Access Program regulatory reform — that allow some patients with serious conditions, under practitioner supervision, to access psilocybin and MDMA outside of clinical trials on a case-by-case basis. These are meaningful regulatory developments that represent genuine movement away from absolute prohibition, and they reflect a growing evidence base from clinical trials, including robust Phase 2 and Phase 3 research on psilocybin for depression and MDMA for PTSD. But "legalization" overstates the legal change substantially: psilocybin and MDMA remain Schedule III controlled substances with no approved indication. "For depression" mischaracterizes who is eligible and under what conditions. The comparison country whose actions most closely match the claim is Australia, not Canada. The claim is therefore technically false on its specific terms even though it points toward a genuine policy trend.
