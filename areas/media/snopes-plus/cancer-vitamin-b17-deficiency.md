# Cancer is caused by vitamin B17 deficiency and can be cured by eating apricot seeds

## 1. The Claim

One of the most persistent threads in alternative cancer medicine holds that malignant disease is not a cellular proliferation disorder but rather a simple nutritional deficiency — specifically an absence of a substance some promoters call "vitamin B17." The cure, proponents insist, is straightforward: consume apricot kernels, the crushed pits of Prunus armeniaca, or their purified extract, known variously as amygdalin or laetrile. Cancer cells, the argument goes, are uniquely vulnerable to a compound these seeds contain, while healthy cells are protected by an enzyme that neutralizes it. Eat enough seeds, take enough pills, and the body heals itself.

The story has circulated in books, pamphlets, documentaries, and health forums since at least the 1950s, enjoying periodic resurgences whenever a sympathetic documentary is produced or a social media channel with a large health-skeptic following takes up the cause. Ernst T. Krebs Jr., the American biochemist most closely associated with popularizing laetrile therapy in the mid-twentieth century, developed the "trophoblastic theory of cancer" — arguing that cancer cells resemble placental trophoblast cells and share their vulnerability to amygdalin-derived cyanide. He coined "vitamin B17" specifically to suggest nutritional status rather than pharmaceutical intervention, framing conventional medicine's rejection of laetrile as suppression of a cheap, non-patentable remedy that threatened drug industry profits.

In its contemporary form the claim is specific: cancer is definitionally caused by B17 deficiency; Western diets, having abandoned bitter almonds, apricot pits, and other amygdalin-rich seeds, have stripped us of our natural cancer protection; and consuming apricot kernels — often recommended at doses of five to ten daily — reverses this deficiency, starving or poisoning malignant cells while leaving healthy tissue unharmed. Some versions extend the claim to chemotherapy, asserting that laetrile is more effective and causes none of chemotherapy's side effects. The suppression narrative is central to many articulations: a corrupt FDA, a pharmaceutical cartel, and captured medical journals are said to have jointly buried decades of positive evidence.

The emotional stakes of the claim are real. It is directed overwhelmingly at people who have received a cancer diagnosis, or who care for someone who has — the most psychologically vulnerable population in any therapeutic marketplace. The combination of a simple, natural, inexpensive solution with a compelling conspiracy framework makes this claim exceptionally resistant to correction.

## 2. What's Actually True

The chemistry of amygdalin begins with a fact that is technically correct but interpretively inverted by advocates: amygdalin does produce hydrogen cyanide in metabolic processing. Amygdalin is a cyanogenic glycoside — a sugar-bonded compound that, when hydrolyzed by bacterial enzymes in the gut or by the enzyme beta-glucosidase in chewed plant tissue, releases glucose, benzaldehyde, and hydrogen cyanide (HCN). This is a real reaction that occurs when apricot kernels are eaten. The advocates' claim is that this reaction is selectively toxic to cancer cells because they contain high levels of beta-glucosidase, while normal cells contain a protective enzyme, rhodanese, that neutralizes the cyanide.

That model is pharmacologically incoherent. Multiple lines of evidence contradict it:

**On enzyme selectivity**: Systematic enzyme assays have not established that cancer cells contain meaningfully higher beta-glucosidase activity than normal tissues, or that rhodanese activity is specifically elevated in healthy cells. Even if such differences existed in some tumors, the cyanide produced from amygdalin metabolism in the gut enters the systemic circulation indiscriminately before reaching any tumor. The selective-toxicity model requires that cyanide be preferentially metabolized at tumor sites, which has no supporting mechanism and has not been demonstrated in controlled studies.

**On clinical evidence**: The most important direct clinical test of laetrile was a Phase II study funded by the U.S. National Cancer Institute and published in the New England Journal of Medicine in 1982 (Moertel et al.). The study enrolled 178 patients with confirmed cancers for whom conventional treatments were available. Patients received intravenous amygdalin followed by oral maintenance doses alongside the "metabolic therapy" regimen recommended by laetrile advocates. No complete or partial tumor regressions were observed. Twenty-one percent of patients showed disease stabilization at the first evaluation, a rate indistinguishable from placebo effects and spontaneous fluctuation in measurement. At later evaluations, all patients showed disease progression. Several patients developed symptoms of cyanide toxicity. The study concluded that laetrile provided no anti-cancer benefit and posed measurable toxicity risk.

A 2006 Cochrane systematic review by Milazzo et al. identified all controlled studies of laetrile for cancer and found no reliable evidence of an anti-tumor effect in any of them. The review noted that the evidence base was weak in methodological quality, but that no positive signals had emerged even from studies most favorable to laetrile.

**On the "vitamin" designation**: "Vitamin B17" is a marketing term invented by Ernst Krebs Jr., not a recognized nutritional category. The established B vitamins (B1 through B12, plus folate, biotin, and pantothenic acid) are defined by specific metabolic functions — coenzyme roles, biosynthetic precursor roles — and by the evidence of deficiency diseases when they are absent from the diet. Scurvy results from vitamin C deficiency. Pellagra from niacin deficiency. No deficiency syndrome corresponding to amygdalin absence has ever been identified. The human body has no known metabolic pathway that requires amygdalin or that produces a functional deficit when amygdalin is not consumed. "B17" was designed to invoke nutritional legitimacy without the burden of meeting any of the criteria that nutritional science uses to establish vitamins as such.

**On regulatory history**: The U.S. Food and Drug Administration banned interstate commerce in laetrile in 1977, not as a matter of suppression, but following regulatory review that found no substantial evidence of therapeutic efficacy and significant evidence of cyanide harm. Federal courts upheld the ban. The Supreme Court ruled in United States v. Rutherford (1979) that even terminally ill patients did not have a constitutional right to unapproved treatments of unproven efficacy. The Mexican clinic system — particularly the Cínica del Mar in Tijuana, which became a major laetrile destination — existed largely because the ban drove U.S.-based patients across the border.

**On toxicity**: Apricot kernels contain amygdalin concentrations ranging from approximately 0.5 to 3.5 mg per gram kernel weight. At frequently recommended doses of five to ten kernels daily — which some advocates push higher — the cyanide load can reach acutely toxic thresholds. The European Food Safety Authority reviewed apricot kernel safety in 2016 and concluded that eating just three kernels in a serving size could exceed tolerable cyanide intake for adults, and that eating one small kernel could exceed the limit for toddlers. Case reports of cyanide poisoning from apricot kernel consumption include deaths in children, adults, and infants given apricot kernel preparations as alternative therapy. A 2019 Australian case series documented multiple hospitalizations from apricot kernel-based "cancer therapy," with several patients requiring antidote treatment (hydroxocobalamin).

## 3. Why People Believe This

Understanding why this claim persists requires taking seriously the emotional and epistemic conditions that make it believable.

**A cancer diagnosis creates extraordinary pressure toward alternative frameworks.** Conventional oncology offers treatments that are frequently brutal, often temporarily disfiguring, and not guaranteed to succeed. The language of probability ("five-year survival rates," "response rates") feels cold when applied to one's own body. The promise of a simple nutritional fix — no hair loss, no nausea, no financial devastation from hospital bills — carries enormous appeal precisely because conventional medicine cannot offer analogous simplicity. The worse the prognosis a patient receives, the more plausible a suppressed alternative remedy becomes.

**The conspiracy framework performs a specific cognitive function.** If negative studies on laetrile were conducted honestly, the claim is simply false and can be abandoned. But if those studies were suppressed, falsified, or designed to fail by a pharmaceutical industry whose profits depend on chemotherapy, a patient need never confront the negative evidence directly. The suppression narrative is unfalsifiable by design: every contradicting study becomes evidence of the conspiracy. This structure is common across alternative medicine claims but is particularly powerful in oncology, where institutional mistrust runs deep among patients who feel they have been given a death sentence.

**There is a real anti-cancer logic embedded in the claim, however incoherently applied.** Some chemotherapy agents do work through cytotoxic mechanisms — they kill cells. Targeted therapies do exploit metabolic differences between tumor cells and normal tissue. The idea that a natural compound might exploit a biochemical vulnerability in cancer cells is not incoherent in principle; it describes how several legitimate anti-cancer compounds work. The advocates of laetrile are applying a real framework (differential cytotoxicity) to a compound that does not in practice achieve it, but the framework itself is not irrational as an abstract idea.

**Testimonials are genuinely persuasive.** Oncology produces a certain number of spontaneous remissions in patients with solid tumors — events whose mechanism is poorly understood but whose existence is documented. When a patient who ate apricot kernels for three months experiences tumor regression, the attribution is obvious and emotionally compelling even if causally incorrect. The absence of controlled studies documenting this connection does not, from within the experience, feel like relevant evidence.

**"Natural" carries persuasive authority independent of pharmacology.** The cultural context in which laetrile advocacy operates includes a broader current of skepticism about synthetic pharmaceuticals and enthusiasm for plant-derived remedies. Apricot kernels are food items. Their health properties are not subject to the same skepticism as a pill. The naturalistic framing — this substance comes directly from nature, has been used by traditional cultures, predates industrial medicine — positions it outside the commercial motivations that patients reasonably attribute to pharmaceutical companies.

## 4. Verdict

**REFUTED — AND ACTIVELY DANGEROUS.** Amygdalin/laetrile is not a vitamin, does not correct a deficiency state, and has no demonstrated anti-tumor efficacy in controlled human trials. The clinical evidence is unambiguous: the NCI-funded Phase II trial showed no tumor regressions; the Cochrane review found no credible positive signal across the available evidence base; regulatory agencies in the United States, European Union, and Australia have banned or issued warnings against laetrile products based on both the absence of efficacy evidence and documented cyanide toxicity.

The "vitamin B17" designation is a fabrication designed to confer nutritional legitimacy on a substance that meets none of the recognized criteria for vitamin status. The enzyme-based selective-toxicity model that underpins the mechanism claim has not been validated in human tissue studies. The suppression narrative is structured to be unfalsifiable and functions to insulate the claim from contradicting evidence rather than to represent a factual historical record.

Most critically, the claim is not merely false but harmful in two distinct ways: directly, through the documented toxic and lethal effects of cyanide poisoning from apricot kernel consumption; and indirectly, through the documented pattern of patients deferring or abandoning conventional treatment in favor of laetrile therapy, sometimes until their cancers have progressed to untreatable stages.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The laetrile case is one of the most thoroughly studied episodes in alternative medicine history and has generated substantial scholarship on how fraudulent cancer remedies arise, persist, and cause harm.

**The geography of laetrile treatment is revealing.** The FDA ban in 1977 created a cross-border treatment economy in which American patients, often in late-stage cancer, traveled to Mexican clinics to receive laetrile. This medical tourism pattern — jurisdiction arbitrage for treatments whose home country has banned them — has recurred with other alternative cancer remedies. It illustrates a structural problem: banning a treatment in one jurisdiction does not eliminate demand, and may intensify the therapeutic mystique by confirming the suppression narrative.

**The pattern established legal precedent.** United States v. Rutherford reached the Supreme Court in 1979 because laetrile advocates argued that terminally ill patients had a constitutional right of privacy in their medical decisions that extended to choosing unapproved treatments. The Court unanimously rejected this argument, establishing that the FDA's jurisdiction over drug safety and efficacy applied to terminal patients as well as to those with recoverable conditions. This ruling continues to shape Right to Try legislation debates, with ongoing disputes about the conditions under which patients should be able to access unproven treatments.

**The biochemistry of cyanogenic glycosides is genuinely interesting outside this clinical context.** Amygdalin is part of a large family of plant defense compounds. Plants use cyanogenic glycosides as anti-herbivory defenses. The cassava plant contains linamarin, another cyanogenic glycoside, whose detoxification is critical food processing knowledge in regions that depend on cassava as a staple crop. Understanding these compounds has practical applications in food safety and plant biochemistry. The tragedy of the laetrile episode is partly that a real and interesting chemistry was recruited into a fraudulent therapeutic narrative, obscuring the legitimate science of cyanogenic compounds.

**Laetrile's persistence prefigures modern health misinformation ecology.** The claim has adapted to each media generation: from books and clinic brochures in the 1970s, to early internet health forums in the 1990s and 2000s, to YouTube documentaries, Instagram health influencers, and TikTok wellness communities in the present. The content is remarkably stable — the same Krebs-era arguments recirculate — but the distribution infrastructure changes. The lesson for media literacy is that the longevity of a health claim is not evidence of its validity; it may instead reflect the efficiency of particular psychological mechanisms that sustain misinformation independently of any evidentiary update.

**Apricot kernels are still sold in health food contexts.** In the United Kingdom, the Food Standards Agency has periodically issued warnings against the consumption of more than one to three bitter apricot kernels daily, as products continue to circulate in health food markets with cancer-adjacent messaging that stops just short of explicit therapeutic claims. The regulatory challenge of claims that employ insinuation, testimonials, and "food supplement" categorization to avoid drug regulation while reaching a therapeutically motivated audience illustrates a broader enforcement gap in food-drug borderline products.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has addressed laetrile and amygdalin claims over multiple years, noting both the lack of evidence for anti-cancer efficacy and the documented risks of cyanide poisoning. The platform's treatment of this claim reflects a broader pattern in how fact-checkers engage with alternative medicine: the adjudication of the core claim (does it work?) is generally straightforward given the quality of the clinical evidence, but the conspiracy layer (is it suppressed?) requires additional work because it involves historical institutional claims rather than pharmacological ones.

Cancer Research UK maintains a detailed page on laetrile that represents one of the more comprehensive public-facing treatments of the evidence, covering mechanism claims, the Moertel clinical trial, the Cochrane review, and the toxicity record. The Mayo Clinic, the Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (which hosts an "About Herbs" database entry on amygdalin), and MD Anderson Cancer Center have all published patient-facing materials that unequivocally characterize laetrile as ineffective and potentially deadly.

The Australian Therapeutic Goods Administration maintains a register of adverse event reports involving amygdalin and laetrile products. The European Food Safety Authority's 2016 opinion on the safety of apricot kernels is the most recent major regulatory science document and incorporates updated toxicokinetic modeling that strengthens the case against consumption at the doses advocates recommend.

The gap that fact-checkers face with this claim is not primarily evidentiary — the evidence is clear — but distributional. Each new documentary, social media campaign, or book release inserts the claim fresh into contexts where the audience has not encountered the prior debunking. Fact-check articles are reactive and depend on enough readers seeking out context to find them. The suppression narrative actively discourages readers from trusting the sources that have done the debunking, directing them instead toward testimonials and advocates who confirm the original claim. This epistemological trap — built into the architecture of the claim itself — is what makes its persistence structurally different from myths that simply haven't been corrected yet.
