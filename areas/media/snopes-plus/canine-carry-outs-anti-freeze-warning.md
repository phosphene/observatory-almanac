## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In March 2015, a wave of Facebook posts warned dog owners that Canine Carry Outs (also commonly spelled "Canine Carryouts"), a widely available commercial dog treat manufactured by the Big Heart Pet Brands division of the J.M. Smucker Company, contained "anti-freeze" as a hidden ingredient and were therefore "poison" to dogs. The posts typically featured photographs of the product packaging alongside alarmed captions claiming the ingredient propylene glycol — listed on the label — was the same substance found in automobile radiator antifreeze and was toxic to pets. Similar rumors had circulated in earlier years and would resurface again in 2016 when a veterinarian in Little Rock, Arkansas, Dr. Chloe Charlton, publicly claimed that a canine patient had been "poisoned" by the treats and that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration had recommended their removal from shelves.

Snopes Staff (2015) assigned this claim the verdict **False**. The determination rested on several empirically verifiable findings: propylene glycol is clearly listed on the Canine Carry Outs ingredient label and is therefore not "hidden"; propylene glycol is a chemically distinct compound from ethylene glycol, which is the primary toxic component in most automotive antifreeze formulations; propylene glycol is classified by the FDA as "generally recognized as safe" (GRAS) under 21 CFR 582.1666 for use in human food, and is approved for use in animal feed for all species except cats; and the FDA has never issued a warning, recall, or safety alert regarding Canine Carry Outs at any point in the product's commercial history. The 2016 claims by Dr. Charlton introduced additional confusion but, when examined by investigative pet-health journalist Dave Baker of Petful.com, were found to contain numerous inconsistencies, and Dr. Charlton declined to be interviewed about them (Baker, 2016).

This entry examines the chemistry underlying the propylene glycol–ethylene glycol confusion, the regulatory framework governing food-grade glycols, the specific toxicological profile of propylene glycol in canine physiology, the epistemic structure of ingredient-name-based food scares, the social dynamics that powered this particular rumor's spread, and the residual knowledge gaps that remain despite the claim's falsity.

## §2 The Propylene Glycol Confusion: Chemistry and Regulation

The core factual error animating this rumor is the conflation of two chemically distinct glycol compounds: propylene glycol (PG, also known as 1,2-propanediol, C₃H₈O₂) and ethylene glycol (EG, also known as 1,2-ethanediol, C₂H₆O₂). Both are dihydric alcohols (diols) with hygroscopic properties and freezing-point-depressant characteristics, and both can technically function as antifreeze agents. The similarity ends there. Their toxicological profiles diverge so radically that one is an approved food additive consumed daily by millions of humans and animals, while the other is a well-documented lethal poison responsible for thousands of accidental poisoning deaths in humans and animals annually.

Ethylene glycol is the primary active ingredient in most conventional automobile radiator antifreeze formulations. Its toxicity arises not from the parent compound itself but from its metabolic products: when metabolized by alcohol dehydrogenase and aldehyde dehydrogenase in the liver, ethylene glycol is converted through glycolaldehyde and glycolic acid to oxalic acid, which precipitates as calcium oxalate crystals in the renal tubules, causing acute kidney failure (Brent, 2001, *New England Journal of Medicine*, Massachusetts Medical Society). The lethal dose in dogs is approximately 4.4 mL/kg (Thrall et al., 1984, *American Journal of Veterinary Research*, American Veterinary Medical Association). Even sub-lethal exposures can produce permanent renal damage.

Propylene glycol, by contrast, is metabolized through fundamentally different pathways. It is oxidized to lactic acid and pyruvic acid — both normal intermediary metabolites in mammalian energy metabolism — and is subsequently fully metabolized via the tricarboxylic acid cycle (Ruddick, 1972, *Toxicology and Applied Pharmacology*, Academic Press). The Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR, 1997) of the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services explicitly states that propylene glycol is "generally recognized as safe" and is used as a direct food additive to absorb extra water and maintain moisture in medicines, cosmetics, food products, and tobacco. The FDA codified this GRAS status in 21 CFR 582.1666 (Code of Federal Regulations, Title 21, Volume 6), which permits its use in human food without quantitative limitation beyond current good manufacturing practice. For animal feed, propylene glycol is likewise approved for all species with a single critical exception: cats. The prohibition for felines stems from the documented capacity of propylene glycol to cause Heinz body formation in feline erythrocytes, a species-specific hemolytic vulnerability not shared by dogs or humans (Christopher et al., 1989, *Veterinary Pathology*, SAGE Publications).

Purina, responding to the recurring confusion, stated in its publicly available FAQ (archived): "Propylene glycol is approved as a food additive in human food and in feed for animals, except cats, in the U.S. and Canada... Propylene glycol can be used as a non-toxic anti-freeze, just as salt can be used as an 'anti-freeze'" (Purina, 2015, FAQ archives). This analogy is chemically apt: sodium chloride depresses the freezing point of water and is routinely used as road de-icing material, yet no reasonable person characterizes table salt as "antifreeze" when it appears on a food ingredient label.

The regulatory distinction is unambiguous. The Environmental Protection Agency regulates ethylene glycol as a hazardous substance under the Emergency Planning and Community Right-to-Know Act (EPCRA) and the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA). Propylene glycol, by contrast, is explicitly exempt from CERCLA hazardous substance designation and is not listed as a hazardous air pollutant under the Clean Air Act, despite being listed as a volatile organic compound in some state-level regulatory frameworks (LaKind et al., 1999, *Journal of Exposure Analysis and Environmental Epidemiology*, Nature Publishing Group). The two substances occupy entirely different regulatory universes precisely because their toxicological profiles are fundamentally different.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The "Ingredient Name = Danger" Fallacy in Food Safety Discourse

The Canine Carry Outs antifreeze scare exemplifies a specific and recurrent epistemic failure pattern in public food-safety discourse: the inference that if a chemical compound has any industrial or hazardous application, its presence in a food product constitutes evidence of danger. This fallacy — which might be termed the "dual-use inference" or the "ingredient name = danger" heuristic — operates by exploiting the gap between chemical nomenclature (which is precise and context-independent) and risk assessment (which is dose-dependent, pathway-dependent, and context-specific).

The structure of the fallacy is straightforward: (1) Identify a chemical name on a food label. (2) Discover — or be told via social media — that the same chemical has a non-food application that sounds alarming. (3) Conclude that the food product is dangerous because it contains a substance "also used in" the alarming application. This reasoning pattern is logically identical to arguing that water is dangerous because it is also used as an industrial solvent and coolant in nuclear reactors. The error lies in treating chemical identity as a sufficient condition for hazard equivalence across contexts, ignoring dose, concentration, route of exposure, and metabolic pathway.

Schwarcz (2004, *The Fly in the Ointment: 70 Fascinating Commentaries on the Science of Everyday Life*, ECW Press) documented this pattern extensively across food chemistry scares, noting that the "also used in" argument is one of the most reliable generators of viral food panic because it requires no scientific literacy to construct and produces a visceral emotional response disproportionate to its informational content. The dihydrogen monoxide hoax — in which water is described using its chemical name and a list of its industrial applications to demonstrate how easily people can be frightened by unfamiliar chemical terminology — has been used since the 1990s as a teaching tool to illustrate exactly this cognitive vulnerability (Zempel, 1994, as cited in Shermer, 2002, *Why People Believe Weird Things*, Holt Paperbacks).

In the specific case of propylene glycol, the "also used in" linkage to antifreeze is particularly potent because antifreeze poisoning is a well-known and genuinely serious veterinary emergency. Pet owners are routinely warned about the dangers of ethylene glycol — it has a sweet taste attractive to animals, and ingestion of even small amounts can be lethal. This legitimate and well-publicized danger creates a cognitive availability heuristic (Tversky & Kahneman, 1973, *Cognitive Psychology*, Academic Press) in which the word "antifreeze" is pre-associated with acute toxicity, sweetness-as-lure, and pet death. When propylene glycol is then framed as "a type of antifreeze," the pre-loaded emotional valence of the antifreeze concept transfers wholesale to propylene glycol, bypassing the chemical distinction that is the only relevant fact.

The epistemic novelty for fact-checking and science communication is that correcting this class of misinformation requires not merely providing the correct chemical facts — which Snopes did competently — but diagnosing and naming the reasoning pattern itself, so that audiences develop resistance to the next iteration of the same fallacy applied to different compounds. Propylene glycol in dog treats today; carrageenan, sodium benzoate, or butylated hydroxytoluene in human food tomorrow — the fallacy template is infinitely reusable unless the template itself is identified and critiqued (Goldacre, 2008, *Bad Science*, Fourth Estate).

## §4 Canine Toxicology: What Is and Isn't Dangerous to Dogs

Understanding why propylene glycol is safe for dogs requires situating it within the broader landscape of canine toxicology — a field in which the relevant dangers are well-documented but widely misunderstood by the general public, creating fertile ground for scares that attach to the wrong substances while genuine hazards receive comparatively less attention.

The substances that pose serious, well-documented toxic risks to dogs include ethylene glycol (discussed above), xylitol (a sugar alcohol that causes rapid insulin release and potentially fatal hypoglycemia in dogs; Dunayer, 2004, *Veterinary Therapeutics*, Veterinary Learning Systems), theobromine and caffeine in chocolate (Stidworthy et al., 1997, *Veterinary Record*, BMJ Publishing Group), certain species of Allium (onions, garlic, leeks, and chives, which cause oxidative hemolysis; Salgado et al., 2011, *Interdisciplinary Toxicology*, Slovak Toxicology Society), and grapes and raisins (which cause acute renal failure through a mechanism that was only recently identified as involving tartaric acid; Wegenast et al., 2022, *Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association*, AVMA). Each of these represents a genuine and sometimes lethal species-specific toxicity.

Propylene glycol does not belong on this list. The canine oral LD50 (lethal dose for 50% of test subjects) for propylene glycol is approximately 9 mL/kg body weight (Gaunt et al., 1972, *Food and Cosmetics Toxicology*, Pergamon Press) — a figure so high that a 30-pound (13.6 kg) dog would need to consume approximately 122 mL of pure propylene glycol to reach the LD50 threshold. Given that propylene glycol constitutes a minor fraction of dog treat formulations — typically present at concentrations of a few percent by weight for moisture retention — a dog would need to consume an extraordinarily improbable quantity of treats in a single sitting to approach toxic exposure levels. This basic dose-response calculation, while absent from most viral social media posts about the issue, effectively resolves the safety question on its own.

The one legitimate species-specific concern regarding propylene glycol involves cats, not dogs. Christopher et al. (1989, *Veterinary Pathology*, SAGE Publications) demonstrated that cats metabolize propylene glycol in a manner that produces elevated levels of Heinz bodies — denatured hemoglobin inclusions in red blood cells — at dietary concentrations that are harmless will be tolerated by other species. Based on this research, the FDA amended its regulations in 1996 to prohibit the use of propylene glycol in cat food (21 CFR 589.1001). This species-specific prohibition is sometimes cited by anti-propylene-glycol advocates as evidence that the substance is broadly toxic, but this reasoning commits the ecological fallacy in reverse: deriving a general conclusion (PG is toxic to animals) from a species-specific finding (PG causes Heinz body formation in feline erythrocytes due to their unique hemoglobin biochemistry).

The 2016 incident involving Dr. Chloe Charlton of Little Rock, Arkansas, injected a veneer of veterinary authority into the rumor cycle. According to media reports aggregated by Baker (2016, Petful.com), Charlton claimed that a canine patient had been "poisoned" by Canine Carry Outs and that she had contacted the FDA, which had purportedly recommended the product's removal from stores. Baker's investigative reporting found multiple inconsistencies in these claims: no FDA warning or recall was ever issued, no formal adverse event report could be located, and when Baker attempted to interview Charlton about the specific clinical presentation, diagnostic findings, and FDA communication she referenced, Charlton declined to participate. The absence of any corroborating FDA documentation — the FDA's pet food recall database is publicly searchable — effectively negates the authoritative framing that Charlton's veterinary credentials lent to the claim (Baker, 2016).

## §5 The Social Spread of Pet Food Safety Scares

The Canine Carry Outs antifreeze scare followed a dissemination pattern that has become characteristic of consumer-product safety scares in the social media era: initial posting by a concerned but scientifically uninformed individual, rapid amplification through emotionally motivated sharing ("better safe than sorry" logic), periodic resurgence driven by new trigger events (in this case, the Charlton incident), and remarkable persistence despite authoritative debunking.

The psychology underlying pet food scares specifically — as distinct from human food scares — has received some scholarly attention. Herzog (2010, *Some We Love, Some We Hate, Some We Eat*, Harper Perennial) documented that the human-companion animal bond generates protective impulses of sufficient intensity to override normal epistemic caution: when a pet owner encounters a claim that a product might harm their animal, the asymmetric cost structure of the decision (the perceived catastrophic cost of ignoring a true warning versus the minimal cost of avoiding a product "just in case") strongly favors sharing and acting on the warning regardless of its evidential basis. This is a specific application of the precautionary principle heuristic documented in behavioral economics literature (Sunstein, 2005, *Laws of Fear: Beyond the Precautionary Principle*, Cambridge University Press), but amplified by the emotional salience of the companion animal relationship.

The 2007 melamine pet food contamination crisis — in which melamine-adulterated wheat gluten from Chinese suppliers caused renal failure and death in thousands of dogs and cats, resulting in the largest pet food recall in U.S. history (Burns, 2007, *Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association*, AVMA) — created a lasting trauma in the pet-owning public that substantially lowered the threshold for believing subsequent pet food safety claims. The melamine crisis demonstrated that pet food supply chains could indeed contain undisclosed toxic contaminants, that regulatory oversight could fail, and that corporate assurances of safety could be wrong. These lessons were legitimate. But the cognitive residue of the crisis also created a generalized suspicion of pet food ingredients that does not distinguish between genuine contamination events and chemically illiterate misreadings of ingredient labels.

Social media platform architecture further amplifies this dynamic. Facebook's algorithmic preference for high-engagement content, combined with the emotional intensity of pet safety content, creates a distribution advantage for alarming pet food claims over their corrections. Vosoughi et al. (2018, *Science*, American Association for the Advancement of Science) demonstrated that false news stories spread faster, farther, and more broadly than true stories across all information categories on social media, with "novelty" — the surprising or alarming quality of the information — identified as the primary driver of differential sharing. A claim that a popular dog treat contains "antifreeze poison" has precisely the novelty structure that maximizes sharing velocity, while the correction — involving chemical nomenclature, regulatory classifications, and dose-response relationships — lacks novelty and requires cognitive effort to process.

The temporal pattern of the Canine Carry Outs rumor is also instructive. The claim did not appear once and subside; it appeared, was debunked, subsided, and then resurfaced at least once (in 2016) with fresh pseudo-evidence (the Charlton claims). This "rumor recurrence" pattern is well-documented in misinformation research (Berinsky, 2017, *British Journal of Political Science*, Cambridge University Press) and appears to be exacerbated by the architecture of social media platforms, where old content can be re-shared and re-contextualized indefinitely and where correction posts rarely achieve the same reach as the original claims they address. Each recurrence introduces the claim to audiences who missed both the original claim and its debunking, effectively resetting the misinformation cycle.

The economic consequences of such scares, while difficult to quantify precisely, are non-trivial. Unsubstantiated product safety scares can cause measurable declines in product sales, prompt unnecessary veterinary visits (with associated costs to pet owners), and erode consumer trust in food regulatory systems that are, in this specific case, functioning exactly as intended. The FDA's GRAS classification of propylene glycol reflects decades of toxicological research and regulatory review; the fact that a Facebook post can effectively override this entire apparatus in the court of public perception represents an epistemic failure with real economic and social costs.

## §6 Verdict, Knowledge Gap, and Epistemic Assessment

**Verdict: FALSE**

Canine Carry Outs dog treats do not contain "anti-freeze" in any toxicologically meaningful sense. The treats contain propylene glycol, a compound classified as generally recognized as safe (GRAS) by the FDA, approved for use in both human food and animal feed (excluding cats), and chemically and toxicologically distinct from ethylene glycol, the toxic compound in automotive antifreeze. Propylene glycol is clearly listed on the product's ingredient label and is not hidden. The FDA has never issued a warning, recall, or adverse event notice regarding Canine Carry Outs. The 2016 claims by Dr. Chloe Charlton of an FDA recommendation for product removal were not corroborated by any FDA documentation and were characterized by multiple inconsistencies identified by Baker (2016, Petful.com).

**EARC Classification: A (Analytical — ingredient misidentification)**

The primary gap this claim exploits is analytical: the conflation of two chemically distinct compounds (propylene glycol and ethylene glycol) due to their shared capacity to function as antifreeze agents. Resolution requires basic organic chemistry literacy — specifically, the understanding that chemical identity determines toxicological profile, not application category. The secondary gap is regulatory: the claim that propylene glycol is "hidden" or unapproved in pet food is directly contradicted by publicly available FDA regulations (21 CFR 582.1666) and the ingredient label itself.

**Epistemic Class: Ingredient-scare misinformation.** This class of misinformation is characterized by the "dual-use inference" fallacy — reasoning from a chemical's non-food application to its danger in food contexts — amplified by the emotional valence of pet safety concerns and the cognitive residue of genuine past pet food safety failures (particularly the 2007 melamine crisis). The pattern is highly reusable across different chemical compounds and product categories, making it a structural rather than episodic misinformation phenomenon.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- No large-scale epidemiological study has specifically examined long-term health outcomes in dogs consuming propylene-glycol-containing treats at typical commercial concentrations over multi-year periods. The safety assessment rests on acute and sub-chronic toxicology studies plus the GRAS classification process. While this is standard and sufficient for regulatory purposes, the absence of a canine-specific longitudinal dietary study leaves a narrow evidential gap that, while not supporting the "antifreeze poison" claim, represents an area where additional research would strengthen the evidence base.
- The specific clinical details of the 2016 Charlton case — what symptoms the dog presented, what diagnostics were performed, and what alternative etiologies were considered — were never made publicly available and cannot be independently evaluated. Charlton's refusal to be interviewed (Baker, 2016) prevents assessment of whether the case represented a genuine idiosyncratic adverse reaction, a misdiagnosis, or a fabrication.
- The precise mechanism by which the March 2015 Facebook rumor originated — whether it was an organic expression of consumer concern, a competitive attack by a rival brand, or an algorithmically amplified repost of earlier versions of the same claim — is not documented.
- Comparative data on propylene glycol concentration across different commercial dog treat brands would contextualize whether Canine Carry Outs contains higher, lower, or typical levels relative to the market category, but this data is not publicly compiled.
