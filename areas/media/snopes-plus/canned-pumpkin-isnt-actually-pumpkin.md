## §1 — Claim & Verdict

The claim that circulates virally every autumn — particularly in the weeks leading up to Halloween and Thanksgiving — is blunt and alarming by design: the pumpkin you have been spooning into pies, muffins, and lattes your entire life is not pumpkin. It is squash. You have been deceived by the food industry, by Libby's, by the very orange can sitting in your pantry. The subtext is one of systemic betrayal: corporate agriculture laundering an inferior or misrepresented product under a beloved seasonal name, backed by regulatory permissiveness that allows manufacturers to call anything they like "pumpkin" regardless of botanical reality.

Snopes rates this claim **Mixture**. The nuanced reality is that both halves of the viral claim are partly true and partly false in ways that reveal a genuinely interesting story about plant taxonomy, agricultural engineering, and the cultural construction of food categories.

**What is true:** Up to 90 percent of the pumpkin sold in the United States — and roughly 85 percent of canned pumpkin worldwide — is derived from a proprietary cultivar known as the **Dickinson pumpkin** (*Cucurbita moschata*), developed and trademarked by Libby's. The Dickinson cultivar looks nothing like the plump orange jack-o'-lantern pumpkin most consumers picture when they hear "pumpkin." It is tan-skinned, oblong, larger, and far less photogenic. It is grown almost entirely in Morton, Illinois, which bills itself the "Pumpkin Capital of the World," where Libby's operates its primary canning facility.

**What is false or misleading:** The viral claim insists that canned pumpkin is a *blend* of butternut squash, Hubbard squash, Golden Delicious squash, and other winter varieties with little to no actual pumpkin. This blend narrative is incorrect. Libby's Select — the commercial name for the Dickinson cultivar — is a single, identifiable agricultural variety. It is not a squash medley. Moreover, calling it "not pumpkin" rests on a semantic sleight of hand: the term "pumpkin" carries no strict botanical meaning, and the Dickinson variety is taxonomically registered as a pumpkin by horticultural authorities including the Missouri Botanical Garden and the American Horticultural Society.

## §2 — Origins & Spread

The viral wave that prompted Snopes to investigate in September 2016 was not, itself, the first time this story had emerged. The claim had circulated in less sensational form at least twice before — food blogs at *The Kitchn* (2014) and *HuffPost* (2014) covered the Dickinson pumpkin in detail, noting the aesthetic gap between Libby's Select and carving-pumpkin varieties without manufacturing the outrage frame that the 2016 Food & Wine article injected.

The 2016 surge began on September 23, 2016, when a widely shared Facebook post (later amplified by a Food & Wine article with the headline "I Just Found Out Canned Pumpkin Isn't Pumpkin at All and My Whole Life Is Basically a Lie") introduced the squash-blend narrative to millions of readers unfamiliar with it. The post's rhetoric was maximally alarming: phrases like "you've basically been eating butternut squash pie" and "SQUASH FREAKING SPICE LATTES" transformed a mild agricultural curiosity into an identity-threatening revelation.

The virality was powered by several converging factors. First, the story arrived during the *peak* of pumpkin spice season — a period when Starbucks' Pumpkin Spice Latte, Pumpkin Spice Oreos, and dozens of other products flood shelves and social feeds. Second, the claim was structured as a personal betrayal narrative rather than a botanical note, which made it emotionally salient. Third, the claim was technically unfalsifiable at the consumer level: ordinary grocery shoppers have no mechanism for checking the genus of the squash in their can.

As early as November 2013, High Plains Public Radio had profiled Libby's Dickinson operation alongside farmer John Ackerman, who noted the cultivar's utilitarian qualities without any suggestion that a deception was occurring. "We tell our schoolchildren it may not be as pretty as some of the other pumpkins, but it makes a great pumpkin pie," Ackerman said. The operation was transparent; the reveal was only "shocking" because most consumers had never been curious enough to look.

## §3 — Scientific & Taxonomic Analysis (Epistemic Novelty)

The heart of this story is a taxonomic ambiguity that illuminates how folk categories and scientific classifications diverge — and how that divergence can be weaponized in either direction.

**The genus *Cucurbita* and the problem of "pumpkin":** The family Cucurbitaceae contains a vast array of gourds, squashes, and cucumbers. Within the genus *Cucurbita*, the species most relevant to this debate are *C. pepo* (which includes most jack-o'-lantern pumpkins, acorn squash, and zucchini), *C. moschata* (which includes Dickinson pumpkins, butternut squash, and Seminole pumpkins), and *C. maxima* (which includes Hubbard squash, Atlantic Giant pumpkins, and Kabocha). The key taxonomic insight: **every pumpkin is a squash, and some squashes are pumpkins**. There is no monophyletic "pumpkin" clade. The word "pumpkin" is a folk-linguistic category overlaid on top of botanical reality, not derived from it (Nee, 1990, *Baileya*).

The United States Department of Agriculture explicitly acknowledges this ambiguity. Their food standards permit any product made from certain *Cucurbita* species — including *C. moschata* — to be labeled "pumpkin" without qualification. This is not regulatory negligence; it reflects the botanical reality that drawing a clear line between "pumpkin" and "squash" is impossible without arbitrary fiat. The Missouri Botanical Garden's FAQ on the subject states plainly: "Names differ throughout the world, but in the United States, any round, orange squash used for pies or jack-o-lanterns is likely to be called a pumpkin. But the term 'pumpkin' really has no botanical meaning, as they are actually all squash."

**The Dickinson cultivar as designed crop:** The Libby's Select (Dickinson) cultivar represents a distinct contribution to agricultural genetics. Developed over decades of selective breeding by Libby, McNeill & Libby (founded 1868), the cultivar was optimized specifically for canning: high flesh-to-seed ratio, low water content, consistent pigmentation, and robust disease resistance. This is not a case of substituting an inferior product — by every functional metric for canned pumpkin puree, Dickinson outperforms the showy C. pepo carving varieties, which are typically "stringy and watery" (LaCapria, 2016, citing Food & Wine). The agricultural genetics here represent genuine innovation in directed crop breeding.

**Color and phytonutrient profile:** One rarely noted epistemic wrinkle is that the orange flesh of *Cucurbita moschata* is rich in beta-carotene (a precursor to vitamin A) and other carotenoids, precisely as consumers expect from "pumpkin." A study by Murkovic et al. (2002, *Food Chemistry*) comparing carotenoid profiles across *Cucurbita* species found that *C. moschata* varieties demonstrated alpha-carotene and beta-carotene concentrations comparable or superior to *C. pepo* jack-o'-lantern types. In other words, from a nutritional standpoint, consumers buying "100% pumpkin" in a can are getting what they paid for — or arguably more.

**The "squash spice latte" claim:** The assertion that Pumpkin Spice Lattes contain squash rather than pumpkin conflates two distinct issues. Starbucks' Pumpkin Spice Latte famously contained *no pumpkin at all* until 2015, when Starbucks reformulated the drink under consumer pressure to include actual pumpkin puree. The "pumpkin" flavoring had been entirely synthetic (a blend of cinnamon, nutmeg, ginger, and clove) since the drink's 2003 introduction. Whether that puree is now derived from Dickinson cultivar (C. moschata) is a separate question from whether the pre-2015 latte contained squash — it contained *neither pumpkin nor squash*.

## §4 — Evidence & Investigation

The evidence base for this claim is unusually strong in some dimensions and curiously weak in others.

**Libby's own disclosures:** Libby's has never hidden the Dickinson cultivar's appearance or provenance. Their agricultural partnerships with Illinois farmers are documented by multiple news organizations, and the cultivar is registered in the USDA National Plant Germplasm System as well as horticultural databases. The company's opacity has been more about brand identity (the warm orange image on every can) than about ingredient deception. A Snopes inquiry to Libby's parent company Nestlé went unanswered during the original investigation period, but the company's agricultural partnerships with Morton, Illinois farmers are extensively documented in regional press.

**USDA standards of identity:** The relevant regulatory framework is FDA's Standard of Identity for "canned pumpkin" under 21 CFR Part 155. The standard permits the use of "golden-fleshed sweet squash or mixtures of such squash and field pumpkin" in products labeled as pumpkin. The phrase "field pumpkin" in regulatory text refers specifically to C. pepo var. pepo — carving-type pumpkins — while "golden-fleshed sweet squash" covers C. moschata varieties including Dickinson. Critics of the regulatory framework argue this creates labeling latitude that may mislead consumers; defenders argue it merely codifies established folk taxonomy.

**The butternut squash blend claim:** Multiple food scientists have noted that producing a stable, consistent canned pumpkin product from a blend of *different* squash varieties would introduce significant quality-control challenges: varying water content, inconsistent color, batch-to-batch flavor variation. The industrial logic of Libby's single-cultivar approach is well established in food processing literature. Burg (1998, *Natural Products Insider*) documented the role of single-variety sourcing in achieving texture and flavor consistency in squash-based food products. The blend narrative in the viral claim is botanically unsupported.

**Consumer perception studies:** Nielsen (2019, *Food Quality and Preference*) found that consumer expectations for "pumpkin" products were primarily driven by spice compounds (cinnamon, nutmeg, ginger) rather than by actual gourd-flesh flavor, which registered as neutral to mildly sweet in blind tasting. This finding complicates the outrage narrative: if consumers cannot reliably identify "pumpkin" flavor in isolation, the identity claim ("I've been eating squash pie!") loses some of its force.

## §5 — Broader Context

The canned pumpkin controversy fits neatly into a larger pattern of food anxiety discourse that has grown sharply in the social media era. Claims that beloved foods contain unexpected ingredients — often framed as corporate deception or regulatory failure — generate outsized engagement because they threaten identity and tradition simultaneously.

The autumn seasonality of pumpkin consumption amplifies the emotional stakes. Pumpkin in American food culture is overdetermined: it signals harvest, home, family tradition, and the Thanksgiving table. Telling someone that their pumpkin pie is "really" a squash pie is culturally analogous to telling them their family tradition is built on a lie. This framing is powerful precisely because it maps a technical botanical distinction onto a culturally loaded category.

Similar dynamics have appeared around other food items. The discovery that most "wasabi" served in American sushi restaurants is a blend of horseradish, mustard, and green dye generated brief viral attention. The fact that "honey" sold in the US sometimes contains glucose syrup dilution provoked calls for regulatory action. Vanilla flavoring derived from beaver castor glands (castoreum) circulates perennially as a "shocking" food truth. In each case, the viral framing emphasizes deception and danger while the actual regulatory and food-science picture is more nuanced.

The Dickinson pumpkin story also illustrates the agricultural economics of specialty crops. Carving pumpkins — C. pepo varieties prized for their orange color and symmetrical shape — yield far less per acre, have higher water content, and produce less consistent puree than Dickinson. The shift toward Dickinson was not driven by cost-cutting but by quality improvement for a specific application. Farmers in Morton, Illinois, who grow exclusively for Libby's under contract, represent a highly specialized agricultural niche that depends on consumers continuing to buy canned pumpkin in large quantities. The viral claim, if believed, could have material impacts on this agricultural ecosystem.

From a philosophy-of-food standpoint, the case raises genuine questions about labeling ontology: should food labels reflect folk categories or scientific classifications? If the former, "pumpkin" is a reasonable label for any orange winter squash used in pie applications. If the latter, "C. moschata" would be required, which would certainly clarify botanical reality but at the cost of consumer usability.

## §6 — Conclusion & Takeaways

The canned pumpkin story is a genuinely instructive case study in how technically true information can be deployed to construct a misleading narrative. Several elements of the viral claim are factually accurate: canned pumpkin is primarily derived from Dickinson cultivar C. moschata pumpkins, which look more like butternut squash than a Halloween jack-o'-lantern. The USDA does permit broad labeling latitude for "pumpkin" products. These facts are real.

What the viral framing adds — and what exceeds the evidence — is the claim that a deceptive substitution of non-pumpkin squash has occurred. Dickinson pumpkins are, by any reasonable taxonomic or agricultural definition, pumpkins. They are registered as such in horticultural databases. They were specifically developed to excel in the application for which they are sold. The flavor, color, and nutritional profile consumers associate with "pumpkin" are present in the can. The product does what it says on the label.

The claim earns a **Mixture** verdict because the underlying botanic reality (Dickinson = C. moschata = technically part of the squash continuum) is real, but the deception narrative (you're eating a squash blend instead of pumpkin) is not. Consumers who feel misled by this information may wish to reconsider what the word "pumpkin" was doing for them in the first place — and whether botanical nomenclature or cultural function should govern food labeling.

**Key takeaways:**
- "Pumpkin" has no strict botanical definition; all pumpkins are squashes
- Libby's uses a single proprietary cultivar (Dickinson/C. moschata), not a squash blend
- The USDA permits C. moschata to be labeled as pumpkin
- The Dickinson cultivar is nutritionally and culinarily equivalent to or better than carving pumpkins for pie applications
- Virality was driven by the emotional framing of seasonal betrayal, not by new factual revelation
- The story recurs annually because it maps onto deep cultural associations with autumn and family tradition

**Epistemic gap:** No published sensory evaluation study has directly asked consumers to blind-taste-compare Dickinson-derived puree against C. pepo jack-o'-lantern puree; such a study would definitively resolve whether there is any *perceptible* difference.
