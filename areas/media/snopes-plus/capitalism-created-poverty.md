# Did Capitalism Create Poverty?

## 1. The Claim

Capitalism created poverty. Before markets, enclosures, and global trade networks
imposed themselves on subsistence communities, people lived in genuine sufficiency.
Capitalism disrupted those arrangements, dispossessed people of common land and
traditional economies, and generated the extreme immiseration that we now call
poverty. The decline in absolute poverty statistics that economists like to cite
is either a measurement artifact, a modest recovery from capitalism-induced
destruction, or evidence of poverty reduction that would have happened anyway
under alternative systems. The real story is that global capitalism produces and
requires poverty — a reserve labor army kept precarious to discipline workers
and suppress wages.

This is a view held by a range of left-wing economists, social theorists, and
development critics, and it has significant intellectual substance. It is not
a fringe position. But the full empirical picture is substantially more complicated
than the claim suggests, and the claim as typically stated contains a causal
inference error that obscures rather than clarifies the actual relationship between
market economies and poverty.

## 2. What's Actually True

Three things are simultaneously true, and the claim requires all three to be visible:

**First: extreme poverty has declined dramatically under market-oriented globalization.**
The World Bank defines extreme poverty as living on less than $2.15 per day (2017
purchasing power parity). In 1990, approximately 36% of the world's population
lived in extreme poverty. By 2019, that figure had fallen to approximately 8.5%.
This is the largest reduction in extreme poverty in recorded human history, and
it occurred during a period of intensified global economic integration and
market liberalization. The correlation is not contested; the causal interpretation is.

The most dramatic reductions occurred in East and Southeast Asia — particularly
China, South Korea, Taiwan, Vietnam, and later Bangladesh and parts of sub-Saharan
Africa — through export-oriented manufacturing, trade liberalization, and rising
wages in previously agricultural or subsistence economies. These are not accidents.
People moved from rural poverty into industrial poverty, and then into something
closer to economic participation. The mechanism matters: it was not aid, not
redistribution, and not resource extraction that produced these declines — it was
market participation, with all its exploitative dimensions.

**Second: inequality within countries has increased, and the measurement of poverty
is genuinely contested.**
The Roser-Hickel debate is the most important methodological controversy in recent
development economics. Max Roser (Our World in Data) presents the declining
extreme poverty figures as evidence of globalization's success. Jason Hickel
(anthropologist and degrowth economist) argues that the picture is severely
distorted by: (a) an arbitrary poverty line that captures only the most dire
immiseration; (b) failure to account for loss of access to commons and non-market
subsistence; (c) the fact that at higher poverty lines — $7.40/day, which is a
more realistic minimum for adequate nutrition and health — the majority of the
global population remains in poverty; and (d) national income concentration,
which means that even as average incomes rise, the gains disproportionately
accrue to those already wealthy.

Hickel is right that the $2.15 line is low. He is right that commons enclosure
is a real and underweighted cost. He is right that relative poverty and inequality
are meaningful metrics. Where the argument becomes harder to sustain is in its
causal direction: the claim that things would have been better without market
integration requires a counterfactual that Hickel does not construct rigorously.
Pre-capitalist poverty was also extreme — life expectancy was shorter, infant
mortality higher, famine more common, access to medicine nonexistent. The baseline
against which capitalism is measured matters enormously.

**Third: pre-capitalist poverty was also extreme.**
This is the point that the "capitalism created poverty" framing most consistently
elides. Before enclosures and market integration, poverty was not absent — it was
structural, violent, and largely invisible to historical accounts that romanticize
subsistence. The average peasant in 14th-century England lived at or near
subsistence. Famines were regular and devastating. Common land access provided
a buffer, but not sufficiency. What capitalism disrupted was not abundance; it
was a different form of precarity and constraint.

The most defensible reading of the historical evidence: capitalism transformed
the nature of poverty — from subsistence precarity to wage-labor precarity —
while also generating the productive capacity that eventually made poverty reduction
possible. Whether that transformation was net positive or net negative depends
heavily on timeframe, measurement choice, and what counterfactual you're comparing
against. It is not a question that resolves cleanly in either direction.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The "capitalism created poverty" claim resonates for several reasons, some
empirically grounded and some not.

**The enclosure evidence is real and damning.** The English enclosure movement
(roughly 1500-1800) forcibly removed millions of peasants from common land,
eliminated subsistence farming arrangements that had sustained communities for
centuries, and produced the urban proletariat that industrial capitalism required.
This was not an abstract process — it involved physical dispossession, Parliament
acts privatizing shared resources, and displacement that caused genuine suffering.
Marx's account of "primitive accumulation" describes something that actually
happened. The error is generalizing from this specific historical process to a
universal causal claim about capitalism and poverty.

**Global inequality is stark and visible.** The wealth disparities visible in
the contemporary world — between wealthy OECD nations and sub-Saharan Africa,
between corporate executives and garment workers in Bangladesh — are products
of global economic arrangements. Those arrangements include colonial extraction,
terms-of-trade disparities, debt structures, and intellectual property regimes
that demonstrably benefit wealthy nations at the expense of poor ones. The
existing global order is not neutral. The claim that it actively produces poverty
captures something real about structural inequality, even if it overclaims on
the causal history.

**The poverty line debate activates genuine methodological concern.** When critics
note that the dramatic poverty reduction story depends on a $2.15/day line, they
are making a legitimate methodological point. Most serious economists acknowledge
that the choice of poverty line is not innocent — it determines the story you
tell. Using a $7.40/day line (a reasonable minimum for adequate nutrition, health,
and minimal social participation) produces a much less sanguine picture. The
impression that capitalism solved poverty is partly an artifact of where you
draw the line.

**Anti-capitalism functions as identity for significant intellectual communities.**
For Marxist, post-colonial, and degrowth scholars, the "capitalism created poverty"
frame is connected to a larger analytical apparatus that has genuine explanatory
power in other domains. The attachment to the specific causal claim is partly
a function of ideological coherence — giving it up would require revising a larger
theoretical framework, which has costs beyond the empirical question.

This dynamic operates symmetrically on the other side. For libertarian and market-liberal
economists, the "capitalism ended poverty" narrative functions as confirmatory evidence
for a framework that argues markets are the dominant mechanism of human progress. Giving
up the poverty-reduction narrative would require confronting the distributional failures
that the same market-oriented globalization has produced within wealthy countries. Both
sides are protecting a framework, not just evaluating a claim. Recognizing this symmetry
is part of what it means to evaluate the evidence honestly.

## 4. Verdict

**Contested, with directional clarity on the sub-claims.**

- **Did capitalism *reduce* extreme poverty?** Substantially yes, particularly in
  Asia, during the period of market-oriented globalization. The correlation is
  strong and the mechanism is plausible.
- **Did capitalism *create* poverty?** This is where the claim fails. Pre-capitalist
  poverty was also extreme. Capitalism transformed poverty's character and
  distribution, and in specific historical episodes (enclosures, colonial extraction)
  actively dispossessed communities. But the counterfactual — abundant pre-capitalist
  sufficiency — is not historically supported.
- **Has capitalism *solved* poverty?** No. At meaningful poverty thresholds, the
  majority of the world's population remains poor. Inequality within nations is
  rising in much of the world. Environmental costs of growth are increasingly
  unignorable. The work is not done.
- **Does capitalism *require* poverty?** This is the most sophisticated version
  of the claim. Reserve labor army theory (Marxist) argues yes. The empirical
  evidence is ambiguous — tight labor markets do produce rising wages, which is
  inconsistent with structural necessity of poverty. But monopsonistic labor
  markets, trade competition, and automation systematically suppress wages in
  ways that are consistent with structural pressure.

The honest verdict: capitalism is neither the creator of poverty nor its straightforward
solution. It is a system that has produced both dramatic poverty reduction and
persistent structural inequality, and understanding that duality requires holding
both facts in view simultaneously.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The capitalism-and-poverty debate is embedded in a larger methodological
controversy about how to measure human welfare and what counts as progress. It is
also inseparable from the history of development economics — a field that has cycled
through several major paradigm shifts since the post-WWII era, each revising the
previous consensus on what causes poverty and what cures it.

**The Washington Consensus and its failure** represent the clearest case study.
The IMF- and World Bank-endorsed program of liberalization, privatization, and
fiscal austerity that was applied across Latin America and sub-Saharan Africa in
the 1980s and 1990s produced mixed results at best and catastrophic outcomes at
worst. Countries that followed the program most faithfully — including Argentina,
which adopted currency board orthodoxy until its 2001 collapse — did not converge
toward wealthy-nation income levels. Countries that maintained state capacity and
selective protectionism — including China, South Korea, and Botswana in different
ways — achieved substantially better outcomes. The Washington Consensus is now
generally acknowledged to have been applied too rigidly, to have ignored
distributional effects, and to have underestimated the importance of state
institutions and social protection. That acknowledgment from within the mainstream
development establishment is itself a significant data point in the capitalism-
poverty debate.

**The Easterlin paradox** (1974) established that beyond a threshold, rising
income does not produce rising self-reported happiness at the national level.
Countries can have high and rising GDP while citizens report declining wellbeing.
This suggests that the standard development economics measure — GDP per capita —
is an insufficient proxy for what we actually care about. The relationship between
market growth and human flourishing is not linear.

**Degrowth economics** (Hickel, Kallis, Demaria) argues that wealthy nations can
improve wellbeing while reducing resource use — that "green growth" is insufficient
given planetary boundaries, and that the goal should be to meet human needs within
ecological limits rather than to maximize GDP. This framework provides an alternative
to both uncritical growth and the "capitalism created poverty" causal claim: the
issue is not markets per se but growth imperatives and resource metabolism.

**The development consensus** has shifted significantly since the Washington
Consensus era. The IMF and World Bank now acknowledge that distributional
outcomes matter alongside aggregate growth, that inequality can undermine
sustainable development, and that social protection programs (which are not
strictly market mechanisms) have been crucial to poverty reduction in
successful cases. Brazil's Bolsa Família and South Korea's active industrial
policy both involved significant state intervention in markets.

**Environmental limits** represent the most serious challenge to the poverty
reduction narrative. The growth-led poverty reduction of the last 30 years
occurred at significant environmental cost — carbon emissions, deforestation,
biodiversity loss, water depletion. It is not clear that the same growth model
can continue without ecological collapse that would devastate the populations
that have most recently escaped extreme poverty. This is not a reason to
romanticize pre-capitalist poverty, but it is a reason to question whether
the current model can deliver ongoing poverty reduction.

The poverty-capitalism debate ultimately resolves into a question about what
economic arrangement could deliver adequate material wellbeing for all humans
within ecological limits. That question is genuinely open. "Capitalism created
poverty" forecloses it prematurely by misspecifying the causal history.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Fact-checkers have addressed the poverty reduction statistics but have largely
avoided the deeper causal claim. The typical approach — "World Bank data shows
extreme poverty declining" — is accurate as far as it goes but doesn't engage
with the Roser-Hickel debate about what those statistics actually measure.

PolitiFact and Full Fact have occasionally addressed claims about poverty
reduction timelines and the measurement of poverty thresholds, usually rating
as "Mostly True" the claim that poverty has declined under globalization, while
noting measurement caveats. What they have not done is seriously engage with
the causation question — whether poverty reduction happened *because of* or
*despite* specific features of market capitalism, or what the counterfactual
would have looked like.

The "capitalism created poverty" claim itself rarely appears as a direct
fact-check target because it is typically embedded in political argument rather
than stated as a factual assertion. When politicians or advocates claim that
"capitalism has lifted hundreds of millions out of poverty," fact-checkers
mostly confirm the statistics while noting inequality concerns. When critics
claim capitalism causes poverty, the same level of scrutiny is less consistently
applied.

What rigorous fact-checking of this claim requires:

- **Distinguishing** the poverty-creation claim from the poverty-persistence claim
  from the poverty-reduction claim.
- **Engaging** with the measurement debate (which poverty line, which populations,
  which timeframe).
- **Confronting** the counterfactual problem: what would poverty levels look like
  under a non-capitalist global economy, and what historical evidence supports
  the comparison?
- **Acknowledging** that the evidence base here is not in the viral-claim domain
  — it is in academic development economics, where serious people genuinely
  disagree.

The claim is one where confident debunking in either direction is epistemically
unjustified. The intellectually honest verdict is contested, with the methodology
of comparison itself being the contested variable.

Fact-checkers should also be alert to the way the framing shifts depending on the
political context of the claim. When a left-wing activist says "capitalism created
poverty," the implicit claim is often about structural causation — the global economic
order systematically generates immiseration. When a right-wing commentator says
"capitalism ended poverty," the implicit claim is often about historical trajectory —
look at the data, things got better. Both speakers are selecting from the same
evidentiary landscape. Both are omitting the data that complicates their narrative.
The fact-checker's task is to provide the fuller picture that neither side offers
spontaneously.

The most rigorous position remains: capitalism is neither the origin of poverty nor
its sufficient cure. It is a system that has demonstrably reduced extreme poverty in
certain contexts and under certain conditions, while generating persistent structural
inequality, concentrating gains among those already advantaged, and imposing
environmental costs that are themselves a form of deferred poverty for future generations.
Holding all of that simultaneously is harder than a verdict — and more accurate.
