# Was a Man Named Carl Twinly Arrested For Pretending to be a Cow?

## 1. The Claim

Circulating primarily on social media beginning in April 2022, the story claimed that a man named Carl Twinly, of Beaumont, Texas, was arrested and charged with public indecency and what the story described as "masturbation by deception" after he allegedly dressed in a dairy cow costume and participated in a local 4-H milking competition, where he was apparently "milked" by thirteen contest participants before his deception was discovered.

The claim was accompanied by a photograph appearing to be a police booking image — a mugshot — of a person in a cow costume. The image's visual presentation (institutional lighting, forward-facing pose, visible details consistent with a law enforcement booking photograph) lent the story an air of documentary authenticity. Many viewers encountering the claim on social media never traced it to its source but instead treated the mugshot as corroborating evidence that the story involved a real arrest.

The claim spread through two parallel channels: first, users who found it genuinely funny regardless of its truth value and shared it for entertainment purposes, and second, users who appeared to believe it was a real news item and shared it in that spirit. This dual pathway — willing fiction-sharing and sincere misbelief operating alongside each other — is common in the spread of stories from satire websites, and it complicates the standard epistemic analysis. The claim's spread was not entirely driven by credulousness; it was also driven by the entertainment value of a story that people would enjoy even knowing it was fake.

The most explicit version of the claim stripped all satire labeling, presenting the text of the fictional Ringsssss.com story as if it were a straightforward news report. This decontextualized republication is the specific mechanism Snopes addressed, and it is a distinct phenomenon from the original satirical publication.

## 2. What's Actually True

The Snopes fact-check, published April 12, 2022, and written by Dan Evon, rates the claim **Labeled Satire**: the story is fictional, originating from a self-described humor and satire website, and has no factual basis in any real arrest, any real person named Carl Twinly, or any real 4-H milking competition incident of the described nature.

The original story appeared on Ringsssss.com, published in December 2021. Ringsssss.com carries a disclaimer on its "About Us" page identifying itself as "a fabricated satirical newspaper and comedy website." The disclaimer acknowledges that the content is invented.

The mugshot, however, is a genuine law enforcement photograph. It depicts a real person — not Carl Twinly — who was actually arrested while wearing a cow costume. The actual events behind the photograph are both verified and substantially less dramatic than the Ringsssss.com story. The photograph depicts Michelle Allen, a woman from Middletown, Ohio, who was hired to wear a cow costume to promote a local haunted trail in 2008. She was subsequently arrested on suspicion of disorderly conduct after allegedly becoming intoxicated, chasing children, and interrupting traffic in the area. Fox 19, the local television news affiliate, reported that Allen "was getting in the way of traffic and chasing children while wearing her cow suit" and was also accused of urinating on a neighbor's porch.

The real story, documented by Fox 19's reporting (2008) and verified by Snopes' image analysis (Evon, 2022), involves an actual person arrested in a cow costume but for entirely different reasons than those in the fictional Twinly narrative. The photograph was a genuine booking image applied — by parties unknown, and without attribution — to a wholly fictional story crafted around a different cow-costume scenario.

In March 2022, months after the original Ringsssss.com publication, the fictional story was republished on the website NewJerseyBrief.com, where it was presented without satire labeling as a genuine news item. This republication triggered the viral circulation that prompted the Snopes fact-check.

## 3. Epistemic Analysis: The Decontextualization Pipeline and Mugshot Credibility

Snopes' fact-check performs its core function correctly and efficiently. It traces the claim to its satirical origin, confirms the fictional nature of "Carl Twinly," identifies the real source of the mugshot and the real circumstances behind it, and notes both the Ringsssss disclaimer and the decontextualized republication that drove circulation. What the Snopes article does not address — legitimately, given its operational scope — are the structural mechanisms by which a clearly labeled satire story becomes viral misinformation, and the particular epistemic weight that mugshot photographs carry as apparent documentation.

**The epistemic novelty here lies in the semiotics of law enforcement imagery as a credibility vector for fictional stories.**

Mugshot photographs occupy an unusual epistemic zone in the information ecosystem. They are produced within an institutional context (law enforcement photographing persons in custody) that carries substantial authority signals: they are not staged, not selfies, not promotional images. The forward-facing pose against a neutral background, the institutional quality of the lighting and coloring, and the implicit procedural context all function as strong authenticity cues. Research on visual credibility — including work by Messaris and Abraham (2001), who argued in *Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly* that photographic realism creates a "'window on reality' effect" independent of the conceptual accuracy of the accompanying text (Messaris, P., and Abraham, L., 2001, "The Role of Images in Framing News Stories," *Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly*, 78(2), 270–283) — suggests that audiences treat photographs as direct evidence in ways they do not treat verbal claims.

The use of a real mugshot to illustrate a fictional story exploits precisely this credibility asymmetry. Readers encountering a story accompanied by a genuine law enforcement photograph receive a powerful non-verbal signal that something documented happened, even when the accompanying text is entirely invented. The cognitive shortcut — "there is a real document here, therefore something real occurred" — can override the interpretive effort required to assess whether the text attached to the image accurately describes the photographed event.

This is a variant of a documented phenomenon in visual misinformation research. Sundar (2008) noted in *Communication Research* that photographs function as credibility cues independent of their contextual accuracy, with readers rating news stories accompanied by photographs as more believable than structurally identical stories without photographs (Sundar, S.S., 2008, "The MAIN Model: A Heuristic Approach to Understanding Technology Effects on Credibility," in Metzger and Flanagin, eds., *Digital Media, Youth, and Credibility*, MIT Press, 73–100). The specific case of actual documentary photographs (mugshots, crime scene images, official documents) attached to fictional stories represents an intensification of this basic mechanism.

The decontextualization pipeline deserves analysis as a distinct phenomenon from simple fabrication. The Carl Twinly story did not spread because people fabricated it and presented it as news from the beginning. It spread because: (1) a satire site published clearly labeled fiction, (2) the fiction was copied elsewhere without the satire label, (3) the copy attached a real, contextually unrelated photograph that carries strong authenticity cues, and (4) social media sharing dynamics stripped remaining contextual information. At each step, the epistemic status of the claim degraded, and the credibility signals became misleading.

## 4. EARC Assessment and Gap Analysis

**EARC Mode: R — Refute.** The claim is fictional. Carl Twinly does not exist. No such arrest occurred. The mugshot depicts a real person in unrelated circumstances.

**Gap Category: satire-misread-as-news.** The claim falls into the well-documented category of satirical content that travels after being decontextualized from its source, accompanied by genuine photography that is misleadingly applied. The EARC assessment must account for several layers:

1. **The fictional claim layer**: Carl Twinly was arrested for cow-impersonation. FALSE. No such person, no such event.

2. **The mugshot layer**: The photograph depicts a real police encounter. TRUE, but for entirely different reasons (Michelle Allen, Middletown, Ohio, 2008, not Twinly, Texas, 2021).

3. **The decontextualization layer**: The story was republished as genuine news. TRUE. NewJerseyBrief.com removed the satire framing.

The gap in Snopes' coverage lies in its treatment of the decontextualization mechanism as simply "this is satire" rather than as a structural feature of the modern information environment requiring dedicated epistemological tools. Additionally, Snopes does not examine the sociological and legal landscape around costume-based public incidents — a real category of law enforcement interaction that forms the backdrop against which this particular fictional story is set.

## 5. Wider Context: Costume-Based Public Order Incidents and Satire Site Ecosystems

Despite Carl Twinly being fictional, the real Michelle Allen case reveals the existence of an actual category of law enforcement encounter: adults in animal or character costumes behaving in disorderly ways in public contexts. This category is sufficiently common to warrant scholarly attention.

Legal analysis of costume and disguise in criminal and public order contexts touches questions of identity presentation, intent, and the limits of acceptable behavior in public space. Prosser and Keeton's historical analysis of identity-related tort law (Prosser, W.L., 1984, *Prosser and Keeton on Torts*, 5th ed., West Publishing) establishes that disguise per se is not illegal in most jurisdictions but may become relevant in conspiracy, fraud, or public order contexts when used to deny identity in materially harmful ways.

The 4-H agricultural context referenced in the Twinly story — though fictional — points to a real institutional environment (agricultural shows, county fairs, livestock competitions) in which costuming and animal role-play have a complex cultural history. The American tradition of livestock show mascots, promotional performers, and agricultural education involving costumed characters interacts awkwardly with the sexual humor at the heart of the Ringsssss story. The satire works by transplanting sexual explicitness into an institutional context coded as rural, family, and agricultural — a comedic incongruity whose appeal is independent of its factual accuracy.

The Ringsssss.com ecosystem is part of a broader category of low-production-cost satire sites that produce content specifically designed to be credible enough to mislead but protected from legal liability by disclaimer. Research by Tandoc, Lim, and Ling (2018), classifying types of "fake news" along dimensions of facticity and intent, placed satirical content low on facticity but noted its capacity to mislead when decontextualized (*Journalism Studies*, 19(8), 1094–1112). The World News Daily Report (which appears in related fact-checks on similar stories) represents a similar site typology. These sites appear to generate content whose implicit business model is precisely the virality generated by misleading sharing — they benefit from their content being misread as genuine news even while maintaining legal distance through satirical labeling.

## 6. Conclusion

The claim about Carl Twinly is **Labeled Satire**, as Snopes correctly establishes. No person by that name was arrested, no such milking competition incident occurred, and the underlying story was invented by a self-described satire website in December 2021 before being republished as apparent news in March 2022.

The mugshot attached to the claim is real but depicts Michelle Allen, a Middletown, Ohio woman arrested in 2008 for disorderly conduct while wearing a cow costume for a commercial promotion — circumstances that share the costume element but bear no other resemblance to the fictional Twinly story.

The epistemic interest of the case lies in the decontextualization pipeline: a clearly labeled satirical fiction, a genuine documentary photograph applied without context, redistribution that stripped satire signals, and social media dynamics that amplified a credibly illustrated false story. Mugshot photographs function as credibility vectors because of their institutional origin (Messaris and Abraham, 2001, *Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly*) and their visual authority signals (Sundar, 2008, *Digital Media, Youth, and Credibility*, MIT Press), making them powerful tools for lending false stories documentary weight.

The larger gap in standard coverage of this case concerns the structural features of the information environment that convert labeled satire into unlabeled misinformation through successive copying, rather than the moral failure of any individual poster or platform.
