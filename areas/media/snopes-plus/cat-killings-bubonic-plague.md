# Medieval Cat Killings Made the Bubonic Plague Worse

*An epistemological audit of the claim that Pope Gregory IX's "Vox in Rama" caused mass cat exterminations that unleashed the Black Death.*

---

## 1. The Claim

The story, as it circulates across social media, popular history blogs, YouTube documentaries, and even some published books, runs approximately like this:

> In 1233, Pope Gregory IX issued a papal bull called "Vox in Rama" that declared cats—especially black cats—to be instruments of Satan. This triggered mass cat killings across medieval Europe, which eliminated the natural predator of rats. Without cats to keep rat populations in check, the rats exploded in number and carried the fleas that spread Yersinia pestis—the bubonic plague—killing a third of Europe's population between 1347 and 1351. The pope, in his religious fanaticism, inadvertently caused one of the deadliest pandemics in human history.

The story is clean, causal, and deeply satisfying. It has a villain (the medieval Church), a victim (the humble cat), and a catastrophic consequence (the Black Death). It functions simultaneously as an environmentalist parable about ecosystem disruption, an anti-clerical polemic about religious ignorance, and a tragic-ironic historical narrative about unintended consequences. It is also, in virtually every particular that makes it compelling, wrong.

This is not merely a matter of factual correction. The cat-plague myth is an epistemological case study—a specimen under glass that reveals how modern audiences construct, propagate, and defend historical narratives that feel true without ever requiring evidence. Examining it properly requires us to move beyond the binary of "confirmed" or "debunked" and into the more uncomfortable territory of understanding why false causal chains are so durable, what the actual documentary and archaeological records show, and how modern epidemiology has complicated the very model of plague transmission on which the myth depends.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

### 2.1 "Vox in Rama" Is a Real Document

The starting point of the myth is not invented from whole cloth. "Vox in Rama" is an authentic papal document, issued by Pope Gregory IX on June 13, 1233. It was addressed to Konrad of Marburg—a controversial German inquisitor—along with two other German clerics. The document exists; medievalists have studied it; its text is known.

What the document actually says, however, is considerably more specific and stranger than the myth allows. "Vox in Rama" is a response to reports that Konrad had sent to Rome about alleged Luciferian cult activity in the Rhineland. The document describes, in vivid and often bizarre detail, the supposed rituals of these heretical groups. Among the imagery: a black cat "about the size of an average dog" that descends backwards down a statue during initiation ceremonies. The initiates allegedly kiss the cat under its tail as part of their satanic worship.

This is not a decree against cats. It is not a directive to kill cats. It is not even primarily *about* cats. It is a papal letter acknowledging and commenting on reports of heretical activity, using the kind of lurid anti-heretical rhetoric that was standard in thirteenth-century inquisitorial correspondence. The cat appears as one element in a catalog of alleged demonic ritual—alongside toads, pale men, and various acts of sexual deviance that were routinely attributed to heretical groups in this period.

### 2.2 The Document's Classification and Reach

Under the formal taxonomy of papal documents, "Vox in Rama" is technically a *decretal*—a letter issued in response to specific questions or reports—rather than a *papal bull* in the formal sense of a universally promulgated decree. This distinction matters enormously. A papal bull was broadly distributed and carried the full weight of universal Church law. A decretal addressed to three German clerics about a specific regional heresy investigation was an administrative communication with a narrow audience.

There is no evidence that "Vox in Rama" was widely disseminated beyond its original recipients. It was not read from pulpits. It was not posted on church doors across Christendom. It was not, in any administratively meaningful sense, a Europe-wide directive. The idea that a letter to three men in Germany triggered continent-spanning cat pogroms requires an extraordinary claim about medieval communication networks that has zero documentary support.

Konrad of Marburg himself was murdered in 1233—the same year the document was issued—by people who objected to his inquisitorial zeal. His death effectively terminated the specific investigation that "Vox in Rama" addressed. The document became, in practical terms, a dead letter almost immediately.

### 2.3 Medieval Attitudes Toward Cats

The myth requires that medieval Europeans hated and feared cats to such a degree that a papal nudge could have triggered mass extermination. The actual medieval record is considerably more nuanced.

Cats in medieval Europe occupied a complex cultural position. They were valued—sometimes highly valued—for rodent control. Manorial records, household inventories, and agricultural treatises from the period consistently document cats as working animals essential to grain storage. The Welsh legal code *Cyfraith Hywel* (Laws of Hywel Dda), compiled in the tenth century but reflecting earlier practice, set specific penalties for killing or stealing cats and established their monetary value based on their proven ability to catch mice. A cat that had caught mice was worth more than a kitten that hadn't yet proved itself. This is not a culture that was systematically exterminating its cats.

At the same time, cats did carry negative symbolic associations in some theological and literary contexts. Black cats could symbolize witchcraft or demonic activity—but so could black dogs, goats, toads, and numerous other animals. These symbolic associations coexisted with the practical reality that households kept cats and depended on them. The idea that symbolic demonization translated directly into physical extermination would require medieval people to be incapable of distinguishing theology from pest control—an assumption that says more about modern condescension toward the medieval period than about medieval reality.

### 2.4 The Archaeological Evidence

One piece of evidence that occasionally gets recruited into the myth is a Cambridge archaeological study that recovered the remains of seventy-nine cats from medieval contexts. The cats showed signs of having been skinned—their pelts removed—which the excavators described as "unusual" in the archaeological record.

This finding is real, but its interpretation in the context of the myth is precisely backwards. The Cambridge cats were skinned for their *fur*—for use in the manufacture of gloves, hat linings, and other goods. Cat fur was a minor but documented medieval commodity. The fact that these cats were butchered commercially, rather than burned in religious purges, actually provides evidence *against* the mass extermination narrative and *for* the more prosaic reality that medieval people treated cats as economic resources. Butchered cat remains from the thirteenth century, found alongside other animal processing waste, tell us about the medieval fur trade, not about papal-inspired pogroms.

Additional archaeological work across medieval European sites has found cat remains in contexts that indicate continued domestic cat-keeping throughout the period supposedly characterized by mass killings. Cats appear in domestic waste deposits, in association with human dwellings, and in patterns consistent with pet-keeping and working-animal management. The archaeological record does not show a discontinuity consistent with mass extermination.

### 2.5 The Only Documented Mass Cat Killing

If one is looking for historical evidence of organized, large-scale cat killing in Europe, there is exactly one well-documented case—and it postdates the Black Death by four centuries. Robert Darnton's celebrated study *The Great Cat Massacre and Other Episodes in French Cultural History* (1984) describes an incident in Paris in the 1730s in which a group of printing apprentices killed cats belonging to their master's wife as an act of social protest and dark humor. Darnton's analysis is a landmark of cultural history precisely because the event was unusual enough to warrant detailed examination. It was a specific, localized episode rooted in eighteenth-century labor relations, not evidence of a medieval pan-European pattern.

The absence of any comparable documentation from the thirteenth through fourteenth centuries is not merely an argument from silence. Medieval chroniclers, who recorded everything from unusual weather to the price of grain, who cataloged heresies and their suppressions in exhaustive detail, who documented the mass persecution of Jews and lepers during the Black Death itself, left no record of organized cat killings. For a continent-wide campaign of animal extermination to have occurred and left no trace in the abundant surviving chronicles, court records, municipal ordinances, or ecclesiastical documents of the period would require a conspiracy of silence more remarkable than the alleged killings themselves.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### 3.1 The Seduction of Neat Causality

The cat-plague story succeeds because it offers what epistemologists might call a *closed causal loop*: a single cause (papal decree), a clear mechanism (cat removal destroys pest control), and a massive effect (the Black Death). This is precisely the kind of causal structure that human cognition finds most satisfying and most persuasive.

The problem is that real historical causation almost never works this way. The Black Death had causes that were biological (the virulence of *Yersinia pestis*), ecological (trade routes connecting Central Asia to Europe via the Silk Road and Mediterranean shipping), climatic (the cooling period of the fourteenth century and its effects on rodent habitats and human vulnerability), social (population density, malnutrition, prior waves of famine), and epidemiological (the mechanisms of transmission itself, which as we will see are more complex than the simple rat-flea model). Reducing this web of causation to "the pope killed the cats" is not just wrong—it represents a fundamental misunderstanding of how pandemics emerge.

The appeal of monocausal explanations is well-documented in cognitive science. Humans have a strong preference for stories with identifiable agents (the pope), clear intentions (demonizing cats), and proportional consequences (mass death). We are less comfortable with explanations that involve multiple interacting variables, contingent events, long-term ecological processes, and the fundamental role of chance. The cat-plague story gives us an agent where epidemiology gives us complexity.

### 3.2 The Anti-Medieval Bias

The myth draws considerable power from what historians call the "Dark Ages" narrative—the persistent popular belief that the medieval period was one of unrelieved ignorance, superstition, and religious tyranny. This framework, which has been systematically dismantled by professional medievalists over the past century, remains remarkably durable in popular culture.

Within this framework, it seems *obviously* plausible that a medieval pope would order cats killed based on superstition, and that medieval people would be ignorant enough to comply. The myth requires no evidence because it aligns with priors that feel self-evident. Medieval people were stupid and superstitious; the Church was authoritarian and irrational; bad things happened because of religious ignorance. QED.

This is circular reasoning masquerading as historical knowledge. The conclusion (medieval cat killings) is assumed because it's consistent with the premise (medieval irrationality), and the premise is maintained because it generates conclusions like this one. At no point does evidence enter the loop.

The actual medieval period produced the university system, Gothic architecture, significant advances in agricultural technology, sophisticated legal codes, complex financial instruments, and a philosophical tradition of considerable rigor. Medieval people were not modern people—their knowledge base, cosmological assumptions, and institutional structures were genuinely different from ours—but they were not the cartoon figures that the "Dark Ages" narrative requires. They could and did distinguish between theological symbolism and agricultural necessity.

### 3.3 Internet Mythology and the Retroactive Citation Problem

Historian Mike Dash, who has investigated the origins of the cat-plague story specifically, concludes that it is "almost certainly a modern internet-based fabrication" dating to the late 1990s. The earliest book references appear in Donald Engels' *Classical Cats: The Rise and Fall of the Sacred Cat* (1999) and Malcolm Lambert's *The Cathars* (1998)—both published at the dawn of the internet age and neither providing primary source citation for the specific claim of mass papal-ordered cat killings.

This chronology is revealing. The 1990s internet was the first environment in which historical claims could circulate widely without passing through the editorial and peer-review filters that had previously mediated the relationship between professional historians and public audiences. A plausible-sounding claim could propagate from a single source—a Usenet post, an early website, a forwarded email—to thousands of readers in days, acquiring the patina of established historical fact through sheer repetition.

What happened next is a phenomenon that media scholars call *citogenesis*—the process by which a claim circulates online, gets picked up by a published source (a book, a magazine article, a documentary), and then gets cited *back* as though the published source constituted independent verification. The published source, however, merely recorded what was already circulating online. The chain of citation is circular: the internet cites the book, the book reflects the internet, and the claim appears to be "well-established" without ever having been established at all.

The cat-plague story appears to have followed exactly this trajectory. Claims that appeared on early internet sites were picked up by popular history authors, who presented them without primary source references. These books were then cited by subsequent authors, by Wikipedia editors, by documentary filmmakers, and by social media accounts. Each citation made the claim look more authoritative. Each repetition made it feel more familiar. The result is a historically baseless claim that now appears in published books, in television documentaries, and across millions of social media posts, defended by people who genuinely believe it is "well-documented history."

### 3.4 The Cat as Modern Totem

There is an additional, often overlooked factor in the myth's persistence: the cultural status of cats in contemporary Western society. Cats are among the most popular companion animals in the developed world. Internet culture has elevated cats to a position of near-sacred importance—they are the subjects of viral videos, the stars of memes, and the beneficiaries of an enormous global industry of pet products and veterinary care.

The cat-plague myth aligns perfectly with contemporary cat veneration. It presents cats as the heroes of a story in which their removal causes catastrophe—a narrative that confirms the modern intuition that cats are essential, valuable, and unjustly persecuted. The emotional charge of the myth is not really about medieval history; it is about modern people's relationship with their pets, projected backward seven centuries.

## 4. Verdict

The claim that Pope Gregory IX's "Vox in Rama" caused mass cat killings that worsened the Black Death is **false** across every dimension that matters:

- **The document dimension:** "Vox in Rama" is real but does not order cat killings. It describes alleged heretical rituals involving a black cat, among many other elements, in a letter addressed to three German clerics.
- **The distribution dimension:** The document was not widely circulated. It was a narrowly addressed decretal, not a universally promulgated decree.
- **The behavioral dimension:** There is no documentary or archaeological evidence of mass, systematic cat killings in medieval Europe. Medieval records show cats valued for rodent control and integrated into household economies.
- **The timeline dimension:** "Vox in Rama" was issued in 1233. The Black Death reached Europe in 1347. The 114-year gap between the alleged cause and the alleged effect is, by itself, devastating to the claim. No population of cats would require 114 years to recover from even a genuine mass killing; cats breed rapidly and would have repopulated within a generation—a decade or two at most.
- **The epidemiological dimension:** As we will see in the next section, the entire model of plague transmission that the myth depends on—rats carrying fleas that bite humans—is itself under significant scientific revision.
- **The provenance dimension:** The claim appears to be a modern internet fabrication from the late 1990s, subsequently laundered through popular publications without primary source verification.

Any one of these failures would be sufficient to reject the claim. Together, they constitute an overwhelming case.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### 5.1 The Epidemiology the Myth Gets Wrong

The cat-plague story depends on a simple ecological model: cats kill rats, rats carry plague fleas, therefore fewer cats means more rats means more plague. This model, which might be called the "Rattus rattus orthodoxy," has dominated popular understanding of the Black Death for over a century. It is also, according to an increasingly robust body of recent research, probably wrong—or at best radically incomplete.

The traditional model holds that the bubonic plague was transmitted primarily by *Xenopsylla cheopis*, the Oriental rat flea, which rode on *Rattus rattus*, the black rat. When rat populations died of plague, the fleas jumped to human hosts, transmitting *Yersinia pestis* through their bites. This model was established in the early twentieth century based on studies of modern plague outbreaks in India and has been the textbook explanation ever since.

However, research from the 2010s and 2020s has increasingly questioned whether this model adequately explains the epidemiology of the medieval Black Death. Several lines of evidence are in tension with the rat-flea orthodoxy:

**Transmission speed:** Mathematical modeling of the Black Death's spread across Europe shows rates of transmission that are difficult to reconcile with a rat-flea-human chain. Rats are relatively sedentary animals with limited ranges. The speed at which the Black Death moved—crossing entire regions in weeks—suggests transmission mechanisms that do not require rats as intermediaries.

**Human ectoparasite models:** Research published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* has demonstrated that models incorporating human fleas (*Pulex irritans*) and body lice (*Pediculus humanus corporis*) as primary vectors can reproduce the observed mortality curves of the Black Death more accurately than rat-flea models. In these models, the plague spreads directly from human to human via human parasites, without requiring rats at all.

**Archaeological and genetic evidence:** Analysis of *Yersinia pestis* DNA recovered from medieval plague burials has confirmed the identity of the pathogen but has not confirmed the rat-flea transmission model specifically. The genetic evidence tells us what killed people; it does not tell us how the pathogen reached them.

**The rat problem:** For the traditional model to work, there should be evidence of massive rat die-offs preceding or accompanying human plague outbreaks. Medieval chroniclers, who recorded plague symptoms in graphic detail, rarely mention mass rat deaths. Modern observers in plague-endemic regions, by contrast, consistently note that rat die-offs are conspicuous events that precede human outbreaks by days or weeks. The absence of such observations from medieval sources is puzzling if the rat-flea model is correct.

**Pneumonic plague transmission:** A significant proportion of plague cases may have been pneumonic rather than bubonic—transmitted by respiratory droplets from person to person, with no animal vector required. The relative proportions of bubonic and pneumonic plague in the medieval pandemic remain debated, but if pneumonic transmission was significant, the entire ecological model involving rats, fleas, and cats becomes less relevant.

The implications for the cat-plague myth are stark. Even if medieval Europeans *had* exterminated their cats (which they didn't), and even if this had caused rat populations to increase (which is undemonstrated), the effect on plague transmission might have been negligible—because the plague may not have depended primarily on the rat-flea pathway in the first place. The myth gets the history wrong *and* the science wrong.

### 5.2 A Fact the Myth Ironically Inverts: Cats and Plague

There is a final empirical irony that the cat-plague myth is completely unaware of. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), domestic cats are highly susceptible to *Yersinia pestis* infection. Cats can contract plague from rodent prey or flea bites, develop clinical plague—including pneumonic plague—and transmit the disease directly to humans through respiratory droplets, bites, or scratches.

In the modern United States, where plague still occurs at low levels in the Southwest, domestic cats are one of the most important sources of human plague exposure. Veterinary literature documents numerous cases of cat-to-human plague transmission it. Between 1977 and 1998, the CDC recorded multiple incidents in which humans contracted pneumonic plague directly from infected cats.

The myth posits cats as humanity's defenders against plague. The epidemiological reality is that cats can be plague *vectors*—carriers that bring the disease into close contact with human populations. A medieval Europe with more cats might have experienced *more* human plague exposure, not less, depending on the ecological dynamics in play. The myth's central premise is not just unsupported; it may be precisely inverted.

This is not to say that cats caused the Black Death—that would be as monocausal and simplistic as the myth itself. It is to say that the ecological relationships between cats, rats, fleas, and plague bacteria are vastly more complex than the neat food-chain model the myth assumes. Cats eat rats, yes. But cats also carry fleas, contract plague, develop pneumonic forms of the disease, and can transmit it to humans directly. Inserting cats into the plague ecology as simple "good guys" reflects a cartoon understanding of epidemiology.

### 5.3 The 114-Year Gap as Epistemological Red Flag

The timeline problem deserves emphasis because it illustrates a broader principle about how historical claims should be evaluated. "Vox in Rama" was issued in 1233. The Black Death arrived in Europe in 1347. The gap is 114 years.

In that 114-year interval, Europe experienced the reign of Frederick II, the fall of the Latin Empire of Constantinople, the Seventh and Eighth Crusades, the Mongol invasions of Poland and Hungary, the establishment of the Inquisition as a permanent institution, the pontificates of seventeen popes after Gregory IX, the rise of the Franciscan and Dominican orders as major institutional forces, the development of the Scholastic philosophical tradition, and numerous other transformative events.

The idea that a single papal letter from 1233 maintained sufficient cultural force to still be shaping rodent ecology in 1347 is not a historical argument. It is a magical thinking that attributes to documents a kind of immortal causal agency they cannot possess. Even the most consequential papal decrees—genuine bulls with universal force, backed by institutional enforcement—did not maintain unbroken causal chains over 114 years. The suggestion that a minor decretal addressed to three men (one of whom was murdered the same year) did so is extraordinary to the point of absurdity.

The 114-year gap should function as an immediate epistemological red flag. When a causal claim spans more than a century, the burden of proof is enormous. The claimant must demonstrate not just that event A occurred and event B occurred, but that A's effects persisted continuously and specifically enough to be a proximate cause of B. In this case, no one has even attempted to meet that burden, because no one who has seriously investigated the claim has found any evidence to work with.

### 5.4 How Internet Myths Acquire the Appearance of History

The cat-plague story is a near-perfect specimen of a broader phenomenon: the manufacture of historical "common knowledge" through digital circulation. It is worth understanding the mechanism in detail because it applies to dozens of other widely believed historical claims.

**Stage 1: Plausible fabrication.** Someone—the original author is unknown—constructs a causal narrative that combines a real but obscure historical document ("Vox in Rama") with a real but distant historical event (the Black Death) and fills the gap with an invented mechanism (mass cat killings). The narrative is plausible in the sense that each individual element has some basis in reality; the fabrication lies in the connections between them.

**Stage 2: Digital propagation.** The story spreads through forums, blogs, social media, and content mills. Each retelling adds small embellishments. The cat grows from one element in "Vox in Rama" to the central subject of the document. The decretal becomes a "papal bull." The three German recipients become "all of Christendom." The alleged cat killings become "millions" or "genocidal." These embellishments are individually small but cumulatively transformative.

**Stage 3: Authority laundering.** The story appears in a published book or a television documentary. The author or producer encountered it online, found it compelling, and included it—possibly with a citation to another secondary source that also encountered it online. The published version now serves as an "authoritative source" that subsequent writers can cite. The story has moved from internet rumor to "published history" without ever passing through primary source verification.

**Stage 4: Defensive entrenchment.** When the story is challenged, its defenders can point to published books and documentaries. "It's in *this* book!" becomes an argument, even though the book's source was the same internet ecosystem that produced the challenge. The circular nature of the citation chain is invisible to most participants. The story now feels too well-established to question.

**Stage 5: Identity integration.** The story becomes integrated into the identity narratives of groups that find it useful—cat lovers who want historical vindication, anti-clerical commentators who want evidence of religious harm, environmental activists who want parables about ecosystem disruption. At this stage, challenging the story feels like a personal attack on the people who have adopted it. Evidence becomes secondary to identity, and correction becomes socially costly.

This five-stage process is not unique to the cat-plague myth. It describes the lifecycle of numerous historical myths that circulate online, from the claim that medieval people believed the Earth was flat (they didn't), to the claim that Napoleon was unusually short (he was average height for his time), to the claim that Vikings wore horned helmets (they didn't). The cat-plague story is merely one of the most elaborate and epistemologically instructive examples.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

### 6.1 The Snopes Assessment

Snopes has investigated this claim directly, tracing the documentary evidence for "Vox in Rama," examining the archaeological record, consulting veterinary and epidemiological sources on cats and plague, and reviewing the historiographic literature on the claim's origins. Their assessment aligns with the analysis presented here: the claim is false across multiple independent dimensions.

What Snopes demonstrates particularly well is the *layered* nature of the debunking. This is not a simple case where one fact is wrong. The claim fails at the level of what the document says, at the level of how widely it was distributed, at the level of whether mass cat killings occurred, at the level of whether such killings would have affected plague dynamics, and at the level of where the claim itself originated. Each layer of failure is independent; the claim does not become true if you grant any one of the contested premises.

### 6.2 The Methodological Challenge of "Absence of Evidence"

One of the most challenging aspects of debunking the cat-plague myth is that it requires asserting negatives. There is *no* evidence of mass cat killings. There is *no* evidence that "Vox in Rama" was widely distributed. There is *no* documentary record of organized feline extermination. Critics of the debunking can always respond: "Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence."

This response, while containing a kernel of epistemological truth, is massively overdeployed in defense of baseless claims. Absence of evidence *is* evidence of absence when:

1. The alleged event would have been conspicuous enough to generate records.
2. The period in question produced abundant records of comparable events.
3. No one has found evidence despite looking for it.

All three conditions are met here. Mass animal killings across a continent would have been recorded by the same chroniclers who recorded mass human killings, mass animal plagues, unusual weather events, and the price of commodities. The thirteenth and fourteenth centuries produced enormous volumes of surviving documentation—monastic chronicles, municipal records, court rolls, ecclesiastical correspondence, and private letters. Historians who have specifically looked for evidence of papal-ordered cat killings have found none. At some point, repeated failure to find evidence for a claim that should have left evidence, if true, *is* evidence that the claim is false.

The epistemological principle at work is what philosophers call *expected evidence*: if a proposition is true, we can predict what evidence we would expect to find. If we look for that evidence systematically and don't find it, our confidence in the proposition should decrease. The cat-plague myth predicts that we should find papal directives ordering cat killings (we don't), secular records of cat extermination campaigns (we don't), archaeological evidence of mass cat destruction distinct from fur-trade butchering (we don't), and a historiographic tradition discussing these events that predates the internet (we don't). At every point where the myth makes a prediction, the prediction fails.

### 6.3 The Standard for Historical Claims

The cat-plague story provides a useful case study for establishing a minimum standard that historical claims should meet before they are accepted as factual. This standard might include:

**Primary source citation.** A historical claim should be traceable to a primary source—a document, artifact, or archaeological finding from the period in question. The cat-plague myth fails this test entirely; no primary source documents mass cat killings.

**Mechanism plausibility.** The proposed causal mechanism should be consistent with what we know about the relevant processes. The cat-plague myth fails because its ecological model (cats suppress rats, rats carry plague fleas) is both oversimplified and increasingly inconsistent with epidemiological research.

**Timeline coherence.** The proposed cause should be proximate enough to the proposed effect to constitute a plausible mechanism. A 114-year gap with no demonstrated continuity of effect fails this test.

**Historiographic provenance.** A well-established historical fact should have a traceable historiographic tradition—a chain of scholarly discussion extending back through the period when the evidence was first examined. Claims that appear suddenly in popular sources without prior scholarly discussion should be treated with suspicion. The cat-plague myth appears in the late 1990s with no prior historiographic tradition.

**Resistance to alternative explanations.** A good historical claim should not be easily explained away by simpler alternatives. The cat-plague myth's evidence base—skinned cat bones in Cambridge, a papal letter mentioning a cat—is more economically explained by the fur trade and standard anti-heretical rhetoric than by a secret history of continental feline genocide.

### 6.4 What This Case Teaches About Information Hygiene

For the general reader, the cat-plague myth offers several practical lessons in information hygiene:

**Beware the elegant explanation.** Monocausal narratives that explain complex events through a single dramatic cause are almost always wrong. Real historical causation is messy, multicausal, and resistant to narrative simplification. If an explanation feels like a satisfying story—complete with villain, victim, and twist ending—it is probably functioning as entertainment rather than history.

**Check the primary sources.** When a historical claim is presented, ask: what is the primary source? Not "what book mentions this?" or "what website says this?" but "what document from the period in question supports this claim?" If no one can produce a primary source, the claim is unverified regardless of how many secondary sources repeat it.

**Respect the timeline.** Causal claims require temporal proximity or demonstrated continuous mechanisms. A cause separated from its alleged effect by more than a century requires extraordinary evidence of continuous causal linkage. Demand that evidence before accepting the claim.

**Notice the emotional payload.** Claims that generate strong emotional responses—anger at the Church, sympathy for cats, satisfaction at the ironic twist—are claims where your critical faculties are most likely to be compromised. The stronger the emotional resonance, the more carefully you should examine the evidence.

**Follow the citation chain to its origin.** When multiple sources cite each other, trace the citations backward to the original source. If the chain is circular—if the oldest source is from 1998 and cites no primary evidence—then the appearance of scholarly support is an illusion. The number of sources repeating a claim is not evidence for the claim if all the sources ultimately derive from the same unsupported assertion.

## Conclusion: The Myth as Mirror

The cat-plague story tells us almost nothing about the thirteenth century, but it tells us a great deal about the twenty-first. It reveals our appetite for neat causal narratives, our willingness to project modern concerns onto the past, our vulnerability to circular citation chains, and our tendency to mistake repetition for verification. It shows how the internet can manufacture historical "common knowledge" in less than a generation—creating claims that feel ancient and well-established when they are, in fact, younger than the web browsers through which they spread.

The real medieval world—the world of "Vox in Rama" and the Black Death—was more complex, more interesting, and more alien than the myth allows. It was a world where cats were skinned for fur and kept for pest control simultaneously, where papal letters could describe demonic black cats the size of dogs without anyone concluding that all cats should be killed, where plague traveled by mechanisms that epidemiologists are still working to understand, and where the causes of catastrophe were distributed across ecological, climatic, biological, and social systems in ways that defy reduction to a single story.

The myth replaces all of this complexity with a fable. The fable is satisfying. The history is better.
