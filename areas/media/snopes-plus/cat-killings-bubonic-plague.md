# Did Medieval Cat Killings Make The Bubonic Plague Worse?

## 1. The Claim

The story moves through the internet with the comfortable authority of settled history. In 1233, Pope Gregory IX issued a decree called *Vox in Rama* that branded black cats as agents of Satan. This papal bull, the story goes, triggered continent-wide persecutions of cats across medieval Europe, reducing cat populations so severely that rats flourished unchecked. A century later — around 1347 — the Black Death arrived from Central Asia, and the decimated cat population meant there was nothing to stop the plague-carrying rats from spreading the *Yersinia pestis* bacterium across the continent. Fifty million people died. The church's cat-phobia killed half of Europe.

It is a morally satisfying fable: religious superstition caused a practical catastrophe on a continental scale, and the cats — those long-persecuted creatures of folk evil — have been the victims of institutional malice all along. It circulates on Halloween, in folklore threads, in pop-history YouTube videos, and in countless "dark history" social media slides.

It is also almost certainly a fabrication that dates to the late 1990s.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

### *Vox in Rama* Is Real, But Narrowly Targeted

*Vox in Rama* is a genuine document — a 1233 papal decretal (technically a letter with administrative and juridical authority) written by Pope Gregory IX. It was sent to Konrad of Marburg, a German priest who was conducting inquisitorial activities in the Rhineland, along with the Archbishop of Mainz and the Bishop of Hildesheim.

The letter describes in vivid and disturbing detail the rituals allegedly practiced by heretical cults in that region of Germany. Among the ritual objects mentioned: a black cat, which initiates kiss "on its hindquarters" as part of a ceremony of demonic allegiance. The Latin original describes this as "a black cat about the size of an average dog, descends backwards, with its tail erect."

This passage is the entire basis for the papal-anti-cat-decree claim. But notice what it doesn't say: it does not declare cats evil, it does not instruct Christians to kill cats, and it is not addressed to the Church universal. It describes what heretics allegedly did with a cat as part of their reported rituals — the cat was victim of the ritual, not the author of it. The letter is an inquisitorial document targeting specific German heretics, and even its authenticity as a reliable account of actual practices is questionable: there is no corroborating evidence that the rituals it describes actually took place.

Critically, the document was sent to three recipients in a specific German diocese. It was not a papal bull addressed to Christendom. Whether it even reached the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II is uncertain — there is no evidence of that transmission in the historical record. The idea that this document sparked a Europe-wide anti-cat crusade is not supported by any primary-source evidence.

### Evidence For Medieval Cat Killings Is Thin and Localised

There is archaeological evidence that medieval Europeans killed cats — but it is modest, localised, and explicable without reference to supernatural persecution.

A 1995 study by Luff and Moreno-García, "Killing Cats in the Medieval Period: An Unusual Episode in the History of Cambridge, England," examined the remains of 79 medieval cats and found evidence that they had been skinned — for fur, not for religious reasons. The paper's authors chose to describe the episode as "unusual," precisely because it was not typical of broader medieval practice. That qualifier is significant: the most frequently cited archaeological evidence for medieval cat-killing is explicitly described by the archaeologists who found it as an anomaly.

Robert Darnton's *The Great Cat Massacre and Other Episodes in French Cultural History* (1984) does document a famous mass cat killing — but it occurred in Paris in the 1730s, as a labour protest by printing apprentices against their master and his wife. It has nothing to do with the Bubonic Plague, the Middle Ages, or papal authority over cats.

### Cats Are Highly Susceptible to Plague Too

Even if Europe had been systematically depopulating its cats in the 13th and 14th centuries — which the evidence does not support — the causal chain from "fewer cats" to "worse plague" breaks down on biological grounds.

Domestic cats are not plague-resistant. *Yersinia pestis*, the bacterium responsible for bubonic plague, infects rodent-borne fleas, which then jump to other hosts. The CDC classifies cats as highly susceptible to plague — more so than many other mammals. Cats that hunt and kill plague-carrying rodents can and do contract plague themselves. A highly efficient mousers in a plague-infested environment is not a plague-prevention asset; it is a plague victim.

More fundamentally, the argument assumes a continuous, functional predator-prey relationship in which cats kept medieval European rat populations in check. The epidemiology of the Black Death does not work this cleanly. *Yersinia pestis* spreads via fleas, and the 14th-century pandemic is now believed by many historians and epidemiologists to have been driven by human ectoparasite transmission (fleas on humans, not just rats), prolonged pneumonic plague spread through droplet transmission, and possibly other routes. The role of rats — and by extension, the cats that might have preyed on them — is more contested in plague historiography than the popular account suggests.

### The Actual Origin of the Story

According to historian Mike Dash, an expert on historical misinformation who has researched the claim directly, the cat-killing-plague story is "almost certainly a modern internet-based fabrication" originating in the late 1990s or early 2000s.

Snopes researchers traced the myth to at least two late-1990s books: Donald Engels' *Classical Cats: The Rise and Fall of the Sacred Cat* (1999), and Malcolm Lambert's *The Cathars* (1998), according to Dash's research on the AskHistorians subreddit. From those books, the claim appears to have migrated onto early internet forums and blogs, and from there to achieve the kind of memetic saturation it now enjoys.

This is a well-documented pathway for historical pseudo-facts. A speculative claim appears in a published book with some scholarly apparatus; it circulates on blogs and early internet platforms before fact-checking infrastructure exists to challenge it; it achieves enough repetition to acquire the patina of common knowledge; and it becomes difficult to dislodge because every new person who encounters it also encounters the confident tone in which it is delivered.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### The Shape of a Perfect Irony

The story has the formal properties of a great historical irony: hubris punished. The institution that declared war on cats got a plague as a result. Cause and effect are separated by enough time to seem cosmically just rather than nakedly retributive. The victims — cats — are sympathetic in the contemporary cultural economy, where cat fondness is practically a personality type. The perpetrators — medieval church authorities — carry all the weight of popular suspicion about medieval religion's irrationality.

This narrative shape is extraordinarily sticky. It tells a story we want to believe: that stupidity has consequences, that cruelty recoils on the cruel, that there is a kind of historical karma operating. When a story does this much emotional and ideological work, it is very difficult to dislodge with mere evidence.

### The Miseries of Medieval Historiography

Medieval European history is poorly understood in popular culture and highly susceptible to anachronistic projection. The popular imagination of the medieval period is a collage of superstition, cruelty, Church tyranny, and scientific ignorance — a caricature that serves contemporary purposes, allowing the present to define itself against a dark and primitive past.

Actual medieval history is far more complicated: an era of tremendous intellectual activity (the founding of universities, the recovery and transformation of classical philosophy), contested religious authority (not the monolithic Church of popular imagination but a fractious, multi-polar institution), and significant variation across time, region, and social position.

The cat-plague story fits the caricature perfectly. It requires a medieval Church that has the administrative power to mandate continent-wide cat persecution and the theological motivation to do so — a version of the medieval Church that historians would largely not recognise. That the document cited (*Vox in Rama*) was addressed to three people in one German diocese, and does not even tell Christians to kill cats, is a detail that the popular story cannot accommodate.

### Pre-Existing Symbolic Valence

Cats *do* have complicated symbolic histories in medieval Europe. Black cats and witchcraft connections are real folk traditions, well-documented in later periods particularly in connection with witch trial records. The cat as familiar spirit, the cat as devil's associate — these motifs circulate in medieval and early modern literature and religious polemic. They are not invented by the viral claim; they are real cultural material.

This provides the claim with a ready-made symbolic environment that makes it plausible. Of course the medieval Church persecuted cats — that fits what we know about the cat-as-evil-omen tradition. What we do not know (and what the claim adds without evidence) is that this produced systematic mass killing on a scale sufficient to affect the European rat population before the Black Death.

### Causality Is Satisfying, Complexity Is Not

The story requires a simple causal chain: decree → cat killings → rat population boom → plague worsened. This chain has the appealing tidiness of mechanism — it is not merely a cultural claim but a claim about how one thing caused another in a way we can understand. The biological complexity of actual plague transmission — the contested role of rats vs. human fleas, pneumonic spread, climate factors, trade network dynamics — does not make for a compelling social media post.

Simple causal mechanisms, especially those involving a morally culpable actor (the Church), spread because they satisfy the human appetite for explainable suffering.

## 4. Verdict

**The claim is false** — both in its historical assertions and in its causal reasoning.

*Vox in Rama* did not decree the persecution of cats across Europe. It was a narrowly targeted inquisitorial document sent to three German ecclesiastics, describing heretical rituals that may never have actually taken place. There is no historical evidence of continent-wide medieval cat killings in the century before the Black Death. The biological argument is also flawed: cats are themselves susceptible to plague, and the epidemiology of the Black Death involves more complex transmission routes than a simple rat/cat/flea model can capture.

The story is, as historian Mike Dash concluded, almost certainly a modern fabrication that appeared in print in the late 1990s and subsequently achieved internet immortality. It is a morally satisfying fable about the consequences of religious irrationality — and it is not true.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### The Internet Myth Genesis Cycle

The cat-plague story is instructive as a case study in how modern historical myths originate. Unlike ancient folk myths that accumulate over generations, this story has a traceable birth in the late 20th century publishing cycle and early internet culture.

The pathway: speculative academic claim (published in a book with scholarly apparatus but without rigorous sourcing) → absorption into internet folklore during a period when fact-checking was rudimentary → cross-pollination between early blogs and history forums → critical mass of repetition that confers apparent credibility → resistance to correction because the claim is now "everyone knows."

This cycle has produced dozens of now-widespread historical pseudo-facts: that Napoleon was short (he was average height by modern standards, and above average for his era: the rumour derived partly from British propaganda); that the Great Wall of China is visible from space (it is not — its width is far too narrow); that ancient Romans had vomitoria for emptying their stomachs after feasts (vomitoria were exits in amphitheatres). These errors share the cat-plague story's formal properties: they are just plausible enough to survive casual scrutiny, they tend to confirm existing stereotypes, and they are more interesting than the true story.

### What Actually Stopped — and Didn't Stop — The Black Death

The Black Death (1347–1351) killed between 30 and 60 percent of Europe's population in some of the worst-affected regions. Its spread and its eventual slowing are subjects of genuine historical and epidemiological debate.

Contemporary understanding attributes the primary transmission route to the bite of infected fleas, most commonly carried by the black rat (*Rattus rattus*), which had expanded its range across Eurasia via trade networks. Secondary pneumonic plague transmission, in which infected individuals spread the disease through respiratory droplets, likely accelerated urban mortality. Some researchers have proposed that human body lice played a larger role than previously assumed.

What eventually suppressed the pandemic was not ecological, but epidemiological: the death of enough susceptible hosts, combined with geographic dispersal of the pathogen and possibly some degree of acquired immunity in survivors' descendants. Urban sanitation improvements in subsequent centuries played a role in reducing subsequent outbreaks, but these came much later.

None of this involves cats in any meaningful way.

### Real Medieval Cat Persecution

This does not mean cats had an easy time in medieval Europe. There are genuine documented cases of cat abuse in early modern European folk tradition, usually ritual and seasonal rather than systematic. In Belgian folklore, cats were thrown from towers as part of Easter festivals — a tradition that continued into the early 20th century and is now commemorated in the Kattenstoet (Cat Parade) of Ypres. In 17th-century France, burning bags of cats at midsummer bonfires was, in limited areas, a local custom.

These practices were localised, occasional, and not organised by papal decree. They emerged from folk superstition rather than official church policy. The distance between these scattered local customs and a continent-wide systematic persecution capable of altering rat population dynamics is vast — and the cat-plague story collapses that distance entirely.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes' treatment of this claim is effective at identifying the key historical problems but stops somewhat short of the full biological analysis. The core finding — that *Vox in Rama* was narrowly targeted, that mass cat killings are not documented, that the story appears to originate in late 1990s publications — is solid and appropriately sourced.

The most valuable contribution to the historical record comes from historian Mike Dash's email correspondence with Snopes and his Reddit posts on AskHistorians, which identify the probable origin points in Lambert (1998) and Engels (1999). This source-tracing is exactly what rigorous fact-checking requires: not just identifying that a claim is false, but establishing how it came to exist.

What the typical fact-check treatment underemphasises is the biological dimension — the fact that cats are themselves plague-susceptible and could not have functioned as the disease-prevention mechanism the story assumes. This closes the causal argument more completely than the historical critique alone.

Spencer McDaniel's essay "Were Cats Really Killed En Masse During the Middle Ages?" (Tales of Times Forgotten, 2019) provides a careful survey of the primary and secondary evidence that complements the Snopes fact-check well.

The persistence of this myth despite multiple clear debunkings is a testament to the difficulty of correcting claims that operate primarily through narrative satisfaction rather than factual assertion. The cat-plague story is not believed because people have examined the evidence; it is believed because it feels true in the way that just-so stories feel true. Fact-checking can correct the record, but cannot easily replace the emotional satisfaction the story provides.
