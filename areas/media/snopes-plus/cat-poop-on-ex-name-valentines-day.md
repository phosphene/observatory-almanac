## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In early February 2021, social media posts circulated claiming that the Lexington Humane Society in Lexington, Kentucky, was running a Valentine's Day fundraiser in which donors could pay $10 to have an ex-partner's name written in a cat litter box, whereupon a shelter cat would defecate on it. The promotional language was deliberately provocative and humorous: "Cats can be spiteful creatures, and trust us, they are more than happy to take a #2 on your former #1," the organization stated in its campaign materials. Donors could participate by commenting on the Lexington Humane Society's Facebook post or sending the organization a direct message to designate the name to be written.

Snopes, in an investigation authored by Madison Dapcevich and published on February 11, 2021, rated this claim **True**. The fundraiser was real, officially organized by the Lexington Humane Society, and was announced on February 3, 2021 under the tagline of a "smear campaign." The $10 donations were designated to support the shelter's spay/neuter program, medical fund, or Project Purrfect, a feline education and rehoming initiative. The fundraiser was scheduled to end on February 12 at 1:00 PM EST, though the litter boxes with names written in them were to remain on display through February 15. By the time of Dapcevich's reporting, the campaign had raised over $1,600 (Dapcevich, 2021, *Snopes*).

This is a straightforward factual confirmation: the claim accurately described a real event organized by a legitimate nonprofit animal welfare organization. There is no ambiguity in the verdict, no contested evidence, and no alternative interpretation. The fundraiser happened exactly as described. The epistemic interest of this case lies not in the verification itself — which was simple — but in what the fundraiser reveals about the evolving landscape of nonprofit fundraising strategy, the role of humor and social media virality in charitable giving, and the broader cultural phenomenon of Valentine's Day counter-programming as a marketing and engagement tool.

The Lexington Humane Society's campaign was not isolated. It emerged alongside similar creative fundraising efforts by other organizations during the same Valentine's season: Hotels.com offered a virtual "dumpster reservation" for exes, and the San Antonio Zoo ran a promotion allowing donors to name a cockroach or rodent after an ex, which would then be fed to a larger animal at the facility. These parallel campaigns suggest a broader trend in nonprofit and corporate marketing that warrants examination beyond the simple factual question of whether any individual campaign was real.

## §2 The Evidence Base

The evidentiary basis for this claim is robust, well-documented, and drawn primarily from the Lexington Humane Society's own public communications.

**Primary Source: Lexington Humane Society Announcement.** On February 3, 2021, the Lexington Humane Society posted on its official Facebook page announcing the Valentine's Day "smear campaign" fundraiser. The post described the mechanics clearly: for a $10 donation, supporters could have an ex-partner's name written in a cat litter box at the shelter. The post invited participants to comment on the announcement or send a direct message to designate the name. The organization specified that funds would be directed to its spay/neuter program, its medical fund, or Project Purrfect, its feline education and rehoming initiative. The post's language was intentionally humorous, featuring the line about cats being "spiteful creatures" willing to "take a #2 on your former #1" (Lexington Humane Society, 2021, *Facebook*).

**Fundraising Results.** By the time of Dapcevich's Snopes article on February 11, 2021, the campaign had raised more than $1,600. This figure represents a minimum — the fundraiser was scheduled to continue through February 12 at 1:00 PM EST, meaning additional donations likely came in after the Snopes piece was published. While $1,600 may appear modest in absolute terms, it represents meaningful revenue for a local humane society, particularly given the minimal overhead costs of the campaign: the infrastructure consisted of litter boxes, markers, and cats that the shelter already housed. The cost per dollar raised was almost certainly negligible, making the campaign highly efficient from a development perspective (Dapcevich, 2021, *Snopes*).

**Timeline and Logistics.** The campaign was announced February 3, with donations accepted through February 12 at 1:00 PM EST. The litter boxes with names were to remain in place through February 15, encompassing both Valentine's Day itself and the day after. This timeline was structured to maximize the Valentine's season engagement window while providing a clear end date for operational purposes.

**Parallel Campaigns.** Dapcevich's investigation contextualized the Lexington Humane Society's fundraiser within a broader wave of ex-themed Valentine's promotions. Hotels.com offered a virtual "dumpster reservation" service where users could symbolically deposit their ex-partner's name. The San Antonio Zoo ran its recurring Valentine's promotion allowing donors to name a cockroach or rodent after an ex, which would then be fed to a larger animal at the zoo. These parallel campaigns, documented in Dapcevich's reporting, establish the Lexington event as part of a recognizable marketing genre rather than an isolated novelty (Dapcevich, 2021, *Snopes*).

**Verification Method.** The Snopes verification process for this claim was straightforward: the claim originated from the organization's own public social media accounts, the fundraiser was publicly accessible, and the organization made no effort to conceal or restrict participation. This is the simplest category of fact-check — verifying that a publicly announced event by an identifiable organization actually occurred — and represents the highest-confidence end of the fact-checking spectrum. There was no reliance on anonymous sources, no contested documentary evidence, and no factual claims requiring independent scientific analysis.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty

While the factual verification of this claim is trivially simple, the case presents genuine epistemic novelty in several domains that extend well beyond the binary question of truth or falsehood.

**The Gamification of Charitable Giving.** The Lexington Humane Society's fundraiser represents an increasingly common strategy in nonprofit fundraising: the transformation of donations from abstract financial transactions into participatory, emotionally engaging experiences. Traditional fundraising research has extensively documented the role of donor psychology in charitable giving. Bekkers and Wiepking (2011, *Nonprofit and Voluntary Sector Quarterly*) identified eight mechanisms driving philanthropic behavior, including awareness of need, solicitation, costs and benefits, altruism, reputation, psychological benefits, values, and efficacy. The litter box campaign leveraged several of these simultaneously: the psychological benefit of cathartic humor (writing an ex's name and having it symbolically degraded), the social reputation signal of public participation on Facebook, and the awareness of specific organizational needs (spay/neuter, medical care, rehoming). What is epistemically novel is the degree to which the campaign centered the donor's emotional experience rather than the beneficiaries' need — a shift that fundraising scholars have noted as characteristic of the social media era (Saxton and Wang, 2014, *New Media & Society*).

**Humor as an Epistemic Lubricant in Nonprofit Communication.** The campaign's success depended entirely on humor — specifically, the culturally shared understanding that Valentine's Day includes not only romantic celebration but also a counter-narrative of heartbreak, resentment, and therapeutic catharsis regarding former partners. This counter-narrative has deep roots in popular culture and has been commercially exploited at least since the emergence of "Anti-Valentine's Day" marketing in the early 2000s. The epistemic question is whether humor functions as a legitimate tool for public communication about animal welfare, or whether it risks trivializing the underlying needs. Research on humor in prosocial communication suggests it can increase message engagement and sharing behavior without necessarily diminishing the perceived seriousness of the underlying cause (McGraw and Warren, 2010, *Psychological Science*). The "benign violation" theory of humor proposed by McGraw and Warren suggests that the litter box campaign succeeded precisely because it violated expectations about nonprofit fundraising in a way that was perceived as harmless and playful rather than offensive. The campaign treats the indignity visited upon the ex's name as a benign violation — socially transgressive but ultimately victimless — while the donation itself channels real money to genuine animal welfare programs.

**The Virality-Verification Paradox.** This case illustrates a pattern that is becoming increasingly common in fact-checking: claims that go viral precisely because they sound too amusing, outrageous, or creative to be real, prompting fact-checking investigations that confirm the claims are true. The fact-check thus serves not its traditional function of debunking misinformation but rather a confirmatory function — reassuring an amused public that the entertaining story they encountered is accurate. This represents an evolving role for fact-checking organizations. Traditional fact-checking emerged as a response to political misinformation and rumor (Graves, 2016, *Deciding What's True: The Rise of Political Fact-Checking in American Journalism*), but an increasing proportion of fact-checks address claims that are unusual or entertaining rather than politically consequential. Whether this represents mission drift or legitimate public service is an open question in journalism studies. One can argue that confirming delightful truths builds public trust in fact-checking institutions, which strengthens their credibility when they deliver less welcome verdicts on more consequential claims.

**Transactional Symbolism and the Economics of Catharsis.** The $10 price point is itself epistemically interesting. It is low enough to be an impulse purchase, yet high enough to generate meaningful aggregate revenue from a viral campaign. Behavioral economics research on charitable giving has documented that suggested donation amounts significantly influence donor behavior (Desmet and Feinberg, 2003, *Journal of Economic Psychology*), and the $10 figure occupies a well-established psychological sweet spot for what might be termed "micro-philanthropy" — charitable acts small enough to feel effortless but concrete enough to feel meaningful. The campaign effectively monetized a specific negative emotion (resentment toward an ex-partner) and converted it into a positive outcome (animal welfare funding), a form of emotional arbitrage that is both psychologically sophisticated and ethically unproblematic.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Dapcevich's fact-check was appropriately scoped — the question was whether the fundraiser was real, and it was. However, from a broader analytical perspective, several dimensions of the story merit additional exploration that fell outside the investigation's purview.

**Total Revenue and Operational Impact.** The reported figure of "$1,600+" was captured before the fundraiser ended. No follow-up reporting established the final revenue total. More importantly, the investigation did not examine the campaign's secondary effects on organizational visibility: how many new followers, email subscribers, or general-purpose donations the viral attention generated for the Lexington Humane Society. In nonprofit communications, the value of a viral campaign often exceeds its direct revenue by an order of magnitude when secondary engagement effects are included. Research on viral charity campaigns — such as the 2014 ALS Ice Bucket Challenge, which raised over $115 million (ALS Association, 2014, *Annual Report*) — demonstrates that direct revenue from a specific campaign can be dramatically exceeded by the halo effect on overall organizational fundraising. Whether this occurred for the Lexington Humane Society following the litter box campaign is undocumented.

**Animal Welfare Implications of the Humor Frame.** The investigation did not explore potential concerns about the campaign from an animal welfare ethics perspective. While the physical welfare of the cats was not at issue — cats use litter boxes regardless of what is written in them — animal advocacy organizations have occasionally raised concerns about campaigns that instrumentalize animals for human emotional purposes. The framing of cats as "spiteful creatures" willing to participate in a symbolic revenge act anthropomorphizes feline behavior in a way that could be critiqued from an animal ethics standpoint, even if the practical impact on the animals was nil. The investigation accepted the humorous framing at face value without examining whether any animal welfare organizations had objections.

**The Replication Pattern.** The San Antonio Zoo's cockroach-naming promotion and Hotels.com's virtual dumpster reservation were noted but not analyzed as a pattern. These parallel campaigns suggest that ex-themed Valentine's Day promotions have become a recognized marketing genre, potentially with diminishing returns as the novelty fades. The investigation did not explore the lineage of such promotions — the San Antonio Zoo, for instance, has run its cockroach-naming campaign for multiple years — or assess whether the Lexington Humane Society's campaign was original or inspired by prior examples. Understanding the genealogy of viral nonprofit campaigns has practical value for other organizations considering similar strategies.

**Participant Privacy and Social Dynamics.** The mechanic of publicly commenting an ex's name on a Facebook post raises questions about privacy, consent, and social dynamics that went unexamined. Writing a former partner's name in a public forum, even in a humorous context, could be perceived as a form of mild public shaming. The investigation did not explore whether any named individuals objected to having their names used, or whether the Lexington Humane Society received any complaints. While the campaign was clearly intended as lighthearted, the ethics of publicly naming private individuals in association with feces — even symbolically — is not entirely uncomplicated.

## §5 Contextual Framing

The Lexington Humane Society's Valentine's Day litter box campaign exists at the intersection of several broader cultural, economic, and organizational trends.

**The Professionalization of Animal Shelter Fundraising.** American animal shelters have undergone a significant transformation in fundraising sophistication over the past two decades. What were once primarily donation-driven operations relying on direct mail appeals and adoption fees have evolved into organizations employing social media managers, professional development staff, and creative campaign strategies. The ASPCA's annual fundraising revenue exceeded $300 million by the late 2010s (ASPCA, 2019, *Annual Report*), driven in significant part by sophisticated multi-channel marketing. Local shelters like the Lexington Humane Society operate on vastly smaller budgets but increasingly adopt the creative communication strategies pioneered by national organizations. The litter box campaign exemplifies this professionalization: it was a low-cost, high-engagement social media campaign that generated both revenue and organizational visibility at minimal expense.

**Valentine's Day Counter-Programming as a Marketing Category.** The commercial exploitation of Valentine's Day-adjacent negative emotions — sadness, bitterness, and resentment associated with ended relationships — is a well-established marketing category. "Galentine's Day" celebrations (popularized by the television show *Parks and Recreation*), "Anti-Valentine's Day" parties and products, and breakup-themed promotions have all become routine features of the early February commercial landscape. The litter box fundraiser fits comfortably within this tradition, positioning the Lexington Humane Society as a participant in a broader cultural conversation rather than an organization asking for money in a vacuum. This contextual embeddedness likely contributed to the campaign's virality: it was immediately legible to audiences familiar with the genre of Valentine's counter-programming.

**Lexington, Kentucky, and Regional Philanthropy.** Lexington is a mid-sized city (population approximately 323,000 in the 2020 U.S. Census) with a strong tradition of animal culture, most notably as the heart of Kentucky's horse industry. The city's cultural identification with animals may have provided fertile ground for an animal-welfare fundraiser that might have landed differently in a community less attuned to human-animal relationships. The campaign's success raises questions about the importance of community context in nonprofit campaign design — an area that remains underexplored in fundraising research, which tends to focus on universal psychological mechanisms rather than locally specific cultural factors.

**The Role of Fact-Checking in Amplification.** The Snopes fact-check itself almost certainly contributed to the campaign's reach. By the time Dapcevich published her article on February 11, the fundraiser was in its final days. A Snopes "True" verdict on an amusing story functions as an endorsement of the story's shareability — readers who encounter a Snopes confirmation are more likely to share the original story, confident that it will not embarrass them by later being revealed as false. This creates a feedback loop in which fact-checking amplifies the very content it evaluates, a dynamic that has been noted but insufficiently studied in journalism research.

## §6 Verdict Calibration and Takeaways

The epistemic posture for this claim is straightforward: **confirmed true, with high confidence.** The Lexington Humane Society ran exactly the fundraiser described in the viral claims. Cats pooped on names written in litter boxes. Donors paid $10 per name. The money went to legitimate animal welfare programs. The campaign raised over $1,600 before the reporting window closed.

What makes this case worth preserving in a truth vault is not the verification — which was simple — but the surrounding epistemic landscape. The campaign illuminates the evolving relationship between humor, social media virality, and charitable giving. It demonstrates that fact-checking increasingly serves a confirmatory function for claims that are "too good to be true" but turn out to be entirely real. And it illustrates the growing sophistication of small nonprofit organizations in leveraging cultural moments for fundraising purposes.

For readers, the takeaway is twofold. First, creative nonprofit fundraisers that sound absurd are frequently real; skepticism about their existence should not default to disbelief but rather to straightforward verification via the organization's own public communications. Second, the $1,600-plus raised by writing names in litter boxes should be understood not as the total value of the campaign but as a floor estimate of its direct revenue, with the campaign's real value almost certainly measured in organizational visibility, new supporters, and public goodwill that extended well beyond the literal litter boxes.

Sometimes a cat poops on a name, and good things follow. The epistemic lesson is that truth is often stranger and more delightful than fiction, and that the impulse to fact-check can serve not only to debunk but to celebrate the genuinely creative corners of human social organization.
