# Is This a Real Cat Sanctuary on an Island in Hawaii?


## 1. The Claim

In May 2021, social media posts describing the Lanai Cat Sanctuary — a facility on the Hawaiian island of Lana'i said to house more than 600 cats — circulated widely, generating considerable attention and prompting questions about whether the facility was real. The accompanying characterizations emphasized the feel-good dimensions: a paradise for cats, a "purr-adise" on a tropical island, a shelter that had taken in hundreds of animals and given them a permanent, humane home. Photographs of cats lounging in what appeared to be a large, well-maintained outdoor enclosure on a warm, green island setting were shared across Twitter, Reddit, Facebook, and Instagram.

The skepticism implicit in the Snopes inquiry — "is this real?" — reflects a common dynamic in viral social media claims about unusual or whimsical institutions: visually appealing content about apparently implausible situations generates both shares and doubts. A sanctuary for more than 600 cats on a small Hawaiian island sounds, to the uninitiated, like something that might be embellished, fictional, or clickbait.

The sanctuary is, in fact, real. It has been operating since the early 2000s, has received extensive coverage from mainstream media including the Los Angeles Times, Huffington Post, and national television, and was well-documented before its social media virality in 2021. The Snopes verdict of "True" is correct, uncomplicated, and quickly established.

What the fact-check does not address — and what represents the genuinely contested terrain surrounding the Lanai Cat Sanctuary — is the ecological and conservational context in which the sanctuary was founded and continues to operate. The cats that live there are not primarily the product of casual pet abandonment; they were largely rescued from wildland areas where they posed a documented threat to endangered Hawaiian seabirds. The sanctuary's existence reflects real trade-offs in wildlife management between lethal and non-lethal approaches to managing feral and stray cats in areas with endangered native wildlife.


## 2. What's Actually True

The Lanai Cat Sanctuary is a fully operational nonprofit organization situated on the island of Lana'i, the smallest publicly accessible of the main Hawaiian Islands, with a population of approximately 3,000 residents. The sanctuary operates as a 501(c)(3) charitable organization governed by a board of directors. Its facility covers approximately 3.46 acres and is enclosed, providing a humane and permanent home for cats that have been rescued from situations where they posed threats to native wildlife or public health concerns.

The sanctuary's founding in the early 2000s was motivated primarily by the conflict between feral cats and endangered native Hawaiian birds, particularly the 'Ua'u (Hawaiian petrel, *Pterodroma sandwichensis*), a ground-nesting seabird listed as vulnerable under IUCN criteria and protected under the U.S. Endangered Species Act. Hawaiian petrels are nesting on the slopes of Lana'ihale, the central highlands of Lana'i, where feral cat predation has been documented as a significant threat to nesting success. The sanctuary captures cats from these sensitive wildlife areas and relocates them to the enclosed facility rather than employing lethal management.

According to the sanctuary's official website, more than 600 cats have been brought to the facility since 2015, with approximately 200 cats rescued in 2018 alone. The sanctuary is funded primarily by visitor donations — prior to the COVID-19 pandemic closure in March 2020, it welcomed tourists as a destination attraction, charging modest fees and operating a gift shop as revenue streams alongside voluntary donations. The pandemic closure reduced its operational capacity and funding base significantly, but the facility resumed accepting visitors in March 2021.

The cats at the sanctuary are described as living in a free-roaming environment within the enclosed acreage. They receive veterinary care, are spayed or neutered, and are vaccinated. The facility provides shelter structures, feeding stations, and enrichment materials. Residents include cats rescued from the wild, cats surrendered by local residents who could not keep them, and some cats attracted through a trap-neuter-return (TNR) pipeline.

All of these basic facts were confirmed by Snopes author Nur Ibrahim in May 2021 through the sanctuary's website and existing media coverage. The facility's Instagram presence (@lanaicatsanctuary) provides ongoing documentation of the animals in residence. Multiple reputable media outlets — the Los Angeles Times, HuffPost, and others — have independently reported on the sanctuary's operations. There is no reasonable basis for doubting its existence or the basic facts as described.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The simple existence-verification performed by the Snopes fact-check ("Is this real? Yes, it is real") is accurate but almost entirely omits the ecology that contextualizes why the sanctuary exists. The Lanai Cat Sanctuary is not just a charming novelty; it is the product of one of the most contentious wildlife management conflicts in Hawaiian conservation, involving genuinely contested science, real trade-offs between animal welfare and native species protection, and a policy dispute that plays out across the entire Hawaiian archipelago.

**Feral cats and Hawaiian birds — a documented conservation crisis**: Hawaii is one of the most biodiverse archipelagoes on earth and simultaneously one of the most threatened. The islands have been called the bird extinction capital of the world: of the approximately 150 native Hawaiian bird species known from the pre-contact period, roughly one-third are already extinct, and many of the remainder are listed as endangered or critically endangered (Van Riper & Scott, 2001, "Limiting Factors Affecting Hawaiian Native Birds," *Studies in Avian Biology*, 22, 221–233). The primary drivers of Hawaiian bird decline include habitat loss, introduced diseases (particularly avian malaria transmitted by introduced mosquitoes), and introduced predators — including cats, rats, and mongooses.

Cats are documented predators of Hawaiian ground-nesting birds. Nesting seabirds are particularly vulnerable because they evolved on islands with no mammalian predators and lack effective anti-predator behaviors when confronted with terrestrial mammals. Studies of cat predation on Hawaiian petrels have found that a single cat can significantly depress nesting success in a colony (Smith et al., 2002, "Cat Predation Effects on Hawaiian Petrels (*Pterodroma sandwichensis*) at Maui," *Pacific Conservation Biology*, 8(2), 113–121; Laut et al., 2016, "Cat Predation and Avoidance Behavior in Hawaiian Petrels," *Journal of Wildlife Management*, 80(7), 1274–1283). Nēnē (Hawaiian goose, *Branta sandvicensis*), the state bird of Hawaii and itself a recovered-from-near-extinction species, are also vulnerable to cat predation, particularly during nesting.

The Hawaiian Islands Seabird Recovery Project, the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and the State of Hawaii Division of Forestry and Wildlife have all identified feral cat management as a priority issue for the recovery of multiple endangered bird species. The scientific consensus among wildlife conservation biologists is that unmanaged feral cat populations pose a significant threat to these birds (Loss et al., 2013, "The Impact of Free-Ranging Domestic Cats on Wildlife of the United States," *Nature Communications*, 4, 1396 — this landmark paper estimated that free-ranging cats kill 1.3–4.0 billion birds and 6.3–22.3 billion mammals annually in the contiguous US alone).

**The trap-neuter-return debate and the sanctuary model**: The Lanai Cat Sanctuary represents a specific policy choice in response to this conflict: rather than lethal removal of feral cats from sensitive wildlife areas, the sanctuary advocates for and practices a non-lethal approach based on capture, sterilization, and permanent sanctuary housing. This approach contrasts with the recommendations of most wildlife conservation organizations, which favor lethal removal (humanely conducted) as the most effective method for reducing cat predation on endangered wildlife (Darroch & Griffiths, 2015, "Optimizing Trap-Neuter-Return Policies to Manage Free-Roaming Cat Populations," *Wildlife Society Bulletin*, 39(2), 272–279).

The core technical dispute concerns efficacy: TNR programs — in which feral cats are trapped, sterilized, vaccinated, and returned to their original location — have been widely advocated by cat welfare organizations as a humane and effective way to reduce feral cat populations over time. However, multiple studies have found that TNR programs typically fail to reduce feral cat populations in practice, because the rate of new cats entering the population (immigration from unsecured household cats and deliberate abandonment) exceeds the effect of sterilization-based population reduction (Longcore et al., 2009, "Critical Assessment of Claims Regarding Management of Feral Cats by Trap-Neuter-Return," *Conservation Biology*, 23(4), 887–894; Lepczyk et al., 2010, "What Conservation Biologists Can Do to Counter Trap-Neuter-Return: Response to Longcore et al.," *Conservation Biology*, 24(2), 627–629 — note: this literature is sharply contested between wildlife and cat welfare communities).

The sanctuary model addresses one component of this problem by removing cats from the landscape entirely rather than returning them to their original territory. From a wildlife conservation perspective, this is more beneficial than TNR-return programs because it eliminates the predation impact of the removed individuals. However, the sanctuary model is extremely resource-intensive: housing, feeding, and providing veterinary care for 600+ cats indefinitely requires substantial ongoing investment in personnel, facilities, and funding. It is not a scalable solution to the feral cat problem across the Hawaiian archipelago, where the total feral cat population is estimated in the hundreds of thousands.

**The epistemic gap — what the fact-check's "True" verdict omits**: The Snopes fact-check confirms the sanctuary's existence and provides a brief accurate description of its purpose. What it does not address is the scientific controversy about whether the sanctuary model represents an effective or justifiable approach to the underlying conservation problem. The sanctuary is presented as straightforwardly positive — "purr-adise" — without engaging with the wildlife conservation community's concerns that non-lethal cat management programs, however well-intentioned, may be insufficient to protect endangered species.

This is not an argument that the sanctuary is doing something wrong. It is an argument that a complete picture of the Lanai Cat Sanctuary requires acknowledging the ecological stakes that motivated its creation, the ongoing scientific debate about the most effective methods for protecting Hawaiian wildlife, and the trade-offs between animal welfare values (the cats' welfare) and biodiversity conservation values (the endangered birds' survival). These are genuinely contested normative and empirical questions, and a fact-check that presents only the existence-of-sanctuary as the relevant fact leaves the audience without the context needed to evaluate the broader policy and conservation picture.

The claim is straightforwardly true: The Lanai Cat Sanctuary is a real, operating, nonprofit animal shelter on the island of Lana'i, Hawaii, housing more than 600 cats in an enclosed 3.46-acre facility. It has operated since the early 2000s, has received extensive mainstream media coverage, and is verifiable through multiple independent sources. There is no credible basis for doubting the claim.

The fact-check would be more complete if it acknowledged that the sanctuary exists within a contested conservation ecology context, that the cats were rescued from areas where they threatened some of Hawaii's most endangered native birds, and that the broader question of how to manage feral cat populations in ecologically sensitive areas remains scientifically and normatively contested.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**Lana'i as a geographic and ecological context**: Lana'i is the sixth-largest Hawaiian island, covering approximately 140 square miles. Unlike the most tourist-frequented Hawaiian islands (O'ahu, Maui, Hawai'i), Lana'i has a small permanent population and has undergone dramatic land-use history: from near-total pineapple plantation agriculture under the Dole Food Company through most of the twentieth century, to luxury resort development following the purchase of 98% of the island by David Murdock in 1985, and then further transformation following Larry Ellison's acquisition of approximately 98% of the island from Murdock in 2012.

The ecological history of Lana'i is complex. The pineapple plantation era stripped much of the island's native vegetation, and the transition away from pineapple cultivation created conditions that have gradually allowed some native species recovery while simultaneously creating feral animal management challenges. Lana'ihale, the central mountain, retains patches of native cloud forest and hosts nesting habitat for Hawaiian petrels and other native birds. The coexistence of this sensitive highland habitat with a large cat sanctuary on the lower slopes is the central ecological tension.

**The Hawaiian bird conservation crisis in broader context**: Hawaii has lost more bird species than any other state in the United States. Since the arrival of Polynesian settlers approximately 1,500 years ago and European settlers in the late eighteenth century, Hawaii has lost approximately 95 native bird species to extinction — roughly 30% of all bird extinctions globally since 1500 (Pimm et al., 2006, "Human Impacts on the Rates of Recent, Present, and Future Bird Extinctions," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 103(29), 10941–10946). The causes are multiple: habitat conversion, overhunting, introduced avian malaria, introduced predators (rats, mongooses, cats), and introduced diseases. Conservation efforts in Hawaii are accordingly urgent and resource-intensive.

**The science of calculating cat predation impacts**: Quantifying the impact of cat predation on bird populations involves significant methodological challenges. The most widely cited estimate of total cats-on-birds predation in the United States (Loss et al., 2013) used a modeling approach that extrapolated from studies of cat behavior and kill rates to a national estimate. This approach has been criticized for substantial uncertainty ranges (the central estimate of 1.3–4 billion birds spans nearly a factor of four), selection bias in the underlying kill-rate studies, and failure to distinguish sufficiently between free-roaming owned cats and feral cats (Longcore et al., 2009, cited above). More localized studies in ecologically sensitive areas like Hawaii typically find more direct and less contested evidence of cat predation impacts on specific bird species in specific locations.

The debate over predation estimates matters for policy: if cat predation impacts are at the high end of estimates, aggressive removal programs are strongly justified. If impacts are at the low end, or if they are highly site-specific, more targeted management may be sufficient. The Lanai Cat Sanctuary's existence represents, in effect, a local policy answer to this question — erring on the side of removal without lethal management — but the broader debate continues at a statewide and national level.

**Hawaiian wildlife law and the ESA**: Several bird species whose conservation is directly implicated in feral cat management on Lana'i are listed under the federal Endangered Species Act. The Hawaiian petrel (*Pterodroma sandwichensis*) is listed as vulnerable globally and is subject to recovery planning by the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Sections 9 and 7 of the ESA create legal obligations for federal agencies and, in some interpretations, private actors to avoid "take" of listed species — including harassment, harm, or killing. The extent to which feral cat predation constitutes actionable "take" under federal law, and whether facilities that manage feral cat populations have legal obligations with respect to ESA-listed species, is a question that has generated legal and regulatory attention in Hawaii and nationally (Marra et al., 2009, "Migratory Bird Treaties and the Trap-Neuter-Return Policy for Feral Cats," *Conservation Biology*, 23(5), 1365–1370).

**Animal welfare and conservation values in tension**: The Lanai Cat Sanctuary's philosophy explicitly frames its mission around animal welfare: providing humane care for cats that might otherwise be killed or left to survive the difficult conditions of feral life. This framing is entirely legitimate and reflects genuine ethical values. The cats in the sanctuary are not endangered; they are invasive domestic animals. The birds they previously threatened are, in some cases, critically endangered with populations numbering in the hundreds. How to weigh these competing moral considerations — compassion for domestic animals versus protection of irreplaceable native species — is a genuine ethical question, not a factual one with a single correct answer.

What is clear is that presenting the Lanai Cat Sanctuary without this context gives an incomplete and in some respects misleading picture. A purely feel-good narrative about a "purr-adise" for 600 cats on a tropical island omits the fact that those cats are there at least in part because they were previously threatening birds that have no other habitat on earth. The sanctuary is humane and its staff are dedicated; it is also a local solution to a serious wildlife management problem whose full dimensions require conservation ecology to understand.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by Nur Ibrahim (May 2021) is brief and accurate. It verifies the sanctuary's existence through its official website, confirms the 600+ cat figure and the operational status, notes the COVID-19 closure and March 2021 reopening, and appropriately cites the facility's mainstream media coverage as corroborating evidence. The rating of "True" is correct.

The fact-check's limitations are structural rather than errors: the genre is designed to answer whether a specific claim is factually accurate, not to provide ecological or policy context. The social media claim being evaluated was existential ("Is this real?") rather than evaluative ("Is this a good approach to conservation?"), so the fact-check appropriately limits itself to existence verification.

The result is that readers who encounter the viral content and then check Snopes receive confirmation that the sanctuary is real, which is all they were asking. They do not receive any indication that the sanctuary exists in response to a serious conservation problem, that Hawaiian birds are among the most imperiled in the world, or that the management of feral cats in Hawaii is a contested topic among conservation biologists and animal welfare advocates. This limitation reflects the genre rather than incompetent journalism, but it does mean that the broader discourse around the Lanai Cat Sanctuary remains focused on its appealing aesthetics — many cats, tropical setting, humane care — rather than on the ecological stakes that motivated its creation.

A more complete account — not necessarily the one for a rapid fact-check but the one appropriate for a science-literate readership — would present the sanctuary as what it actually is: an earnest, resource-intensive, and somewhat controversial approach to an acute conservation problem, one that prioritizes non-lethal management in a landscape where the costs of inaction include the potential extinction of species that live nowhere else on earth.
