# Does This Photo Show a Cat's Tongue Under a Microscope?

**Snopes Verdict: TRUE** — The viral scanning electron micrograph (SEM) image depicting a cat's tongue surface, widely described as looking like "a bunch of little tongues sewn together," is authentic. It was captured by professional science photographer Stephen Gschmeissner using a Field Emission Gun Scanning Electron Microscope (FEG-SEM) and verified independently by multiple expert sources.

---

## §1 — Claim and Verdict Summary

The claim under review is that a viral image showing a magnified surface of a domestic cat's tongue is a genuine scanning electron micrograph. The image was first shared to Reddit in May 2019 by user katica123, with the caption "A cat's tongue under a microscope looks like it's made of other smaller tongues," and accumulated over 46,500 upvotes. The image re-circulated widely through 2022 and beyond. Snopes investigator Madison Dapcevich assessed the claim as **True** in a fact-check published April 1, 2022 (updated January 27, 2023, to reflect confirmation from the photographer).

The image shows the tongue's surface covered in dense, backward-facing papillae — keratinized projections that, when magnified and colorized for photographic print, create the uncanny visual impression of many smaller tongue-like forms nested within the primary tongue surface. The image was licensed through the Science Photo Library and had been more heavily color-saturated in the versions circulating on social media compared to the original stock library version, but the underlying photographic content was not altered.

The verdict was initially "Unproven" pending confirmation from the photographer, and was upgraded to "True" once Stephen Gschmeissner replied directly to Snopes by email, confirming the photograph was taken from a domestic cat tongue specimen provided by a veterinary colleague.

---

## §2 — Evidence and Source Analysis

The Snopes verification relied on four independent evidentiary streams: (1) reverse image search and library provenance; (2) direct confirmation from the photographer; (3) expert biological corroboration from a papillae researcher; and (4) a technical literature review on scanning electron microscopy and comparative tongue anatomy.

**Reverse Image Search and Library Provenance.** A reverse image search on TinEye returned 76 results, one of which linked to the Science Photo Library (SPL) catalog entry for the image, described as "Cat tongue surface, SEM." The Science Photo Library, a well-established commercial archive specializing in scientific and medical imagery, confirmed to Snopes via a spokesperson that the image is authentic. The SPL description notes the colorization applied to the image, confirming the social-media versions were more saturated but not otherwise modified.

**Photographer Confirmation.** Stephen Gschmeissner is a well-credentialed scanning electron microscopist whose work has been published in peer-reviewed journals and major news outlets including *Nature*, *The New York Times*, and *Wired*. In an email to Snopes, Gschmeissner confirmed the photograph was taken from a domestic cat tongue specimen obtained from a veterinary colleague, processed conventionally for SEM imaging (fixed, dehydrated, critical-point dried, coated in gold), and imaged with a Field Emission Gun Scanning Electron Microscope at a magnification factor of 15 when printed at 10 centimeters wide. Gschmeissner's career spanning the late 1990s through the present encompasses thousands of SEM colorizations of biological specimens; his methodology is both documented and reproducible.

**Expert Biological Corroboration.** Alexis Noel, a research engineer at the Georgia Tech Research Institute whose research on cat grooming and tongue papillae was published in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (Noel, A. C., & Hu, D. L., 2018, vol. 115, no. 49, pp. 12377–12382), provided an important nuance. Noel noted that cat papillae as examined using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging in her research tend to appear more conical in form, not tongue-like. However, she acknowledged that this discrepancy is likely attributable to the difference in imaging modality: micro-CT visualizes internal volumetric structure, while SEM captures surface topology. When the rigid, scoop-shaped papillar tips are captured at high magnification from a frontal angle under the FEG-SEM, they resolve as the distinctive concave hollow forms that create the "mini-tongues" visual effect. Noel also cross-referenced the image against tongue samples from bobcats, cougars, lions, snow leopards, and tigers, noting that across felid species, the external papillar surface presents similarly under SEM.

**Microscopy Technical Verification.** The claim that SEM can achieve the magnification levels and surface detail visible in the image is well-supported. Keklikoglou, K., et al. (*Journal of Imaging*, 2021, vol. 7, no. 9, p. 172) evaluated multiple scanning devices and confirmed SEM can reach magnifications of up to 1,000,000×, while light microscopy is limited to approximately 1,000×. The image's magnification factor of ×15 at 10 cm print width is conservative and entirely within standard SEM operating parameters. The FEG-SEM specifically, as employed by Gschmeissner, is noted for superior resolution compared to conventional thermionic-emission SEMs, producing sharper detail of sub-micron surface features — which is evident in the crisp edges of the papillar openings visible in the image.

## §3 — Scientific and Epistemic Context

The cat tongue papillae constitute one of the most functionally sophisticated surface structures in vertebrate sensory-mechanical biology, and the Gschmeissner image inadvertently catalyzed a significant uptick in public and scientific interest in feline grooming mechanics.

**Functional Morphology of Feline Papillae.** The papillae of the domestic cat (*Felis catus*) that appear as "tongues within tongues" are more precisely termed filiform papillae — specifically an elaborated, keratinized variety. Noel & Hu (2018) demonstrated that these are not merely abrasive rasping structures but precision fluid-wicking devices: each papilla is shaped like a scoop with a hollow channel at its tip, capable of capillary-action transport of saliva deep into the fur during grooming. The depth of fur penetration achieved by salivary wicking is proportional to the length and curvature of the papilla, which varies systematically across felid species by body mass — larger cats have longer, more deeply curved papillae. This relationship was quantified across six species (house cat, bobcat, cougar, lion, snow leopard, tiger) in the *PNAS* study, establishing a morphometric allometric scaling law for papillar geometry.

**The Thermoregulatory Function of Grooming.** Noel & Hu (2018) also documented that feline grooming serves a significant thermoregulatory function, with salivary evaporation from wetted fur providing cooling efficiency comparable to sweat-based evaporative cooling in humans. Given that domestic cats lack functional sweat glands across most of their body surface, the tongue-and-papillae system is a critical component of the animal's heat-management physiology. This finding reframed papillar morphology from a purely hygienic or prey-processing adaptation to a dual-purpose thermo-hygienic system.

**Epistemic novelty for this entry:** The public reception of the Gschmeissner cat tongue image illustrates a specific category of viral science image that might be termed "true uncanny" — images depicting authentic biological structures that trigger aesthetic responses (wonder, mild revulsion, delight) normally reserved for speculative or fantasy imagery. The "tongues within tongues" description, which became the image's primary virality driver, is technically accurate but rhetorically structured to produce maximum cognitive dissonance: every viewer's mental model of a "tongue" is a singular structure, so the recursive description generates aesthetic surprise disproportionate to the biological reality. This rhetorical feature — accurate description structured for maximum counter-intuitive impact — is a distinguishing marker of legitimate viral science communication that systematically differs from misinformation, which tends to use vague or incorrect descriptions. Tracking this rhetorical signature across viral biology images could provide a useful automated classification feature for credibility-scoring systems.

**Scanning Electron Microscopy as Scientific Art.** Gschmeissner's career represents a fascinating intersection between scientific instrumentation and aesthetic visualization practice. His decision in the late 1990s to begin colorizing SEM images — which are inherently grayscale, as electrons do not carry color information — was primarily driven by communicative intent: to render the sub-visible world legible and emotionally engaging to non-specialist audiences. The colorization of biological SEM imagery is now routine in science communication, but raises an epistemically important question: does colorization constitute a form of data transformation that alters evidential status? The consensus view in the scientific imaging community (see, e.g., *Journal of Microscopy*, various editorial standards documents) is that colorization is acceptable provided it does not obscure structural features, which is the case for the Gschmeissner cat tongue image.

## §4 — Verification Methodology

The Snopes methodology for this case was stronger than typical image-claim investigations because it combined three independent confirmatory tracks: library provenance (SPL), direct photographer confirmation, and independent biological expert review. The initial "Unproven" rating was appropriate given that the SPL confirmation alone — while strong — could theoretically have applied to a fabricated stock image sold to SPL in good faith. The upgrade to "True" once Gschmeissner responded directly was correctly calibrated.

The methodology gap most relevant to this type of claim is the absence of a pixel-level forensic analysis. Standard practice for scientifically significant image verification would include examination of image metadata (Exif data), noise pattern analysis to detect composite splicing, and potentially comparison with known FEG-SEM output signatures. These steps were not explicitly reported by Snopes. This is a minor methodological limitation because the triangulation across three independent evidence streams (library, photographer, biologist) amply compensates.

The role of Alexis Noel's expert testimony is particularly valuable: she provided not only confirmation but productive engagement with a surface-level discrepancy (conical vs. "tongue-like" appearance) that could have introduced doubt. By explaining the imaging-modality source of the discrepancy, her input simultaneously confirmed authenticity and deepened scientific understanding of the subject — an ideal form of expert testimony in a fact-check context.

## §5 — Gap Analysis and Open Questions

**Gap 1 — Full methodology documentation.** Gschmeissner confirmed the basic processing steps (fixing, dehydration, critical-point drying, gold coating), but the specific FEG-SEM model, the accelerating voltage used, and the detector configuration (secondary electron vs. backscattered electron imaging) were not archived in the Snopes record. These parameters would allow any FEG-SEM facility worldwide to reproduce the image conditions and independently confirm that the structural features visible are consistent with actual cat tongue anatomy rather than preparation artifacts.

**Gap 2 — Colorization palette justification.** The color scheme applied to the image (typically depicted in pinks, purples, and blues) is entirely artificial. Snopes confirms this is aesthetically driven, but there is no published documentation of Gschmeissner's colorization rationale for this specific image. For communicative purposes, a documented colorization methodology would clarify which structural elements are being highlighted by color assignment.

**Gap 3 — Comparative imaging across cat breeds.** Noel's cross-species comparison covered wild felids but not domestic cat breed variation. Maine Coons, Persians, and other long-haired breeds may have slightly different papillar morphology due to selective breeding effects on tongue anatomy — an unexplored area relevant to feline grooming efficacy research.

**Gap 4 — Social media color enhancement disclosure.** The social media versions of the image were more heavily color-saturated than the SPL original. No platform — Reddit, Twitter, Facebook — displayed this disclosure during the image's peak virality. The absence of a contextualizing caption disclosing colorization and saturation enhancement is a persistent gap in how SEM images circulate online, contributing to misunderstanding about what such images do and do not show.

## §6 — Observatory Synthesis and Implications

This entry exemplifies a category of science image claim that the Observatory can classify as "true-but-requires-context": the image is authentic, but the context required to correctly interpret it (the SEM imaging modality, the colorization process, the papillar function, the magnification scale) is stripped away during viral transmission. The result is wide public exposure to a real biological fact accompanied by a progressively thinner interpretive framework.

The broader implication for media literacy is that image authentication is necessary but not sufficient for science communication. Confirming that an image is real answers one question while leaving several others unanswered: What does the image actually show? What does it mean biologically? Has the image been color-processed in ways that affect perception? How was the object prepared for imaging? For scientific images specifically, these secondary questions carry genuine epistemic weight. A verified-authentic but decontextualized SEM image of a benign tongue structure causes little harm, but the same image-verification-without-contextualization pattern applied to, say, a clinical pathology image could produce seriously misleading public-health signals.
