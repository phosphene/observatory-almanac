## The Claim

In late 2024 and early 2025, images and videos of a peculiar caterpillar began circulating widely across Reddit, Instagram, X (formerly Twitter), and Facebook. The creature at the centre of the posts was unlike most people's mental image of a caterpillar: instead of the familiar hairy or segmented larva, viewers were confronted with something that looked improbably like a gummy candy — round, translucent, slightly iridescent, and almost completely featureless in the way of eyes, legs, or facial structure. The dominant impression was of a small, softly luminous jellybean that somehow appeared to be alive and moving.

The claim, as it circulated, had two components. The first was simply a statement of biological reality: this creature exists. The second, often embedded in caption and comment framing, was an implicit scepticism — because the organism looked so toy-like, so confectionery in its form, that many viewers assumed they were looking at a computer-generated image, a resin art piece, or an elaborate hoax. Reddit threads debated whether the creature was genuine. Commenters posted sceptical responses to Instagram videos. On X, the posts attracted hundreds of thousands of impressions and sustained discussion about image manipulation.

The claim being evaluated, then, is whether the "jellybean caterpillar" is a real, living organism — not a digital fabrication, not a resin craft, not a product of AI image generation.

The answer is unambiguous: it is real.

## What's Actually True

The jellybean caterpillar belongs to the family Limacodidae, an order within Lepidoptera (the moths and butterflies) that contains roughly 1,600 described species worldwide, with approximately 50 species identified in North America alone (Butterflies and Moths of North America, 2025). The family's common names — slug caterpillar moths, cup moths — hint at the distinctive morphological features that make Limacodidae larvae so immediately strange to unacquainted observers.

The larval stage of Limacodidae moths is characterised by a set of anatomical adaptations that collectively produce the jellybean appearance. Most caterpillars in other lepidopteran families move using two sets of limbs: true legs near the head and prolegs further down the abdomen. Limacodidae larvae have reduced or absent prolegs. Instead of walking or looping, these caterpillars glide, moving on a series of flattened suckers along their ventral surface — a locomotion style that the entomological literature consistently describes as slug-like (North Carolina State University, Department of Entomology, 2025). The motion is smooth and almost hydraulic rather than the segmented crawl typical of other caterpillar groups.

The body form that results — smooth, compact, rounded — is partly a product of this locomotion system and partly of the strongly curved, often almost spherical body plan that characterises many species in the family. In certain species, particularly those in tropical Asian genera, the larval body is so smooth, so uniformly curved, and so translucently pale that the internal organs are faintly visible through the body wall. The cuticular surface has very low textural relief compared to most caterpillars. Under natural lighting conditions or in close-up photographs, the combination of pale translucency, rounded form, and smooth surface genuinely produces a visual impression closely resembling a small gummy candy.

David Cheng, a researcher at the University of Florida's Kawahara Lab within the Department of Entomology and Nematology, confirmed this identification in a statement to Snopes (Dapcevich, 2025). Cheng noted that he could not confirm the specific species shown in all the circulating social media images — the family Limacodidae contains many species with visually similar larvae — but that the general category of "jellybean-like caterpillars" is well established in entomological science and does correspond to real members of the Limacodidae family.

Critically, Cheng also flagged a significant biological caveat that the viral posts largely omitted: despite their candy-like appearance, Limacodidae caterpillars are not uniformly benign. Many species in the family are adorned with specialised hollow spines or urticating hairs connected to venom glands. Contact with these spines typically causes a sharp stinging sensation, localised redness, and in some individuals a more significant allergic inflammatory response. Some North American species — including the puss caterpillar (*Megalopyge opercularis*), the saddleback caterpillar (*Acharia stimulea*), and the io moth caterpillar (*Automeris io*) — are among the most medically significant stinging caterpillars in the region, known to produce reactions severe enough to require medical attention (Forrester et al., 2018, *Toxicon*). The jellybean visual appearance, in other words, masks a genuine defensive biology.

Adult Limacodidae moths are typically small to medium-sized, with dense covering of scales and hairs that creates a soft, almost fuzzy appearance — considerably less visually surprising than the larval form. Front wings are usually dark brown with irregular patches of contrasting colour. They are predominantly nocturnal. The metamorphic transition from the alien-looking larva to the comparatively unremarkable moth adds its own dimension of biological strangeness.

## Why People Believe This (and Why Scepticism Is Understandable)

The disbelief that greeted jellybean caterpillar posts is worth taking seriously as a cultural and psychological phenomenon rather than simply dismissing it as naivety.

**The problem of prior expectations.** Human beings form mental models of biological categories, and those models shape what we accept as plausible. The mental model of "caterpillar" is fairly well established from childhood education and everyday observation: somewhat hairy, segmented, with visible legs, often green or striped. Limacodidae larvae in their most candy-like forms violate nearly every element of this model simultaneously. They are smooth rather than hairy, seemingly unsegmented in their body wall, nearly legless in external appearance, and often in colours — pale translucent cream, luminous green, occasionally pastel pink — that do not match the typical caterpillar colour palette most people carry. The violation is so comprehensive that the organism pattern-matches more readily to "art object" or "CGI creature" than to "caterpillar."

**The AI and CGI saturation effect.** By 2024–2025, the internet's visual ecosystem had been substantially altered by the wide availability of AI image generation tools capable of producing extraordinarily convincing photorealistic images of organisms that do not exist. Hyper-real AI-generated animals had circulated widely and been frequently mistaken for genuine photographs. The jellybean caterpillar posts arrived in this environment, and viewers reasonably applied heightened scepticism. The question "is this real or AI?" was not paranoid — it was calibrated correctly to the ambient conditions of digital image distribution in this period. The answer, in this case, happens to be that the organism is genuine, but the sceptical default was epistemically appropriate.

**The epistemic novelty of internet-mediated biodiversity discovery.** There is a broader phenomenon worth naming here: the internet has progressively expanded the effective range of biological knowledge available to general audiences, exposing them to organisms from ecosystems and taxonomic groups that were previously invisible outside specialist literature. Deep-sea creatures, obscure tropical invertebrates, unusual fungal formations, and extreme-morphology insects are now routinely encountered and shared by people with no prior biological training. The consequence is a regular collision between well-formed layered prior expectations and genuinely extreme morphological reality — a collision that generates the "is this real?" response even for authentically documented species. Limacodidae larvae are only one example in a long series of organisms whose photographic documentation has circulated virally and prompted genuine ontological uncertainty about whether the visible form can possibly be biological.

**The cute-but-deadly inversion.** Part of what gives Limacodidae caterpillar posts their particular charge is the thematic opposition between the candy aesthetic and the venomous biology. The visual form signals sweetness, softness, harmlessness — and the actual biology delivers spines and venom. This inversion is narratively satisfying in the way that many nature content pieces are: the pleasant exterior concealing danger. The posts that circulated most widely often included some version of this contrast as part of their framing, noting the cuteness and then revealing the sting. The inversion is real — Limacodidae larvae do have this quality — and the framing is valid, but it also contributes to the sense that the posts are constructed stories rather than straightforward documentation.

## The Science Behind the Appearance

The Limacodidae family represents an interesting case study in convergent morphological evolution. The slug-like body form has evolved independently in several distantly related caterpillar groups, suggesting that the reduced-proleg, horizontal-gliding body plan confers genuine adaptive benefits in certain ecological contexts. The precise selective advantages are not fully established, but hypotheses include reduced visibility to predators from above (a low, flat profile against a leaf surface), enhanced grip on smooth leaf surfaces, and possible mimicry of other organisms less attractive to predators (Wagner, 2005, *Caterpillars of Eastern North America*, Princeton University Press).

The transparency or translucency characteristic of some Limacodidae larvae is also biologically interesting. Transparent or semi-transparent body forms in caterpillars are unusual relative to the broader Lepidoptera, which more often rely on colour-based camouflage or warning colouration (aposematism). The translucency of some Limacodidae species may serve a crypsis function on certain leaf types where ambient light transmission through the leaf creates a similarly luminous visual environment, though this hypothesis requires empirical testing across specific species-habitat pairs.

The venomous spine system found in many Limacodidae species is itself a substantial area of entomological research. The structures responsible for stinging are typically called urticating spines or setae, and they come in several morphological types. In spiny Limacodidae genera, the spines are hollow, fragile, and connected to venom-producing cells in the body wall. Contact causes the tips to break, releasing venom into the skin (Hossler, 2010, *Dermatologic Therapy*). The venom composition varies by species and includes combinations of histamine, phospholipase enzymes, and protease compounds — an overlap in mechanism with some wasp venoms that explains the burning quality of the sting.

The diversity of morphology within Limacodidae is substantial. At one end of the spectrum are the smooth, round, candy-like species that generated the viral posts. At the other are species like the saddleback caterpillar (*Acharia stimulea*), which is vivid green with a brown saddle marking and equipped with horns bearing dense clusters of venomous spines — striking, dangerous-looking, and again widely shared on social media as an example of remarkable insect design. The same family produces both extremes. This breadth of form within a single family is a reminder that Limacodidae has had extensive evolutionary time and ecological diversity across which to develop variant larval strategies.

The worldwide distribution of the family — with major radiation in tropical Asia and Southeast Asia, where the most extreme morphological variants including the most candy-like larvae are concentrated — means that many of the viral images of jellybean caterpillars originate from Southeast Asian observations. Species in genera such as *Parasa*, *Darna*, *Setora*, and others common in tropical Asia include some of the most visually striking Limacodidae larvae, and their documentation by naturalists and citizen scientists through platforms such as iNaturalist has significantly increased their global visibility in recent years.

## Broader Context: Viral Biology and What It Gets Right

The jellybean caterpillar phenomenon sits within a recognisable pattern of online biology communication: an organism with genuinely extreme morphological features is documented and shared, generates widespread disbelief, achieves confirmation, and becomes a de facto ambassador for a biological group that most people have never encountered. The organism's documented reality functions as a kind of advertisement for the actual diversity of the living world.

There is genuine epistemic value in this pattern, even when it is mediated through entertainment-adjacent sharing and viral mechanics. The surprise people feel when they learn that jellybean caterpillars are real — that an organism genuinely looks like a piece of candy, that evolution has produced something this visually improbable — is a legitimate cognitive response to accurate information. It should update priors not just about Limacodidae but about the broader range of morphological space that evolution has explored, and about the limits of human prior expectations as a guide to biological reality.

The Snopes entry on the jellybean caterpillar, published April 20, 2025 (Dapcevich, 2025), correctly rated the claim True while providing the essential biological context: the caterpillars are identified as Limacodidae, their slug-like locomotion and reduced prolegs explain the distinctive body form, and the potential for venomous response in spiny species is flagged as an important rider on the "harmless candy" visual impression. This is the correct framework for presenting the information — confirming the extraordinary reality, grounding it in taxonomy and mechanism, and adding the caveat that corrects the most dangerous inference the visual impression might generate.

What the Snopes article does not fully explore — and what deserves additional attention for a scientifically literate audience — is the extent to which Limacodidae represents a genuinely interesting evolutionary case study beyond the viral appearance. The family is ancient, with fossil evidence placing limacodid-type larvae in Eocene amber (Nel et al., 2014, *ZooKeys*). Their slug locomotion system is a significant departure from the ancestral lepidopteran body plan and represents an independent solution to the problem of larval movement on smooth plant surfaces. Their global distribution and high species richness — roughly 1,600 described species, likely with substantial undescribed diversity in tropical regions — make them ecologically significant as herbivores across a wide range of plant families.

The viral "jellybean caterpillar" posts are, in short, a rare case where popular fascination with an organism's appearance is a perfectly reasonable entry point into genuinely interesting science. The cuteness is not incidental to the biology; it is a consequence of specific morphological adaptations that have their own functional logic and evolutionary history. Understanding why they look like jellybeans turns out to be the same thing as understanding how they move, how they defend themselves, and how they have diversified across tropical and temperate ecosystems worldwide.

## Verdict

**True.** The jellybean caterpillar is real. It belongs to the family Limacodidae — slug caterpillar moths — a globally distributed lepidopteran family of approximately 1,600 species. The candy-like appearance is produced by genuine morphological features: a smooth, often translucent body wall, a reduced prolog system replaced by ventral suckers, and a compact rounded body form associated with the family's characteristic slug-like gliding locomotion. While the soft appearance may suggest harmlessness, many Limacodidae species carry venomous spines capable of causing dermatological reactions ranging from mild stinging to significant inflammation. The viral posts that circulated in 2024–2025 documented a real organism. The scepticism they generated reflects both reasonable epistemic caution in an environment saturated with AI-generated imagery and the limits of human prior expectations when confronted with genuinely extreme biological morphology.
