## §1 — The Claim and Its Social Context

Few viral animal welfare warnings have achieved the sustained multi-year circulation of the purple paws alert. Since May 2017, posts warning pet owners and passersby to "pick up any cat with purple paws and take it to the nearest shelter because it's being used as bait by dogfighters" have circulated continuously across Facebook, neighborhood social media platforms (including Nextdoor), and regional community networks. The warning has been re-shared millions of times across multiple years, demonstrating unusual longevity for what is, at its evidentiary base, a single unverified story from one animal rescue organization in Southwest Florida.

The claim contains several specific elements: that organized dogfighting rings mark bait animals with color-coded dyes on their paws or fur; that these dyes serve as identifiers for wagering (spectators bet on which "color" will die first); that the practice is widespread; and that encountering a cat with colored paws is a reliable signal of dogfighting involvement. Each claim, examined against the available evidence, falls somewhere between "unconfirmed" and "actively contradicted by investigations."

The emotional architecture of the warning is near-optimal for viral sharing: it protects a beloved domestic animal (cats), it invokes a genuinely horrific form of criminal animal abuse (dogfighting), it provides a clear and simple action directive ("pick up the cat"), and it creates an apparent everyday relevance (anyone might see a cat with unusual paw coloring). This combination activates both protective animal welfare instincts and the social reward of sharing potentially life-saving information.

The problem is that the specific causal claim — dyed paws as bait animal markers — has not been confirmed by any law enforcement investigation, any ASPCA special investigations unit, any Humane Society of the United States (HSUS) anti-dogfighting program, or any independent animal welfare researcher, despite multiple rounds of inquiry specifically prompted by the viral post.

## §2 — The Evidence Record

### 2.1 The Origin: Naples Cat Alliance, May 2017

The claim traces to a single Facebook post by the Naples Cat Alliance, a rescue organization in Naples, Florida, on May 15, 2017. The post described their receipt of a cat — dubbed "Mr. Purple Paws" — from local animal control, whose paws had been dyed purple. The post asserted that dog fighters use markers to color the white parts of cats and kittens so they can bet on which color will die first, and that the cats are "color-coded and then thrown to packs of dogs."

When Snopes contacted the Naples Cat Alliance for additional details, the organization acknowledged that it did not have direct knowledge confirming dogfighting involvement. Their inference was based on: (a) the cat's origin area, which they described as "well known for dog fighting"; (b) the non-water-soluble nature of the purple dye; (c) information they said they had received from local law enforcement about dogfighting; and (d) the cat's apparent friendliness, which they interpreted as consistent with ease of capture.

The Naples Cat Alliance explicitly stated: "Can we definitively say he was a victim of dog fighting, no. We based it on info and location."

This admission — that the claim was speculative rather than evidential — was not transmitted in the viral iterations. The post was shared on the assumption of verified fact.

### 2.2 Dogfighting: What the Evidence Does and Does Not Show

Dogfighting is illegal in all 50 U.S. states and the District of Columbia (federal prohibition since 2007 under 7 U.S.C. § 2156, as amended by the Animal Welfare Act), as well as in most countries globally. The practice is nonetheless documented as ongoing through regular law enforcement interdictions, with large-scale federal cases (including those coordinated by the ASPCA, HSUS, and FBI) identified annually in multiple U.S. states.

Do dogfighters use live animals as bait? This question has a more nuanced answer than the binary it is often presented as.

**What is documented**: Multiple law enforcement cases in the United States and United Kingdom have resulted in convictions for using cats, rabbits, and other small animals in illegal dogfighting contexts. The BBC reported confirmed cases in the UK involving pet theft and use in dog-baiting events (BBC News, 2019). An ABC7 News investigation documented confirmed U.S. cases involving small animal bait use (ABC7, 2016). The practice exists.

**What is not documented**: The specific claim that cats' paws are systematically colored/dyed as part of a wagering system — to identify individual animals for spectator betting purposes — has not been confirmed by any investigation as of the available record. The ASPCA's special investigations unit, responding to Snopes' inquiry, stated explicitly: "Although dogfighting remains a prevalent and barbaric practice in the U.S., we have not encountered this practice [of using cats as bait] in our ongoing efforts to combat dogfighting across the country."

The RSPCA, investigating similar claims in the UK involving kittens allegedly sold on Facebook as dogfighting bait, concluded after investigation: "We are satisfied this is not the case." Their special operations unit, which investigates organized animal crime, stated it has never found "strong evidence" to support claims of dog fighters setting their dogs on smaller animals before dumping them.

This is not absence of evidence taken as evidence of absence — it is an active investigatory absence. Organizations that specifically investigate dogfighting have specifically looked and specifically not found the wagering-by-colored-bait-animal practice described in the viral warning.

### 2.3 Alternative Explanations for Dyed Cat Paws

Multiple commenters on the original Naples Cat Alliance post and in subsequent community discussions offered alternative explanations for dyed paw coloration in cats that are prosaic rather than criminal:

- **Novelty grooming or aesthetic dye**: Some pet owners use pet-safe dyes for aesthetic reasons, including paws and fur. Pet-safe dyes in unusual colors including purple are commercially available.
- **Medical or veterinary marking**: Veterinary practices occasionally use colored markers or dyes to identify animals during treatment, particularly for monitoring clipped or shaved areas, administered injection sites, or surgical regions.
- **Children's play or household accident**: Cats that have walked through spilled markers, craft paints, or food dyes may display colored paws. Non-water-soluble dyes (as described in the Naples case) could derive from craft supplies and persist through normal licking.
- **Trap-Neuter-Return (TNR) program marking**: Some TNR programs mark feral cats after neutering to facilitate identification without recapture — though this typically involves ear tipping rather than paw dye.

None of these alternatives received systematic investigation in the viral claims, which treated purple paws as a near-certain signal of dogfighting.

### 2.4 Similar Claims in the UK and Europe

The UK has seen parallel warnings about dyed cats and dogfighting. In 2016, news reports described kittens found with Magic Marker coloring on their bodies, with dogfighting associations suggested (HuffPost, 2016). Local animal welfare investigations in Ohio described cats found "dyed" with possible dogfighting connections (WKBN, 2016). All these reports include identical disclaimer language: welfare staff "admitted they had absolutely no evidence about the dog-baiting theory" (spokesperson quoted in UK reporting) or described involvement as "possible."

The geographic spread and multi-year persistence of structurally identical unverified claims suggests a common template: animal with unusual dye coloration + known local dogfighting activity = inference of bait animal. This inference is repeatedly made by local animal welfare workers and shared as confirmed fact, without ever receiving confirmation from systematic investigation.

## §3 — Scientific Context and Epistemic Novelty

### 3.1 The Epistemology of Unproven Animal Welfare Claims

The purple paws claim occupies a distinctive epistemic category: a claim sufficiently plausible that investigation is warranted, sufficiently horrific that rapid sharing feels morally justified, but sufficiently unverified that treating it as fact potentially causes its own harms. The ASPCA's statement that dogfighting is "prevalent and barbaric" while simultaneously stating they have not encountered the specific bait-coloring practice in their investigations captures this duality exactly.

What is the appropriate epistemic response to this category of claim? Standard deductive frameworks for fact-checking — is this true or false? — inadequately model the situation. The practice being claimed may or may not exist; it has not been confirmed, but investigative absence is not the same as confirmed denial. The Snopes "Unproven" verdict is precisely calibrated for this.

**Epistemic novelty**: The purple paws case is unusual in that the originating organization itself acknowledged its claim was speculative, but this acknowledgment was not transmitted in viral spread. This creates a traceable epistemic degradation chain: the original post had an uncertainty qualifier ("we can't definitively say") that was removed by all subsequent sharing. Studies of how social transmission modifies epistemic markers in claims suggest that uncertainty language is systematically stripped in retelling because it reduces the emotional impact of a story and its sharability (Rosnow & Fine, 1976, *Rumor and Gossip*; DiFonzo & Bordia, 2007, *Rumor Psychology*). The purple paws case provides a near-ideal documentation of this mechanism.

### 3.2 The Harm Potential of the Warning

The viral warning is not harmless. Several categories of potential harm have been identified:

**Unnecessary animal seizure and shelter burden**: If any cat found with colored paws (from any cause) is picked up and transported to a shelter, this creates: unnecessary animal stress; shelter capacity burden; potential separation of owned cats from their homes; and, in the case of feral or community cats, removal from established TNR territory.

**False positive warning fatigue**: Repeated circulation of unverified warnings that do not lead to confirmed rescues tends to produce warning fatigue — a well-documented phenomenon in public health communication (Lum et al., 2016, *PLOS One*, in the context of emergency alerts) where repeated unconfirmed alerts reduce vigilance for genuine threats.

**Distortion of anti-dogfighting intelligence**: Law enforcement and animal welfare investigators oriented toward "cats with dyed paws" as a doctrinal signal may allocate investigative attention to a criterion that doesn't distinguish genuine bait animals from innocent pets.

**Anti-community fear**: In neighborhoods where dogfighting suspicion is already elevated, the warning can amplify inter-neighbor tensions and produce harassment of individuals whose pets are found with any unusual marking.

### 3.3 Dogfighting Intelligence: What We Actually Know About Bait Animals

The academic literature on dogfighting, while limited (given the covert nature of the activity), provides better sourcing than the viral warning infrastructure. Forsyth and Evans (1998, *Society and Animals*) examined dogfighting operations in the U.S. South and documented that animal theft for multiple purposes — including bait use — is associated with dogfighting rings, but did not document the colored-paw identification system. The American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA, 2019) and HSUS have published detailed briefing documents on dogfighting logistics; neither references paw coloring as a known operational practice.

Conversely, the Humane Society of the United States' anti-dogfighting investigations have documented "bait animals" to include dog breeds that are smaller or weaker, not primarily cats, as the primary bait training medium for fighting dogs — though cats and other small animals have appeared in some cases (HSUS, 2021, *Dogfighting Fact Sheet*).

## §4 — Amplification Analysis

The purple paws warning demonstrates exceptional amplification durability — most viral false claims peak and decay within days to weeks; this claim has circulated with consistent volume since May 2017. Several factors contribute:

**Evergreen emotional architecture**: Unlike COVID-19 specific claims, the purple paws warning is not time-perishable. It can be re-shared at any time with equal emotional resonance. The first share and the thousandth share are functionally identical.

**Community platform affinity**: The warning found a natural habitat on hyperlocal platforms — Nextdoor, neighborhood Facebook groups, community pages — where pet safety warnings have high expected value for the audience and low trust barriers. Unlike political misinformation, pet safety warnings don't trigger partisanship skepticism; they are read on the presumption of good faith.

**Rescue organization origination**: The fact that the claim originated from an animal rescue organization — rather than an anonymous or politically charged source — conferred substantial credibility that insulated it from easy dismissal.

**Action directive**: The warning includes clear behavioral instruction ("pick up the cat, take it to a shelter"), which increases sharing intention among people who experience a moral mandate to pass the action-relevant information along.

**No authoritative definitive denial**: Unlike the hand sanitizer claim, where the chemistry could be precisely and finally disproven, the purple paws claim cannot be definitively falsified — it can only be assessed as "not confirmed despite investigation." The persistence of genuine uncertainty (dogfighting is real; some bait use exists; the specific dye-coding claim hasn't been confirmed) prevents the kind of unambiguous corrective that stops other misinformation genres.

## §5 — Corrections and Key Findings

**Dogfighting is real and prevalent in the United States.** This is confirmed by annual law enforcement operations and is not in dispute.

**Cats and other small animals have been used as bait in some documented dogfighting cases.** This is confirmed by specific case reportage in both the U.S. and UK.

**The specific claim — that paw coloring is used to mark bait animals for wagering purposes — has not been confirmed.** Multiple law enforcement agencies and national animal welfare investigators, specifically queried about this practice, have stated they have not encountered it.

**The originating organization acknowledged its claim was speculative.** The Naples Cat Alliance explicitly stated it could not definitively confirm dogfighting involvement.

**Alternative explanations for colored paws exist** and have not been ruled out in any reported case of "purple paws" discovery in news coverage.

**The viral warning potentially causes its own harms**, including unnecessary animal seizure, shelter burden, and false positive fatigue.

**The claim belongs to a documented pattern** of structurally identical unverified dyed-animal-dogfighting claims appearing in multiple countries since at least 2016.

## §6 — Verdict and Broader Implications

Snopes' rating of **Unproven** is the most defensible rating available for this category of claim. The claim cannot be proven false (the practice may exist but remain undocumented); it cannot be proven true (no confirmed cases have been documented despite targeted investigation). The appropriate epistemic stance is suspended judgment paired with a recommendation that any genuine animal welfare concern be reported to professional investigators rather than immediately acted upon via self-directed rescue.

The broader implications of the purple paws case extend well beyond its specific content. It demonstrates that animal welfare misinformation operates by different amplification rules than political or public health misinformation: it persists longer, travels on community trust rather than partisan networks, cannot be reliably debunked because the core concern (animal abuse) remains real and documented even if the specific mechanism is unconfirmed, and causes a category of diffuse harm that is harder to measure than the harm caused by, say, a false medical claim.

For animal welfare organizations specifically, the purple paws case provides a template for what not to do: publish an anecdote-based inference as an urgent warning without completing the basic investigative step of consulting law enforcement or national welfare organizations for corroboration. The Naples Cat Alliance's well-intentioned post, which explicitly acknowledged its speculative basis, was converted by social media into a years-long false certainty that continues to circulate.

For the fact-checking community, this case illustrates why "Unproven" is an essential verdictorial category. The binary True/False framework is insufficient for a domain with genuine evidentiary complexity. The appropriate response to a claim that is plausible, emotionally activating, connected to real harms, but unconfirmed by targeted investigation is not a verdict of either truth or falsity — it is a careful documentation of what is known, what is not known, what has been investigated, and what responsible action looks like under uncertainty.
