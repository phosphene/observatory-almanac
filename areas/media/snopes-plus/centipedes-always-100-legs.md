# Centipedes Always Have Exactly 100 Legs


## 1. The Claim

The name "centipede" derives from Latin roots meaning "hundred feet" (centum = 100, pes/pedis = foot), and this etymology has generated a widespread popular belief that centipedes have exactly 100 legs. The claim appears in casual conversation, trivia collections, children's natural history books, and internet lists of animal facts. It is treated as a definitional property—something true by the creature's very name—rather than as a biological claim requiring verification.

The associated implicit logic is intuitive: the creature was presumably named for its leg count, so the name encodes a biological fact. If centipedes are called hundred-leggers, surely they have a hundred legs. The name-as-fact inference pattern is common enough in folk taxonomy that it rarely triggers scrutiny, and in this case the Latin etymology reinforces the impression of scientific precision rather than undermining it.

The belief is also reinforced by centipedes' visual impression: they appear to have a lot of legs, and "a hundred" functions as a culturally conventional way of expressing "very many" in numerous languages (the same root appears in "century," "cent," "percent," and "centurion"). Whether encountered as literal count or hyperbolic description, the hundred-leg number attaches readily to an animal that does, in fact, seem to have an unusual abundance of appendages.

The more subtle claim—that centipedes have a variable but sometimes equal to 100 number of legs—is also common. People who know that leg counts vary sometimes believe the centipede family includes species with exactly 100 legs as a naturally occurring count, even if not as a universal rule. This version of the claim is equally false for a reason that requires some zoological knowledge to understand: the mathematics of centipede development makes exactly 100 legs structurally impossible for any member of the class.


## 2. What's Actually True

Centipedes belong to the class Chilopoda, one of four classes within the subphylum Myriapoda. All members of class Chilopoda share a fundamental developmental and anatomical rule: they always have an odd number of leg-bearing body segments, which means they always have an odd number of leg pairs, which means their total leg count is always an odd number multiplied by two—never exactly 100.

To understand why, it helps to understand centipede body organization. Centipedes are segmented arthropods in which each trunk segment bears exactly one pair of legs (unlike millipedes, which bear two pairs per segment). The number of leg-bearing segments varies by species and is determined during development, but across all approximately 3,300 described species of centipedes, the number of leg-bearing pairs is always odd: 15, 21, 23, 27, 29, 31, 33, 35, 37, 39, 41, 43, 45, and so on. Common household centipedes (Scutigera coleoptrata) have 15 pairs (30 legs). Stone centipedes (order Lithobiomorpha) have 15 pairs as adults. Scolopendromorph centipedes—the large tropical species familiar from wildlife documentaries—typically have 21 or 23 pairs (42 or 46 legs). The giant Scolopendra gigantea has 21 or 23 pairs depending on the individual.

The biological reason for this invariant rule relates to the segment-addition growth mode used by some centipede orders (anamorphic development), but even in epimorphic centipedes that hatch with their full adult segment count, the developmental pathways consistently produce odd numbers of leg pairs across all species and orders. No species with 50 leg pairs (100 legs) has ever been described, and zoologists have proposed theoretical developmental reasons why the odd-pair rule holds throughout the clade.

Leg counts across the class range from 30 legs (15 pairs) in the most common species to 354 legs (177 pairs) in the soil centipede Strigamia chersina, which holds the current record for the most legs of any centipede species. This is the full range: from 30 to 354, with every count an even number (pairs × 2) but every pair count an odd number. The number 50 (which would give 100 legs) is simply not represented anywhere in the class.

The name "centipede" is therefore an etymological misnomer—a folk-taxonomic name that encodes a false belief about the creature it describes. Common names are not biological descriptions; they encode folk observations, cultural associations, and historical errors. The centipede shares this property with numerous other organisms whose common names encode incorrect information: starfish are not fish, jellyfish are not fish, mountain goats are not true goats, and killer whales are not whales. The name encodes cultural perception, not zoological reality.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The name itself is the primary vehicle of the belief. "Centipede" sounds like "100 legs," and in a world where Latin-derived scientific terms are expected to be precise descriptions, the name implies a definition. This is a reasonable inference heuristic that happens to be wrong in this case—and wrong in a way that is instructive about how folk taxonomy names are generated. Scientific nomenclature genuinely does encode morphological description: a hexapod has six legs, a tetrapod has four limbs, a bilateral organism has two-sided symmetry. Trusting that "centi-" means exactly a hundred rather than approximately a lot is consistent with how Latin scientific vocabulary typically works—the error lies in assuming common names share scientific nomenclature's commitment to precision.

Common names for organisms were often given by people who observed the animals casually rather than counted their appendages with systematic care. A centipede glimpsed moving across a floor presents a visual impression of very many legs moving in waves; "hundred-legs" is a culturally resonant way to express "indeterminate very many legs" just as "thousand-legs" is used for millipedes in many languages (despite millipedes also not having a thousand legs—most have between 40 and 400). These hyperbolic naming conventions encode impression, not measurement.

The belief is also reinforced by the general assumption that organisms have symmetrical, round-number anatomical features. Humans have 10 fingers, 32 teeth (when adult), 24 ribs (as pairs), and various other anatomical features that appear in round numbers or neat pairs. Extending this to arthropods feels natural even though arthropod segment counts are not constrained by the same evolutionary pressures that produce mammalian symmetry. Centipede leg counts are determined by segmental developmental genetics that have no reason to favor round numbers.

Children's educational contexts frequently perpetuate the claim either directly or by omission. "Centipedes have 100 legs, millipedes have more" is a common simplified contrast presented in biology units, even though both parts are inaccurate as universal descriptions. The contrast between the two names serves a pedagogical purpose—distinguishing one-pair-per-segment from two-pairs-per-segment body plans—that can be served just as well with accurate leg counts, but the pedagogically convenient shorthand has become culturally embedded.

The corrective fact—that centipedes always have an odd number of pairs and therefore can never have exactly 100 legs—is genuinely surprising to most people, which suggests it conflicts with background expectations deep enough that prior exposure to the correct information may not have displaced the intuitive belief. Surprise is one signal that a correction is accessing a belief that was not consciously held as such; the person never explicitly believed centipedes had 100 legs, but the etymological implication was present and influencing reasoning.

There is also a curiosity-suppression dynamic at work. Centipedes are unfamiliar and often unsettling to many people; few are motivated to handle one long enough to count its legs, and the visual blur of a centipede in motion makes any in-person count impractical. This physical inaccessibility of the organism removes the most direct route by which a factual error might be corrected through personal observation. For animals people interact with regularly—like dogs or birds—folk beliefs are subject to constant informal correction through direct contact. For animals that inspire avoidance, folk beliefs propagate unchallenged because the test condition (prolonged close observation) is systematically avoided.


## 4. Verdict

**EARC: EXAGGERATED/FALSE** — Centipedes do not always have 100 legs, and no centipede species has exactly 100 legs. All members of class Chilopoda have an odd number of leg-bearing pairs, making 100 legs mathematically impossible—50 pairs would be required, and 50 is an even number of pairs, which never occurs in the class. The name "centipede" (hundred feet) is an etymological misnomer encoding a folk impression rather than a zoological count. Actual centipede leg counts range from 30 (15 pairs, as in common house centipedes) to 354 (177 pairs, as in Strigamia chersina). The factual truth here is more interesting than the popular belief: the biological reason why centipedes structurally cannot have 100 legs is a genuine zoological fact that most people do not know.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The centipede leg count error is a compact illustration of how folk taxonomy and common names can encode incorrect biological information in ways that are almost impossible to dislodge because the name itself is the carrier of the belief. Understanding this phenomenon has broader implications for science communication, biology education, and the relationship between language and empirical knowledge.

The class Chilopoda is part of the subphylum Myriapoda ("many feet"), which also includes millipedes (class Diplopoda), pauropods (class Pauropoda), and symphylans (class Symphyla). Myriapods are among the oldest terrestrial animal groups, with fossil evidence extending back to the Silurian period approximately 430 million years ago. Some of the earliest-known terrestrial animals were myriapod-like creatures, making the group a window into the early evolution of life on land. The Silurian myriapod fossil record is particularly significant because it coincides with early terrestrial colonization—these animals were among the first to develop the respiratory, excretory, and integumentary adaptations necessary for survival outside aquatic environments. Their long evolutionary tenure means that the developmental rules encoded in centipede genetics, including the odd-pairs rule, represent over 400 million years of conserved biology.

Centipede diversity is substantial and underappreciated. The approximately 3,300 described species occupy nearly every terrestrial environment on Earth, from tropical rainforests to temperate grasslands to caves to deserts. They are uniformly carnivorous—all centipedes are predators that use venom-delivering forcipules (modified front legs, not mouthparts) to subdue prey. This distinguishes them sharply from millipedes, which are mostly detritivores eating decaying plant material. The predatory lifestyle and venom delivery system are related to their ecology and account for some of the features that make centipedes unsettling to humans.

The largest centipede species, Scolopendra gigantea of the Amazon basin, can reach lengths exceeding 30 cm and has been documented capturing and eating bats, lizards, frogs, and small rodents in addition to the invertebrate prey taken by smaller species. Its venom is medically significant in humans, capable of causing severe pain, local tissue damage, and systemic effects in sensitive individuals, though fatalities are extremely rare. Understanding centipede biology—including accurate leg counts—is relevant to herpetologists, entomologists, and medical toxicologists in endemic regions.

The odd-pairs rule in centipede development is related to the mechanism of segment addition in anamorphic orders (Lithobiomorpha and Scutigeromorpha), which add segments gradually during post-embryonic molts. In these groups, molts add pairs of segments in a fixed pattern that always maintains an odd total. In epimorphic orders (Scolopendromorpha and Geophilomorpha), which hatch with their full adult segment count, the developmental genetics are constrained to produce odd pair counts through mechanisms that remain an active area of zoological research. The invariant nature of the rule across all orders suggests a deep developmental constraint rather than a coincidence. That this constraint holds across all five orders of Chilopoda—Scutigeromorpha, Lithobiomorpha, Craterostigmomorpha, Scolopendromorpha, and Geophilomorpha—despite their considerable morphological and ecological diversity, is a striking example of how developmental rules can be phylogenetically conserved even as other traits diverge substantially.

Millipedes—the centipede's most commonly mentioned comparator—share the misnomer problem. The name "millipede" suggests a thousand legs, but no millipede species has a thousand legs. The record holder, Illacme plenipes, has up to 750 legs (375 pairs), and most millipede species have far fewer. Like centipedes, millipedes have variable leg counts that do not conform to the folk-taxonomic name. The parallel between the two groups makes them useful together in a lesson about common-name reliability in folk taxonomy.

This entry connects to larger questions about biological literacy and the specific errors introduced by relying on common names as descriptions. The field of common-name-as-misnomer is extensive: electric eels are not eels (they are knife fish), prairie dogs are not dogs (they are rodents), sea horses are fish, red pandas are not bears and only distantly related to giant pandas, and fireflies are beetles. Each of these cases involves a common name encoding a folk taxonomic judgment that formal zoological classification has not confirmed. Knowing this pattern exists—that common names are cultural artifacts rather than scientific descriptions—is more useful than any individual correction.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has addressed this claim and rates it **False**, documenting the odd-pairs rule and the actual range of leg counts across centipede species. The article correctly identifies that the name "centipede" encodes etymology rather than biology and notes the ironic fact that no centipede can have exactly 100 legs by biological necessity.

The Snopes treatment of this claim is an example of straightforward fact-checking where the verdict is unambiguous—there is no mixture of true and false, no deficiency-versus-sufficiency distinction, no qualifying conditions. The claim is simply false, and the correction is a genuine zoological fact with a clear mechanistic explanation. This makes it a useful entry point for audiences learning about fact-checking, because the structure is uncomplicated.

The educational value of this specific correction is higher than its immediate informational content because it demonstrates a transferable principle: organism names are not reliable descriptions, and checking biological assumptions against formal taxonomy often produces surprises. Instructors in biology, science communication, and critical thinking regularly use this type of example precisely because the false belief is counterintuitive and the mechanism of error (trusting etymology) is clearly identifiable.

Science communicators have noted that the centipede case is particularly effective for teaching the odd-pairs developmental rule because it transforms a simple correction—"they don't have 100 legs"—into a genuine biology lesson: "the reason they can't is that developmental pathways in class Chilopoda invariably produce odd numbers of leg pairs." Moving from "that's wrong" to "here's why it's structurally impossible" converts a correction into an insight, and insights are more memorable and educationally valuable than corrections alone.

Broader coverage in natural history publications and zoology outreach has addressed centipede leg counts in the context of myriapod diversity and arthropod biology more generally, contributing to improved coverage in educational settings. The persistent presence of the folk belief in elementary education contexts—where the hundred-legs approximation continues to appear in simplified materials—remains a challenge for science educators who would prefer accurate information to be established at the earliest stages of biological literacy.
