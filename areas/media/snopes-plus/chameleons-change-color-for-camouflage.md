# Chameleons change color primarily to blend in with their surroundings

## The Claim

Ask almost anyone what chameleons do and they will tell you: chameleons change color to hide from predators. The image is vivid and intuitive — a lizard creeping along a branch, its skin shifting from green to brown to mottled gray, perfectly matching the bark, a living illustration of camouflage at its most sophisticated. This is perhaps the most persistent folk belief in popular zoology, appearing in children's books, idioms about fitting in, advertising for products that "blend into" their environments, and countless nature documentaries that describe color change primarily as a survival strategy against predators. It is also, in its most common telling, significantly wrong about *why* chameleons change color.

The claim is not simply false. Chameleons do change color. Their ability to do so is genuine, their color shifts are spectacular, and camouflage does play some role in their overall survival strategy. But the primary evolutionary drivers of chromatic change in chameleons are social signaling, reproductive communication, thermal regulation, and emotional state — not background matching. The belief gets the phenomenon right while misidentifying its function, which is the characteristic signature of a distorted-but-grounded misconception.

## What's Actually True

The mechanistic understanding of how chameleons change color was fundamentally revised by a landmark 2015 study by Jérémie Teyssier and colleagues published in *Nature Communications*. Prior to this work, the conventional explanation held that color change was driven by the redistribution of pigment-containing organelles (chromatophores) — cells that expanded or contracted to expose or conceal colored pigments. This model, while partially correct, could not adequately explain the speed, brightness, and spectral range of chameleon color changes, particularly the vivid iridescent displays observed during social encounters.

Teyssier et al. discovered a different and more elegant mechanism in the upper layer of chromatophores: photonic crystals composed of guanine nanocrystals arranged in a lattice within specialized cells called iridophores. These nanocrystal lattices act as photonic structures, selectively reflecting light of different wavelengths depending on the spacing between crystals. When a chameleon is in a relaxed state, the lattice spacing is tight and reflects shorter wavelengths — blues and greens that tend to blend with foliage. When the animal is excited, threatened, or engaging in social display, the iridophore cells swell and the lattice spacing increases, shifting the reflected wavelength toward longer wavelengths: yellows, oranges, and reds. This is not pigment redistribution — it is structural color change driven by mechanical deformation of photonic crystal arrays, a system of extraordinary elegance and speed.

Critically, the conditions that trigger this nanocrystal lattice adjustment are not visual cues from the environment prompting background-matching. They are internal physiological and social stimuli. Excitement, rivalry, courtship, fear, and thermal stress drive the swelling and contraction of iridophore cells. The animal is not "reading" its surroundings and adjusting its palette accordingly — it is expressing internal states through structural color.

The social signaling function is not a minor qualifier to camouflage; it is the dominant and evolutionarily primary function of chromatic change in most chameleon species. Research by Devi Stuart-Fox and Adnan Moussalli, particularly their 2009 study on dwarf chameleons (*Bradypodion* species) published in *PLOS Biology*, provided compelling evidence for this. Using spectrophotometric measurements and controlled behavioral experiments, Stuart-Fox and Moussalli found that color changes during male-male contests were far more pronounced and rapid than any camouflage-related adjustments. Males that changed color more dramatically in agonistic contexts won a higher proportion of contests — demonstrating direct fitness consequences for social chromatic signaling. The predictive power of social context for color change was substantially greater than the predictive power of background color.

The thermal regulation function is equally well-documented. Chameleons are ectotherms — they rely on external heat sources to regulate body temperature — and darker colors absorb more solar radiation. In the morning, before behavioral thermoregulation has had its full effect, chameleons darken significantly to absorb heat; as body temperature rises toward the preferred range, they shift toward lighter colors that reflect more radiation. This thermoregulatory color change operates independently of both camouflage and social signaling and follows predictable daily cycles that are entirely driven by temperature, not background appearance.

What about camouflage? Chameleons do possess a baseline palette that tends toward green and brown tones appropriate for arboreal or scrub habitats, and some species show resting coloration that provides reasonable background matching. Passive camouflage — maintaining a neutral baseline color that blends with typical habitat — is real and contributes to predator avoidance. But this is very different from the dynamic, active, real-time background-matching that the popular myth describes. The dynamic color-change system — the nanocrystal lattices firing, the vivid shifts across the spectrum — is overwhelmingly deployed for signaling and thermal regulation, not for "chameleon creeps along branch and matches the bark precisely."

There is also a more basic biological point: most chameleon predators are birds with excellent color vision, and snakes that locate prey through heat and movement rather than visual pattern matching. A chameleon attempting camouflage by dynamic color shift would face the challenge that the *process* of shifting is itself visually conspicuous — a rapidly color-shifting object is arguably *more* detectable than a static one. Passive resting camouflage (staying still, maintaining a green baseline) is the actual predator-avoidance strategy; the dynamic shift system serves other masters.

## Why People Believe This

The chameleon-as-camouflage belief is one of the most durable animal myths in popular culture, and it persists for reasons that go beyond simple ignorance. It is a belief reinforced by narrative logic, visual plausibility, and decades of popular-science presentation.

First, the phenomenon is genuinely real and visually dramatic. Chameleons do change color, the changes are observable, and the result often does look like background matching — particularly when a calm, thermally satisfied chameleon sitting on a green branch appears green. The naive observer sees a green lizard on a green branch and infers camouflage. The inference isn't absurd; it is simply incomplete.

Second, camouflage is the most narratively satisfying explanation. It fits a familiar template — the vulnerable prey animal that must hide from predators — and it is a story with a clear protagonist (the chameleon), a threat (predators), and a mechanism (color change). Social signaling among lizards is less visually accessible and less immediately compelling as a narrative. When nature documentaries need to fill thirty seconds of screen time showing a chameleon changing color, "it's hiding from that snake" is a simpler, more gripping explanation than "it's communicating its fitness and thermal status through photonic crystal lattice adjustments."

Third, chameleons have become cultural symbols of adaptive concealment. The word "chameleon" itself is used as a metaphor for someone who blends in, adapts to social environments, or changes to fit expectations — uses that all derive from and reinforce the camouflage narrative. This metaphorical usage then feeds back into belief: people who have absorbed the metaphor assume it must reflect the biological reality that inspired it.

Fourth, until 2015, the mechanistic picture was genuinely incomplete. The chromatophore model — pigment redistribution — was the accepted explanation in textbooks, and it could be loosely integrated with a camouflage story even if it didn't quite support it mechanistically. The Teyssier et al. discovery of photonic crystal lattice dynamics fundamentally changed the picture, but textbook revisions lag scientific publications by years or decades, and popular science presentations lag further still.

Finally, many chameleon species do use color change in contexts that resemble camouflage: cryptic resting coloration, stress-induced darkening when hiding, and color shifts that incidentally match environmental features. These partial matches create confirmation bias. When people see a chameleon shift toward brown on a brown surface, they interpret it as active camouflage even if the underlying driver was social stress or thermal adjustment that happened to produce a brown coloration in that context.

## Verdict

**Distorted-but-grounded.** The core observation is correct — chameleons change color — and some role for camouflage is real, particularly in passive resting coloration. But the dominant functions of dynamic color change are social signaling (competitive displays, mate selection, threat responses) and thermoregulation, not background matching. The mechanism involves photonic nanocrystal lattice dynamics in iridophore cells responding to internal physiological states, not environmental reading followed by palette adjustment. The popular version captures the phenomenon while inverting the functional priority. It is wrong in the way that matters: if you believed chameleons change color primarily for camouflage, you would systematically mispredict when, how rapidly, and under what conditions the changes occur. You would expect more color change near predators and less change during sunny mornings. The opposite is closer to true.

## The Wider Picture

The chameleon case illustrates a broader pattern in how functional explanations of animal traits get popularized: survival value (especially predator-avoidance) tends to crowd out other explanations in popular accounts. Camouflage is a survival story; social signaling is a social story. The former maps onto universal human concerns about danger and survival; the latter requires engaging with intraspecific competition, sexual selection, and lizard social dynamics, which are less intuitively gripping. This isn't unique to chameleons. The eye is commonly described as evolving "to see predators and prey," obscuring the substantial role of sexual selection and intraspecific signaling in visual system evolution. Many animal features that serve primarily social functions are described in survival terms in popular accounts.

The 2015 Teyssier et al. paper is worth dwelling on for its scientific elegance as much as its corrective function. The discovery that chameleons have essentially evolved biological photonic crystals — structures that manipulate light through nanoscale physical architecture rather than chemical pigmentation — is one of the more remarkable findings in recent comparative biology. The nanocrystal lattice system is sophisticated enough to produce spectral reflectances that span from blue-green to red-orange within milliseconds, with spectral precision that exceeds what simple pigment redistribution could achieve. The physical optics of structural color — the same principles that produce iridescence in butterfly wings and peacock feathers — are at work in chameleon iridophores, but with the additional property of mechanical tunability. This places chameleons among a small set of organisms known to use tunable photonic structures for rapid signaling.

The social signaling literature on chameleons also opens onto fundamental questions in evolutionary biology. Stuart-Fox and Moussalli's work showed that color change ability co-evolved with the degree of social complexity and male-male competition in *Bradypodion* species — populations with more intense male competition showed greater chromatic repertoire and more dramatic agonistic displays. This pattern suggests that the social function of color change was not a secondary elaboration of a camouflage system but a primary driver of chromatic complexity. Camouflage may have been the ancestral function in early squamates, but the evolutionary elaboration of the nanocrystal lattice system appears to have been driven primarily by social selection pressures.

This matters for how we understand the evolutionary origins of animal signals. If sophisticated dynamic color change evolved under sexual and social selection rather than predator pressure, it suggests that intraspecific communication can be a more powerful driver of trait elaboration than interspecific interaction. The evolutionary costs of dramatic color displays — the metabolic cost of maintaining complex iridophore systems, and the potential predator-attracting cost of bright, conspicuous social displays — were apparently outweighed by the fitness benefits of effective social signaling. Chameleons essentially traded some camouflage currency for superior communication ability, which tells us something about the relative fitness value of social signaling in their ecology.

The thermoregulatory function adds another layer. Chameleons have been observed to adjust chromatic behavior seasonally and across altitudinal gradients in ways that track thermal environment, suggesting that the color-change system is genuinely multipurpose — serving signaling, thermoregulation, and background matching to varying degrees depending on context, species, habitat, and time of day. This context-dependence makes the popular single-function account (it's for camouflage) especially misleading: even if we granted that camouflage is *a* function, the chameleon color system is a multifunction communication platform in which camouflage competes for bandwidth with more dominant uses.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The chameleon camouflage myth is unusual among animal behavior misconceptions in that it has not been widely addressed by mainstream fact-checking organizations. Snopes has not published a dedicated entry on the claim, and it does not appear in standard "animal myths debunked" coverage at the level it deserves. This may be because the myth is technically a distortion rather than an outright falsehood — chameleons do change color, and they are better at "camouflage" in the broader sense than most animals — which makes the myth harder to frame as a clear true/false claim.

Where the myth has been addressed, it tends to appear in science journalism following the 2015 Teyssier et al. paper, which generated significant mainstream coverage. Articles in *New Scientist*, *Science*, and *National Geographic* correctly reported the nanocrystal lattice discovery and its implications for the camouflage narrative. However, these corrections reached a far narrower audience than the countless nature documentaries, children's books, and casual science summaries that continue to lead with the camouflage explanation.

A rigorous fact-check of this claim would rate it "mostly false" or "misleading" rather than "false," because the core observation — chameleons change color — is accurate. The distortion lies in the attributed function. A well-constructed fact-check verdict would acknowledge the partial truth of passive camouflage, note the primary role of social signaling and thermoregulation demonstrated in peer-reviewed research, cite the Teyssier et al. 2015 mechanistic work and the Stuart-Fox and Moussalli 2009 functional work, and conclude that the popular belief correctly identifies what chameleons do while substantially misidentifying why they do it.

The persistence of this myth in the face of good contrary evidence is itself a case study in how biological misconceptions resist correction when they are embedded in cultural metaphor and narrative. "Chameleon" meaning "one who adapts to blend in" is too useful and too well-established to be displaced by the more accurate but less narratively tidy fact that chameleons are, in their primary dynamic function, social signalers dressed in photonic crystal technology. Correcting the functional account of chameleon color change requires not just presenting better evidence but dislodging a metaphor from its cultural niche — a considerably harder task.

The most accurate popular summary would be: chameleons are social signalers dressed in nanoscale photonic architecture who happen to have a background-matching baseline palette. The camouflage is the static setting; the dynamic system is something far more sophisticated. Science communication that leads with social signaling and treats camouflage as the secondary, passive function would be both more accurate and, arguably, more compelling — the nanocrystal lattice story is more remarkable than the camouflage story. The persistence of the myth reflects a failure of science storytelling as much as a failure of public reception.
