## §1 — Claim & Viral Context

The claim is seemingly trivial but has proven remarkably durable: many consumers insist that the popular cheese-flavored cracker now sold under the name "Cheez-It" was previously called "Cheez-Its" or "Cheez-Itz," and that the name was quietly changed — without announcement — at some point in the recent past. The conviction is held with genuine subjective certainty by many who articulate it. Social media posts, Reddit threads, and dedicated Mandela Effect wikis document numerous accounts from people who report a strong visceral memory of the alternate spelling, often coupled with the characteristic cognitive dissonance of recognizing that the current box displays something different from what they "remember."

This belief is a canonical example of what has come to be called the "Mandela Effect" — a term coined by paranormal researcher Fiona Broome in 2009, after she discovered that many conference attendees shared her (incorrect) recollection that Nelson Mandela had died in prison in the 1980s. The term quickly expanded to encompass any widespread collective misremembering of a fact, brand name, logo, or cultural artifact. The Cheez-It/Cheez-Its/Cheez-Itz confusion is a paradigm-case member of the Mandela Effect canon alongside the Monopoly Man's monocle, the Berenstain/Berenstein Bears spelling, and whether C-3PO has one silver leg.

The Snopes fact-check, published September 21, 2022, was authored by David Emery, a West Coast-based writer and editor with 25 years of fact-checking experience. The article notes that the debate "has been simmering for years on social media, mainly in the form of memes and posts" — indicating that the confusion predates the fact-check by a substantial period. Snopes's intervention was responsive to a sustained, organic pattern of consumer confusion rather than a sudden viral spike.

## §2 — Snopes Assessment

Snopes rated this claim **True** — confirming that the name on boxes of Cheez-It crackers is, and has always been, "Cheez-It" (singular). The fact-check addresses the historical record with two primary pieces of evidence: the Cheez-It website's own documentation indicating the product first hit store shelves in 1921 under the "Cheez-It" name, and a newspaper advertisement from 1930 clearly displaying the "Cheez-It" branding.

The product was created by Weston Green and his father in Dayton, Ohio, with the recipe formulated in 1921. The brand has been owned, over the decades, by Sunshine Biscuits and then by the Kellogg Company (now Kellanova, following various M&A activity). Across all ownership eras, documentary evidence of the brand name consistently shows the singular "Cheez-It" form.

Snopes notes, with an editorial note of some delicacy, that referring to the crackers in the plural — as in "a handful of Cheez-Its" — is perfectly grammatical English. The brand name is singular; a collection of instances of the product requires the plural form in ordinary discourse. This is identical to the logic of "a Lego" (singular piece) versus "a bunch of Legos" (colloquial plural in ordinary use, though the LEGO Group's preferred usage is "LEGO bricks"). The existence of the colloquial plural "Cheez-Its" may meaningfully contribute to the confabulation: people who commonly say "pass me the Cheez-Its" may over time reconstruct the brand name as the form they most frequently use verbally, rather than the typographically correct singular form on the box.

The Mandela Effect is invoked explicitly in the Snopes article as a possible framework for explaining the collective misremembering. Snopes defines the Mandela Effect as "a collective misremembering of a fact or event, producing a feeling of disorientation or cognitive dissonance," while noting that some believers interpret Mandela Effect instances as evidence of "slippage" between parallel universes. The cognitive explanation — confabulation — is offered as the scientific alternative. The article cites the definition of confabulation from a guide for mental health professionals in the *International Journal of Neurology and Neurotherapy* (2017): false memories consisting of exaggerations, misplacings, or total inventions of events.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty

The Cheez-It/Mandela Effect case offers an opportunity to examine the **cognitive genealogy of specific false memories** — that is, to trace not just that a false memory exists but *why* this particular false memory takes the specific forms it takes (Cheez-Itz, Cheez-Its) rather than other possible incorrect variants.

The plural-to-brand-name transfer hypothesis is the most parsimonious cognitive explanation for the "Cheez-Its" variant. In everyday discourse, the plural "Cheez-Its" is used with high frequency because speakers naturally pluralize count nouns when referring to multiple items. Memory research on the "typicality effect" (Rosch & Mervis, 1975, *Cognitive Psychology*) demonstrates that the most frequently encountered form of a concept tends to be encoded with greater strength and retrieved more readily than less-frequent variants. If consumers predominantly encounter, use, and hear the plural "Cheez-Its" in conversation while the singular "Cheez-It" appears primarily on packaging examined briefly at point of purchase, the plural form will be better consolidated in episodic and semantic memory — and will be reconstructed as the "correct" name when queried.

The "Cheez-Itz" variant is epistemically distinct and requires a different explanation. It may reflect a phonological encoding artifact: consumers who learn the brand name aurally and attempt to reconstruct its spelling may apply a common English orthographic pattern for the /ɪts/ phoneme in brand contexts (cf. "Ritz," "Fritz," "Blitz") that happens to produce the "-itz" form. This is not a recall error so much as a spelling reconstruction error — a failure of orthographic memory that is distinct from episodic memory.

The neuroscientific literature on source-monitoring errors (Johnson, Hashtroudi, & Lindsay, 1993, *Psychological Bulletin*) provides a well-validated mechanism for the broader Mandela Effect phenomenon. Source monitoring refers to the cognitive processes by which individuals attribute memories to their origins or sources. When source monitoring fails — as it frequently does for visually similar or semantically related materials — people misattribute the origin of a memory, generating "false" recall that feels subjectively genuine. In the Cheez-It case, the source of "Cheez-Its" in memory (the consumer's own speech) is misattributed to the brand itself (the packaging), creating confident false recall.

The "parallel universe" explanation for Mandela Effect phenomena has zero empirical support in mainstream physics or cognitive science. However, its persistence as a folk explanation for shared false memories is an interesting phenomenon in its own right. Vyse (2014, *Believing in Magic: The Psychology of Superstition*, 2nd ed., Oxford University Press) connects superstitious and paranormal explanations to the need for cognitive closure — a motivational state in which ambiguity or unexplained phenomena generate psychological discomfort that drives individuals toward any available explanatory framework, even unverified ones.

The psychological function of the "parallel universe" explanation for Mandela Effect beliefs is worth articulating: it transforms an embarrassing cognitive error (I was wrong about something I felt certain about) into an interesting discovery (reality itself may have changed). The latter is narratively far more satisfying and emotionally less threatening than the former, which may partly explain why neuroscientific explanations for Mandela Effect instances are resisted even when clearly presented.

## §4 — Evidentiary Landscape

**Cheez-It Company / Kellogg's.** Brand documentation confirms the product was developed in Dayton, Ohio in 1921. The original 1921 logo displayed on the official Cheez-It website shows the singular "Cheez-It" branding, continuous with modern packaging. Primary historical source corroborating Snopes's verdict.

**Newspaper advertisement (1930).** Displayed in Snopes article, this advertisement clearly shows "Cheez-It" branding, providing a contemporaneous documentary source from 9 years after product launch. Establishes that the singular form is not a recent brand refresh.

**Rosch, E., & Mervis, C. B. (1975).** "Family resemblances: Studies in the internal structure of categories." *Cognitive Psychology*, 7(4), 573–605. Foundational paper establishing the typicality effect: the most-encountered instance of a category concept is encoded most strongly and retrieved most readily — relevant to why the frequently-heard plural "Cheez-Its" may outcompete the packaging-visible singular "Cheez-It" in memory.

**Johnson, M. K., Hashtroudi, S., & Lindsay, D. S. (1993).** "Source monitoring." *Psychological Bulletin*, 114(1), 3–28. Comprehensive review of source monitoring and its failure modes. Source monitoring failure — misattributing the origin of a memory — is the primary mechanism underlying false memories of the "Cheez-Itz" or "Cheez-Its" brand name.

**Schacter, D. L. (2001).** *The Seven Sins of Memory: How the Mind Forgets and Remembers*. Houghton Mifflin. Accessible synthesis of memory research; "misattribution" (Sin 4) directly addresses the source monitoring errors that produce Mandela Effect phenomena. Confabulation discussed as filling of memory gaps with plausible but incorrect content.

**Roediger, H. L., & McDermott, K. B. (1995).** "Creating false memories: Remembering words not presented in lists." *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 21(4), 803–814. The DRM paradigm paper; demonstrates experimentally that humans reliably "remember" words never presented to them when those words are semantically associated with presented items. Highly relevant to brand-name confabulation.

**"Confabulation: A Guide for Mental Health Professionals." (2017).** *International Journal of Neurology and Neurotherapy*, 4(1):070. Cited in Snopes article; provides clinical mental health definition of confabulation used in the article's explanation of false memory formation.

**Loftus, E. F. (2005).** "Planting misinformation in the human mind: A 30-year investigation of the malleability of memory." *Learning & Memory*, 12(4), 361–366. Review of Loftus's landmark misinformation effect research, demonstrating that post-event information can be absorbed into original memories and "remembered" as having been part of the original experience — directly applicable to how social media discussions of "Cheez-Itz" can strengthen false memories in individuals who encounter them.

**Gap in evidentiary base:** No published study has directly examined brand-name confabulation as a specific sub-category of source monitoring error in naturalistic consumer memory contexts. The Mandela Effect literature largely relies on self-report data from online aggregation platforms, which have substantial selection bias (only those who feel strongly motivated to report participate). A pre-registered empirical study testing whether consumers more readily misrecall product names they frequently encounter in verbal vs. visual-only contexts would provide directly relevant data for the Cheez-It confabulation hypothesis.

## §5 — Verdict Reasoning & EARC Classification

**Classification: E (Established)**

The historical record of the Cheez-It brand name is Established: contemporaneous documentary evidence spans from 1921 to the present, showing consistent use of the singular "Cheez-It" across multiple ownership periods. There is no credible competing documentary evidence for any other brand name variant. The Established classification reflects not only the high quality of historical evidence but the absence of any documented period — even briefly — when the name differed from "Cheez-It."

**Why the collective misremembering is scientifically interesting:** The False Memory literature provides a robust explanatory framework for what is actually a mundane but cognitively instructive phenomenon. Multiple independent mechanisms — typicality effect, source monitoring failure, phonological reconstruction, social contagion of false recall — converge on the same false belief, making it unusually stable and resistant to correction. The practical implication for consumer cognition is that strongly held certainty about a remembered fact is not sufficient evidence of that fact's truth, a conclusion with broad applicability to eyewitness testimony, historical memory, and brand perception.

**Gap summary:** The primary gap is the absence of adequate public communication about the cognitive mechanisms of collective misremembering. Popular culture has adopted the "Mandela Effect" as a named phenomenon with a paranormal explanatory gloss; memory science has the conceptual tools to explain every documented instance without invoking parallel universes, but these explanations are not culturally salient. This entry's contribution is to make the cognitive mechanism — source monitoring failure, typicality encoding, and social contagion of false recall — explicit in the context of the specific Cheez-It case.
