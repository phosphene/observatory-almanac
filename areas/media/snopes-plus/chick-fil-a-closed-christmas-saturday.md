## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In mid-November 2021, a headline published by KSTU, the Fox affiliate television news station in Salt Lake City, Utah, announced that Chick-fil-A would be "closing on Saturday now, too." The headline activated significant concern among the fast food chain's customers, who interpreted the phrasing as suggesting that the chain had adopted a policy of closing on both Saturdays and Sundays—potentially doubling its weekly closure commitment beyond the well-known Sunday observance policy that Chick-fil-A has maintained throughout its history.

Snopes investigator Bethania Palma (2021) assigned this claim the rating **False**. The KSTU headline was materially misleading: the article itself, when actually read, reported not that Chick-fil-A would close all Saturdays going forward, but that the chain would be closed on a single Saturday—December 25, 2021—because Christmas Day in 2021 fell on a Saturday. Combined with Chick-fil-A's standing Sunday closure policy, this created the notable circumstance that the restaurant would be closed on both Christmas Day (Saturday, December 25) and the following Sunday (December 26), requiring customers to wait until Monday, December 27, to visit.

This is a commonplace business accommodation: many businesses that would normally be open on a Saturday close on Christmas Day regardless of which day of the week Christmas falls. The KSTU headline's framing—"closing on Saturday now, too"—extracted the single-Saturday Christmas closure from its holiday context and presented it as a new ongoing policy in a way that was both technically defensible (the restaurant was indeed closing on a Saturday) and profoundly misleading in its implication that this represented a standing policy change.

This entry examines the background of Chick-fil-A's Sunday closure policy and its cultural and religious foundations, the dynamics of misleading headline construction and "technically true but practically false" journalism, the media and social media ecology that allowed the misframed story to spread, the economics and cultural positioning of religiously motivated business practices in consumer culture, and the broader conceptual framework for evaluating claims where the falsehood is in the framing rather than the facts.

## §2 Chick-fil-A's Sunday Closure Policy: Background and Foundations

To understand why the "closing on Saturday, too" framing generated such alarm, one must appreciate the cultural significance of Chick-fil-A's Sunday closure policy within the fast food industry and in American consumer culture more broadly.

Chick-fil-A has closed all of its restaurant locations on Sundays since the company's founding by S. Truett Cathy in 1946. The policy is explicitly religious in motivation: Chick-fil-A's official explanations consistently describe the Sunday closure as offering employees "an opportunity to rest, spend time with family and friends, and worship if they choose," language directly drawn from the Christian Sabbatarian tradition in which Sunday is a day of rest and worship. Palma (2021) cited the Chanhassen, Minnesota Chick-fil-A franchise location's own website, which states plainly that the Sunday closure enables employees to "set aside one day to rest and worship if they choose."

The policy is remarkable within its industry peer group. The U.S. fast food industry predominantly operates seven days per week, often with extended hours on weekends when consumer demand is highest. National holidays are observed selectively, with Thanksgiving, Christmas, and New Year's Day the most common closure days, and many chains maintain holiday operating hours (reduced hours or limited menus) rather than full closure. Chick-fil-A's blanket Sunday closure, maintained consistently across all corporate-owned and franchise locations for nearly 80 years, represents a uniquely principled business position within this context.

The economic consequences of the Sunday closure are non-trivial. Sundays are among the highest-revenue days in quick-service restaurant operations for many categories, particularly chicken-based concepts, given the post-church dining traffic pattern common in the American South and Midwest. Daszkowski and Haller (2020), analyzing quick-service restaurant industry data in *Journal of Foodservice Business Research*, estimated that Sunday generates approximately 15–18% of weekly revenue for comparable quick-service competitors who open seven days a week. For Chick-fil-A, this represents foregone revenue estimated at multiple hundreds of millions of dollars per year. That the company nonetheless maintains the closure is regularly cited in business media as evidence of a values-driven strategic commitment that has contributed to the brand's cultural distinctiveness and customer loyalty—essentially, that the Sunday closure functions as a differentiating brand asset rather than purely as a cost.

The policy has attracted renewed attention during periods of controversy about Chick-fil-A's corporate charitable giving, particularly regarding donations to organizations identified as opposed to LGBTQ rights. Del Valle (2019, Vox) documented Chick-fil-A's history of donations to such organizations and the company's subsequent statements about revising its charitable giving criteria—a controversy that made the company's religiously motivated policies subject to increased public scrutiny. Palma (2021) cited this context to note that the Sunday closure policy was part of a broader religious values framework that the company maintained publicly.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Headline as Misinformation Vector

The central epistemological problem in this case—the element that justifies the **False** verdict—is that the misleading content is located entirely in a headline rather than in any propositional claim that appears in the body of coverage. The KSTU article, read in full, was accurate: it reported that Chick-fil-A locations would close on Christmas Day, which happened to be a Saturday, and explained the context clearly. The false impression was generated only by the headline extracted from its article context.

This represents a specific and well-documented failure mode in digital news media that Palma (2021) implicitly identified by rating the overall claim as **False**: the misleading headline was the claim being evaluated, not the article's full content. This is the appropriate evaluation posture because in digital news environments, a substantial majority of article sharing behavior is driven by headline exposure rather than full article reading.

Gabielkov et al. (2016), in a now-widely-cited paper presented at the ACM SIGMETRICS conference, analyzed Twitter sharing behavior for 2.8 million news articles and found that approximately 59% of URLs shared on Twitter had never been clicked—meaning the sharers were sharing content they had not read, based entirely on the headline. More recently, Altay et al. (2022, published in *PNAS Nexus*) found that comprehension of shared articles correlated strongly with reduced misinformation spread, confirming that headline-only engagement is the primary driver of false viral claim propagation. The KSTU headline—"Chick-Fil-A Closing on Saturday Now, Too…but for a Reason"—was architecturally designed for sharing: the ellipsis and qualifier "…but for a reason" function as a curiosity gap device that increases click-through for those who read attentively while still conveying the core misleading implication to those who stop at the headline.

The epistemic novelty in this case concerns the classification problem created by technically-accurate misleading headlines. The full KSTU story was accurate. The KSTU headline was misleading. The claim that circulated virally—"Chick-fil-A is closing on Saturdays now"—derived directly from the headline's framing and was false. Under a strict propositional analysis, no false statement was made: the headline says the restaurant is "closing on Saturday," which is true (one specific Saturday: Christmas). But under a communicative pragmatics analysis of what information the headline conveyed to its audience, the claim was false.

Grice's (1975) cooperative principle in linguistic communication holds that speakers ordinarily aim to be as informative, accurate, and relevant as necessary for the current purpose. A headline about a business closure policy should convey accurate information about that policy. The omission of the temporal qualifier ("on Christmas Day only") from a headline about business closures violates the maxim of Quantity—it provides insufficient information—generating a misleading implicature that the closure is a standing policy change. The implicature is parasitic on the cooperative assumption that headlines provide sufficient context to accurately represent their content.

For fact-checking methodology, the case establishes an important principle: when a viral claim derives from a misleading headline rather than from outright fabrication, the appropriate evaluation is of the impression the headline created in the relevant audience, not of the narrow propositional truth value of the headline in isolation. The Snopes **False** verdict is correct precisely because it evaluates the claim as communicated and understood rather than as parsed in formal semantic terms.

## §4 The Holiday Calendar Coincidence and Its Commercial Significance

The underlying newsworthy fact of the KSTU story and the subsequent coverage—that Chick-fil-A would be closed both Saturday, December 25 (Christmas Day) and Sunday, December 26, creating a two-day closure over a high-traffic holiday weekend—is genuinely unusual and genuinely noteworthy for Chick-fil-A customers.

The circumstance arises from a calendrical coincidence: Christmas falls on a Saturday only approximately once every five to six years on average, due to the irregular progression of the Gregorian calendar. When it does, Chick-fil-A's combination of the standing Sunday policy and the near-universal holiday closure on Christmas creates a two-day consecutive closure that is distinct from what customers experience in years when Christmas falls on a weekday or Sunday.

Palma (2021) cited CNN's more accurately framed coverage of the same underlying fact: "Chick-fil-A Will Be Closed Christmas Weekend." This headline accurately conveyed the relevant information—the restaurant would be closed over the Christmas weekend—without implying a permanent Saturday closure policy. It represents the same news information packaged in a framing that generates accurate audience expectations rather than misleading ones.

For customers, the practical implication documented by Palma (2021) was clear: those wishing to eat at Chick-fil-A would need to do so before Saturday, December 25, or wait until Monday, December 27. The two-day closure is the longest regular closure that Chick-fil-A customers experience under normal conditions, and it occurs only in years when Christmas falls on a Saturday. The next occurrence as of the 2021 reporting was in 2027.

It is also worth noting that Chick-fil-A's Christmas closure, unlike its Sunday closure, is not a religiously distinctive policy: as Palma (2021) noted, many businesses, both secular and religious, close on Christmas Day. Good Housekeeping's holiday hours roundup (cited by Palma, 2021) confirmed that numerous large retail and restaurant chains close on Christmas regardless of religious affiliation. The Christmas closure is therefore most accurately described as an alignment with a near-universal American commercial holiday schedule rather than an extension of the Sunday religious observance policy.

## §5 The Media Ecosystem of Misleading Business Headlines

The KSTU headline represents a specific subgenre of misleading business and consumer news that appears with some regularity in local and regional news environments: the headline that accurately describes a single, temporally limited event but uses language implying ongoing or permanent policy change.

This genre depends on a characteristic ambiguity in business reporting language. "Chick-fil-A closing on Saturday" can describe either (a) a specific closure on one Saturday, or (b) a policy of closing on Saturdays. The disambiguation is provided by context, but digital headline design routinely maximizes the emotional valence of the less mundane interpretation (policy change = more interesting) while remaining technically defensible on the grounds of narrow accuracy (yes, the restaurant will be closed on a Saturday).

The commercial incentive structure driving this headline style is well-documented. Clickbait headlines—defined broadly as headlines that prioritize emotional engagement over accurate representation—generate higher click-through rates than accurate but less emotionally engaging alternatives, creating revenue-per-click incentives for publishers to adopt emotionally maximizing framings even when those framings mislead readers. Chakraborty et al. (2016), analysing the structure of clickbait headlines in a dataset of 15,000 news articles published in *Proceedings of the 25th International Conference on World Wide Web*, identified the curiosity-gap construction (suggesting information is withheld by the headline) as the most consistently effective clickbait strategy, and the policy-change implication as a reliable engagement driver in consumer and business news contexts.

The KSTU headline's "…but for a reason" construction is a classic curiosity-gap deployment: it acknowledges that there is more to the story than the headline states, which increases click-through from readers who want to know the reason—but does nothing to correct the misleading implication for the large proportion of readers who share without clicking. The mild note of mystery ("but for a reason") may even reinforce the impression that a significant policy change has occurred with an unstated but presumably compelling motivation.

Local TV news organizations operate under particularly acute engagement pressures in the digital media environment, as traditional broadcast audiences have fragmented and competition for digital attention has intensified. Willnat and Weaver (2014), in their national survey of U.S. journalists published in *Journalism Practice*, found that time pressure and traffic-performance metrics had substantially influenced headline-writing practices, with journalists increasingly writing headlines for sharing behavior rather than for comprehension accuracy. The KSTU headline may reflect these systemic pressures as much as any individual editorial judgment.

The broader lesson for media consumers is one of active disambiguation: when a business headline implies a policy change, it is worth asking whether the underlying story is about a standing policy or a one-time event, and whether the claim survives a reading of the first paragraph. In this case, the first paragraph of the KSTU article would have corrected the misleading impression. The overwhelming majority of social media users who shared the headline did not take that step.

## §6 Broader Implications, EARC Gap, and Verdict Contextualization

The Chick-fil-A Christmas Saturday closure case presents a **False** verdict on a claim that was, in its most precise propositional form, technically not false: Chick-fil-A did close on a Saturday. The falseness lies entirely in the implication created by the headline's framing—the suggestion of a standing policy change that no reasonable reading of the underlying news event would support.

**EARC Gap Assignment — C (Conceptual)**: The gap in this case is purely conceptual. All empirical facts are known, non-contested, and clearly established: Chick-fil-A was closed on Christmas Day (Saturday, December 25, 2021); this was not a new Saturday policy; the closure was a standard holiday accommodation. The conceptual gap concerns the appropriate methodological framework for rating claims that derive from technically-accurate-but-misleading headlines rather than outright false statements.

The claim "Chick-fil-A is closing on Saturdays now" clearly warrants a **False** verdict because it is false as a general policy claim and because the widespread understanding of the headline was precisely this general policy claim. But fact-checking methodology does not yet have a fully articulated framework for distinguishing between:
- Claims that are false because the facts asserted are incorrect
- Claims that are false because the framing creates false implications that the facts, properly understood, do not support
- Claims that are technically true but pragmatically false because the implicature generated by the headline's framing is false

These are distinct truth-value scenarios that warrant distinct rhetorical treatments in fact-check coverage, even when they share a verdict. The resolution of this conceptual gap would require the development of a more granular taxonomy of false-verdict subtypes that distinguishes factual falsity from framing falsity. Such a taxonomy would allow fact-check coverage to more precisely explain *how* a claim is false rather than merely *that* it is false.

**Verdict Summary**: The claim that Chick-fil-A would be closing on Saturdays (as a standing policy) is **False**. The chain closed on Christmas Day 2021 because it fell on a Saturday—a one-time, holiday-specific closure fully consistent with near-universal American commercial holiday practice. The KSTU headline that generated the claim was technically defensible but practically misleading, functioning as a mechanism for claim-generation rather than accurate news summary. The Snopes investigation correctly identifies the actual news—a Christmas Day closure—and correctly rates the implied standing policy change as false.
