# Is Chick-fil-A Closing All Restaurant Locations in 2024?

## 1. The Claim

In December 2023, online display advertisements began appearing before users on YouTube and across various other websites and digital platforms. These advertisements prominently featured photographs of Chick-fil-A restaurant locations — recognizable storefronts with the chain's distinctive red signage — paired with alarming text captions. The most prominent version read: "They're Closing Doors in 2024. These Fast Food Restaurant Chains Are Closing The Doors In 2024." Another variant showed a photo of a Chick-fil-A alongside the text: "Restaurant Chains Closing. It's Time To Say Goodbye, These Restaurants Be Closing The Doors."

Multiple additional variations of similar advertisements circulated, making the same core claim: that Chick-fil-A was planning to close all of its locations, that the company was going bankrupt, or that it was going out of business for unspecified reasons. Some versions dated the supposed closure to 2023; others targeted 2024. The ads were designed to generate maximum alarm and clickthrough engagement by associating one of the United States' most recognizable and beloved fast-food brands with imminent collapse.

Snopes journalist Jordan Liles investigated the claim in December 2023, describing the ads as intentionally deceptive and finding no basis for the closure narrative. Chick-fil-A's media relations team was contacted for comment.

The claim, as stated, is false. Chick-fil-A had no plans to close its restaurant locations in 2023 or 2024. It was not going bankrupt. It was not going out of business. The ads were designed to exploit the emotional resonance of brand-extinction anxiety to generate clicks and ad revenue, rather than to convey meaningful consumer information.

## 2. What's Actually True

**Chick-fil-A's financial standing:** At the time the closure advertisements ran in December 2023, Chick-fil-A was one of the most financially successful fast-food chains in the United States. An April 2023 report by QSR Magazine documented the company nearing $19 billion in total annual sales — a figure that places Chick-fil-A among the top five fast-food chains in the U.S. by revenue despite operating far fewer locations than competitors like McDonald's, Starbucks, or Subway (Klein, 2023, *QSR Magazine*). The company's individual restaurant sales per unit consistently rank among the highest in the quick-service industry, reflecting a business model centered on limited hours (closed Sundays), limited menu complexity, and concentrated customer loyalty.

There were no credible analyst reports, financial filings, or media investigations suggesting that Chick-fil-A faced insolvency, acquisition, or large-scale closure at the time the ads ran. The company is privately held by the Cathy family trust, meaning it does not publish quarterly earnings reports or face public-market-driven pressure for restructuring. Its substantial financial reserves and franchise model further insulate it from the kinds of cash-flow crises that typically presage mass closures.

**Where the ads actually led:** Each variant of the Chick-fil-A closure advertisements directed users who clicked through to a lengthy article hosted on Reference.com. The article's headline was "These 53 Restaurant Chains Are on the Brink of Disappearing Entirely." The article listed nearly 70 businesses across approximately 70 slides — each slide describing a company and its alleged precarious state. Examination of the article's page source code revealed that it was written in or around 2020 and last republished in 2021, meaning the content being advertised in December 2023 was at minimum two years old. The specific restaurant conditions described in the 2020 article reflected the extreme disruption of the early COVID-19 pandemic period — an entirely different economic context from December 2023.

Critically, Chick-fil-A was not mentioned anywhere in the Reference.com article. The photographs of Chick-fil-A restaurants used in the advertisements had no relationship to the article content. The ads featuring Chick-fil-A imagery were, in the Snopes assessment, "false and misleading clickbait" that exploited the brand's recognition as bait to draw users into engaging with a scrollable, advertising-laden piece that mentioned the brand nowhere in its text.

**The advertising arbitrage mechanism:** The structural architecture of these ads is a well-documented internet monetization scheme known as advertising arbitrage. As Snopes journalist Jordan Liles explained in a dedicated explainer article (Liles, 2022, *Snopes*), advertising arbitrage operates on a simple economic premise: the operator of an article or slideshow expects to earn more revenue from the advertisements displayed across the article's many pages than they spend purchasing initial display advertisements to attract visitors to that article. The incentive to use clickbait headlines is therefore directly financial — the more emotionally provocative and misleading the initial advertisement, the higher the clickthrough rate, and the larger the pool of users scrolling through slides and generating ad impressions. Accuracy is not an incentive in this structure; virality and emotional provocation are.

The Chick-fil-A closure ad fits this profile precisely: it used a high-recognition brand name, paired it with a high-anxiety outcome (business failure/closure), and deployed that combination to drive traffic to a long-form slideshow designed to maximize advertising impressions. The accuracy of the advertisement was irrelevant to the financial incentives that produced it.

**Other false closure variants:** The Snopes investigation noted that similar false closure advertisements ran using photographs of other fast-food chains, with comparable "closing in 2024" framing, during the same period. The pattern was not specific to Chick-fil-A; it was a template applied to high-recognition American fast-food brands generally.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The Chick-fil-A closure claim operates through several documented mechanisms of misinformation uptake that are well-studied in communication research and behavioral economics.

**The cultural salience of brand mortality:** Restaurant and retail closures carry disproportionate cultural weight relative to their objective economic significance. Since approximately 2016, American media coverage of retail and restaurant closures — often framed as the "retail apocalypse" — has created a background cultural narrative of familiar brand extinction that serves as a ready template for misreading new information. Research on media framing effects finds that prior narrative schemas substantially influence how new claims are evaluated: when an existing cultural template says "American chains are closing," a new report that a specific chain is closing requires less cognitive effort to accept than to reject (Scheufele & Tewksbury, 2007, *Journal of Communication*). The false Chick-fil-A ads were inserted into an existing cognitive groove.

**The credibility parasitism of authentic imagery:** The advertisements used genuine photographs of actual Chick-fil-A restaurant locations. This use of authentic imagery is a well-documented technique in digital misinformation: real photographs lend visual credibility to false textual claims, activating the implicit heuristic that "if the image is real, the story is probably real" (Nightingale, Wade, & Watson, 2017, *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*). Even audiences with moderate media literacy who might scrutinize the textual claim may be anchored by the photographic authenticity. In this case, however, reverse image searching — a technique Snopes employed for the related China burger brand investigation — would have revealed that the photographs were simply stock or press images of existing restaurants, not documentation of any closure event.

**The epistemic novelty of corporate-destination mismatch:** There is a distinctive cognitive gap that advertising arbitrage schemes exploit: the complete informational disconnect between the advertisement's implied content and the destination article's actual content. Audiences generally assume a semantic relationship between a headline and the linked article. When that relationship is entirely absent — when the advertisement shows Chick-fil-A closing and the article never mentions Chick-fil-A — the deception operates at a level of boldness that many audiences do not expect and therefore do not account for in their prior credibility discounting. Research on deceptive advertising distinguishes between *misleading* content (which distorts or omits) and *false* content (which invents); advertising arbitrage clickbait frequently crosses into outright fabrication, a degree of dishonesty that exceeds what many audiences extend as the baseline assumption about commercial communication (Richards & Curran, 2002, *Journal of Advertising*). The mismatch between what is advertised and what the click delivers is a feature of the monetization scheme, not an error — and its audacity is itself a form of protection, because audiences who do click through and discover no Chick-fil-A mention are more likely to be confused than to conclude the ad was simply invented.

**Loss aversion and familiar brand attachment:** Chick-fil-A is a brand with unusually high customer loyalty and strong positive affective associations among its regular customers. Behavioral economics research consistently demonstrates that potential losses are weighted more heavily in human decision-making than equivalent potential gains — loss aversion — and that this effect is amplified when the potential loss involves a positively-valenced, personally-familiar object (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979, *Econometrica*). For a Chick-fil-A customer who anticipates the loss of a favored restaurant, even a casually encountered digital ad claiming closure will generate sufficient emotional activation to compel engagement, even in the absence of credibility signals that would normally inhibit clickthrough.

## 4. The Evidence in Full

The following evidentiary record supports the False verdict:

1. **No closure announcement or plan:** Chick-fil-A made no public announcement of plans to close all restaurant locations in 2023 or 2024. No financial news sources, press releases, SEC filings (the company is private), or franchisee communications documented any such plan.

2. **Chick-fil-A's financial performance:** QSR Magazine's April 2023 report documented the company approaching $19 billion in total annual revenue (Klein, 2023), with per-unit sales metrics among the highest in the quick-service industry.

3. **Advertisement content was false:** The specific ads featured photos of actual Chick-fil-A locations juxtaposed with "closing doors" text. The photos were authentic but not evidence of closure; the text was fabricated.

4. **Destination article did not mention Chick-fil-A:** The Reference.com article to which all ads linked — "These 53 Restaurant Chains Are on the Brink of Disappearing Entirely" — was a 2020/2021 piece describing COVID-era restaurant difficulties. It listed approximately 70 businesses across approximately 70 slides. Chick-fil-A appeared nowhere in the article.

5. **Article was at least two years old:** Page source code analysis indicated the article was written circa 2020 and last republished 2021, making its use in December 2023 advertising a deliberate choice to maximize slide count (and page advertisement impressions) from a pre-existing piece.

6. **Advertising arbitrage identification:** The structural characteristics of the ads — emotionally provocative false claim, high-recognition brand image, link to a lengthy multi-slide article served with numerous ad placements — matches the documented pattern of advertising arbitrage schemes that Snopes has investigated and documented across multiple cases (Liles, 2022, *Snopes*).

7. **Multiple false variants:** The same advertising template was applied to other fast-food brands during the same period, confirming the pattern was systematic rather than an isolated error.

8. **Snopes investigator contact with Chick-fil-A:** Jordan Liles contacted Chick-fil-A media relations for comment. The Snopes article notes the inquiry was made and an update would be published upon response.

## 5. What Remains Uncertain

**Whether individual restaurants have future closure plans:** While the claim of chain-wide closure is false, Chick-fil-A, like all restaurant operators, periodically closes individual underperforming locations as part of normal business operations. The false advertisement conflated the normal operational cycle of any large chain (which includes occasional individual closures for lease, performance, or market-entry reasons) with a total business failure — a conflation that is factually inaccurate but may partially rest on awareness that some individual locations do close. The ad provided no evidence relating to any specific location.

**Long-term trajectory of privately-held restaurant franchises:** Chick-fil-A's current financial health does not guarantee its future performance. The quick-service restaurant industry is subject to demographic shifts, health-trend disruptions, competitive pressure, and supply-chain variability. Forecasting that a currently successful chain will inevitably face difficulty is not accuracy, however — it is merely probabilistic reasoning applied to all businesses under uncertainty.

**EARC classification:** Classified A (Assessed/Ambiguous). The claim is false with high confidence — no evidence of closure plans existed at the time of the advertisements, and the company's financial profile was robustly opposite to closure conditions. The "ambiguous" component reflects the inherent inability to fully falsify futures (all businesses can theoretically close) without undermining the plain sense of the false claim as stated.

## 6. Verdict and Further Reading

**Verdict: FALSE.** No component of the claim that Chick-fil-A was closing all restaurant locations in 2024 (or 2023) was accurate. The company had made no such announcement, faced no financial conditions pointing toward such an outcome, and was instead approaching all-time high annual revenues. The advertisements that propagated this claim were advertising arbitrage clickbait — a monetization scheme in which a false, emotionally provocative banner ad drives traffic to a long-form article bearing no relationship to the ad's subject matter, in order to generate advertising revenue from page impressions. The Reference.com article linked from the ads did not mention Chick-fil-A and was written two or more years before the ads ran.
