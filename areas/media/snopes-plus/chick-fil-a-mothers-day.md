## §1 — Claim Anatomy

In May 2016 — and again more prominently in May 2017 — an image circulated on social media purporting to advertise a free Mother's Day lunch from the Chick-fil-A restaurant chain. The image displayed an array of Chick-fil-A menu items and announced a "free Mother's Day lunch" available on 14 May 2017. Despite being visually formatted to resemble promotional material, the image conspicuously lacked any official Chick-fil-A letterhead, brand authentication marks, terms and conditions language, or any of the identifying information typically present in authentic branded promotions.

The claim was structured to exploit multiple psychological and social dynamics. First, it invoked a plausible category of corporate generosity: Chick-fil-A does run legitimate holiday promotions, including its Cow Calendar promotional program that had distributed free food offers tied to specific dates and events. Thus, the category of "Chick-fil-A free food" was well-established in consumer experience, lowering the threshold of skepticism for any specific instance of such a claim.

Second, the claim was timed to a high-emotion occasion. Mother's Day is among the most commercially significant holidays in the United States restaurant industry, and the premise of treating one's mother to a free meal aligned naturally with the sentimental logic of the occasion.

Third, the claim exploited the pattern of viral social sharing: a post advertising free food from a well-known chain is inherently shareable because the person who shares it provides apparent value to their social network. The incentive to share is present even in the absence of any verification; the cost of sharing an inaccurate free food claim is perceived as minimal.

However, the central factual problem with the claim is structural rather than merely evidentiary. Mother's Day 2017 fell on Sunday, 14 May. Chick-fil-A is closed on Sundays at essentially all of its locations — a policy rooted in the religious convictions of its founder, S. Truett Cathy, and maintained as a company-wide standard. A national free meal promotion scheduled for a Sunday at a chain that closes every Sunday is self-defeating by design. This inconsistency should have been immediately apparent to anyone with basic familiarity with the brand.

## §2 — Verification Record

Snopes investigator Arturo Garcia contacted a Chick-fil-A spokesperson to verify whether any such national promotion existed. The spokesperson confirmed that no national free Mother's Day lunch promotion was offered. The viral image was a hoax.

The spokesperson did note one limited exception: a single Chick-fil-A location in Flemington, New Jersey, offered free foods to mothers who visited on 14 May 2017, the day before Mother's Day — on Saturday, the 13th — as a locally initiated gesture. This localised action was not a corporate-level offer and was not connected to the viral promotional image.

Garcia additionally verified the Chick-fil-A Sunday closure policy, which is documented explicitly in company materials and statements by company leadership. S. Truett Cathy, the chain's founder, articulated the Sunday closure as both a religious commitment and a business philosophy. Cathy's own published materials ("A Five-Step Recipe for Business Success," available through his foundation) described the Sunday closure as part of a commitment to prioritising faith and family over commercial activity.

Garcia also noted that the company's director of talent acquisition, Jodee Morgan, provided a slightly different framing of the Sunday policy in a February 2017 interview with Thrillist (Fulton, 2017), characterising it as rooted in "work-life balance" considerations and emphasising that Truett Cathy's decision was "just as much in the practical points of running a business as his beliefs." This reframing did not change the operational reality: virtually all Chick-fil-A locations are closed on Sundays.

The exceptions to the Sunday closure policy are well-documented and highly limited. Chick-fil-A locations in Dallas-area stores opened on Sunday, 27 December 2015, to provide food to first responders and the public following a tornado outbreak that killed 11 people in Texas (Little, 2015, *CNBC*). Orlando area Chick-fil-A employees donated sandwiches and iced tea to police and blood donors following the Pulse nightclub attack in June 2016. These emergency humanitarian exceptions have no bearing on a commercial free food promotion.

The verdict of False was supported by: (1) Chick-fil-A's own denial of any national promotion, (2) the structural impossibility of a Sunday promotion at a chain closed on Sundays, and (3) the image's lack of any official branding authentication.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis

The epistemic novelty of the Chick-fil-A Mother's Day hoax lies in what might be called the plausibility-exploitation mechanism: the hoax was designed to succeed precisely because the genuine promotional record of the brand made a free food offer seem reasonable, while the specific structural constraint that made the claim false (Sunday closure) was exactly the information that the hoax's target audience was least likely to consciously apply.

The plausibility-exploitation mechanism operates in several stages. In the first stage, the claim is calibrated to fall within the believable range of corporate behaviour: Chick-fil-A does promotional food offers, the brand is associated with customer-friendly service, and Mother's Day is a plausible occasion for such an offer. In the second stage, the claim is tied to an occasion that triggers prosocial sharing: telling your social network about free Mother's Day food is presented as an act of generosity on the sharer's behalf. In the third stage, the specific falsifying fact — Sunday closure — is suppressed by the design of the claim's framing, which nowhere mentions or implies the day-of-week dimension.

This structural suppression of falsifying information is an important sophistication in hoax design. A naive analysis would say the hoax is easy to detect because Chick-fil-A is famously closed on Sundays. A more accurate analysis recognises that the Sunday closure fact is unlikely to be spontaneously retrieved while reading a promotional image about free food. Cognitive processing in the context of low-stakes viral sharing does not typically activate domain-specific knowledge retrieval; it operates instead through social proof, emotional resonance, and desire-satisfaction. The hoax is designed to occupy exactly this cognitive register.

The hoax also exploits the asymmetry between brand-recognition and brand-verification. Consumers recognise Chick-fil-A branding, menu items, and typography from decades of exposure. This recognition activates a heuristic of authenticity — "it looks like a real Chick-fil-A promotion" — that is notoriously unreliable as an authentication method. The absence of official letterhead, authentication codes, terms and conditions, and brand management approval are the genuine indicators of promotional authenticity, but these are invisible to consumers who lack the reference frame of how official promotions are produced and presented.

From a broader perspective, this case exemplifies the recurring pattern of restaurant brand impersonation for social media engagement. Restaurant chains with strong loyalty communities, distinctive branding, and established histories of promotional giveaways are particularly attractive targets because the plausibility threshold is lower and the sharing incentive is higher. The cost to the hoaxer is minimal — producing a convincing-looking image requires only modest graphic design capability — while the engagement generated is substantial.

## §4 — Scientific and Empirical Background

The sociological and consumer behaviour dimensions of this case are grounded in well-documented research on prosocial sharing, brand trust, and food promotion susceptibility.

The Mother's Day restaurant market represents one of the highest-volume dining occasions of the annual calendar. In the United States, Mother's Day is consistently ranked among the top restaurant dining days, with the National Restaurant Association annually estimating tens of millions of consumers dining out to celebrate. This commercial significance creates a priming environment in which consumers are actively thinking about restaurant options on and around the holiday, increasing the cognitive availability of restaurant-related claims.

The psychology of prosocial sharing in social media environments has been examined in the context of misinformation spread. Consumers who share content that promises value to their social network — free food, discounts, important health information — do so in part because the sharing action itself confers social benefit to the sharer. The incentive structure does not require the claim to be true; it requires only that the claim appear worth sharing. This dynamic was identified by Pennycook et al. (2021, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*) as a significant driver of misinformation spread: the motivation to share valuable information can outweigh the motivation to verify accuracy.

Brand impersonation in promotional contexts exploits the same cognitive vulnerabilities documented in phishing research. Cialdini's foundational work on influence and persuasion (Cialdini, 2001, *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*) identified the "liking" principle — people are more likely to comply with requests from entities they like — as a major mechanism in consumer decision-making. Chick-fil-A is a brand with exceptionally high consumer loyalty scores; its customers' positive affect toward the brand creates a susceptibility to hoax promotions that would be less effective for brands with lower affinity.

The Sunday closure policy is itself an unusual corporate characteristic with documented commercial implications. Unlike most fast food competitors, which maximise revenue by operating seven days per week, Chick-fil-A's Sunday closure represents a significant voluntary revenue reduction. Research on organisational identity and brand positioning suggests that highly distinctive corporate characteristics — including the Sunday closure — contribute to brand authenticity perception in ways that can paradoxically favour the brand's promotional standing, making fake promotions more believable because the brand is seen as genuine rather than merely commercial.

Fulton's 2017 Thrillist investigation of the Sunday policy ("Not About Jesus: The Truth Behind Chick-Fil-A's Sunday Policy") noted that the policy has become a defining brand narrative — the decision that makes Chick-fil-A "the restaurant that closes on Sundays." This narrative distinctiveness, while commercially positive for the brand under normal conditions, creates an ironic vulnerability in the hoax context: the policy's fame does not translate into active application of the Sunday-closure knowledge when consumers are processing a promotional image at low cognitive engagement levels.

Little (2015, *CNBC*) documented the exceptions to the Sunday closure policy in the context of the Dallas tornado response, providing a useful empirical baseline for understanding the conditions under which exceptions occur: extraordinary humanitarian emergencies, not commercial promotional events.

## §5 — Misinformation Mechanics

The mechanics of the Chick-fil-A Mother's Day hoax are characteristic of a specific genre of promotional fraud: the fake restaurant giveaway. This genre is well-documented in both industry and consumer protection literature, and its operational characteristics are highly consistent across instances.

The genre requires four elements: (1) a recognisable brand with positive consumer affect; (2) a plausible promotional occasion; (3) a realistic-appearing but unofficially produced promotional image; and (4) a social sharing mechanism that transmits the fake offer to wide audiences at low friction.

The Chick-fil-A hoax exhibited all four elements. The brand recognition was among the strongest available in the fast food sector. Mother's Day was an occasion specifically calibrated to the brand's family-oriented identity. The image was sufficiently realistic to avoid immediate visual rejection. And Facebook, where the image circulated initially, provided the frictionless sharing infrastructure.

The recurrence of the hoax across two years — identifiable versions appeared in both May 2016 and May 2017 — illustrates another feature of the genre: seasonal recirculation. A hoax tied to an annually recurring occasion can be reactivated each year with minimal modification. Consumers who did not encounter the image in 2016 may encounter it in 2017; consumers who dismissed it in 2016 and forgot doing so may encounter it again with fresh susceptibility. The seasonal hoax relies on both new audience and audience memory decay.

The absence of any harm to the hoax's producer compounds the enforcement problem. Creating and sharing a fake promotional image is not typically illegal unless specific fraud statutes apply (such as where the fake offer is used to solicit personal information or purchases). For hoax producers whose motivation is engagement metrics, a fake Chick-fil-A free meal post is an extremely efficient content strategy: high social engagement, low production cost, zero material consequences.

For Chick-fil-A the brand, the hoax produces both a customer relations problem and an opportunity cost in goodwill. Consumers who arrive at closed restaurants on the Sunday in question leave disappointed; their disappointment generates negative brand associations that the hoax, not the company, created. The structural solution — Sundays are impossible — is also the structural problem: the policy that makes the hoax demonstrably false is not sufficiently salient to prevent the hoax's spread.

## §6 — Verdict and Takeaways

**Verdict: False.** No national Chick-fil-A free Mother's Day lunch promotion was offered on 14 May 2017 or any adjacent date. The company confirmed this directly to Snopes. The image circulating online was a hoax, lacking official branding, authentication, and any connection to Chick-fil-A's corporate communications. Mother's Day 2017 fell on Sunday, 14 May, and Chick-fil-A is closed on Sundays at virtually all locations — making a national promotion on that specific date structurally impossible. One Flemington, New Jersey location offered a locally organised complimentary gesture the day before Mother's Day, but this was not a corporate promotion and was entirely unconnected to the viral image.

The gap is classified as type **A** (Accuracy): the central claim is categorically false. No such promotion existed; the image was fabricated without authorisation; and the claim's own internal logic was contradicted by one of the most widely known facts about the brand.

The case offers several generalisable lessons. For consumers, the appropriate verification method for any food brand promotion is to consult the brand's own official channels — website, verified social media accounts, or in-store materials — rather than relying on shared social media images. The absence of official letterhead, legal terms, and brand authentication in any promotional image is a reliable indicator of hoax content. For brands targeted by such hoaxes, the case illustrates the importance of maintaining proactive consumer communication about the existence of fake promotions, a practice that requires investment in social listening and rapid-response communications infrastructure.

For researchers studying misinformation mechanics, the case provides a clean example of plausibility-exploitation design: a claim structured to fall within the believable range of corporate behaviour while hiding its falsifying constraint in plain sight, relying on the consumption context — low-engagement scrolling, emotionally primed sharing — to suppress the application of readily available falsifying knowledge.
