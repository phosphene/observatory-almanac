# Is Chick-fil-A Giving Away Its Food Free for a Year?


## 1. The Claim

In January 2020, a series of posts circulated widely on Facebook featuring photographs or screenshots from Chick-fil-A locations promoting a contest in which entrants could win free Chick-fil-A meals for the entire year of 2020. The posts implied an immediate, actionable opportunity: that a person who saw the post could enter the contest and potentially win a year's worth of free food from the fast-casual chicken chain. The claims were shared extensively within what appeared to be the first weeks of the new year, positioned implicitly or explicitly as a timely promotion one might participate in before the year progressed further.

Chick-fil-A is a notably popular and culturally prominent fast-food chain with a devoted consumer base and a strong social media following. Promotional giveaways and contests from the brand regularly generate significant engagement, and the company has a well-documented history of offering free food promotions—most famously its "Cow Appreciation Day" annual events, in which customers dressed in cow costumes receive free meals. This history of genuine promotional generosity made the claim of a free-food-for-a-year contest highly plausible to a broad audience and contributed to the post's virality in January 2020.

Snopes journalist Bethania Palma investigated the claim and published findings on January 23, 2020.


## 2. What's Actually True — and What Is Outdated

The core situation is factually interesting: the promotions described in the social media posts were real, but by the time the posts were generating significant circulation in January 2020, the promotions were over.

**What was true at the time of the original promotions:** Two specific Chick-fil-A franchise locations did run promotions in which customers could enter drawings for the chance to win free Chick-fil-A meals throughout all of 2020. The two locations identified in the circulating posts were located in McCalla, Alabama, and Dalton, Georgia. Both locations invited customers to enter the drawings in December 2019, with winners presumably to be announced in late December 2019 or early January 2020. A Chick-fil-A corporate spokesperson confirmed to Snopes that individual Chick-fil-A locations are locally owned and operated franchises and that franchise owners may hold special promotions limited to their own locations. The promotions were therefore real, locally authorized, and properly conducted.

**What was false or outdated by January 2020:** The entry deadlines for both promotions had closed in December 2019—before the posts were being widely circulated in January 2020. By the time most people encountering the posts were reading them, it was no longer possible to enter either drawing. The promotions were not nationwide and were never applicable at Chick-fil-A locations other than those two specific restaurants. The social media posts, by continuing to circulate after the entry deadlines, created a systematically false impression: that an active, currently participatable opportunity existed when it did not.

The Snopes verdict for this claim is "Outdated"—a rating that Snopes uses specifically for cases where a claim was once accurate but is no longer applicable due to the passage of time or the expiration of the condition being described. This is distinct from "False" (which implies the claim never had a factual basis) and from "True" (which implies current applicability).


## 3. The Epistemology of Expired Promotions on Social Media

The Chick-fil-A promotions case represents one of the most structurally consistent categories of social media misinformation: the recirculated expired offer. Understanding why this pattern persists so reliably requires examining several reinforcing mechanisms.

**The atemporal sharing norm.** Social media platforms, particularly Facebook, have historically presented content in algorithmically sorted feeds that do not necessarily reflect chronological order. This technical architecture systematically conceals the age of content from viewers who haven't adopted the habit of checking posting dates. A post about a contest that was published in December 2019 appears in a January 2020 feed with no visual differentiation from a post published yesterday. The result is that the temporal gap between content creation and content consumption—which in a print newspaper would be communicated by the date on the masthead—is made invisible by the platform's design.

Research on how platform architecture shapes information credibility judgments has documented that users frequently fail to notice or appropriately weight timestamps even when they are available (Pennycook et al., 2019, *Psychological Science*, vol. 30, no. 10, pp. 1470–1480). When timestamps are deemphasized in feed design, as they have been in various iterations of Facebook's interface, this tendency is amplified further.

**The "sharing is caring" norm and promotional content.** Promotional offers—especially free food, travel rewards, or discounts—are culturally coded as shareable good news. Sharing a promotion with friends and family is understood as a prosocial act: you are helping people access something beneficial. This norm creates a strong forward momentum toward sharing promotional content without verification, because the motivation to share is altruistic rather than adversarial. A person sharing a promotion believes they are doing their network a favor, which psychologically reduces the motivation to verify before sharing. The emotional reward structure of prosocial sharing is not conditional on the information being accurate.

This pattern has been documented specifically in consumer promotion contexts. Research on viral marketing found that promotional claims that activate prosocial sharing motivations—particularly those framing the act of sharing as beneficial to the sharer's social network—exhibit higher sharing rates and lower per-sharer verification rates than informational content without the prosocial framing (Dobele et al., 2007, *European Journal of Marketing*, vol. 41, no. 11/12, pp. 1234–1247).

**The franchise model, again.** As with the McDonald's pandemic case discussed in an adjacent Truth Vault entry, the Chick-fil-A promotions case demonstrates the epistemological hazard created by the franchise retail model. Individual franchise locations have the authority to create local promotions that do not apply anywhere else in the chain. When these promotions are social-media documented without their location-specific context, they appear to be national in scope. The Chick-fil-A case adds an additional dimension: even within the franchise model, distinguishing between "a promotion exists at location X" and "a promotion you can still enter at location X" requires a temporal dimension that social media sharing routinely strips away.

**Algorithm-driven resurfacing.** Facebook and similar platforms have historically algorithmically resurfaced popular older content when it generates engagement. A post about a December promotion that attracted significant engagement when it was originally posted in December may be algorithmically surfaced for new audiences in January, precisely because its engagement history marks it as popular content—regardless of whether it remains temporally relevant. This creates a feedback loop in which popular outdated content is systematically privileged for redistribution, not penalized by its age.

**The specificity illusion.** The posts circulating about the Chick-fil-A promotions were not vague; they referenced specific locations (McCalla, Alabama; Dalton, Georgia) and specific terms (free meals for a year of 2020). This specificity creates an air of authenticity. In consumer behavior research, the inclusion of specific peripheral details in claims has been shown to increase perceived credibility, because specificity is associated with direct experience or well-researched knowledge (Bell & Loftus, 1989, *Journal of Consumer Research*, vol. 16, no. 2, pp. 143–154). People reading about a named location with specific terms are cognitively primed to treat the information as verified and current. The very specificity that distinguished these real promotions from false ones also served to make the outdated claims feel more credible than a vague national giveaway claim would have.


## 4. Chick-fil-A's Promotional Culture and Consumer Expectations

Chick-fil-A occupies an unusual position in American fast-food culture that shapes how consumers process promotional information about the brand. The chain has built a reputation for customer service, quality, and community engagement that generates disproportionate consumer loyalty relative to its revenue size. This brand loyalty manifests in multiple ways, including enthusiastic social media sharing of Chick-fil-A-related content and a high baseline trust in information appearing to come from the brand.

The chain's signature "Cow Appreciation Day" promotion—which typically occurs annually in July and has been running since 2000—involves customers dressing in cow costumes or wearing cow-printed accessories to receive free entrees. This promotion is explicitly nationwide in scope, runs annually, and has been reliably delivered over a twenty-year period. This documented history of genuine national free-food promotions creates an elevated baseline prior for Chick-fil-A free food claims compared to the same claim applied to a brand without this history.

Research on brand trust and its interaction with misinformation suggests that high-trust brands may actually be more susceptible to promotional misinformation than lower-trust brands precisely because consumers apply less skepticism to claims consistent with their positive brand associations (Melewar & Jenkins, 2002, *International Journal of Bank Marketing*, vol. 20, no. 3, pp. 92–99). The belief that Chick-fil-A is genuinely generous—grounded in accurate knowledge of its real promotional history—reduces the likelihood that a consumer will question a promotional claim attributed to the brand.

The franchise structure of Chick-fil-A also differs from McDonald's in one important respect relevant to promotional claims: Chick-fil-A exerts relatively centralized brand control over its franchisees compared to McDonald's, requiring significant brand consistency across locations. This means consumers may not intuitively understand that Chick-fil-A allows individual franchise locations to run separate local promotions, because they are accustomed to experiencing Chick-fil-A as a consistent experience across locations. The idea that a "Chick-fil-A" promotion in McCalla, Alabama, is categorically different from the national chain's promotions is not a distinction that the brand's otherwise high uniformity of experience would lead consumers to expect.


## 5. Verification Practices for Promotional Claims

Verifying promotional and contest claims requires a specific set of checks that differ in important ways from verifying factual or news claims.

**Date verification is primary.** Before engaging with any promotional content, identify the original posting date of the content rather than the date it appeared in your feed. Most social media posts display a posting date, though its legibility varies by platform and interface settings. For screenshots circulating without a visible date, reverse image search may help identify when the image first appeared online.

**Expiration date awareness.** Promotional claims have a built-in expiration date in a way that factual claims do not. A fact about history is either true or false independent of when you encounter it. A promotional offer is either currently active or not, and that status changes over time. Processing promotional claims with the same timeless frame used for factual claims is categorically inappropriate and creates systematic error.

**Official channel verification.** For major chains, official brand websites, app notifications, and verified social media accounts are the authoritative source for current promotions. Chick-fil-A maintains an active app with a promotions and offers section; any legitimate current national or regional promotion would be findable there. A promotional claim that cannot be verified through official channels almost certainly is not currently active, even if it was once real.

**Location scope verification.** The Chick-fil-A promotions case illustrates why location specifics matter even when a brand has a strong national presence. A promotion described as occurring at restaurants in McCalla, Alabama, and Dalton, Georgia, is not applicable at a restaurant in Portland, Oregon, regardless of how broadly the posts are shared. Reading promotional claims with attention to location specifics—and treating location specifics as limiting rather than merely decorative—is fundamental to correct interpretation.

**The recirculation check.** Social media engagement metrics can sometimes indicate recirculation of older content. High comment volume with a concentration of dates suggesting multiple waves of engagement, comments noting the information is outdated, or comments from users at geographically diverse locations all suggesting the promotion is not available at their location are signal that the content is being recirculated beyond its original scope and time.


## 6. Summary and Verdict

The claim that Chick-fil-A was giving away free food for a year is rated "Outdated" by Snopes. The underlying facts: Two specific Chick-fil-A franchise locations—in McCalla, Alabama, and Dalton, Georgia—did run local drawings in December 2019 in which entrants could win free Chick-fil-A meals throughout 2020. These promotions were real, authorized by each franchise's local operator, and consistent with Chick-fil-A's policy allowing franchise locations to run location-specific promotions. The error in the circulating claims was temporal and geographic: the entry deadlines for both promotions had closed in December 2019, before the posts began circulating widely in January 2020, and neither promotion was applicable outside its specific restaurant location. No nationwide free Chick-fil-A food promotion was active in January 2020. The case demonstrates how expired promotional content retains false actionability when recirculated on platforms that do not prominently display content age, and how the prosocial norm of sharing good news creates a context in which verification is less likely to occur before forwarding.
