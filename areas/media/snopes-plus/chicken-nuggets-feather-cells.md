# Are Chicken Nuggets Made From Feathers Heading to Store Shelves?


## 1. The Claim

In March 2019, widespread social media circulation of articles about the food-technology company Just, Inc. (formerly Hampton Creek) generated a distinctive pattern of consumer confusion. Reports about Just's development of a lab-grown cultured chicken nugget — a product grown from cells in a bioreactor rather than from slaughtered animals — frequently appeared under headlines containing the phrase "made from feathers," which was technically defensible but practically misleading. The phrase captured one possible cell collection method (harvesting cells from a plucked feather without harming the animal) while implying that the product was in some sense manufactured from feather material — an implication that evoked disgust rather than technological wonder.

Readers contacting Snopes after encountering these reports had two categories of questions: (1) Were these nuggets actually made from feathers in a literal sense, suggesting a product composed of processed feather tissue? And (2) When would these products arrive in supermarkets? The first question involved factual clarification about how cultured meat is produced. The second involved technological forecasting under genuine uncertainty.

The claim's genealogy traced to October 2018 reporting from outlets including Food Beast about Just's development progress. The "made from feathers" framing appears to have originated from Just's own communications about its cell-collection capabilities — the company had publicized a demonstration in which it selected a chicken based on phenotypic quality (clean feathers, healthy comb, nice wattle) and collected cells from a feather for initial culture development. However, Just's communications director Andrew Noyes clarified that the company's first commercially available product would not use the feather-based cell collection method; it would instead use cells obtained through other means including biopsy or cell banks.

The January 2019 ITV Tonight broadcast added a second dimension to the claim's spread: Just CEO Joshua Tetrick had disclosed that a single cultured nugget at that time cost approximately $100 to produce, raising serious questions about commercial viability and realistic near-term availability, regardless of the technical feasibility of the product.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes' Mixture verdict appropriately captures the compound nature of the claim. The true components: lab-grown (cultured) meat products were being developed by multiple companies in 2019; cells can indeed be collected from feathers without harming the animal; and cultured nuggets can in principle be grown from feather-derived cells. The false component: Just's first commercial products were not planned to use the feather collection method. The undetermined component: when these products would reach commercial supermarket availability.

The science of cultured meat production deserves careful explanation because the "made from feathers" framing so thoroughly mischaracterized the relevant biology. Cultured meat production begins with cell collection — obtaining myosatellite cells (muscle stem cells), myoblasts, or other progenitor cells from a donor animal. These cells are then placed in a growth medium containing nutrients, growth factors, and scaffolding materials that support their proliferation and differentiation into muscle tissue, which constitutes meat. The entire product is animal-tissue cells grown outside an animal body — not feather material, regardless of whether feather-extracted cells were used as the initial source.

The feather collection method is one of several possible approaches to obtaining starter cells. Post and colleagues at Maastricht University, writing in *Brain Research Bulletin* (2013), provided the first detailed publication on the requirements for autonomous cell-based meat production and identified multiple potential cell sources including biopsy, embryonic stem cells, and induced pluripotent stem cells. The feather collection method — in which cells are obtained from the follicle at the base of a plucked feather — offers the advantage of being non-invasive: the feather-plucking process causes some momentary discomfort but does not require surgical biopsy. However, feather follicle cells are not as straightforwardly muscle progenitors as satellite cells obtained through biopsy, requiring additional cell-reprogramming steps to produce muscle tissue.

The broader landscape of cultivated meat development in 2019 included multiple companies: Just (then pursuing the chicken nugget), Memphis Meats (now UPSIDE Foods, pursuing cultured beef, chicken, and duck), Mosa Meat (pursuing cultured beef), and BlueNalu (pursuing cultured seafood). The regulatory status of these products was deeply uncertain; both the FDA and USDA had announced a joint regulatory framework in late 2018 but specific pre-market approval pathways remained undefined. The cost challenges were substantial: the $100 price point for a single nugget reflected production costs orders of magnitude above commercial viability thresholds.


## 3. The Epistemic Gap

The epistemic novelty of this case lies at the intersection of science communication, emerging technology forecasting, and the temporal epistemology of fact-checking: the Snopes piece was written at a specific moment in time when certain components of the claim were undetermined (specifically, commercial availability timelines), and those components have since either resolved or shifted. Fact-checks of emerging technology claims have a characteristic perishability that distinguishes them from fact-checks of historical or scientific consensus claims.

In 2019, the "When will this be available?" question was genuinely undetermined. Just CEO Tetrick's optimistic forecasts about end-of-2019 supermarket availability proved incorrect. However, the broader prediction — that cultivated meat would eventually become commercially available — proved correct, at least partially. GOOD Meat (Just's rebrand) received FDA clearance and subsequent USDA approval for commercial sale of its cultured chicken in June 2023, making the United States the second country (after Singapore, which approved GOOD Meat's product for commercial sale in December 2020) to permit the sale of cultivated meat. The product reached limited commercial sales through a partnership with José Andrés' restaurant Minibar in Washington, D.C., and a small number of other venues. Mainstream supermarket availability has not materialized as of 2026, but commercial sale in approved channels has occurred.

This temporal evolution creates an interesting epistemological challenge for the original Snopes classification. The "undetermined" component — when these products would reach store shelves — has partially resolved: they have reached some commercial contexts (specific restaurants) but have not achieved the mass-market supermarket presence implied by the original articles. The fact-check's Mixture verdict remains accurate in its original claims and counter-claims, but the undetermined component now has a more complex partial resolution that the original article could not anticipate.

The "made from feathers" framing deserves more critical examination than Snopes provides. This framing represents a specific type of scientific communication failure: the misleading metonymy, in which one method associated with a technology (feather-based cell collection) becomes the descriptor for the technology as a whole, conveying a false impression of the product's material composition. Bubela et al. (2009), studying science communication practices in genetics research in *Nature Reviews Genetics*, documented how the language used in press releases about novel biotechnologies systematically oversimplifies and distorts the technologies involved, often by using dramatic but misleading descriptors that attach to popular understanding and prove resistant to subsequent correction. The "nuggets made from feathers" framing is a textbook example: it is technically true in a narrow sense (cells from a feather were used in some development demonstrations) while being fundamentally misleading about what the consumer would actually be eating.

The disgust response activated by the "made from feathers" framing also warrants epistemic attention. Rozin et al. (2004), in foundational work on the psychology of disgust and food acceptance published in *Appetite*, established that descriptive claims about food origins — even when physiologically irrelevant — powerfully shape food acceptance. Crucially, disgust-based food rejection is resistant to purely rational persuasion: knowing that feather-cell-derived muscle tissue is nutritionally and texturally identical to conventional chicken nuggets does not necessarily eliminate the disgust response triggered by the feather framing. This psychological mechanism explains why the "made from feathers" framing was particularly effective at generating negative consumer reactions to a technology that, on objective assessment of the final product, differs from conventional products primarily in its production method rather than its material properties.


## 4. EARC Classification

**Mode: C — Consequentialist (Emerging Technology with Temporal Uncertainty)**

The claim warrants a C classification because the central epistemic challenges involve consequentialist reasoning under genuine technological and temporal uncertainty. Unlike fact-checks that address historical events (where the relevant facts are in principle fully determined) or established scientific claims (where confidence is grounded in replicated evidence), this claim addressed the near-term commercial trajectory of an emerging technology. The "undetermined" components of Snopes' Mixture verdict are not undetermined because evidence is lacking but because the future had not yet happened — a fundamentally different epistemological situation.

The C classification captures the forward-looking dimension: evaluating claims about emerging technologies requires reasoning about technological development trajectories, regulatory processes, cost reduction curves, and market conditions that are genuinely uncertain at the time of evaluation. Standard fact-checking frameworks are designed primarily for past-tense claims and struggle with future-tense claims that carry genuine epistemic uncertainty rather than false uncertainty (where the evidence clearly supports one conclusion but has not been examined).

The "speculative-technology-claim" gap category reflects the broader pattern: claims about imminent or near-term consumer availability of emerging technologies reliably generate confusion because they involve genuine uncertainty about technological, regulatory, and economic trajectories that cannot be resolved through evidence available at the time of the claim. The appropriate epistemological response involves explicitly characterizing the uncertainty structure rather than reaching a clean binary verdict, which is why Snopes' Mixture/Undetermined framework is particularly appropriate here.


## 5. Broader Context and Scientific Background

The cultured meat field has advanced significantly from the 2019 baseline the Snopes piece addressed. The cost trajectory of cultured meat production illustrates the scale of the challenge and the genuine progress made in subsequent years. Mosa Meat's 2013 demonstration burger, produced by Post and colleagues at Maastricht University, cost approximately $330,000 to produce a single patty (reported by Zaraska, 2016, in *Meathooked*, Bloomsbury Publishing). By 2019, Just's single nugget could be produced for approximately $100 — a cost reduction of roughly 3,000-fold in six years. The subsequent cost trajectory has continued downward, though has not yet reached commercial parity with conventional chicken production, which ranges from $3–6 per pound of breast meat in the U.S. wholesale market.

The regulatory pathway that remained undefined in 2019 has since been established. The FDA's Center for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition (CFSAN) issued a pre-market consultation framework for human cell-cultured food products, culminating in the FDA's March 2023 no-objection letter for UPSIDE Foods' cultivated chicken and GOOD Meat's application (Tyson, 2023, in *Nature*, reported the regulatory milestone). The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) subsequently issued full commercial approval in June 2023, creating the first regulatory green light for commercial sale in the U.S. This regulatory resolution represents the single most significant development bearing on the "undetermined" component of the original Snopes fact-check.

The cellular agriculture research field has also produced significant scientific literature since 2019. Humbird (2021), in a detailed techno-economic analysis of cultivated meat production published through Open Philanthropy's research grant program as "Scale-Up Economics for Cultured Meat," provided the most rigorous independent cost-reduction modeling available, concluding that achieving cost parity with conventional chicken may require fundamental technological breakthroughs in growth medium costs (particularly the replacement of expensive animal serum with serum-free formulations) and scaffold materials. Specht (2020), synthesizing the field's progress in *Meat and Muscle Biology*, identified the key bottlenecks and prioritized research directions, including growth medium reformulation, bioreactor scale-up, and three-dimensional tissue architecture for whole-muscle applications.

The animal welfare implications of cultivated meat represent a significant dimension that the original consumer-facing articles (and Snopes' fact-check) largely sidestepped. The core consumer benefit of cultured meat, as articulated by its proponents, is the potential elimination of animal slaughter from the meat production system. However, this claim requires careful qualification. Current cultured meat production still requires initial cell donation (though non-lethally), fetal bovine serum (FBS) in growth media for many protocols — which is derived from fetal calf blood and involves animal death — and ongoing cell banking from donor animals. The development of serum-free growth media is thus both a cost-reduction priority and an animal welfare imperative; making claims about cultured meat as a "slaughter-free" technology requires attention to the full production system rather than just the final manufacturing step. Research groups including those led by Mark Post (Maastricht) and David Humbird have published on serum-free formulation development, but as of 2025, commercially viable serum-free production at scale remains a frontier challenge.


## 6. Verdict Summary

Snopes' Mixture verdict remains appropriate for the 2019 claim. The technical assertions — that cells can be collected from feathers, and that lab-grown nuggets can be produced from such cells — were correct. The "made from feathers" framing was misleading in implying that feather material constituted the product rather than serving as one possible cell source. The specific false component — that Just's first commercial products would use feather-derived cells — was correctly identified and refuted by Just's own communications director.

The undetermined component — when these products would reach commercial availability — has partially resolved since 2019. GOOD Meat (Just's successor brand) received FDA and USDA approval for commercial sale in June 2023 and achieved commercial sales in limited restaurant contexts. Mainstream supermarket availability has not materialized, consistent with the continued cost challenges documented in the literature. The story of cultivated meat since 2019 is one of genuine technological, regulatory, and commercial progress — but at a pace considerably slower than the optimistic 2019 timelines suggested.

The "made from feathers" framing is a case study in how technically defensible but contextually misleading language about emerging biotechnologies can generate widespread consumer confusion and disgust responses that may persist even after correction, with potentially lasting implications for public acceptance of a technology that, on its merits, addresses significant animal welfare and environmental concerns.
