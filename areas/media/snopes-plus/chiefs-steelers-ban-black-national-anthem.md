# NFL teams banned the "Black National Anthem" from games


## 1. The Claim

The claim holds that the National Football League, or specific franchise teams within it — most commonly identified as the Kansas City Chiefs and the Pittsburgh Steelers — issued bans prohibiting the performance of "Lift Every Voice and Sing" before games. In some versions the claim attributes the supposed ban to named head coaches, naming Andy Reid of the Chiefs and Mike Tomlin of the Steelers specifically. The claim spread primarily through social media sharing and was positioned as evidence of institutional resistance by NFL franchises to the league's racial justice initiatives.

"Lift Every Voice and Sing" — a hymn whose lyrics were written by James Weldon Johnson in 1900 and set to music by his brother J. Rosamond Johnson, subsequently adopted by the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People as the "Negro National Anthem" in 1919 — has been referred to informally as the "Black National Anthem" in popular discourse since at least the mid-twentieth century. A surge of attention around the song in 2020 followed the NFL's decision to incorporate it into league pre-game programming in response to nationwide protests over racial injustice. The NFL's embrace of the song was itself controversial in some quarters, and it was in this politically charged context that the fabricated ban claim found an audience ready to believe it.

The claim fits into a broader pattern of stories positioning NFL franchises — understood by some audiences as bastions of patriotic traditionalism — as resistant to what critics characterised as politicisation of the sport. The names of Reid and Tomlin in the claim are not incidental: Andy Reid is one of the most decorated coaches in NFL history, with a large fan base and high public visibility. Mike Tomlin, notably, is one of the most prominent African American head coaches in the sport, which gives the claim a particularly pointed internal logic — positioning Tomlin as a Black coach who banned a song of Black civil rights significance would, if true, be a compelling and newsworthy narrative.

The claim re-emerged in multiple waves and in multiple forms. One iteration framed the ban as a protest against the league's broader social justice messaging. Another positioned it as a response to fan complaints about pre-game ceremony duration. None of the versions cited any official statements, team communications, league announcements, or credible reporting. The claim's origins trace to a satirical piece that was not labelled clearly enough to prevent wide secondary sharing that stripped its satirical framing entirely.


## 2. What's Actually True

**"Lift Every Voice and Sing" has been a regular NFL pre-game feature since 2020.** In July 2020, the NFL announced that a recorded performance of "Lift Every Voice and Sing" would be played before every Week 1 game of the 2020 regular season — 16 games across the opening weekend. The decision was part of a multi-component social justice initiative announced by NFL Commissioner Roger Goodell in the immediate aftermath of the murder of George Floyd in Minneapolis on 25 May 2020, which triggered nationwide protests and a significant moment of institutional reckoning across American corporate and cultural organisations.

The NFL's social justice package in 2020 included, in addition to the song integration: a commitment of $250 million over ten years directed to addressing systemic racism; the addition of on-field messages "End Racism" and "It Takes All of Us" painted in end zones; and helmet decals honouring victims of systemic racism and police violence (Jordan, A., 2020, *Journal of Sport and Social Issues*). The song integration was the most visible and most contentious single element of this package, attracting significant commentary both from those who welcomed it and from those who objected to what they characterised as the introduction of a racially segmented symbolic vocabulary into major league sporting ritual.

**The song has continued to appear at major NFL events.** Far from being banned or withdrawn under pressure, "Lift Every Voice and Sing" has remained a presence at high-visibility NFL programming. It was performed at Super Bowl LVIII in February 2024 — by Andra Day, preceding Post Malone's performance of "America the Beautiful" and Reba McEntire's performance of "The Star-Spangled Banner." This three-song pre-game sequence was the most widely watched television event of the year in the United States, with an estimated audience of over 123 million viewers (Nielsen, 2024, cited in Nielsen Media Research quarterly filings). The song's inclusion in the Super Bowl pre-game sequence represents an active institutional embedding, not a practice under review or rollback.

**No ban was announced, issued, or reported.** There is no record in NFL official communications, team press releases, coach press conference transcripts, beat reporter coverage, or sports journalism of any kind of a ban on "Lift Every Voice and Sing" by any NFL franchise. Andy Reid has not made any public statement on the song in any recorded forum. Mike Tomlin, for his part, has spoken publicly about racial justice issues in American society in terms inconsistent with the character implied by the ban claim — Tomlin has specifically addressed questions about the NFL and racial equity in post-game and press conference settings on multiple occasions and has not expressed opposition to the song's performance. Fact-checking organisations including Snopes confirmed the claim as false following investigation.

**The song's history and institutional status.** Understanding the claim requires understanding the song. "Lift Every Voice and Sing" was first performed publicly on 16 February 1900 by 500 schoolchildren in Jacksonville, Florida, at a celebration of Abraham Lincoln's birthday. Johnson's three-stanza text addresses themes of struggle, perseverance, faith, and communal solidarity that resonated deeply with African American communities during the Jim Crow era. The NAACP's designation of the song as the "Negro National Anthem" in 1919 formalised a relationship between the song and African American civic and political expression that had been developing organically since its composition (Ramsey, G.P., 2003, *Race Music: Black Cultures from Bebop to Hip-Hop*, University of California Press). The song was performed prominently during the Civil Rights Movement of the 1950s and 1960s and has retained ceremonial significance in African American institutional and community life across subsequent decades.

The NFL's 2020 decision to include the song thus placed the league in an explicit relationship with this history — a relationship that generated both support from advocates of racial equity in sport and opposition from commentators who framed the inclusion as divisive or inappropriate. It was this contested atmosphere that made the ban claim plausible to audiences already primed by the controversy.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The claim exploits the genuine underlying controversy.** Belief in this fabricated ban derives much of its energy from a real and extensively documented political controversy. The inclusion of "Lift Every Voice and Sing" in NFL pre-game programming generated genuine, substantive public debate when it was announced in 2020. Conservative media commentators criticised the NFL's social justice messaging throughout 2020 and into subsequent seasons, with some calling for the league to return to a single national anthem performance (Collins, R., Forbes, 2023). A subset of the audiences reached by these criticisms was already predisposed to believe that at least some NFL personnel resented the league's social justice posture and were working against it internally. A claim that coaches had banned the song was, for these audiences, emotionally coherent with what they believed to be generally true about the institutional culture.

**The claim targets credible-seeming principals.** The selection of Andy Reid and Mike Tomlin as supposed ban-issuers is not arbitrary. Both coaches have high public profiles. More importantly, the choice of Tomlin — a coach whose racial identity makes the ban claim more narratively distinctive — provides the kind of internal irony that makes a fabricated story feel more like insider journalism. Misinformation researchers including Pennycook, G. and Rand, D.G. (2019, *Cognition*, vol. 188, pp. 39–50) have documented that false claims with internally coherent narrative logic are substantially harder for readers to flag as potentially false than claims that are logically incongruent. The coaches named in this claim gave it a coherent, if morally pointed, internal story.

**Epistemic novelty: The "reversal" signalling function.** Social media research has identified a specific credibility-distortion pattern in political rumours about racial justice topics: stories alleging that the very groups or institutions that publicly support a cause are secretly undermining it carry a particular viral charge. This "reversal narrative" pattern — the Black coach banning the Black anthem, the liberal institution quietly retreating from its progressive commitments — functions differently than simple factual falsity. It operates as a signal of insider knowledge, as if the author has access to information about institutional hypocrisy that official communications conceal. This gives the claim a seductive epistemological quality: it feels like it reveals something hidden rather than fabricates something nonexistent. Nyhan, B. and Reifler, J. (2010, *Political Behavior*, vol. 32, no. 2, pp. 303–330) documented how belief in politically congruent misinformation is reinforced by emotional coherence rather than evidential evaluation, and the reversal pattern provides exactly this kind of affective coherence for audiences on both ends of the political spectrum — for those who want to believe NFL institutions resist social justice messaging, and for those who want to believe progressively branded institutions are performatively hollow.

**Satirical misattribution and secondary sharing.** The specific origin of this claim — a satirical article — illustrates the well-documented problem of satire losing its labelling context as it spreads. Tandoc, E.C., Lim, Z.W., and Ling, R. (2018, *Journalism Studies*, vol. 19, no. 2, pp. 138–154) identified in a systematic literature review that satirical news content — content clearly marked as non-factual at its source — consistently generates real-belief downstream when reshared without the satirical framing. The mechanism is straightforward: a reader encounters a satirical piece on its native platform, where the satirical context may be visible; they share it, possibly with an outraged or amused caption; the next reader down the chain sees the shared content without the original context markers and processes it as a factual claim. By the third or fourth generation of sharing, the satirical origin is invisible to most readers.


## 4. Verdict

**False — fabricated from satirical content, no basis in NFL policy or team communications.** No NFL team, including the Kansas City Chiefs or Pittsburgh Steelers, has issued any ban on the performance of "Lift Every Voice and Sing." Named coaches Andy Reid and Mike Tomlin made no stated or reported prohibition of the song. The claim originated in satirical content, was stripped of its satirical context in secondary sharing, and spread as a factual claim in politically primed audiences already engaged with debates about the NFL's social justice posture. The factual situation is the direct reverse of the claim: the NFL introduced the song in 2020 and has continued to feature it at major events including the Super Bowl. Snopes rated this claim as false after investigation.


## 5. The Wider Picture

This claim illustrates several intersecting dynamics in the contemporary misinformation landscape, each worth examining separately.

**The NFL as a cultural battleground.** The NFL has served as a primary arena for public debate about race, patriotism, and political expression in American professional sport since at least 2016, when quarterback Colin Kaepernick began kneeling during the national anthem to protest police brutality and racial injustice. The controversy over Kaepernick's protest — his eventual departure from the league, the NFL's initial response, and the subsequent reversal of league posture in 2020 — established the NFL as an institution whose relationship with racial justice is treated by wide audiences as a proxy for broader cultural battles. When the NFL introduced "Lift Every Voice and Sing" in 2020, it did so in the context of this already-saturated symbolic environment. The claim that teams banned the song is in one sense a continuation of the Kaepernick controversy by other means: a story about NFL institutions resisting a racially charged symbolic commitment.

**Why "Lift Every Voice and Sing" is a particularly charged target.** The sociological function of the song in American public culture involves complexities that straightforward fact-checking cannot fully address. The description of the song as the "Black National Anthem" — a descriptor widely used in media coverage and public discourse — implies a relationship with the American national anthem that has been contested on both sides. Scholars of music and nationalism including Turino, T. (2008, *Music as Social Life: The Politics of Participation*, University of Chicago Press) have examined how multiple-anthem ceremonies at public events can function as either affirmative pluralism or symbolic fragmentation depending on the interpretive framework listeners bring. This genuine scholarly debate around the symbolic logic of the ceremony — not a debate about the song's artistic or historical value but about the semiotics of multi-anthem ritual — provided intellectual fuel for the political controversy and thus for the false claims that grew within it.

**The coaches involved.** The implicit suggestion that Mike Tomlin — one of the NFL's longest-tenured African American head coaches, who began coaching the Steelers in 2007 and holds the distinction of never having a losing season — would ban a song of African American civic significance asks audiences to make a specific kind of inferential leap about the relationship between a person's racial identity and their institutional positions. This framing is itself a form of political manoeuvre worth flagging: using the name of a prominent Black figure in a claim about suppressing Black cultural expression creates a particular kind of credibility effect and, if believed, would serve particular interpretive agendas. Fact-checkers should be alert to claims that function not only as misinformation but as implicit arguments about the character or motivations of specific named individuals.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes investigated and rated this claim false, with investigation confirming the satirical origin of the claim and the absence of any corroborating NFL team or league communications. The verdict is clear-cut by the standards of claims fact-checking: the assertion is directly falsifiable by checking official NFL and team communications, neither of which contain any ban announcement. This is a case where the evidentiary standard is relatively straightforward — a ban of this kind would require announcements through official league or team channels, and none exist.

The more instructive dimension for media literacy practitioners is the pre-sharing context: the claim circulated primarily in spaces where audiences were already engaged in debate about the NFL's social justice posture. For readers exercising lateral reading — checking whether independent sources corroborate a claim before accepting it — the absence of any reporting from sports beat journalists (who would readily have covered a genuine ban by a named coach) is a strong signal. A story of this character, if true, would have been reported by ESPN, the Associated Press, local sports outlets, and team beat reporters within hours. Its absence from any verified sports journalism is itself near-definitive evidence against the claim.

For social media literacy, this case illustrates the importance of sourcing satirical content before sharing it. The satirical origin means the claim initially had a correct label — "this is a joke" — that was lost in transit. Checking the original source of any viral claim, rather than accepting the framing provided by the person who shared it, remains the single most effective individual practice for reducing the spread of this category of misinformation. Fact-checking organisations can address the claim only after it spreads; the more durable intervention is developing source-checking habits in audiences before sharing occurs.
