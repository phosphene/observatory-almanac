# Many Children's Snacks Contain Petroleum-Based Ingredients

*Series: The Truth Vault — Claim Forensics from the Observatory*

---

## 1. The Claim

In February 2016, a content creator named David Avocado Wolfe — a prominent figure in alternative health circles, self-described "superfoods expert," and commercial spokesman for as-seen-on-TV appliances — published an article titled "Beware: Top 5 Potentially Cancer-Causing Children's Snacks Made from Petroleum-Based Products." The article was subsequently turned into a viral video that circulated widely across platforms, accumulating millions of views and hundreds of thousands of shares. The core claim was adapted and reshared repeatedly through wellness communities and parenting groups over the years that followed.

The claim had a clear, alarming thesis: that popular children's snacks — specifically Pop Tarts, Gummie Bears and similar fruit snacks, and M&M's — contain chemical additives derived from petroleum, and that these additives cause cancer, tumours, and other serious health effects. The argument was structured around specific named chemicals: TBHQ (tert-butylhydroquinone) in Pop Tarts; Red 3 (erythrosine) in fruit snacks; Blue 1 (Brilliant Blue FCF) and Blue 2 (Indigo Carmine) in M&M's; Yellow 5 (tartrazine) in various products; and Yellow 6 (Sunset Yellow) in M&M's and similar candies. Each chemical was described as "petroleum-based" and linked to a specific health threat — cancer, tumours, organ damage, behavioural problems, or neurological effects.

The claim was designed for maximum parental impact: these are products marketed specifically to children, purchased routinely in school lunchboxes and on Halloween, and trusted as mainstream treats. The implication that they were quietly poisoning children with cancer-causing petroleum chemicals struck a deep parental anxiety nerve.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rated this claim **Mostly False**, and the rating is well-earned. But the analysis requires working through each component, because the claim's falsehood is not total — it is constructed through selective truth, decontextualised science, and category manipulation.

**The "petroleum-based" framing is simultaneously true and misleading.** The compounds Wolfe identified are, in a technical chemistry sense, petrochemical derivatives: their synthesis uses organic precursor chemicals that are typically extracted from oil or natural gas refining. These basic organic building blocks — compounds like benzene, ethylene, and similar small molecules — are the feedstock for most synthetic organic chemistry, including a vast range of pharmaceuticals, plastics, food additives, cosmetics, and agricultural chemicals. By Wolfe's definition of "petroleum-based," virtually every organic synthetic compound a person encounters regularly qualifies. Nylon is petroleum-based. Aspirin's precursor chemistry involves petroleum derivatives. This framing is technically defensible but analytically useless — it treats a relationship to petrochemical building blocks as though it were a meaningful safety indicator, which it is not.

**TBHQ is in Pop Tarts; its safety is contested at high doses, settled at food-grade doses.** Tert-butylhydroquinone is an antioxidant preservative that prevents fats and oils in processed foods from going rancid. It is present in Pop Tarts and many other baked goods. At very high doses in animal studies, TBHQ has been associated with tumours, vision loss, liver enlargement, convulsions, and paralysis. These findings are real — the National Institutes of Health Toxicology Data Network lists them. However, the totality of the research presents a significantly more complex picture: many other studies have found no such effects, and the harmful doses in animal studies are vastly higher than any amount achievable through food consumption within legal additive limits. The European Food Safety Authority conducted a comprehensive review in 2004 and concluded that TBHQ is "not carcinogenic and that further genotoxicity studies are unnecessary." Both the FDA and EFSA approve TBHQ as safe for human consumption at permitted concentrations.

**Red 3 in fruit snacks: the EU ban is mischaracterised.** Red 3 (erythrosine) is a synthetic red dye used in maraschino cherries, fruit cocktail, and some candy products. The claim that it is "banned in Europe" is technically true but severely misleading. The EU banned Red 3 specifically as a hair dye ingredient — not as a food colourant — and the ban resulted from a paperwork failure: the required safety dossier for its use as a hair dye was not submitted under the European Hair Dye strategy assessment process. This is an administrative categorisation outcome, not a scientific safety determination. Both the FDA and EFSA regard Red 3 as non-carcinogenic and safe at food-grade levels. The rat tumour studies that suggested thyroid cancer links in the original research were later determined to reflect a rat-specific endocrine mechanism not applicable to humans.

**Blue 1 (Brilliant Blue) is not linked to tumour production.** Numerous independent studies have failed to find a connection between Blue 1 and cancer or tumour development. Both the FDA and EFSA consider it safe at legally permitted levels. The claim that it "triggers the development of malignant tumors" is rated false by Snopes because the scientific literature does not support it at consumption levels achievable through food.

**Blue 2 (Indigo Carmine) has a single anomalous rat study.** One study found a statistically significant increase in tumour incidence in male rats exposed to indigo carmine. The significance of a single study in a single animal model, using doses not achievable through human food consumption, should be understood in the context of the overall body of research: both the FDA and EFSA review that evidence and find it insufficient to characterise Blue 2 as carcinogenic to humans at permitted food levels.

**Yellow 5 and behavioural concerns.** Yellow 5 (tartrazine) sits in a different category from the others because the concern about it — hyperactivity in children — has more scientific substance than the cancer claims, though it is still contested. A 2007 study in The Lancet found a modest association between a mixture of food dyes (including Yellow 5 and Yellow 6) and sodium benzoate with increased hyperactivity in children. The UK Food Standards Agency recommended phasing out six specific dyes following the study; the EU required warning labels. The FDA conducted its own review and concluded that the evidence did not demonstrate a causal link strong enough to require action. The hyperactivity concern is the food dye claim with the most scientific support — but it is still not a cancer claim, and even the most cautious regulatory responses treated it as a behavioural precaution rather than a carcinogenicity ruling.

**Yellow 6 and the adrenal tumour findings.** Yellow 6 (Sunset Yellow) has been associated with adrenal and kidney tumour incidence in male rats in some studies. Regulatory bodies in both the US and EU have reviewed this evidence and concluded that the mechanism is rodent-specific and not applicable to human physiology at food-grade exposure levels. Both the FDA and EFSA approve Yellow 6 within established daily intake limits.

The bottom line: these chemicals exist in these products. Some of them have been associated with health effects in high-dose animal studies. The harm projections are not directly applicable to human consumption at food-grade concentrations. Regulatory bodies have reviewed the available evidence and approved these additives within specific limits. Wolfe's presentation selects the most alarming findings from the research record and strips out the dose context, comparative evidence, and regulatory conclusions that would accurately characterise the state of the science.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The "toxic snacks for children" claim format is one of the most persistent and emotionally effective structures in food misinformation, and understanding why it works requires looking at several factors simultaneously.

**Children are the most powerful emotional accelerant.** Claims structured around harm to children bypass critical evaluation in predictable ways. Evolutionary psychology aside, the material stakes for parents are high — getting this wrong, if the claims were true, would be catastrophic. When uncertainty exists (and some genuine uncertainty about additive safety does exist), the precautionary response is to take the alarming framing seriously. Wolfe's framing was explicitly structured around parental fear: these are children's snacks, products given to kids at school and parties, under brand names parents trust.

**"Petroleum-based" is a powerful categorical insult.** The phrase performs a category violation — taking something understood as safe (food) and contaminating it with something understood as dangerous (petroleum). Even though the actual petrochemical lineage of these compounds is chemically mundane and largely irrelevant to safety, the label sticks because it triggers a disgust response. Disgust psychology is fast and resistant to correction by slow analytical thinking. Knowing that "petroleum-based" means the same thing as "made using standard synthetic organic chemistry methods" requires more cognitive work than the initial disgust reaction demands.

**David Avocado Wolfe's platform was large and credible in specific communities.** Wolfe had cultivated a large following in alternative health circles partly through genuinely compelling content about nutrition and superfoods, and partly through a persona that positioned him as speaking uncomfortable truths the mainstream food industry didn't want people to know. His credibility within that community was sufficient for his claims to circulate without significant friction for years after the initial 2016 publication. The post did not require a compelling argument to spread widely — it had a trusted-within-community source and a resonant emotional frame.

**Regulatory approval is not a credibility anchor for this audience.** A key feature of the content that makes Wolfe's claims effective within its target audience is that it anticipates and pre-empts the "regulators approved it" rebuttal. For communities organised around the premise that regulatory agencies are captured by industry interests, FDA approval is a liability, not a defence. The implicit argument — that the FDA saying something is safe means the FDA has been paid to say it — is unfalsifiable within its own framework, meaning that counter-evidence of the form "regulators reviewed this and found it safe" actually reinforces rather than rebuts the claim.

**The claim is perversely difficult to falsify for non-specialists.** To evaluate Wolfe's claims accurately, a reader would need to understand the difference between a study population's dose-response curve and human food-grade exposures, be able to evaluate whether a single animal study is sufficient to establish a safety risk, understand the EU's food dye approval process and the distinction between cosmetic and food regulatory domains, and be familiar with the EFSA review process. None of this is trivial. Fact-checkers who engage with the claim have difficulty conveying the density of evidence in a format as viscerally convincing as "cancer-causing petroleum chemicals in your child's snacks."

## 4. Verdict

Snopes rates this **Mostly False**, and the Observatory endorses that rating with some additional precision.

**The true component:** Several named chemicals in named products are technically derived from petrochemical precursors. Some of these chemicals have been associated with adverse health effects in high-dose animal studies. Yellow 5 and Yellow 6 have been associated with modest hyperactivity effects in one well-regarded human study. All of these compounds are present in the products named.

**The false component:** At the doses found in commercially sold food products within legal additive limits, none of these compounds have been shown to cause cancer in humans. The cancer claims rest on selective citation of high-dose animal studies without disclosing that the doses are non-analogous to human consumption, or that other studies found no effect, or that regulatory bodies reviewing the full evidence body have reached contrary conclusions. The EU cosmetic ban on Red 3 is presented as a food safety determination when it was administrative in nature. Blue 1's cancer link is simply not supported by the scientific literature. The "petroleum-based" framing is technically defensible but analytically misleading, treating petrochemical derivation as a safety-relevant property when it is not.

**EARC assessment:** This is a classic distorted-but-grounded claim in the Evaluated, not formally fact-checked mode: the claim was not ignored by fact-checkers and has a clear Snopes verdict, but the EARC mode reflects that our analysis goes beyond the binary verdict.

The most important analytical point is not whether any single chemical is dangerous, but what the epistemic method of the original claim does. Wolfe's post models a procedure that will generate alarming food safety findings about almost any synthetic food additive: identify a petrochemical precursor, find the highest-dose animal study showing adverse effects, report the findings stripped of dose context, and append the most alarming available mechanism (cancer, neurological damage, organ failure). Applied consistently, this method would condemn the entire modern food supply — because at sufficient doses, essentially every carbon-containing compound has dose-dependent toxicity. The method confuses existence of harm potential with proof of harm at actual exposures.

## 5. The Wider Picture

While the viral claim is mostly false, it exists in a context where legitimate food additive concerns do warrant ongoing attention, and disentangling the genuine policy questions from the misleading packaging matters.

**Artificial food dyes and children's behaviour: the real debate.** The most durable concern in this space is not cancer but hyperactivity. The 2007 McCann et al. study in The Lancet — which found an association between a combination of six food dyes plus sodium benzoate and hyperactivity in children — is the strongest piece of evidence in this literature. The study's methodological quality was sufficient for the UK's Food Standards Agency to recommend voluntary phase-out of those specific dyes and for the EU to require warning labels. The FDA's decision not to act remains controversial and has been revisited multiple times in subsequent years. The hyperactivity concern is real enough to have attracted serious regulatory attention in multiple jurisdictions, even if the cancer claim has not.

**The "rainbow of synthetic dyes" problem.** Much public concern about food dye safety has focused on the visual proliferation of artificial colours in children's food specifically — breakfast cereals, snacks, candy, and drinks marketed to children often contain multiple synthetic dyes in combination. The cumulative exposure picture, and whether combination effects are well-characterised, is a genuine gap in the research. Individual dye assessments may not capture what children experience when they eat multiple dye-containing products daily over years. This is not a conspiracy; it is a standard challenge in mixture toxicology that regulators acknowledge.

**The "generally recognised as safe" problem.** Just as the BVO story illustrates the FDA's "interim" category problem, the food dye story illustrates a parallel challenge with GRAS designations. Many synthetic food additives were approved under safety standards developed in earlier decades using testing methods that were less sophisticated than current tools. The question of whether legacy approvals should be systematically re-evaluated using contemporary risk assessment methods is a genuine one — and the answer of "not a public health priority at this time" that the FDA has historically given reflects resource constraints and regulatory inertia rather than confident safety reassurance.

**The precautionary principle debate.** The divergence between US and EU regulatory approaches to food additives is not simply a question of one system having better science. It reflects different interpretive frameworks: the EU's precautionary principle tilts toward caution when risk is uncertain; the FDA's approach tilts toward requiring demonstrated harm before restricting. Both frameworks have trade-offs. The EU approach may over-restrict compounds that are ultimately safe; the FDA approach may under-protect against compounds whose harms are real but difficult to establish definitively at population scale. Neither framework is obviously superior, and the debate between them is not resolved by pointing out that specific compounds are "banned in Europe."

**Natural versus synthetic dyes: the substitution question.** As public pressure has led some manufacturers to reformulate with natural colourants — beet juice, turmeric, annatto — the question of whether natural substitutes are demonstrably safer has become practically important. Some natural dyes have their own allergy and safety profiles. Annatto has been associated with hypersensitivity reactions in susceptible individuals. The automatic association of "natural" with "safer" is not well supported for food colourants any more than for other food ingredients. The clean label movement's insistence on natural dyes has been responsive to consumer preference but has not been driven primarily by comparative safety evidence.

**Wolfe as a representative figure.** David Avocado Wolfe is notable not merely as an individual misinformer but as a representative of a content archetype that became commercially powerful in the 2010s wellness economy. His brand combined genuine passion for certain aspects of health optimisation with scientific illiteracy and commercial interests in alternative products. He has described gravity as a toxin, mushrooms as intelligent organisms that communicate through the internet, and a range of health conditions as treatable with specific superfoods. His credibility within alternative health communities was not despite these claims but partly because of them — the more extreme the claim, the more effectively it positioned him as outside the captured mainstream. Understanding how this credibility architecture works is more important for media literacy than cataloguing any specific false claim he made.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's analysis of Wolfe's children's snacks claim is thorough within the fact-checking format. The site works through the specific claims claim by claim — TBHQ, Red 3, Blue 1, Blue 2 — assessing each individually with specific sub-ratings (Unproven, Mostly False, False) before arriving at an overall verdict of Mostly False.

This taxonomic approach is one of fact-checking's genuine strengths: it prevents a single accurate component from laundering an overall false conclusion, and it prevents a single false component from invalidating a claim with real substance. The acknowledgement that TBHQ has indeed been associated with adverse effects in some studies, alongside the conclusion that these effects are not applicable at food-grade exposures, is more honest than either "this compound is totally safe" or "this compound is dangerous."

What Snopes does not always do — and this is a structural limitation of the format rather than a specific failing in this case — is address the epistemic method of the original claim. Identifying that each component claim is mostly false or unproven does not convey why the construction of the claim is systematically misleading: that it reads back from alarming findings rather than reading forward from normal exposures, and that applied consistently it would condemn essentially any synthetic food additive. This meta-level critique is what would actually equip readers to assess future claims of this type rather than just this specific one.

The fact-checking literature on Wolfe specifically is instructive. Multiple organisations — Snopes, Science Feedback, Politifact — have repeatedly fact-checked his claims over the years. The pattern of corrections has had limited effect on his audience reach within natural health communities. This is the recidivism problem in food safety misinformation: the same figures, the same formats, and the same underlying claims resurface on new platforms with new packaging. A fact-check that says "Wolfe's cancer snacks claim is mostly false" does not address why the audience is receptive to Wolfe's authority, or what emotional and structural needs his content meets, or why the institutional voices that rebut him have less credibility in that community than he does.

For media literacy, the most actionable insight from this case is the dose question: any claim that a food additive is harmful should prompt the question "at what dose, and how does that compare to what legally permitted use levels allow?" If those numbers are not in the claim, the claim is incomplete at best and misleading at worst. This is a simple epistemic standard that excludes a great deal of food safety virality without requiring any specialist knowledge — just numeracy and the habit of asking for the comparison.

The children's snack story is ultimately not about chemistry. It is about how fear, institutional distrust, and compelling content architecture produce widespread false belief. The chemistry is just the vehicle.
