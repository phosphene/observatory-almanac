# Did a Chinese Company Buy America's 'Most Famous Burger Brand'?

## 1. The Claim

In December 2023, multiple display advertisements appeared before users on YouTube and other websites asserting that "America's Most Famous Burger Brand Is Now Chinese Owned." The advertisements were visually anchored by large, appetizing photographs of cheeseburgers presented in what appeared to be a well-lit restaurant setting. The implicit logic of the advertisement was unmistakable: a quintessentially American cultural institution — the hamburger chain — had been acquired by China, a claim carrying distinct nationalist-economic overtones about control over American consumer culture and food infrastructure.

The advertisements appeared in various design layouts; some showed a single large cheeseburger photograph, others showed multiple burger images. All variants directed users who clicked through to a lengthy article that purportedly substantiated the claim.

Snopes journalist Jordan Liles investigated the advertisements in December 2023. His investigation applied a reverse-image search to trace the provenance of the burger photographs and examined the destination articles to determine whether any U.S. burger chain acquisition by a Chinese company was documented therein.

The claim, as stated, was false. None of the top American burger chains — by any reasonable definition of "most famous," whether by sales volume, brand recognition, or international presence — was owned by a Chinese company at the time the ads ran. The destination article made no such claim and did not mention any Chinese acquisition of an American burger brand.

## 2. What's Actually True

**The provenance of the burger images:** Snopes journalist Jordan Liles performed reverse-image searches on the principal burger photograph used in at least one version of the advertisement. The traced source was a user post on Pinterest, which in turn linked to the food photography blog friesandsalt.tumblr.com — where the photo was identified as taken inside a Five Guys restaurant. Five Guys is headquartered in Falls Church, Virginia (commonly cited as headquartered more broadly in Virginia), is not publicly traded, and had no Chinese ownership stake at the time of the advertisement. A separate burger photograph used in a variant of the ad appeared to have been taken in Cyprus, based on a TripAdvisor user post associated with the image — a restaurant called El Clasico Sports Pub in Nicosia. Neither photograph had any relationship to Chinese business ownership.

**The destination article:** All variants of the Chinese burger brand advertisement directed clicking users to a lengthy article hosted on either Investing.com or StreetInsider.com. The article's headline was "American Companies That Are No Longer American." It was an extensive enterprise piece, comprising nearly 200 businesses and close to 400 paragraphs. Its subject matter was American companies that had been acquired by or substantially owned by foreign entities of any nationality — not specifically China, and not specifically burger chains. Nowhere in the article did the author discuss any U.S. burger brand being purchased by a Chinese company. The visual and textual framing of the advertisements bore no relationship to the article's content: the photographs were of burgers from restaurants with no Chinese ownership; the article described a broad universe of foreign-acquired American companies without specifically mentioning any burger brand acquisition by China.

**America's top burger chains by sales:** Determining which brand constitutes "America's most famous burger brand" is somewhat subjective by cultural measure but more tractable by revenue and sales volume. QSR Magazine, Restaurant Business, and Yahoo Finance independently published analyses of top American fast-food burger chains. All three sources ranked McDonald's first by U.S. system sales, followed by Wendy's in second and Burger King in third (Ahmed, 2023, *Yahoo Finance*; various, 2023, *QSR Magazine*; various, 2023, *Restaurant Business*). The ownership status of each:

- **McDonald's**: Headquartered at 110 N. Carpenter Street, Chicago, Illinois. Traded on the NYSE under ticker MCD. McDonald's Corporation is a publicly traded American company with diffuse global institutional ownership. No Chinese company holds a controlling or majority stake.

- **Wendy's**: Headquartered in Dublin, Ohio. The Wendy's Company is publicly traded on the NASDAQ under ticker WEN. No Chinese company holds a controlling stake.

- **Burger King**: Owned by Restaurant Brands International (RBI), a Canadian multinational corporation headquartered in Toronto, Ontario, Canada. RBI also owns Tim Hortons and Popeyes Louisiana Kitchen. The primary private equity stakeholder in RBI is 3G Capital, a Brazilian-American investment firm, not a Chinese company. RBI shares trade on the NYSE and TSX.

None of these companies had been acquired by a Chinese company or entity. No other major U.S. burger chain — Five Guys, In-N-Out Burger, Shake Shack, Whataburger, or Culver's — was Chinese-owned at the time.

**Advertising arbitrage structure:** As with the concurrent Chick-fil-A closure advertisement campaign, the Chinese burger brand ads follow a documented advertising arbitrage pattern. The financial incentive is to generate ad-impression revenue from users scrolling through a long article, which is funded by a comparatively cheaper initial clickbait advertisement. The emotionally provocative framing — specifically the nationalist claim of Chinese acquisition of an American icon — maximizes clickthrough rates by activating anxiety around foreign economic influence on American institutions, a topic of heightened emotional salience in the political environment of late 2023. The accuracy of the claim is structurally irrelevant to the scheme's financial incentives.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The Chinese burger brand claim is a specifically calibrated instance of a broader category of foreign-ownership anxiety narratives that have documented potency in contemporary American information ecosystems.

**The politics of corporate nationality in an era of economic nationalism:** American political discourse from approximately 2015 onward increasingly framed Chinese economic activity in the United States — acquisitions of American companies, investment in agricultural land, technology transfer — as a national security concern and a form of cultural-economic threat. Legislative activity including CFIUS (Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States) reforms, restrictions on Chinese telecommunications equipment, and debates over TikTok's ownership status all contributed to a background information environment in which "a Chinese company now owns [American thing]" was a plausible and emotionally activated claim template for large segments of the American public. This claim template does not require identification of a specific company or documented transaction to feel credible to audiences already primed to expect Chinese economic acquisition of American assets.

Research on politically motivated reasoning finds that individuals are substantially more likely to accept claims that align with their prior political beliefs without scrutiny, and substantially more likely to subject contrary claims to careful evaluation — a phenomenon known as motivated cognition or directionally motivated reasoning (Kunda, 1990, *Psychological Bulletin*). For audiences predisposed to concern about Chinese economic influence, a claim that a major American burger brand is now Chinese-owned will bypass the evidentiary threshold that might otherwise prompt verification.

**The epistemic novelty of the partially-true foreign-acquisition landscape:** China-backed entities and Chinese companies have made documented acquisitions of American food industry assets in the past decade. Most prominently, WH Group (formerly Shuanghui International, a Chinese company) acquired Smithfield Foods — the world's largest pork processor and U.S. pork supplier — in 2013 for approximately $4.7 billion, a transaction that generated substantial American media coverage and regulatory scrutiny, ultimately approved by CFIUS despite objections. Prior to that, Chinese entities had acquired other American food-adjacent businesses. The real phenomenon of Chinese investment in American food companies provides the vague epistemic scaffolding on which false specific claims can plausibly rest. When a viral ad claims "a Chinese company bought America's most famous burger brand," an audience member who recalls the Smithfield acquisition has an evidential anchor that makes the claim feel more specific-plausible than it would be in total ignorance of China-US food industry transactions.

**The five-guys identification gap as an epistemic failure case:** The burger photograph used in a major version of the advertisement was traceable to a Five Guys restaurant. Five Guys is a widely recognized American fast-food chain that opened in 1986 in Arlington, Virginia, and has expanded to over 1,700 locations globally while remaining privately held by the Murrell family. It is not, by most measures, "America's most famous burger brand" — a position that would generally be assigned to McDonald's by sales volume. A viewer who clicked through the advertisement and independently examined the burger photograph would — upon tracing it to Five Guys — not only fail to find evidence of Chinese ownership (because none exists) but would also note that the photograph was not of any of the top-volume burger chains. This cognitive path requires a reverse-image search competency that is not uniformly distributed among the digital-ad viewing public. The advertisement exploited the gap between what the image actually depicted and what audiences were likely to infer from the advertisement context.

## 4. The Evidence in Full

The following evidentiary record supports the False verdict:

1. **Burger image provenance traced:** Reverse-image search identified the principal burger photograph used in at least one ad version as an image taken inside a Five Guys restaurant in Virginia. Five Guys has no Chinese ownership. A second burger image appeared to depict a Cyprus restaurant (El Clasico Sports Pub, Nicosia).

2. **Destination article confirms false premise:** The article linked from all ad variants — "American Companies That Are No Longer American," hosted on Investing.com or StreetInsider.com — is approximately 400 paragraphs long and covers nearly 200 companies. Nowhere in the article is any American burger brand described as having been acquired by a Chinese company (Liles, 2023, *Snopes*).

3. **McDonald's ownership status confirmed:** McDonald's Corporation, the top U.S. burger chain by sales, is headquartered in Chicago, Illinois and trades on the NYSE. No Chinese entity holds a controlling stake (McDonald's Careers: Corporate Headquarters information, confirmed in Snopes investigation).

4. **Wendy's ownership status confirmed:** The Wendy's Company is headquartered in Dublin, Ohio, and is publicly traded on NASDAQ with no controlling Chinese ownership stake (confirmed via Wendy's corporate information).

5. **Burger King ownership status confirmed:** Burger King is owned by Restaurant Brands International, a Canadian company. RBI's principal private-equity backer is the Brazilian-American 3G Capital. No Chinese company holds a controlling interest (Restaurant Brands International public corporate filings).

6. **U.S. burger chain rankings cited:** Three independent industry publications — QSR Magazine, Restaurant Business, and Yahoo Finance — documented the same top-three ranking (McDonald's, Wendy's, Burger King) for U.S. burger chains in 2023.

7. **Advertising arbitrage identification:** The ads' structure — provocative false claim, clickthrough to unrelated massive article, absent substantiation — matches the advertising arbitrage pattern documented by Snopes across multiple cases.

8. **No mainstream news coverage confirmed acquisition:** A factual acquisition of "America's most famous burger brand" by a Chinese company would have generated substantial mainstream financial and political press coverage, triggering CFIUS review and Congressional scrutiny. No such coverage existed.

## 5. What Remains Uncertain

**Definitional ambiguity of "most famous":** The ads' claim identifies no specific burger brand, using only the comparative "most famous," which is inherently subjective. While sales volume clearly points to McDonald's as the front-runner by most measurable criteria, "fame" could theoretically be assigned to any number of brands by different metrics (cultural recognition, number of international markets, menu items recognized globally, etc.). The claim's deliberate vagueness is a feature of its deceptive design: it is formally unfalsifiable unless one accepts "most famous = highest U.S. sales" as the operationalization. The investigation's conclusion — that none of the leading candidates are Chinese-owned — addresses this ambiguity comprehensively.

**Future foreign acquisitions in the fast-food industry:** The U.S. fast-food industry is subject to mergers, acquisitions, and private equity activity on an ongoing basis. The current absence of Chinese ownership of major American burger chains does not constitute a permanent structural barrier; foreign acquisitions of American restaurant brands have occurred (most notably Chinese-backed Yum China, which operates KFC and Pizza Hut in China under a license structure but does not own the American parent companies). However, no such acquisition had occurred at the time the advertisements ran, making the claim false in the present tense it employed.

**EARC classification:** Classified A (Assessed/Ambiguous). The claim is false: no Chinese company had purchased any leading American burger brand at the time the ads ran, and the destination content did not support the claim. The "ambiguous" component reflects the ad's deliberate definitional vagueness about which burger brand is "most famous." Full confidence in the False verdict is warranted for all major candidates by any reasonable criterion.

## 6. Verdict and Further Reading

**Verdict: FALSE.** No Chinese company had purchased any version of "America's most famous burger brand" at the time the advertisements ran in December 2023. The top three American burger chains by sales — McDonald's, Wendy's, and Burger King — were headquartered in the United States and Canada respectively, with no controlling Chinese ownership. The burger photographs used in the ads were traceable to a Five Guys restaurant in Virginia and to a restaurant in Cyprus, neither with Chinese ownership. The destination article linked from the ads — over 400 paragraphs about American companies with foreign ownership — made no mention of any American burger brand being Chinese-owned. The advertisements were advertising arbitrage clickbait, exploiting political anxieties about Chinese economic influence on American institutions to drive scroll-and-click engagement with a financially motivated but informationally fraudulent advertisement.
