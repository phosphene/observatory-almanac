# "Cracking Your Back / Going to a Chiropractor Fixes Spinal Problems"


## 1. The Claim

The belief that chiropractic care, particularly spinal manipulation or "back cracking," can fix spinal problems and improve overall health represents one of the most widespread alternative medicine practices in the United States. This claim encompasses both specific assertions about treating musculoskeletal conditions and broader claims about chiropractic's ability to address systemic health issues through spinal adjustment. With over 70,000 licensed chiropractors in the U.S. and millions of Americans seeking chiropractic treatment annually, the practice occupies a significant position in the healthcare landscape.


## 2. What's Actually True

The chiropractic claim requires careful disaggregation into multiple components: the efficacy of spinal manipulation as a physical therapy technique, the validity of chiropractic theory regarding "subluxation" and nerve interference, and the scope of conditions appropriately treated through spinal adjustment. These elements have vastly different levels of scientific support and must be evaluated separately to avoid conflating legitimate techniques with unsupported theoretical frameworks.


The verdict emerges from a careful distinction between empirical evidence for specific interventions and the theoretical frameworks used to justify those interventions. The analysis recognizes that effective techniques can emerge from incorrect theoretical foundations, requiring separate evaluation of practice and theory.


## 3. Why People Believe This

This analysis draws from systematic reviews and meta-analyses of chiropractic interventions, clinical practice guidelines from major medical organizations, peer-reviewed research on spinal manipulation techniques, and critical evaluations of chiropractic theory. Particular attention is given to evidence hierarchies, with randomized controlled trials and systematic reviews weighted more heavily than observational studies and expert opinion.

The claim that chiropractic care fixes spinal problems contains important elements of truth alongside significant overstatement and theoretical error. Spinal manipulation, the core technical intervention of chiropractic practice, has moderate-quality evidence supporting its use for acute low back pain and limited evidence for certain other musculoskeletal conditions. For these specific applications, chiropractic treatment can provide clinically meaningful though modest improvements in pain and function.

However, the theoretical foundations of traditional chiropractic—particularly the concepts of subluxation, innate intelligence, and the relationship between spinal alignment and systemic health—lack scientific support. The beneficial effects of chiropractic care appear to result from the mechanical and neurophysiological effects of spinal manipulation rather than the correction of theoretical "subluxations."

The scope of conditions appropriately treated through chiropractic care is narrower than commonly promoted. While evidence supports spinal manipulation for certain musculoskeletal problems, claims about treating non-musculoskeletal conditions like asthma, immune disorders, or childhood developmental issues are not supported by reliable evidence.

The safety profile of chiropractic care varies by technique and anatomical region. Lumbar spine manipulation appears relatively safe when performed by trained practitioners, while cervical manipulation carries higher risks that may outweigh potential benefits for many patients.

The professional heterogeneity within chiropractic complicates evaluation—evidence-based practitioners who focus on musculoskeletal conditions and integrate techniques from physical therapy represent a different approach than traditional chiropractors who emphasize subluxation theory and broader health claims.

From a healthcare policy perspective, chiropractic care appears to have legitimate but limited applications. Integration into evidence-based treatment protocols for specific musculoskeletal conditions is supported by research, while broader claims about systemic health benefits lack empirical foundation.

The persistence and popularity of chiropractic care reflects both legitimate therapeutic effects for certain conditions and the appeal of hands-on treatment approaches that provide personal attention and dramatic mechanical interventions. Distinguishing these elements requires careful attention to evidence quality and scope of application rather than wholesale acceptance or rejection of chiropractic as a healthcare intervention.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Chiropractic emerged in 1895 when Daniel David Palmer, a magnetic healer and self-taught anatomist, performed the first chiropractic adjustment on Harvey Lillard, a janitor who claimed to have lost his hearing after feeling something "give way" in his back. Palmer believed he had discovered a universal healing principle and founded chiropractic on the theory that misaligned vertebrae ("subluxations") interfere with the nervous system, causing disease throughout the body.

Palmer's son, Bartlett Joshua Palmer, developed and promoted this theory through the Palmer School of Chiropractic, establishing the fundamental concepts that continue to define traditional chiropractic philosophy: the existence of vertebral subluxation, the role of "innate intelligence" in healing, and the primacy of spinal adjustment in treating disease. These concepts formed the theoretical foundation of chiropractic education and practice for over a century.

The scientific evaluation of these foundational concepts reveals fundamental problems. The chiropractic subluxation, defined as a misalignment of vertebrae causing nerve interference and systemic health problems, has no anatomical or physiological basis in modern medical understanding. Mirtz, Morgan, and Wyatt's comprehensive 2009 review in *Chiropractic & Manual Therapies* concluded that "there is no scientific evidence that chiropractic subluxation and subluxation-based chiropractic care are effective for disease prevention and health maintenance."

However, the technique of spinal manipulation—the physical procedure of applying controlled force to joints—exists independently of chiropractic theory and has been evaluated on its own merits. The evidence for spinal manipulation presents a complex picture that varies significantly by condition and context.

For acute low back pain, the evidence supporting spinal manipulation is relatively strong. The American College of Physicians' 2017 clinical practice guideline, published in *Annals of Internal Medicine*, recommends spinal manipulation as a first-line treatment for acute low back pain, alongside heat therapy and massage, before considering pharmaceutical interventions. This recommendation reflects multiple systematic reviews and randomized controlled trials demonstrating modest but meaningful improvements in pain and function.

The landmark study by Paige et al., published in *JAMA* in 2017, randomized 750 military personnel with acute low back pain to usual care, usual care plus spinal manipulation, or usual care plus spinal manipulation and exercise. The group receiving spinal manipulation showed significantly greater improvement in pain and disability at four weeks, with effect sizes that were clinically meaningful though modest in magnitude.

Cochrane reviews, representing the highest standard of evidence synthesis, have reached nuanced conclusions about spinal manipulation. The 2019 Cochrane review by Rubinstein et al. found moderate-quality evidence that spinal manipulation provides small, clinically worthwhile improvements for acute low back pain. For chronic low back pain, the evidence is weaker but still suggests small benefits comparable to other common interventions.

For neck pain, the evidence is more limited and the risk-benefit calculation more complex. While some studies show modest benefits for certain types of neck pain, rare but serious adverse events, including vertebral artery dissection leading to stroke, have been associated with cervical spine manipulation. The 2018 Cochrane review by Gross et al. found low to very low-quality evidence for neck manipulation, leading many medical organizations to recommend caution.

The evidence becomes much weaker when examining chiropractic claims beyond musculoskeletal conditions. Despite widespread promotion of chiropractic care for conditions like asthma, colic, ear infections, and immune system disorders, systematic reviews consistently find no reliable evidence for these applications. Ernst and Canter's 2006 systematic review in *Pediatrics* found no convincing evidence that chiropractic treatment is effective for any childhood condition.

The theoretical gap between evidence and chiropractic philosophy has created significant tension within the profession. "Evidence-based chiropractors" or "mixers" incorporate techniques from physical therapy, exercise science, and rehabilitation medicine while de-emphasizing or abandoning subluxation theory. "Straight" chiropractors maintain adherence to traditional chiropractic philosophy and focus primarily on subluxation detection and correction.

This schism reflects deeper questions about professional identity and scope of practice. States vary significantly in their regulation of chiropractic practice, with some allowing chiropractors to perform procedures like injection therapy and others restricting practice to spinal manipulation. The lack of uniform standards complicates efforts to evaluate "chiropractic care" as a unified intervention.

Research on the mechanisms of spinal manipulation suggests that benefits may result from multiple pathways, including mechanical effects on joint mobility, neurophysiological responses to joint mobilization, and contextual factors related to the therapeutic encounter. Bialosky et al.'s 2009 review in *Journal of Manual & Manipulative Therapy* proposed a complex model involving biomechanical, neurophysiological, and psychological mechanisms that operate independently of the theoretical frameworks used to justify the intervention.

The safety profile of spinal manipulation varies by technique and anatomical region. Lumbar spine manipulation appears to have a low risk of serious adverse events, with minor side effects like temporary soreness being common. Cervical manipulation carries a higher risk profile, with estimates of serious adverse events ranging from 1 in 20,000 to 1 in several million treatments, depending on the study methodology and definition of serious harm.

The integration of chiropractic care into mainstream healthcare has proceeded unevenly. Many hospitals and medical centers now employ chiropractors or refer patients for spinal manipulation, but typically within evidence-based protocols that focus on musculoskeletal conditions. The Veterans Administration, following extensive evidence review, covers chiropractic care for veterans but emphasizes conservative, evidence-based approaches.

Educational standards in chiropractic schools have evolved toward greater emphasis on evidence-based practice, though significant variation remains. The Council on Chiropractic Education has implemented standards requiring evidence-based curricula, but traditional chiropractic philosophy continues to influence education at many institutions.

The economic implications of chiropractic care are substantial. Americans spend billions annually on chiropractic services, much of it covered by insurance plans that increasingly require evidence of effectiveness. The integration of chiropractors into accountable care organizations and value-based payment models has incentivized focus on conditions with demonstrated efficacy while potentially reducing emphasis on unsubstantiated applications.

International perspectives on chiropractic vary significantly. While the practice has gained acceptance in countries like Australia and Canada, European health systems generally take a more restrictive approach, often limiting chiropractic practice to specific musculoskeletal applications with demonstrated evidence.

The placebo and contextual effects of chiropractic care deserve particular attention. Manual therapy interventions inherently involve extensive practitioner-patient interaction, hands-on treatment, and often dramatic mechanical procedures that may enhance placebo responses. Distinguishing specific from non-specific effects requires carefully designed studies with appropriate control groups—a methodological challenge that complicates evidence evaluation.

Public health considerations include both potential benefits and harms from widespread chiropractic use. To the extent that chiropractic care provides effective treatment for common conditions like low back pain, it may reduce reliance on pharmaceutical interventions with their own risk profiles. However, delays in appropriate medical care resulting from reliance on chiropractic treatment for serious conditions represent a significant potential harm.

The relationship between chiropractic care and vaccination presents another public health consideration. While not universal, anti-vaccination sentiment is more common among chiropractors than among medical physicians, and some chiropractic organizations have opposed vaccination requirements. This creates potential conflicts between individual chiropractic care and broader public health objectives.

Research infrastructure in chiropractic has expanded significantly, with dedicated research institutions and peer-reviewed journals focusing on spinal manipulation and related interventions. This infrastructure has facilitated higher-quality research but also created potential conflicts of interest when research is conducted or funded by institutions with financial interests in chiropractic education or practice.

The evolution of manual therapy techniques beyond traditional chiropractic adds another layer of complexity. Physical therapists, osteopathic physicians, and other practitioners now perform spinal manipulation techniques similar to those used by chiropractors, often with different theoretical frameworks and training approaches. This convergence suggests that the beneficial elements of chiropractic care may be separable from its specific professional and philosophical contexts.
