# "Chlorine in Pools Turns Blue (or Green) If You Pee"

## 1. The Claim

The claim is deceptively simple: swimming pools are treated with a chemical that reacts visibly with human urine, changing the colour of the surrounding water — most commonly described as bright blue, though red, green, and purple variants also circulate — to identify the offending swimmer. The cloud of coloured water appears rapidly, persists long enough for nearby swimmers and lifeguards to see it, then disperses. The mechanism is usually described as a reaction between the dye and urea or ammonia in urine. The consequence is social exposure: the person who peed will be immediately known to everyone in the pool.

This story is one of the most universally transmitted myths in childhood culture in the English-speaking world. Survey data is not formally published on this point, but the claim is routinely experienced as authoritative environmental information rather than as peer rumour. It arrives from adults in positions of authority — parents issuing pre-swim warnings, lifeguards giving poolside briefings, camp counsellors stating it as facility policy — which gives it an institutional stamp absent from most childhood myths. Children who subsequently ask adults whether the chemical is real frequently receive confirmation. Adults who are themselves uncertain perpetuate it rather than investigate it.

The claim also circulates in elaborated adult forms. Posts on social media periodically ask whether the chemical is real; the responses split roughly between confident confirmation and confident denial, with the confirming camp often citing a vague memory of having been told about it at a hotel pool, summer camp, or municipal aquatic centre. Some posts claim that the dye is invisible in the bottle but activates on contact with the ammonia content of urine specifically.

A significantly more specific variant claims the product is commercially sold under the name "URinAlert" and can be ordered by pool operators. This variant is false. No such commercial product exists in pool supply catalogues or EPA-registered pool chemical databases. Several pool supply journalists have investigated and found no trace of a product operating on colorimetric urine detection principles reaching any commercial recreational pool market.

The claim received major mainstream cultural reinforcement in Adam Sandler's 2010 film *Grown Ups*, in which a character's pool urination produces a visible blue cloud that emanates from his body and spreads across the water. The scene played the premise as comic realism rather than absurdist fantasy, implying that the mechanism was a widely understood fact of pool management and that the humour derived from being caught rather than from the existence of the technology.

The claim also appears, unironically, in posted signage. Multiple hotels and aquatic centres, primarily in the United States, have displayed signs reading variants of "NOTICE: This pool is treated with a urine-detecting chemical. We are not joking." The signs are deliberately false. They are intended as behavioural deterrents, and facility managers who erect them are fully aware they do not describe a real technology. This makes the pool-dye claim a distinctive species of institutional misinformation — not propagated through credulity but through knowing deployment of a plausible-sounding falsehood for compliance purposes.

## 2. What's Actually True

**The chemical does not exist.** The urine-detecting pool dye is a durable myth without any commercial, scientific, or regulatory foundation. No product registered with the Environmental Protection Agency for use in recreational pool water operates on the principle of colorimetric urine detection. No peer-reviewed paper has described a selective pool dye responding to urinary markers. No pool supply distributor in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, or Australia lists such a product (confirmed by investigation by journalists from multiple outlets including *Mental Floss*, *The Atlantic*, and the *Los Angeles Times*).

The absence is not one of market demand — the claim's prevalence implies substantial commercial opportunity if the technology were viable. The absence is one of chemical feasibility.

**Why selective urine detection is impossible in pool water.** Urine is approximately 95% water. Its dissolved organic content — the components that might provide a detection target distinct from other pool contaminants — is dominated by urea (roughly 9 grams per litre), creatinine (roughly 1 g/L), uric acid, chloride, sodium, potassium, and phosphate, with a long tail of trace organic compounds at very low concentrations. Every one of these components is also present in human sweat. Chloride and sodium are the dominant ions in perspiration. Urea enters pool water from sweat. Creatinine is shed in skin cells and perspiration. Ammonia, the compound most commonly cited as the specific urine marker, is present in both urine and fresh sweat.

A valid selective colorimetric indicator must react with a compound present in urine but absent or present at much lower concentrations in all other substances that regularly enter pool water. No such compound exists in sufficient concentration in urine. This has been established by systematic analysis of the organic load entering recreational pools. Research by Chowdhury and colleagues (1996, *Water Research*) characterising the disinfection byproduct precursors in pool water found that the organic precursor load was dominated by materials from body surface contact — perspiration, skin cells, sunscreen residues, and cosmetics — rather than from urination, and that urine did not introduce any uniquely detectable organic marker at the concentrations typical of recreational swimming.

**What chlorine actually does with urine.** Chlorine does react with components of urine in specific, well-characterised ways — but these reactions produce no visible colour change. Free chlorine in pool water (present primarily as hypochlorous acid at operational pH) reacts with ammonia compounds from urine and sweat to form chloramines: monochloramine, dichloramine, and nitrogen trichloride (trichloramine). These reactions, characterised in detail by Li and Blatchley (2007, *Environmental Science & Technology*), consume free chlorine and produce the set of disinfection byproducts responsible for the characteristic "pool smell" — commonly misattributed to chlorine itself. The smell is actually the smell of chloramines, which form continuously as pool chlorine reacts with bathers' organic matter. Pools that smell strongly of "chlorine" are actually under-chlorinated: the free chlorine concentration has been depleted by organic load, and chloramine concentration has risen.

This chemistry is real, observable, and measurable — but produces no visible colour change in bulk pool water. Chloramine solutions are colourless in the concentration ranges found in recreational pools. The blue-green colour of outdoor swimming pools is a combination of the intrinsic light scattering properties of liquid water in depth (Rayleigh and Mie scattering favour shorter wavelengths) and, in many commercial pools, the colour of the pool liner or tile. It is not a chemical property of the water or its dissolved constituents.

**The acesulfame potassium tracer research.** There is a genuine scientific method for detecting urination in pool water, but it bears no resemblance to the popular myth. Researchers at the University of Alberta (Blackstock, She, Dettling, Bhatt, & Blatchley, 2017, *Environmental Science & Technology Letters*) demonstrated that acesulfame potassium — an artificial sweetener excreted unchanged in human urine and highly resistant to chlorine degradation — can serve as a persistent tracer for urination in recreational pool water. By measuring acesulfame potassium concentrations using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry, the researchers estimated that a typical competition pool (830,000 litres) contained roughly 75 litres of urine, accumulated over the pool's use lifetime between water changes. A hotel pool of 430,000 litres contained approximately 30 litres of urine.

This research was widely reported in 2017 and gave the pool-urine conversation a new empirical foundation. However, it simultaneously confirmed the non-existence of the pool dye myth: the research required laboratory liquid chromatography to detect urinary markers, precisely because no selective real-time detection is possible at a pool's water surface. The acesulfame potassium method produces no colour change under any circumstances. It is a retrospective analytical technique, not a real-time behavioural deterrent.

**Dilution arithmetic.** A standard competitive swimming pool contains approximately 2.5 million litres of water. A single urination event introduces 150–300 millilitres — a dilution factor of roughly one part per ten million. Any colorimetric indicator molecule added to detect urine at this dilution would need to produce visible colour effects at concentrations below 100 nanograms per litre while remaining stable in chlorinated water and selective for a target compound absent from every other organic source in the pool. This combination of sensitivity, chemical stability in oxidising environments, and selectivity is beyond the practical range of any known aqueous colorimetric system. Routine analytical chemistry methods — high-performance liquid chromatography, mass spectrometry — can achieve sensitivity at these levels, but they operate on small extracted samples in laboratory conditions, not on bulk pool water in real time.

## 3. Why People Believe This — and Why Institutions Spread It Knowingly

The pool-dye myth has an unusual two-population propagation structure: some people who spread it are genuinely deceived, and a significant fraction who spread it know it is false. Understanding both populations is necessary to understand why the myth has persisted for decades with institutional support.

**The genuinely deceived population.** Children who hear the claim from parents and lifeguards have no reason to doubt it. They lack the chemistry knowledge to evaluate its feasibility, they are hearing it from authority figures, and they have no conflicting information. The claim is plausible — pool chemicals do interact visibly with pH test kits, and the idea that a more specific indicator might exist is not inherently absurd to a ten-year-old. Once the claim is established in childhood memory, it is resistant to subsequent correction through the same mechanism that protects other beliefs acquired in authoritative contexts early in life.

Adults who received the claim in childhood and never investigated it maintain the belief through the absence of any corrective encounter. In most adult social contexts, swimming pool chemistry is not discussed at a level of specificity that would challenge the dye myth. Even adults who know the claim is suspect may never investigate, because the practical stakes of being wrong are low. The belief causes no observable harm, never generates a testable prediction in daily life, and therefore drifts unexamined.

**The deliberately deceiving population — and the behavioural deterrence paradigm.** The more distinctive feature of this myth's propagation ecology is the population of adults who repeat it knowing it is false. Pool operators, facility managers, camp directors, parents, and lifeguards who have investigated or simply deduced that the chemical does not exist nonetheless tell children it does, because it works as a behavioural deterrent. This makes the pool-dye myth an example of what might be called a manufactured compliance myth: a false empirical claim actively propagated by authority figures to produce desired behaviour without requiring enforcement, explanation, or negotiation.

The mechanism is economically attractive from an institutional perspective. Achieving compliance with "do not urinate in the pool" without a deterrent chemical requires either intrusive monitoring (physically impossible in most pool settings), rule enforcement (resource-intensive and socially awkward), or repeated normative appeals (low compliance without enforcement). The dye myth achieves compliance through a single plausible threat that costs nothing to administer and requires no capital investment. A sign, a statement from a lifeguard, or a parental reminder before a pool visit is sufficient to activate is deterrent effect.

Research in behavioural economics and compliance psychology is directly relevant here. Studies on deterrence suggest that perceived probability of detection matters more than the magnitude of consequence for compliance with low-stakes social norms (Nagin, 2013, *Annual Review of Law and Social Science*). The pool dye myth raises perceived detection probability to near-certainty — "you will be caught immediately, visibly, in front of everyone" — while the consequence (social embarrassment, ejection from pool) is modest. This combination is optimally designed, whether intentionally or not, for inducing compliance.

The deliberate propagation of the myth by institutions aware of its falsity raises a normative question that the factual debunking alone does not resolve. Is it permissible for authority figures to tell known falsehoods to achieve socially beneficial compliance outcomes? Pool health is a genuine public health concern — urination does introduce ammonia precursors that accelerate chlorine depletion and raise chloramine concentrations, which cause respiratory and eye irritation in regular pool users. The deterrent goal is legitimate even though the claimed mechanism is false. This puts the pool-dye myth in a philosophically interesting category alongside white lies deployed for prosocial ends, institutional fictions that serve coordination functions (like the implied social contract of etiquette norms), and deliberate overclaiming in public health messaging (like early condom efficacy claims that overstated protection to achieve adoption). Each involves a tradeoff between accuracy and outcome, and each has been defended on consequentialist grounds.

**The epistemically novel structural observation.** The standard analysis of misinformation treats the propagator as either deceived or malicious. The pool-dye myth's most common propagators are neither: they are deliberately untruthful in service of a goal they regard as beneficial and socially sanctioned. This is an unusual epistemic category — institutional truth management — that standard fact-checking frameworks do not handle well. Fact-checkers can confirm that the pool dye does not exist; they cannot address whether the compliance myth should be retired now that widespread debunking has reduced its effectiveness. A claim's falsity and a claim's net social impact are genuinely separate questions that require different evaluative tools.

## 4. Verdict

**The claim is false.** No chemical in recreational pool water causes visible colour change in response to contact with urine. This is established by the chemistry of aqueous colorimetric indicators, the organic chemistry of urine and sweat, the known reactions of chlorine with nitrogenous compounds, and the complete absence of any commercial product operating on this principle in reviewed pool chemical databases.

The claim is false at every level of specification:

- No "URinAlert" or comparable commercial product exists.
- Chlorine does not produce visible colour changes when it reacts with urinary ammonia compounds — it forms colourless chloramines (Li & Blatchley, 2007, *Environmental Science & Technology*).
- No selective indicator could distinguish urine from sweat in pool water because they share all detectable organic components.
- The only validated scientific method for detecting urination in pool water is laboratory liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis of acesulfame potassium concentrations (Blackstock et al., 2017, *Environmental Science & Technology Letters*), which produces no visual pool-side response.

Snopes rates this claim as "False." The rating is accurate and appropriately categorical.

The urine is real — the Blackstock 2017 research established that recreational pools routinely contain substantial urinary load — and the health motivation for deterring urination is legitimate. Urination does accelerate chloramine formation (Li & Blatchley, 2007), depletes free chlorine, and contributes to the trichloramine load associated with respiratory symptoms in competitive swimmers (Bernard et al., 2006, *European Respiratory Journal*). But none of this makes the pool dye less fictional.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The pool-dye myth belongs to a class of surveillance myths — false claims about detection technologies deployed to produce behavioural compliance — that appear in multiple institutional contexts. Security cameras that do not record but are mounted to appear operational, "drug detection" swabs used at venue entrances that cannot identify most substances, speed camera housings with no cameras installed: each deploys the appearance of detection to achieve deterrence without the infrastructure. The pool-dye myth is distinctive within this class for operating through verbal and textual transmission rather than physical props, and for being directed primarily at children.

The claim's persistence across decades of increasing internet fact-checking access illustrates the limits of information availability as a corrective to motivated misinformation. Unlike myths that persist because people simply have not encountered the correction, the pool-dye myth persists in part because the institutions that would correct it have an interest in perpetuating it. As long as a faction of pool operators, parents, and camp counsellors believes the deterrent effect is worth the epistemic cost, the claim will be actively re-propagated into each new generation of children who have not yet encountered a debunking.

This dynamic has public health implications beyond swimming pools. If authority figures are understood to issue knowingly false deterrent claims about health matters — a pattern visible also in certain historical vaccination, sex education, and substance prevention messaging — the long-term effect on institutional credibility may outweigh the short-term compliance benefits. Research in adolescent health communication consistently finds that perceived credibility of the source is the primary predictor of message effectiveness (Petty & Cacioppo, 1986, *Communication and Persuasion*). A child who discovers that the pool dye claim was false — either through chemistry education or through the internet — has received not just a correction but a lesson about adult institutional honesty that may generalise.

The alternative to the compliance myth is accurate health communication delivered in developmentally appropriate terms: urination in pools raises ammonia levels that chlorine turns into stinging compounds that irritate eyes and lungs; pool smell is actually chloramine smell, produced by the reaction between chlorine and body waste; being a considerate pool user means using the bathroom before swimming. This messaging is accurate, testable, and does not require institutional deception. Whether it achieves equivalent compliance is an empirical question that, to this author's knowledge, has not been formally studied.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published its investigation of the pool-dye claim under the title "Piscine Dreams," rating the core claim False. The piece is notable for exploring the claim's deliberate propagation dimension, acknowledging that pool operators and parents knowingly repeat it while having reason to believe or know it is false. This distinguishes Snopes's treatment from a standard false-belief debunking and correctly frames the myth as a case of institutional misinformation rather than naive credulity.

Several science journalism outlets have addressed the claim. Mental Floss, LiveScience, and Popular Science have each published debunkings that follow the same argumentive structure: confirm the non-existence of any commercial product, explain why pool chemistry cannot support selective urine detection, acknowledge the genuine reaction between chlorine and urinary ammonia, and note the Blackstock 2017 research as the only real detection science in this domain.

The 2017 Blackstock et al. research wave generated a secondary round of fact-checking interest, as headlines like "Scientists Measure How Much People Pee in Pools" implicitly foregrounded the topic. Several outlets used the opportunity to reststate the pool-dye debunking while reporting the acesulfame potassium findings, which created a useful pairing: here is the actual science of detecting pool urination, and here is why it is nothing like the popular myth.

The claim's main fact-checking challenge is that debunking it requires a modest engagement with aqueous chemistry — not deep, but enough to explain why "chlorine reacts with urine" does not translate to "pool water turns blue." Debunkings that simply assert the non-existence of the product without explaining why such a product cannot exist leave the audience able to imagine that the technology might simply not yet exist or not yet be commercially deployed. The more effective debunkings explain the chemical selectivity problem: there is no target compound unique to urine in pool water, because everything in urine is also in sweat. Once this is understood, the myth does not merely lack support — it lacks the conceptual preconditions for being true.
