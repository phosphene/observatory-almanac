# "Chop Suey Is Not Real Chinese Food — It Was Invented in America"


## 1. The Claim

The assertion that "chop suey is not real Chinese food — it was invented in America" represents one of the most persistent and multifaceted claims about Chinese-American cuisine. This claim typically encompasses several interlocking assertions: that chop suey has no authentic Chinese origins, that it was created specifically for American palates by Chinese immigrants, and that it represents a fundamental departure from "real" Chinese cooking traditions. The claim often includes specific origin stories, most commonly that the dish was invented by a Chinese chef working for diplomat Li Hongzhang during his 1896 visit to the United States.

The claim's persistence in American cultural discourse reflects deeper anxieties about authenticity, cultural appropriation, and the nature of immigrant cuisines. It has been repeated in cookbooks, restaurant guides, cultural histories, and popular media for over a century, often without examination of its underlying assumptions or supporting evidence.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim contains multiple layers that require separate analysis. First, the assertion that chop suey "was invented in America" depends heavily on how one defines both "invention" and the dish itself. The Li Hongzhang story, while colorful and widely repeated, lacks contemporary documentation and appears to be apocryphal. No records from Li's 1896 American tour mention chop suey, and the story seems to have emerged decades later as a retrospective explanation.

However, the underlying premise — that Chinese-American versions of chop suey represent significant departures from traditional Chinese cooking — contains important elements of truth. The American preparation of chop suey, typically featuring celery, bean sprouts, and a thick, cornstarch-based sauce served over rice, does indeed differ markedly from regional Chinese preparations.

The phrase "not real Chinese food" embeds a particularly problematic assumption about culinary authenticity. This framing suggests that cuisines have fixed, essential characteristics that can be definitively categorized as "real" or "inauthentic." Such binary thinking fails to account for the dynamic, adaptive nature of food cultures and the historical reality of culinary evolution through migration, trade, and cultural exchange.

Archaeological and historical evidence indicates that mixed-ingredient dishes similar to chop suey have existed in various forms throughout Chinese culinary history. The Cantonese term "tsap seui" (雜碎), literally meaning "mixed pieces" or "odds and ends," refers to a category of dishes that combine various ingredients, often including organ meats and vegetables.


The evidence suggests that chop suey's origins are more complex than either simple invention narratives or authenticity dismissals can capture. The dish appears to represent a fusion of established Cantonese cooking traditions with American ingredients and presentation styles, developed through the practical needs of Chinese immigrants running restaurants for diverse clienteles.

Several key findings emerge from systematic analysis:

**Historical continuity**: Dishes recognizable as chop suey variants existed in Guangdong Province before significant Chinese immigration to America. The basic technique of stir-frying mixed vegetables and proteins in light sauces represents an established Cantonese cooking method.

**Adaptive innovation**: Chinese-American restaurant operators did substantially modify traditional recipes to accommodate available ingredients (celery became prominent because it was cheap and readily available in American markets) and local preferences (heavier sauces, larger portions, service over rice rather than as one dish among many).

**Commercial context**: The standardization of chop suey as we know it today occurred primarily within the commercial context of Chinese-American restaurants serving non-Chinese customers. This commercial imperative shaped both ingredients and presentation in ways that diverged from home cooking traditions.

**Regional variation**: Different regions of the United States developed distinct chop suey styles, reflecting local ingredients, customer preferences, and the particular regional origins of Chinese immigrant communities.

The notion that chop suey was "invented" in America appears to conflate innovation with invention. While Chinese-American restaurant operators certainly innovated within existing culinary frameworks, they built upon established cooking traditions rather than creating something new.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemological frameworks underlying discussions of chop suey's origins reveal significant methodological limitations. Most popular accounts rely on anecdotal evidence, restaurant lore, and retrospective storytelling rather than systematic historical research. The Li Hongzhang story, for example, appears to be what folklorists call an "etiological legend" — a narrative that explains the origin of something through a memorable but unverifiable story.

Food historian E.N. Anderson's research methodology in "The Food of China" (1988) provides a more rigorous approach. Anderson traced chop suey-type dishes to specific regional traditions in Guangdong Province, particularly in the Taishan (Toisan) region, which supplied many early Chinese immigrants to America. His work demonstrates that dishes combining mixed vegetables and meats in light sauces were established elements of Cantonese cooking long before Chinese immigration to America began in earnest.

Similarly, Andrew Coe's "Chop Suey: A Cultural History of Chinese Food in the United States" (2009) employs archival research, immigrant testimonies, and restaurant records to construct a more nuanced account of Chinese-American cuisine's development. Coe's methodology reveals how Chinese immigrants adapted familiar cooking techniques and flavor profiles to available ingredients and local tastes, creating hybrid cuisines that were neither purely Chinese nor purely American.

The epistemological challenge lies in determining what constitutes sufficient evidence for claims about culinary origins. Restaurant menus, cookbook recipes, and immigrant letters provide different types of evidence than do systematic archaeological or anthropological studies of food practices. Each source type carries its own limitations and potential biases.

Contemporary food studies scholarship increasingly recognizes that authenticity claims often reflect power relations rather than objective culinary facts. The assertion that certain dishes are "not real" Chinese food can serve to marginalize immigrant communities and their adaptive survival strategies. This doesn't mean all culinary variations are equivalent, but it does suggest that authenticity judgments require careful attention to their social and political contexts.


## 4. Verdict

**COMPLEX/ADAPTIVE**: The claim that chop suey "was invented in America" and is "not real Chinese food" represents a distorted but grounded narrative that contains elements of truth within a problematic framework.

The specific invention stories (particularly the Li Hongzhang legend) appear to be apocryphal, lacking contemporary documentation and bearing the hallmarks of retrospective folklore rather than historical fact. However, the underlying recognition that American chop suey represents a significant adaptation of Chinese cooking traditions contains substantial accuracy.

The more fundamental problem lies in the claim's binary framework opposing "real" and "invented" foods. This framework fails to account for the historical reality of culinary evolution and the legitimate adaptive strategies of immigrant communities. Chop suey represents neither pure invention nor unchanged tradition, but rather a complex process of cultural translation that built upon established Cantonese cooking methods while adapting to American ingredients, tastes, and commercial constraints.

The dish's development illustrates broader patterns in immigrant cuisine: the preservation of core techniques and flavor principles through modified ingredients and presentation styles. Rather than representing a betrayal of Chinese culinary tradition, chop suey demonstrates the resilience and adaptability that has allowed Chinese cooking traditions to survive and flourish in diaspora communities worldwide.

The persistence of invention narratives and authenticity dismissals reflects ongoing cultural anxieties about immigration, cultural change, and the boundaries of legitimate tradition. Understanding chop suey's actual history requires moving beyond these binary frameworks toward more nuanced appreciation of how cultures adapt and survive through change.

The evidence supports neither the simple invention story nor the authenticity dismissal, but rather a more complex account of culinary adaptation that honors both the Chinese roots of the cooking techniques involved and the innovative responses of Chinese-American communities to their new environment. In this context, chop suey emerges not as fake Chinese food or American invention, but as a legitimate example of how food cultures evolve through migration and cultural encounter.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The chop suey authenticity debate connects to broader questions about immigrant cuisines, cultural adaptation, and the politics of food identity. Similar discussions surround other "fusion" dishes created by immigrant communities: Is chicken tikka masala "real" Indian food? Are American pizza styles "authentic" Italian cuisine? Is Tex-Mex "real" Mexican food?

These debates often reflect underlying assumptions about cultural purity and the legitimacy of adaptive change. Food studies scholars like Krishnendu Ray have documented how immigrant cuisines typically undergo a process of standardization and simplification when entering mainstream American food culture. This process serves economic and practical functions but can create versions that differ significantly from regional variations in countries of origin.

The question of what constitutes "real" Chinese food becomes particularly complex when considering China's own vast regional diversity. Guangdong Province alone contains multiple distinct culinary traditions, and what counts as "authentic" Cantonese cooking varies significantly between cities, rural areas, and different historical periods.

Furthermore, contemporary Chinese cuisine continues to evolve through globalization, urbanization, and changing economic conditions. Modern Chinese cities feature international cuisines, fusion restaurants, and adapted traditional dishes that would have been unrecognizable to earlier generations. The notion of a fixed, authentic Chinese cuisine becomes difficult to sustain when examined against this dynamic reality.

The broader implications extend beyond food to questions of cultural identity and belonging. When immigrant communities adapt their cultural practices to new environments, are they preserving their heritage or abandoning it? The answer depends partly on whether culture is understood as a fixed essence or as a living, adaptive process.

Research in migration studies and cultural anthropology increasingly supports the latter view. Cultural practices survive through adaptation rather than despite it. The Chinese-American restaurant industry created dishes like chop suey not as betrayals of Chinese cooking traditions but as strategies for maintaining economic viability while preserving core culinary techniques and flavor principles.

This perspective suggests that authenticity judgments often say more about the social position of judges than about the intrinsic properties of food. Dismissing chop suey as "not real Chinese food" can function to exclude Chinese-Americans from definitions of legitimate Chinese culture, implying that their adaptive strategies somehow diminish their cultural authenticity.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed this specific claim in a dedicated fact-check, though the assertion appears frequently in discussions of Chinese-American food culture. The absence of formal fact-checking attention to this claim is itself significant, as it has allowed various origin stories and authenticity judgments to circulate without systematic verification.

The most commonly cited version involves Li Hongzhang (李鴻章), the influential Chinese statesman and diplomat who visited the United States in 1896. According to this narrative, Li's chef created chop suey when faced with the challenge of cooking for both Chinese and American diners, combining ingredients available in American markets with Chinese cooking techniques. Alternative versions place the dish's invention in California mining camps during the Gold Rush, or credit it to Chinese restaurant owners in New York's Chinatown.
