## The Claim

In late March 2020, as the COVID-19 pandemic deepened and public attention turned to the possibility of pharmacological interventions, a claim began spreading across Instagram, Facebook, and other social platforms: that cinchona bark — the bark of the Andean cinchona tree, also called the "fever tree" — was a natural substitute for chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine, the antimalarial drugs that had been receiving intense media attention as possible COVID-19 treatments.

The posts took several related forms. Some claimed cinchona bark *was* a natural source of chloroquine and could therefore be consumed to achieve the same therapeutic effect. Others pointed out that cinchona bark contains quinine, which is an ingredient in tonic water, and suggested that drinking tonic water might therefore provide coronavirus protection. A third variant framed cinchona bark supplements as a readily available, "natural" alternative to a prescription drug that people with lupus and rheumatoid arthritis were reporting being unable to access due to COVID-related demand surges.

The core claim across all these variations: *cinchona bark, as the natural precursor or equivalent of chloroquine, offers a viable treatment or preventive for COVID-19*.

The interest in chloroquine as a potential COVID therapy had been significantly amplified when US President Donald Trump described the drug as "promising" in a nationally broadcast speech on 19 March 2020, sending demand for both the drug and speculative alternatives surging. It was in this context — political endorsement of an unproven drug, prescription shortage forcing vulnerable patients off their maintenance medications, widespread public panic — that cinchona bark emerged as a folk cure gaining real-world traction.

## What's Actually True

The history of cinchona bark and its relationship to modern antimalarial drugs is genuinely fascinating and genuinely complex — which is one reason why the distortion embedded in the COVID-era claims is both understandable and particularly dangerous.

**The history of cinchona.** The cinchona tree (*Cinchona officinalis* and related species) is native to the eastern slopes of the Andes mountains in South America. Its bark contains a family of compounds known as quinoline alkaloids — principally quinine, but also quinidine, cinchonine, and cinchonidine. Indigenous populations in the region had long used preparations from the bark to treat fevers. The bark was introduced to Europe in the seventeenth century and became the first effective treatment for malaria, transforming European colonial medicine and enabling expansion into malarial equatorial territories that would otherwise have been far more lethal to European forces. For more than three hundred years — from its seventeenth-century European introduction to the twentieth-century development of synthetic alternatives — cinchona bark and quinine were the primary, often the only, pharmacological tool for combating one of humanity's most devastating infectious diseases.

This history gives cinchona bark a genuine place in the story of antimalarial medicine and an authentic botanical and pharmacological significance. The claims circulating in 2020 were not fabricated from nothing. They exploited a real relationship between cinchona bark and the treatment of infectious disease.

**The development of chloroquine.** In 1934, scientists at Bayer (then I.G. Farben) in Germany synthesised the first compound in the chloroquine family, initially called Resochin. Chloroquine was developed deliberately as a synthetic antimalarial, and it was indeed *inspired* by the antimalarial activity of quinine. The researchers understood that quinine worked against the malaria parasite and sought to design a synthetic compound that replicated or improved upon that activity. In this sense, chloroquine has genuine historical and conceptual ancestry in cinchona bark chemistry.

However — and this is the critical point — the chemical structure of chloroquine is fundamentally different from the structure of quinine. The two compounds share a quinoline ring structure, but their pharmacological profiles, mechanisms of action, dose-response curves, and side effect profiles are distinct. Chloroquine is not a derivative of quinine in the sense of being chemically refined from it. It is an independently synthesised compound that was designed to achieve similar effects through a different molecular pathway. Saying that cinchona bark contains chloroquine, or that consuming cinchona bark is equivalent to taking chloroquine, is chemically and pharmacologically incorrect.

**Quinine versus chloroquine versus hydroxychloroquine.** The confusion was further compounded by the presence of multiple related but distinct compounds in public discussion. Hydroxychloroquine (brand name Plaquenil) is a derivative of chloroquine with fewer cardiovascular side effects and broader prescribing. It was the compound more commonly discussed as a potential COVID-19 treatment in the context of lupus and rheumatoid arthritis maintenance therapy. Neither chloroquine nor hydroxychloroquine appears in cinchona bark. Quinine — the compound that does appear in cinchona bark — is pharmacologically distinct from both.

The presence of quinine in tonic water, at concentrations far too low to have any therapeutic antimalarial or antiviral effect, became a secondary claim in the cinchona bark misinformation ecosystem. Posts suggested that drinking tonic water might protect against COVID-19 because tonic water contains quinine, and quinine is related to chloroquine. This chain of inference collapsed at multiple points: quinine is not chloroquine; standard tonic water contains quinine at approximately 83 milligrams per litre (a therapeutic dose of quinine for malaria treatment is typically 500 to 1,000 milligrams three times daily); and even at therapeutic concentrations, there was no established evidence that quinine had any activity against SARS-CoV-2.

**Evidence for chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine in COVID-19.** It is also important to note that the premise underlying all the cinchona bark claims — that chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine were established effective treatments for COVID-19 — was itself unproven at the time and was ultimately not supported by rigorous clinical trial evidence. Early in vitro (test tube) studies showing that chloroquine could inhibit SARS-CoV-2 replication in cell cultures were initially compelling but did not translate into clinical benefit in human trials. Multiple large randomised controlled trials, including the WHO Solidarity Trial and the UK RECOVERY Trial, found that hydroxychloroquine did not reduce mortality or hospitalisation rates in COVID-19 patients when tested at appropriate scale and rigour. The US FDA withdrew its Emergency Use Authorisation for chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine in COVID-19 treatment in June 2020. By mid-2020, the scientific consensus had shifted decisively against these compounds as COVID-19 treatments.

The cinchona bark claims were thus distorted versions of a claim that was itself distorted. People were seeking a natural alternative to a drug that turned out not to work for the disease in question, based on a misunderstanding of the drug's chemical relationship to the natural compound. The layers of distortion were multiple.

**Toxicity risks of cinchona.** Beyond the lack of efficacy, cinchona bark carries genuine and serious toxicity risks that the circulating posts consistently ignored or minimised. The constellation of toxic effects from quinine and related cinchona alkaloids is known collectively as "cinchonism" — a condition that can range in severity from mild to life-threatening. Symptoms include tinnitus and hearing loss, blurred vision and other visual disturbances, nausea and vomiting, abdominal pain, cardiomyopathy, hepatotoxicity, and renal impairment. In severe cases, cinchonism can cause coma. Quinine can also induce life-threatening hypoglycaemia, thrombocytopenia, and severe haemolytic reactions in individuals with glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase deficiency — the same genetic condition that creates risk with high-dose vitamin C.

The FDA withdrew quinine pills from over-the-counter sale in the United States in 2006, specifically because of documented serious adverse events and deaths associated with self-medication for leg cramps. This regulatory action was taken because quinine in non-prescription settings, without medical supervision and appropriate dosing precision, carried an unacceptable risk profile. That background made the 2020 encouragement to self-medicate with cinchona bark products — an even less standardised and less controlled form of exposure — particularly dangerous.

## Why People Believe This

The cinchona bark claim achieved its traction through several distinct and reinforcing mechanisms that illuminate how pandemic misinformation operates at the intersection of genuine science, political amplification, and supplement marketing.

**The amplification of chloroquine by political endorsement.** The timeline of the cinchona bark claim is inseparable from Trump's March 19, 2020 speech endorsing chloroquine as a "promising" treatment. Before that endorsement, public awareness of chloroquine as a possible COVID therapy was limited to people closely following medical and epidemiological reporting. After it, chloroquine became a mainstream topic of popular discussion and political polarisation in the United States and, to varying degrees, internationally. The drug became almost immediately politicised — associated with one political perspective and doubted by another — in ways that complicated evidence evaluation for many people.

In this environment, demand for chloroquine surged immediately. Patients with lupus and rheumatoid arthritis who depended on hydroxychloroquine as a maintenance medication began reporting that their pharmacies were running out. This created a genuine accessibility crisis for vulnerable patients and simultaneously a demand signal for alternatives. The cinchona bark claims emerged precisely into this gap: here was, apparently, a natural, accessible, unregulated alternative to a drug that the government couldn't supply fast enough.

**The genuine historical relationship.** Unlike many pieces of health misinformation that invoke fictional science, the cinchona bark claim rested on a real historical and botanical relationship. Cinchona bark genuinely was the precursor to modern antimalarial medicine. Quinine genuinely did inspire the development of synthetic antimalarials. This real history made the claim seem well-grounded to people who encountered it. Someone who knew that chloroquine was derived from traditional bark treatments would have an entirely plausible-sounding reason to think cinchona bark might do something useful. The claim required no imaginary science, only the erasure of crucial details about chemical structure, dosing, and evidence.

**The "natural is better" framework.** The claim also drew on a deeply embedded cultural framework that positions "natural" remedies as inherently safer, more authentic, or more effective than pharmaceutical products. In this framing, cinchona bark was not merely an alternative to chloroquine — it was the original, pure version of the same medicine, before it had been artificially modified and monopolised by the pharmaceutical industry. This narrative resonated powerfully with people already suspicious of conventional medicine and pharmaceutical companies. The fact that pharmaceutical regulation, clinical trial requirements, and prescription drug access contribute to both safety improvements and supply constraints was reframed as institutional control over what was originally a people's medicine.

**Pandemic profiteering in the supplement industry.** The 2020 pandemic was, among other things, an unprecedented commercial opportunity for the supplement and alternative medicine industries. Europol documented multiple interception operations targeting fake coronavirus cures attempting to enter European markets, including unauthorised chloroquine products. The ecosystem for cinchona bark supplementation was activated and expanded rapidly — suppliers who had previously sold cinchona bark products as niche botanical supplements found themselves with dramatically increased interest, and new sellers emerged to meet demand. Financial incentives aligned with claim propagation in ways that made the misinformation self-sustaining.

**The WHO's uncertainty as permission.** In the early weeks of the pandemic, public health authorities were genuinely uncertain about many things, including the potential of chloroquine-class drugs. That uncertainty was communicated honestly and appropriately, but it was widely interpreted as permission rather than caution. If scientists weren't yet sure chloroquine didn't work, then it remained possible that it did, and by extension that cinchona bark might be worth trying. The communication of scientific uncertainty under conditions of public fear and demand for certainty reliably produces this pattern: honest acknowledgment of what remains unknown gets weaponised as implicit support for unproven interventions.

## Verdict

**Cinchona bark is not a cure for coronavirus, does not contain chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine, and there is no evidence that quinine or other cinchona alkaloids have any activity against SARS-CoV-2.** The claim distorted a genuine historical relationship between cinchona bark and antimalarial drug development into a false equivalence between the plant's compounds and a synthetic pharmaceutical. It simultaneously depended on the premise that chloroquine or hydroxychloroquine were effective COVID-19 treatments — a premise that subsequent rigorous clinical trials failed to support.

More seriously, the claim encouraged self-medication with a botanical product carrying documented serious and potentially life-threatening toxicity risks, in a context where no safe dosing guidance existed, no standardisation of plant material was available, and no medical supervision was possible. The cinchona bark misinformation campaign was not merely ineffective but potentially actively dangerous.

The Truth Vault rating for this claim is **E: Exaggerated** — a genuine historical and botanical relationship between cinchona bark and antimalarial pharmacology was distorted into a false therapeutic equivalence with an unproven COVID-19 drug, without evidence for clinical effect and with suppression of significant safety information.

## The Wider Picture

The cinchona bark episode sits at the intersection of several recurring patterns in pandemic misinformation that extend well beyond the specific claim and the specific disease.

**The recurring pattern of "natural" antimalarials.** The cinchona bark story echoes other instances in which the genuine early history of antimalarial compounds was exploited to make speculative therapeutic claims. During the early AIDS crisis, various herbal and "traditional" remedies were promoted as alternatives to expensive and inaccessible antiretroviral drugs, with varying degrees of distortion of actual botanical pharmacology. Similar dynamics have played out with artemisinin — genuinely discovered in traditional Chinese herbal medicine and developed into a highly effective modern antimalarial — being used as support for claims about other traditional herbs whose compounds have not been similarly validated.

**The political dimension of COVID therapeutics.** The cinchona bark story cannot be fully understood outside the political context of COVID-19 treatment debates. Trump's endorsement of chloroquine made therapeutic claims for related compounds politically significant in ways unusual in the history of health misinformation. People who trusted Trump's assessment of chloroquine had reason to be responsive to claims about natural sources of related compounds. People who distrusted pharmaceutical industry responses to COVID had reason to be attracted to "natural" alternatives. The politicisation of a medical question created audience segmentation that the misinformation could exploit.

**Supply chain dynamics and prescription access.** One genuine harm from the chloroquine enthusiasm — and by extension from the cinchona bark supplementation wave — was the disruption to the prescription supply chain for hydroxychloroquine as a maintenance medication for autoimmune diseases. Tens of thousands of people with lupus and rheumatoid arthritis reported difficulty accessing their regular prescriptions during the period of highest COVID-era demand for hydroxychloroquine. This is a clear instance of how health misinformation can cause harms not only to those who act on the incorrect claims but also to innocent third parties affected by the secondary market disruptions the misinformation creates.

**Pandemic profiteering as a structural problem.** The European law enforcement seizures of fake coronavirus cures — 48,000 packages documented by Europol by late March 2020 — represent only the formally intercepted portion of an extremely large informal market for speculative COVID remedies. The structural problem is not that a few bad actors exploit panic for profit; it is that the existing supplement and herbal remedy industry infrastructures are largely designed to operate in the regulatory grey zone where claims can be made, products can be sold, and the consequence burden falls on consumers rather than producers. Pandemic conditions intensified rather than created this structural problem.

**The continuing legacy of traditional plant medicine.** It would be a mistake to read the cinchona bark misinformation episode as discrediting the entire history of phytotherapy or ethnobotanical research. Cinchona bark really did save countless lives over three centuries. Quinine from the bark was the first rationally deployed pharmacological anti-infective. The framework in which researchers look to traditional botanical uses as initial evidence for pharmacological activity is scientifically legitimate — it is precisely the framework through which artemisinin was discovered. The lesson is not that traditional plant medicine has nothing to offer but that the path from "traditionally used" to "clinically effective" requires rigorous scientific validation, and that the presence of traditional use is not, by itself, evidence of efficacy against a specific novel pathogen.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Like the vitamin C entry in the same Snopes batch, the cinchona bark article was published as a republished piece from *The Conversation* rather than a standard Snopes fact-check. Authored by medicinal plant researchers and published on 31 March 2020, it addressed the circulating claims with botanical specificity and pharmacological precision that standard fact-check formats often cannot achieve. The piece explained the chemical differences between quinine and chloroquine, the history of cinchona bark use in antimalarial medicine, and the toxicity risks of cinchona self-medication in detail appropriate to its academic readership.

The Snopes framing included the standard disclaimer distinguishing republished content from formal fact-checker work. The absence of a formal verdict category reflects this editorial distinction rather than uncertainty about the underlying facts. Independent fact-checking organisations including PolitiFact, Reuters Fact Check, AFP Fact Check, and others addressed chloroquine-related misinformation extensively during the same period, consistently rating claims about natural alternatives to chloroquine as false or misleading.

The specific claim about tonic water as a coronavirus preventive received direct debunking from multiple sources, with the key facts being (a) tonic water quinine concentrations are far below any therapeutic threshold and (b) quinine is not the same compound as chloroquine in any case. These were straightforward factual claims that fact-checkers could handle efficiently.

The broader lesson for fact-checking methodology embedded in the cinchona bark case is the importance of chemical and botanical specificity in health misinformation debunking. A claim built on the real relationship between a plant compound and a pharmaceutical compound cannot be effectively debunked with general statements about "there's no evidence." It requires explaining the specific ways in which the claimed relationship fails to hold: the chemical distinction between quinine and chloroquine, the dose–function relationship for quinine concentrations in tonic water, the absence of COVID-specific efficacy data even for pharmaceutical-grade chloroquine, and the toxicity risks of bark preparations at doses that might attempt to approximate drug-level quinine exposure.

Fact-checkers also face the challenge of the moving target in cases where the premise drug's status was itself under active dispute. The cinchona bark claims fed off uncertainty about chloroquine, and that uncertainty was itself being contested in real time through ongoing clinical trials. An ideal debunking had to simultaneously address the false equivalence between bark and drug, the lack of evidence for the drug itself in the claimed context, and the toxicity risks of the bark alternative — while doing so in a way that would remain coherent if the drug evidence subsequently changed. This kind of layered epistemic uncertainty is among the most difficult challenges in pandemic fact-checking.
