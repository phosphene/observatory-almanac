## 1. The Claim

In July 2021, the British news site The Independent published a headline that quickly went viral: "US cities trial 'sun block' on asphalt to slash city heat and break down harmful traffic pollution." The headline was tantalizing and provocative, conjuring images of municipal workers spreading sunscreen across miles of asphalt in a desperate attempt to combat rising temperatures. As West Coast states were experiencing record-breaking heat waves and wildfires, the idea that cities were applying literal sunblock to their roads captured the public imagination.

The claim, as it circulated across social media and news aggregators, was that American cities were using "sunblock" on pavement to combat extreme heat. The implication was clear: just as humans apply sunscreen to protect their skin from harmful UV rays, cities were applying a similar substance to protect their roads from the sun's radiation.

Snopes addressed this claim in a July 2021 article written by Bethania Palma, rating it "Mixture." The Snopes piece confirmed that some U.S. cities are indeed testing a material called PlusTi, manufactured by Pavement Technology Inc. of Cleveland, Ohio, which is applied to asphalt and contains titanium dioxide—the same substance used in many sunscreens. However, Snopes also made clear that the comparison to sunblock was figurative rather than literal. The material is not sunscreen for roads, despite the headline's implication.

This case study is particularly instructive because it highlights the gap between sensational journalism and genuine scientific innovation. The claim is both true and misleading simultaneously, and understanding why requires unpacking the chemistry of the material, the intentions of the cities deploying it, and the dynamics of media sensationalism. The Snopes "Mixture" rating is appropriate, but the story behind it illuminates important questions about how we communicate science to the public and how headlines can distort even accurate reporting.

## 2. What's Actually True

The core factual claim is true: some U.S. cities are testing a material that is applied to pavement and contains titanium dioxide, the same substance found in many sunscreens. The material, called PlusTi, is a pavement rejuvenator and sealer developed by Pavement Technology Inc., based in Cleveland, Ohio. According to company consultant Ken Holton, whom Snopes interviewed, PlusTi is currently being deployed in approximately ten cities across the United States, including Orlando (Florida), Charleston (South Carolina), Charlotte (North Carolina), and Cincinnati (Ohio).

PlusTi is not a coating that sits on top of the pavement. Unlike sunscreen, which forms a protective layer on the skin, PlusTi is a penetrant that is drawn into the asphalt itself. This is a crucial distinction. The material works from within the pavement matrix rather than forming a surface barrier. It does not wash away with rain, and it does not require reapplication after every storm. This makes it fundamentally different from the sunscreen analogy suggests.

The primary mechanism by which PlusTi works involves the photocatalytic properties of titanium dioxide. When titanium dioxide is exposed to ultraviolet light, it undergoes a chemical reaction that releases free electrons. These free electrons create highly reactive molecules called free radicals that float briefly above the pavement surface and break down pollutants, particularly nitrogen oxides (NOx) from vehicle exhaust. The Charleston Post and Courier, in a detailed article cited by Snopes, reported that this process can reduce nitrous oxide levels by up to 40 percent.

In addition to its pollution-reducing properties, PlusTi also helps keep asphalt cooler. The titanium dioxide reflects and scatters some of the sun's ultraviolet radiation, reducing the amount of solar energy absorbed by the dark pavement. This contributes to the mitigation of the urban heat island effect, a phenomenon in which built-up areas experience significantly higher temperatures than surrounding rural areas due to the heat-absorbing properties of materials like asphalt and concrete.

The chemistry at work is genuinely elegant. Titanium dioxide is a photocatalyst, which means it uses light energy to accelerate chemical reactions without being consumed in the process. This same property is what makes titanium dioxide useful in self-cleaning glass, air purification systems, and certain types of paint. When incorporated into pavement, it creates a surface that actively cleans the air above it while also managing heat absorption.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

The Snopes article on this topic is one of the better examples of the fact-checking genre, and it deserves recognition for its balanced and nuanced approach. The "Mixture" rating is appropriate, and the article does a good job of explaining both the factual basis of the claim and the ways in which it is misleading.

First, Snopes correctly identifies that the "sunblock" framing is figurative, not literal. The article clearly states that "no, cities aren't spreading Coppertone on roads," and it explains the difference between the sunscreen analogy and the actual chemical mechanism of PlusTi. This is a crucial distinction that many readers might miss if they only saw the headline.

Second, Snopes provides direct sourcing from the company developing PlusTi, including a quote from consultant Ken Holton. This gives the article credibility and provides readers with a clear picture of what the technology actually does and where it is being deployed.

Third, Snopes correctly identifies the broader context of heat waves and climate adaptation that gave the story its viral appeal. The article acknowledges that West Coast states were experiencing record-breaking temperatures when the Independent story broke, and it situates the PlusTi trials within the larger conversation about how cities can adapt to a warming climate.

However, the Snopes article has several notable limitations.

First, while the article clarifies that the "sunblock" comparison is figurative, it does not provide an adequate explanation of what PlusTi actually is and how it works. The chemistry of photocatalysis is mentioned but not explained in a way that would be accessible to a general reader. A reader coming away from the Snopes article might understand that PlusTi is not literally sunblock, but they might not have a clear picture of what it actually does or why it matters.

Second, the Snopes article is quite brief and does not explore the broader implications of the technology. PlusTi is part of a larger movement toward "cool pavements"—surfaces designed to reflect more solar radiation and absorb less heat than traditional asphalt. The EPA has published guidance on cool pavements as a strategy for mitigating urban heat islands, and several cities have experimented with reflective coatings, permeable pavements, and other innovative materials. By focusing narrowly on the "sunblock" question, Snopes misses an opportunity to educate readers about this important trend in urban infrastructure.

Third, the Snopes article does not address the potential limitations or downsides of the PlusTi technology. No technology is without trade-offs, and a thorough analysis would consider questions about cost, durability, scalability, and potential environmental impacts. Does the photocatalytic process create any harmful byproducts? How long does the treatment last? How much does it cost compared to traditional pavement maintenance? These are important questions that the Snopes article does not raise.

Fourth, the article does not explore the role of media sensationalism in creating misleading impressions. The Independent's headline was designed to go viral, and it succeeded. The Snopes article corrects the record, but it does not analyze why the headline was misleading or what this tells us about the challenges of communicating complex scientific information through news media. A deeper analysis would examine the incentives that drive sensational headlines and the responsibility of journalists to accurately represent scientific research.

## 4. Why This Matters

The "sunblock on pavement" story matters for several reasons, all of which extend beyond the specific claim about PlusTi.

First, this case illustrates the challenges of communicating complex scientific concepts through popular media. The comparison between PlusTi and sunblock is not entirely wrong—both contain titanium dioxide and both interact with UV light. But the comparison is also deeply misleading, because the mechanisms are fundamentally different. Sunscreen works by absorbing or reflecting UV radiation before it reaches the skin, forming a physical barrier. PlusTi works through photocatalysis, a chemical reaction that uses UV light to create free radicals that break down pollutants. Communicating this distinction to a general audience is difficult, but it is essential if the public is to understand what the technology actually does.

This challenge is not unique to the PlusTi story. From "quantum" everything to "artificial intelligence" to "superfood," the popular press regularly uses analogies that are more misleading than illuminating. The fact-checking community plays a crucial role in correcting these misimpressions, but the corrections often receive far less attention than the original sensational headlines.

Second, this case matters because it highlights the potential of innovative infrastructure technologies to address pressing environmental challenges. Urban heat islands are a serious problem, particularly in low-income neighborhoods and communities of color that often have less tree cover and more impervious surface area. Extreme heat is the deadliest weather-related hazard in the United States, killing more people each year than hurricanes, floods, and tornadoes combined. Technologies like cool pavements, green roofs, and reflective building materials offer promising strategies for reducing heat exposure in urban areas.

The "sunblock" framing, despite its inaccuracy, may have the beneficial effect of drawing attention to these technologies. Many people who would never read an article about photocatalytic pavement rejuvenators might click on a headline about "sunblock for roads." The sensational framing raises awareness, even if it does so in a misleading way. This creates a difficult tension for fact-checkers and science communicators: correcting the inaccuracies without diminishing public interest in the underlying technologies.

Third, this case illustrates the importance of reading beyond the headline. The Independent's article, despite its sensational headline, was a reasonably accurate description of the PlusTi technology. Readers who clicked through and read the full article would have gotten a much more nuanced picture than those who only saw the headline on social media. The gap between headline and article content is a persistent problem in online news, and the Snopes fact-check serves as a corrective for those who may have been misled.

Finally, this case highlights the need for a more sophisticated approach to evaluating claims about technology and science. The "Mixture" rating is appropriate for the specific claim about "sunblock," but it does not capture the full complexity of the situation. The claim is true in the sense that cities are applying a titanium dioxide-based material to pavement that does some of the same things as sunblock. It is false in the sense that the material is not a literal sunblock and works through a different mechanism. Reducing this complexity to a single rating—even a nuanced one like "Mixture"—inevitably loses some of the richness of the story.

## 5. Research Notes

The urban heat island effect is one of the most thoroughly documented phenomena in climate science. Research published by the EPA and academic institutions has shown that urban areas can be 1-7 degrees Fahrenheit warmer during the day and 2-5 degrees warmer at night than surrounding rural areas. This temperature differential is caused by the replacement of natural, vegetated surfaces with dark, impervious materials like asphalt and concrete, which absorb solar radiation and release it as heat.

Cool pavement technologies have been studied extensively as a mitigation strategy. The Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory has conducted research on reflective pavements, finding that increasing the albedo (solar reflectance) of urban surfaces can significantly reduce surface temperatures and ambient air temperatures. The lab's research has shown that raising the albedo of a city's pavements by just 0.1 can reduce ambient air temperature by 0.3 degrees Celsius, a modest but meaningful reduction when scaled across an entire urban area.

Titanium dioxide photocatalysis is a well-established area of materials science. The phenomenon was first observed in the 1970s by Akira Fujishima and Kenichi Honda, who discovered that titanium dioxide electrodes could split water molecules when exposed to UV light. This discovery opened up a vast field of research into photocatalytic applications, including self-cleaning surfaces, air and water purification, and hydrogen production. The application of titanium dioxide to pavement for pollution reduction is a relatively recent development, but it builds on decades of fundamental research.

The Pavement Technology Inc. website and technical documentation for PlusTi provide detailed information about the product's composition, application, and performance. According to the company, PlusTi is a "petroleum-based asphalt rejuvenator and sealer" that contains titanium dioxide nanoparticles. The material is applied using standard pavement maintenance equipment and is designed to penetrate the asphalt surface to a depth of approximately one-quarter inch. The company claims that the treatment can extend the life of asphalt pavement by three to five years, in addition to its heat-reduction and pollution-reduction benefits.

The Charleston Post and Courier's 2021 article on Charleston County's trial of PlusTi is one of the most detailed journalistic accounts of the technology. The article reports that the city applied the treatment to a test section of road and is monitoring its performance over time. It quotes local officials who express cautious optimism about the technology's potential while acknowledging that more data is needed before widespread deployment.

The Independent's original article, which sparked the viral spread of the "sunblock for roads" story, is worth examining for its framing. The article's headline leans heavily on the sunblock analogy, but the body text provides a more nuanced description of the technology. This gap between headline and content is a common feature of modern journalism, driven by the incentives of click-based advertising and social media sharing. The Snopes fact-check serves as a useful corrective, but it reaches a smaller audience than the original headline.

An area that deserves further investigation is the long-term performance and environmental impact of titanium dioxide pavement treatments. While the photocatalytic reaction that breaks down NOx pollutants is well understood in laboratory conditions, the real-world effectiveness of the technology over extended periods is less certain. Factors such as weather, traffic wear, and the accumulation of dirt and debris on the pavement surface may reduce the material's effectiveness over time. Additionally, the environmental fate of the reaction byproducts is not fully characterized.

The equity dimensions of cool pavement technology are another area worthy of exploration. Urban heat islands disproportionately affect low-income neighborhoods and communities of color, which tend to have less tree cover and more impervious surfaces. If cool pavement technologies are deployed primarily in wealthier areas, they could exacerbate existing environmental inequalities. Conversely, if they are targeted to the neighborhoods that need them most, they could be a powerful tool for environmental justice.

## 6. Verdict

The claim that U.S. cities are using "sunblock" on pavement is a mixture of truth and misleading framing. Snopes correctly rates this claim as "Mixture." The material in question, PlusTi, is a real product that is being tested in multiple American cities, and it does contain titanium dioxide, a key ingredient in many sunscreens. It does reduce pavement temperature and break down pollutants. However, referring to it as "sunblock" is a figurative convenience, not an accurate description of its mechanism or purpose.

The Snopes article provides a balanced and accurate account of the technology, clarifying the distinction between the sensational headline and the scientific reality. However, the article is too brief to fully explore the chemistry of photocatalysis, the broader context of cool pavement technology, or the societal implications of urban heat island mitigation. Readers who want a deeper understanding will need to consult additional sources.

This case illustrates both the strengths and limitations of the fact-checking model. Snopes serves a valuable function by correcting misleading impressions and providing accurate information about the PlusTi technology. But the complexity of the story resists easy reduction to a single rating, and the most important lessons—about the challenges of science communication, the dynamics of viral media, and the potential of innovative infrastructure—require more space than a typical fact-check can provide.

The "sunblock for roads" story is a useful reminder that truth is often more complicated than headlines suggest. The cities are doing something real and potentially important with their pavement treatments, but calling it "sunblock" tells only a small part of the story. The full truth is more nuanced, more interesting, and ultimately more valuable than the catchy headline.
