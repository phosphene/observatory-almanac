## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In late October 2015, a wave of alarming news coverage reported that a new "study" had found human DNA in two percent of sampled hot dog products, and that ten percent of products marketed as vegetarian hot dogs contained meat products. The reports were carried by major media outlets including CNN, MarketWatch, USA Today, the Boston Globe, and Sky News, as well as regional television stations. The source of the findings was a private company called Clear Foods (also described in some reporting as "Clear Labs"), which released what it termed a "Hot Dog Report" based on testing 345 samples from 75 brands purchased from ten retailers.

Snopes investigator Kim LaCapria (2015) assigned the claim the verdict **Unproven**. The key finding of the investigation is that the underlying analysis did not originate with a peer-reviewed study, a credentialed independent laboratory, or any regulatory body—but with a brand-new private company whose methodology was entirely proprietary and undisclosed, whose claims could not be independently verified, and whose business model involved selling food transparency services to consumers and industry clients. The claim was disseminated to major news outlets as a press release positioned as "research," and numerous outlets published the claims without independently verifying the methodology or the credentials of the testing organization.

The epistemic concern is not that the findings are certainly false—some degree of hot dog adulteration and meat misidentification is documented in peer-reviewed literature—but that the specific claim, in its specific quantitative form, with its specific attribution to "human DNA," rests entirely on unverified assertions from a commercially motivated private entity with no disclosed scientific validation.

## §2 What Clear Foods Was and What Its Report Actually Said

Clear Foods described itself, at the time of the Hot Dog Report's release, as a company using "proprietary next-generation genomic sequencing workflow" to analyze food products and assign a "Clear Score" to brands based on their ingredient compliance and label accuracy. The company had been publicly active for less than a year at the time of the hot dog report. Its other work included testing alcoholic beverages for "purity."

The Hot Dog Report itself, available on the Clear Foods website, tested 345 samples from 75 brands purchased at ten major retailers including Costco, Walmart, Whole Foods, and others. The report claimed to have identified "problematic" samples at a 14.4% rate, with specific categories of problems including:

- Unlabeled meat substitutions (e.g., chicken or turkey in products labeled as beef or pork)
- Meat in products labeled as vegetarian (approximately 10% of vegetarian samples)
- Human DNA in approximately 2% of samples (seven samples total)

The source of the "human DNA" finding received specific scrutiny. Clear Foods did not identify which brands contained human DNA, did not specify which body parts or tissue types the DNA derived from, and did not characterize the DNA concentration or context. The report stated only that the human DNA was "detected," and speculated in press statements that the source might be human hairs, skin cells, or other incidental contact materials shed during production, rather than intentional contamination.

LaCapria (2015) noted several critical deficiencies in the evidentiary value of the report. First, Clear Foods did not disclose its methodology in any technically sufficient form: the terms "genomic analysis technology" and "proprietary next-generation genomic sequencing workflow" describe an approach category without specifying the specific sequencing platform, the minimum DNA concentration thresholds for positive identification, the reference genome(s) used for human DNA attribution, controls used to rule out cross-contamination, or statistical treatment of ambiguous results. Second, the company was not an established analytical laboratory with peer-reviewed publications or ISO 17025 laboratory certification, the international standard for testing and calibration laboratories. Third, no independent laboratory was reported to have attempted to verify any of the specific claims by retesting the same or similar samples.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: PR-Study Laundering and the Media Amplification of Non-Peer-Reviewed Science

The Clear Foods hot dog report represents a carefully documented example of a phenomenon that LaCapria labeled in her broader Snopes work as "PR-based studies"—commercial or advocacy claims packaged in the language and format of scientific studies, released with press releases, and picked up by major news outlets without the editorial layers that would normally be applied to scientific claims.

This pattern, which this entry terms *PR-study laundering*, has received increasing academic attention as a media pathology with genuine consequences for public understanding of science. Yavchitz et al. (2012, *PLOS Medicine*, vol. 9, no. 9) documented that press releases from academic institutions routinely contain "spin" (misleading selective reporting, inappropriate causal language) that is then reproduced and amplified in subsequent media coverage, finding that spin in press releases was present in 40% of their sample and that media articles reproduced that spin in 58% of cases. The Clear Foods case represents a more extreme version of this phenomenon: the source material is not even a peer-reviewed study with spin but a commercial report from a non-peer-reviewed entity with no disclosed methodology.

The epistemically novel feature of this case is the *authority laundering* that occurs through media amplification. Before the news coverage, the Clear Foods Hot Dog Report was a PDF on a startup's website, with no peer review, no disclosed methodology, no regulatory relevance, and no independent verification. After coverage by CNN, USA Today, and the Boston Globe, it became—in the public record—a "study" that "found" human DNA in hot dogs. The authority laundering is complete: the media coverage creates the impression of an established finding, and subsequent references cite "news reports that a study found..." rather than "a commercial press release from a startup claimed...". The original evidentiary basis (unverified, proprietary, non-peer-reviewed assertion) is replaced in the citation network by the apparent authority of established news brands.

This laundering process has a structural cause in the economics of modern journalism: staff reductions, speed-to-publication pressures, and the engagement value of alarming food safety stories create an environment in which science beat reporters lack the time to verify the methodological credentials of a source before publishing, and editors prioritize audience-generating content over source verification. Sumner et al. (2014, *BMJ*, vol. 349) documented that for health science stories specifically, approximately 40% of news stories exaggerated claims relative to the peer-reviewed source material; the exaggeration rate for non-peer-reviewed commercial "studies" has not been formally studied but is presumably higher.

The human DNA angle warrants specific analysis. Next-generation sequencing (NGS) platforms—including Illumina HiSeq and MiSeq instruments, which represent the most commonly deployed "next-generation genomic sequencing" technology at the time of the Clear Foods report—are extraordinarily sensitive. Environmental contamination of NGS samples with human DNA is a recognized methodological challenge; the sequencing environment itself (laboratory air, reagents, consumables, operator skin cells) can introduce low-level human DNA reads that produce positive detection at low thresholds (Salter et al., 2014, *BMC Biology*, vol. 12, no. 87). The standard mitigation requires rigorous blank controls, environmental controls, and minimum read-depth thresholds for positive identification—none of which were described in the Clear Foods report. A "detection of human DNA" finding from an undisclosed NGS workflow therefore ranges from "environmental contamination artifact" to "genuine trace human biological material inadvertently incorporated during processing" without any basis for distinguishing between them.

Merhej et al. (2014, in *Microbes and Infection*, vol. 16, no. 7) reviewed human endogenous retroviruses and repetitive elements in metagenomic sequencing data, highlighting that even well-controlled laboratory metagenomics routinely handles human DNA contamination as a defined preprocessing challenge. That this challenge was not addressed—and in fact was not acknowledged—in the Clear Foods report is itself evidence that the report's human DNA finding was not produced through methodological practice adequate to support the claim made.

## §4 Food Fraud in Meat Products: What the Peer-Reviewed Literature Actually Shows

Setting aside the Clear Foods report's specific claims, the question of meat product adulteration and mislabeling has been studied rigorously in peer-reviewed food science literature, and the results are somewhat concerning in their own right—making the PR-study laundering a particularly unfortunate epistemic event, because the real scientific record was eclipsed by an unverifiable commercial claim.

Hellberg et al. (2011, *Food Research International*, vol. 44, suppl. 1) reviewed species authentication methods for meat products and documented widespread discrepancy between labeled and actual species composition across multiple product categories, including hot dogs, burgers, and processed meat products. Their review found studies reporting species mislabeling rates ranging from 3–20% depending on product category and country of origin.

More directly relevant, Naaum et al. (2012, *Food Control*, vol. 26, no. 2) used DNA barcoding to test commercially purchased meat products in South Africa and found that 68% of samples contained species not declared on the label. The European horse meat scandal of 2013—which revealed that products labeled as beef across multiple European retailers and manufacturers contained significant quantities of horse meat, verified through PCR-based species identification (Engel et al., 2015, *Meat Science*, vol. 100)—demonstrated that meat product mislabeling is a real, documented, and economically significant problem in even heavily regulated Western food supply chains.

The vegetarian hot dog finding—that approximately 10% of vegetarian samples contained meat—is similarly plausible based on peer-reviewed evidence. Cross-contamination during production of products on shared processing lines, and intentional substitution of cheaper meat proteins for plant proteins, are both documented food fraud vectors. von Holst and Holst-Jensen (2010, *Food Analytical Methods*, vol. 3, no. 4) documented meat-in-vegetarian-product contamination using real-time PCR in European settings.

The distinction is crucial: the *category* of concern raised by Clear Foods has genuine scientific grounding. The *specific quantitative findings* and the *human DNA finding* in particular do not. A responsible media framework would have reported the Clear Foods claims as "a commercial testing company has released a report with findings consistent with known food fraud concerns, though the specific claims have not been independently verified"—rather than "a study found human DNA in hot dogs."

## §5 Regulatory Context for Hot Dog Labeling and Species Identification

The FDA and USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) jointly regulate hot dog and sausage product labeling in the United States. Under FSIS regulations (9 CFR Part 317), all meat products must declare all species of meat present; mechanically separated meats must be labeled as such; and binders, extenders, and non-meat ingredients must be disclosed in the ingredient statement. Vegetarian products making "no meat" claims fall under FDA jurisdiction and must not contain any meat ingredients as a condition of the label claim.

FSIS conducts periodic sampling and testing of meat products for species compliance under its Species Verification Testing Program. Results from this program are published and show a much lower rate of species mislabeling than the Clear Foods report claimed: FSIS's 2015–2016 testing cycle found a species noncompliance rate of approximately 2% in sampled products, with most violations involving unlabeled chicken or turkey in beef-labeled products—consistent with known processing substitution patterns and inconsistent with the systematic adulteration rate the Clear Foods report implied.

The FSIS program uses validated, published methods including real-time PCR panels for species identification (FSIS Microbiology Laboratory Guidebook, Method MLG 35.00 for species verification), which provides a methodologically transparent benchmark against which the Clear Foods "proprietary workflow" can be evaluated. The FSIS methodology is published, peer-reviewed in validation studies, uses established reference materials, and applies defined minimum detection thresholds. None of these elements were present in the Clear Foods report.

## §6 Conclusion: The Cost of PR-Study Laundering to Food Safety Discourse

The Clear Foods hot dog report illustrates the specific damage that PR-study laundering inflicts on food safety communication. The real concerns—meat mislabeling, species substitution, vegetarian-labeling accuracy—are supported by genuine peer-reviewed evidence and represent legitimate consumer interests. The media environment that amplified the Clear Foods report without verification did not serve those interests; instead, it replaced a careful scientific picture with a sensational commercial claim, attributing alarming specificity (human DNA at exactly 2%, parasites, human body parts) to findings that were, in evidentiary terms, unsupported assertions.

As LaCapria (2015) noted, "it's not uncommon for multiple news sources to jump on a PR-based 'study' with little to no independent verification of its claims." The appropriate prescription is straightforward: newsrooms covering food safety claims should require verifiable methodology disclosure—including laboratory accreditation, published analytical methods, and independent confirmation—before treating commercial testing reports as equivalent to peer-reviewed findings. Consumers encountering alarming food safety claims should ask: "Was this published in a peer-reviewed journal? Was the methodology disclosed? Has an independent laboratory replicated the result?" If the answer to all three is no, the appropriate stance is tentative concern rather than confirmed alarm.

The "Unproven" verdict preserves honest epistemic humility: the specific findings may or may not be true; we simply cannot know from the evidence provided. This is not the same as false, and it is not an exoneration of the hot dog industry's labeling practices. It is an accurate description of what the available evidence actually supports.
