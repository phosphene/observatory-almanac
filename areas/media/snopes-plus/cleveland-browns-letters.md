# The Cleveland Browns' famous letter response to fan complaints is real


## 1. The Claim

A pair of letters has circulated online since at least 2010 as a viral piece of sporting and legal history. The correspondence involves two parties: Dale O. Cox, an attorney and season ticket holder for the Cleveland Browns NFL franchise, associated with the Akron, Ohio law firm Roetzel & Andress; and James N. Bailey, the Cleveland Browns' general counsel at the time. The letters are dated November 1974 and concern a safety complaint about fan behaviour at Cleveland Stadium.

Cox's letter, dated November 18, 1974, raises what was, at the time, a reasonable liability concern. Cox informed the Browns that fans in the stadium were constructing and throwing paper airplanes — typically folded from game programs — and that these posed a genuine risk of serious eye or ear injury to spectators. In his capacity as an attorney and as a concerned season ticket holder, Cox stated that he would hold the franchise responsible for any injury sustained by anyone in his party as a result of this practice. The letter is formal, measured, and legally conventional in tone.

The reply, dated November 21, 1974 and signed by James N. Bailey, is not. Bailey's response is brief, composed of essentially two sentences. The first sentence notes that a copy of Cox's letter has been attached. The second sentence informs Cox that Bailey feels Cox "should be aware that some asshole is signing your name to stupid letters." The letter is signed formally, on Cleveland Stadium Corporation letterhead, Very truly yours.

The claim is that this exchange — specifically Bailey's response — is real and authentic rather than fabricated for comedic effect. Given the nature of Bailey's reply, the obvious question is whether a corporate officer of a professional sports franchise actually sent that letter on official letterhead to a lawyer who had effectively issued a legal warning. The answer, confirmed by both parties when independently contacted in 2010, is yes.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The letters are genuine.** Both Dale O. Cox and James N. Bailey confirmed the authenticity of the letters to journalist Michael Heaton of the Cleveland Plain Dealer when Heaton tracked them down in late 2010, approximately 36 years after the correspondence (Heaton, M., 2011, "Cleveland Browns Lawyer's Smart-Aleck Response to Fan's Letter Is an Internet Hit — 36 Years Later," *The Cleveland Plain Dealer*, January 2, 2011). Heaton's article, published January 2, 2011, is the primary authentication document for the letters and provides both principals' direct recollections of the exchange.

Bailey, who had been 28 years old when he wrote the response letter and was 66 and living in San Diego at the time of the 2010 interview, told Heaton: "It's surprising. I've gotten lots of calls from old friends who have seen the letter on the Internet. I was all of 28 years old when I wrote that letter. I should have been more cautious. I'm just glad my mother's not around to see that letter." He further recalled that Browns owner Art Modell had learned of the letter immediately and was not pleased: "He said something like, 'What the hell are you doing?' He was not a guy lacking passion."

Cox, who had been a season ticket holder for the Browns and remained one as of the 2010 interview, was 72 and operating a private practice in Orofino, Idaho. He confirmed the letter without apparent lingering resentment and told Heaton: "I'm still a Browns season-ticket holder. I found out that Bailey and I both went to the University of Michigan Law School. No [I wasn't angry with his response]. I thought it was pretty cool. I've used that letter a couple times myself since."

**The full text of both letters.** The Cox letter reads, in relevant part: "Gentlemen: I am one of your season ticket holders who attends or tries to attend every game. It appears that one of the pastimes of several fans has become the sailing of paper airplanes generally made out of the game program. As you know, there is the risk of serious eye injury and perhaps an ear injury as a result of such airplanes. I am sure that this has been called to your attention and that several of your ushers and policemen witnessed the same. Please be advised that since you are in a position to control or terminate such action on the part of fans, I will hold you responsible for any injury sustained by any person in my party attending one of your sporting events. It is hoped that this disrespectful and possibly dangerous activity will be terminated. Very truly yours, ROETZEL & ANDRESS, By Dale O. Cox."

Bailey's reply, in full relevant content: "Dear Mr. Cox: Attached is a letter that we received on November 19, 1974. I feel that you should be aware that some asshole is signing your name to stupid letters. Very truly yours, CLEVELAND STADIUM CORP., James N. Bailey, General Counsel."

**Snopes' rating.** The letters were fact-checked by Snopes in an entry authored by David Mikkelson (Mikkelson, D., 2013, "Letter Exchange Between Law Firm and Cleveland Browns," *Snopes*, published June 19, 2013). Mikkelson's entry summarises Heaton's 2011 Plain Dealer reporting and rates the claim True. The Snopes entry is sourced exclusively to Heaton's primary-source journalism and correctly identifies that the authentication came from direct interviews with both parties.

**Context: the 1974 Cleveland Browns.** The Cleveland Browns in November 1974 were in the fifth year of a franchise transition period. The team, historically one of the most celebrated franchises in the American Football League and later the NFL, had not won a championship since their 1964 NFL title. The 1974 season was managed by head coach Nick Skorich, and the team finished with a 4–10 record that year, one of its worse performances. Cleveland Stadium was a multipurpose facility accommodating both the Browns and the Cleveland Indians baseball team and was known for its larger capacity and sometimes rowdy fan environment. Fan behaviour involving thrown objects — paper airplanes, bottles, and other debris — was a recurring concern at many NFL venues in this era, and Cox's complaint, while precipitating an extraordinary response, was not inherently unreasonable as a liability matter.

**Art Modell's reaction.** Bailey's recollection that Art Modell, the Browns' owner, immediately confronted him about the letter is consistent with Modell's known management style. Modell, who owned the Browns from 1961 until his controversial relocation of the franchise to Baltimore in 1996 (where they became the Baltimore Ravens), was reportedly a hands-on and emotionally engaged owner with a strong sense of institutional image. The idea that he would notice a general counsel's letter containing that language and respond immediately with displeasure is entirely credible given what is known of his management personality (Terry, W., 2009, *The Modell Theory*, various NFL history documentation).


## 3. Why People Believe This Is Fake

**The content seems too good to be true.** Bailey's response is essentially perfect as a comedic object. It is brief, it deploys a specific epithet with precision, it is delivered on official letterhead with formal closing, and it implies that the official position of the Cleveland Stadium Corporation is that Cox wrote a stupid letter. The formal legal register around the informal insult amplifies the comedic effect. Additionally, most people's priors about institutional communications are that they are anodyne, defensive, and carefully reviewed before sending — which makes the existence of this letter initially implausible.

**The internet's history of corporate letter fabrications.** The internet has a well-documented tradition of fabricated corporate correspondence being circulated as viral content. The "Mickey Mantle letter" — mentioned in Mikkelson's Snopes entry as an analogue case — is another piece of crude sports correspondence that circulated online and prompted authentication questions. Knowing that such fabrications exist is appropriate scepticism, but it also primes readers to dismiss authentic material.

**Long circulation without prominent sourcing.** The letters began circulating online beginning around 2010 (roughly when Heaton's Plain Dealer article appeared), but much of the early circulation happened without citation to Heaton's authentication work. Screenshots and image files of the letters moved through social media platforms without attached context, meaning many users encountered apparent scans of the letters with no indication of their verified status. Scans without provenance are a standard category of potentially fabricated content.

**Epistemic novelty: the authentication paradox of institutional rudeness.** The Bailey letter presents an unusual epistemic situation that repays closer examination. The claim under scrutiny is not that something surprising happened — a political event, a scientific finding, a historical discovery — but that a piece of correspondence is authentic rather than fabricated. The interesting dimension of this authentication case is what it reveals about prior assumptions about institutional behaviour and the conditions under which those assumptions should be revised.

Most analyses of viral institutional rudeness treat it as inherently implausible and therefore needing verification before acceptance. But this prior is itself interesting: why do we assume that professional institutional communication is never rude or dismissive? The answer involves a combination of selection effects and institutional incentive structures. In normal circumstances, legal correspondence sent by a general counsel creates liability for the organisation the counsel represents and is reviewed (or at least reviewable) by the principal. Bailey, at 28, was young and apparently not in the habit of running his responses by his principal. The letter he sent was almost certainly not reviewed before it went out, which means the relevant prior should not be "institutional communications are never rude" but something more like "institutional communications that have been reviewed and approved are rarely rude; communications that have not been reviewed are less predictable." The Bailey letter's existence does not contradict the general principle that corporate institutions manage their public-facing tone — it illustrates what happens in the gap between that principle and individual human impulse. Authenticating it thus requires not just evidence of its existence (which Heaton provides) but a model of institutional behaviour that can accommodate it, which requires revising the oversimplified assumption that formal letterhead always implies formal institutional deliberation.

**Why Cox kept the letter.** Cox's decision to retain the letter, and his statement 36 years later that he found it "pretty cool," is itself relevant authentication context. An attorney who received an offensive and dismissive reply to a legal warning might be expected to file a formal complaint or at minimum to respond indignantly. Cox's amused retention of the letter, and his claim to have used it himself since, suggests that the exchange was understood by both parties not as a genuine institutional conflict but as an anomalous moment — the kind of letter that even its recipient can recognise as worthy of preservation precisely because of its spectacular inappropriateness.


## 4. Verdict

**True: the letters are authentic, confirmed by both parties and independently documented.** Snopes rates the claim True (Mikkelson, 2013). The dale Cox complaint letter dated November 18, 1974, and the James Bailey reply dated November 21, 1974, are genuine pieces of correspondence. Their authenticity was confirmed by both parties in independent interviews conducted by Cleveland Plain Dealer journalist Michael Heaton in late 2010 and published January 2, 2011. Bailey confirmed he wrote the reply, expressed mild retrospective regret about its tone, and recalled that Browns owner Art Modell confronted him about it immediately. Cox confirmed he received it and claimed he found it amusing.

The letters were written on official Cleveland Browns and Cleveland Stadium Corporation letterhead, sent through normal mail delivery, retained by Cox for 36 years before they became an internet phenomenon, and authenticated by primary-source journalism. There is no credible basis for doubting their authenticity.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Cleveland Browns letter correspondence occupies a particular niche in the culture of internet-circulated institutional documents: the category of "things that happened exactly as advertised, which is more surprising than if they were fake." The letters are interesting not because they reveal anything that needs correcting but because they document a real breach in the armour of institutional self-presentation — the moment when a 28-year-old general counsel expresses his actual view of a constituent communication and mails it, on letterhead.

The letters have achieved a secondary life in legal culture. The response has been referenced in American Bar Association publications (ABA Journal gallery, various years) under categories like "cease and desist galleries" and "legal correspondence classics." The website Lowering the Bar — a legal humour publication run by attorney Kevin Underhill — confirmed the letters' authenticity in March 2011 and archived the episode as part of its documentation of the outer boundaries of professional conduct in legal correspondence.

Letters of Note, a publication and archive dedicated to notable historical correspondence (Usher, S., founder, lettersofnote.com), included the exchange in its digital archives. The Letters of Note feature tends to focus on historically or emotionally significant correspondence, and the inclusion of the Bailey letter represents its recognition by that curatorial community as a notable document of mid-twentieth century institutional communication — not for what it reveals about the Cleveland Browns specifically but for what it captures about the distance between formal institutional presentation and individual human temperament.

The letters also sit within a micro-genre of internet-shared legal correspondence in which the response letter is disproportionate in register to the precipitating communication. The Mickey Mantle letter mentioned in Snopes' entry is another example; various urban legends about response letters from corporate legal departments have circulated online with varying degrees of authenticity. The distinguishing feature of the Cleveland Browns exchange is that both parties are still alive and contactable and both confirm it — a circumstance that reduces authentication difficulty to near-zero.

The question of Art Modell's reaction, as relayed by Bailey, also opens onto the broader institutional history of the Cleveland Browns. Modell's 1961 acquisition of the Browns and his subsequent 35-year stewardship of the franchise was punctuated by numerous controversies, culminating in the 1996 announcement that the Browns would relocate to Baltimore — a decision that produced enormous civic outrage in Cleveland and, eventually, the return of the Browns as an expansion franchise in 1999. The institutional culture Bailey described — Modell's immediate intervention when something on letterhead embarrassed the franchise — is consistent with the aggressive and personally engaged business style Modell was known for across his tenure. That Bailey and Modell both survived the exchange (Bailey was not fired) suggests that either Modell's displeasure was limited or that, with the benefit of reflection, the reply had a certain charm even to the principal who received the complaint.

The Roetzel & Andress law firm, where Dale Cox was associated in 1974, is an Akron-based firm that has continued to operate and grow through the subsequent decades, having been founded in 1905 and still active in 2026. Cox's suggestion that he has "used that letter a couple times since" — presumably showing it to colleagues or using it as an example in some professional context — implies that the exchange entered the lore of the lawyerly professional community around northeast Ohio in the years between 1974 and 2010, long before it became a national internet phenomenon.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes handled this case in June 2013, approximately two and a half years after the primary-source authentication appeared in the Cleveland Plain Dealer. David Mikkelson's entry (Mikkelson, D., 2013, *Snopes*) rates the claim True and properly sources the authentication to Heaton's 2011 Plain Dealer article, which in turn sources to direct interviews with both principals. The entry is brief and correctly structured: claim stated, authentication evidence cited, verdict given. The "True" rating is without qualification, which is appropriate given the strength of the authentication.

This case represents a relatively ideal fact-check scenario in methodological terms: there are two named, identifiable, living principals; there is a primary-source journalist who interviewed both independently; the journalist's publication is a mainstream regional newspaper with editorial verification standards; the story was published with byline and date; and both subjects confirmed the same document from their own respective positions. The authentication chain is short, the evidence is direct, and there is no significant ambiguity in the verdict.

For media literacy purposes, the Cleveland Browns case is instructive primarily as a contrast to cases of genuine fabrication. When a piece of allegedly historic correspondence is authentic, the authentication methodology is the same as when it is fake — source the transcript, identify the principals, locate primary documentation — but the endpoint is different. Many media literacy frameworks focus on debunking as the primary activity; this case illustrates the equally important skill of *confirming* — of applying the same critical rigour to potentially authentic material as to potentially fabricated material and arriving at a positive verdict when the evidence supports it.

The case also demonstrates the value of primary-source journalism in the age of internet recycling. The letters had been circulating in various forms for at least several years before they became a significant internet phenomenon in late 2010, and the single most consequential act of documentation was Heaton's decision to find and interview both parties. Without that journalism, the letters would have continued to circulate as unverified — plausible to some readers, suspicious to others — and the Snopes entry could not have been written with the same confidence. Primary-source journalism — defined here as direct contact with the participants in a historical event — remains the most reliable mechanism for authenticating claims that cannot be resolved through document analysis or institutional records alone. The Cleveland Browns letters case is a clean example of a historical factual question whose definitive answer required finding living people and asking them.
