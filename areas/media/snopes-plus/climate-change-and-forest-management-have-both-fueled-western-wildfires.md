# Both climate change and forest management failures have fueled western wildfires


## 1. The Claim

The claim that both climate change and forest management failures have fueled the dramatic increase in western United States wildfire activity has appeared in political, scientific, and journalistic contexts with an unusual structure: it is simultaneously a scientific proposition and a political battleground. It is a scientific proposition because the evidence bearing on it is empirical and peer-reviewed. It is a political battleground because each half of the claim has been selectively adopted and amplified by opposing political actors to serve divergent narratives.

One version of the claim circulates primarily in climate communication and Democratic Party political discourse: large, destructive wildfires are primarily or exclusively a consequence of anthropogenic climate change, which has raised temperatures, intensified drought, and extended fire seasons across the American West. In this framing, pointing to forest management represents either deflection from the real cause or a bad-faith argument used to avoid action on greenhouse gas emissions. This version is associated with climate scientists and advocates who fear that forest management discourse provides cover for inaction on emissions.

The competing version circulates in conservative political discourse and among some forestry professionals and rural land managers: wildfires are primarily or exclusively a consequence of decades of misguided fire suppression policy, which has allowed fuel accumulation to reach historically unprecedented levels; climate change is a secondary or marginal factor; and the solution is therefore aggressive mechanical thinning, logging, prescribed burning, and land management rather than emissions reductions. This version is associated with the timber industry, ranching interests, and some conservative politicians, particularly after the 2018 Camp Fire in California when President Trump attributed the fires to forest management failure.

The well-supported scientific claim — which is the subject of this entry — cuts across this binary. Multiple peer-reviewed analyses find that both anthropogenic climate change and fuel accumulation from fire suppression are significant, interacting, and independently real contributors to observed increases in western wildfire extent, severity, and frequency. The claim that both are real is, in the scientific literature, the mainstream and well-supported position.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The empirical baseline.** Western US wildfire statistics show dramatic increases over the past five decades. Westerling (2006, *Science*) published an influential analysis showing that large wildfire frequency in the western US increased sharply after 1987, correlating with earlier spring snowmelt — itself an indicator of warmer spring temperatures — and longer fire seasons. The paper quantified a fourfold increase in large fires and a sixfold increase in area burned by large wildfires between 1970–1986 and 1987–2003. Westerling, Hidalgo, Cayan and Swetnam (2006, *Science*) further demonstrated that the increase was geographically concentrated in mid-elevation forests of the northern Rockies and was strongly correlated with temperature increase, advancing spring snowmelt timing, and summer drought. This work established the statistical association between observed warming and increased fire activity with high confidence.

Williams, Abatzoglou, Gershunov et al. (2019, *Geophysical Research Letters*) extended this analysis to examine the contribution of anthropogenic climate change specifically, finding that human-caused warming had approximately doubled the area of burned forest in the western US over the period 1984–2015, adding approximately 4.2 million additional burned acres compared to what would have been expected without climate change. The mechanism operates primarily through vapour pressure deficit — the degree to which the atmosphere is drier than saturation — which climate change has increased by making temperatures higher and, in some regions, precipitation patterns more erratic.

Abatzoglou and Williams (2016, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) isolated the anthropogenic climate signal from natural variability and found that human-caused climate change explained approximately 55% of the increase in aridity in western US forests over 1979–2015. The paper estimated that climate change had approximately doubled the area of forest experiencing high fire potential by the late twentieth century. This is a strong, well-replicated empirical result from a peer-reviewed source in a leading journal.

**The forest management record.** The role of fuel accumulation from fire suppression is documented from a different but equally well-established body of scholarship. North American Indigenous peoples managed fire across western landscapes for millennia before European colonisation. Pyne (2001, *Fire: A Brief History*) and Anderson (2005, *Tending the Wild: Native American Knowledge and the Management of California's Natural Resources*) document the systematic use of fire by California Indian tribes and other Indigenous nations for vegetation management, hunting, travel corridor maintenance, and acorn crop enhancement. This burning maintained lower fuel loads and created more mosaic landscape structures than the fire-suppressed landscapes that developed after colonisation.

European American settlement was followed by active suppression of both Indigenous burning practices and natural, lightning-ignited fires. The US Forest Service adopted a formal fire suppression policy in 1910 in the aftermath of the Great Fire — the fires that burned approximately 3 million acres in Idaho, Montana, and Washington in August 1910, killing 85 people. The "10 am policy" — the goal of suppressing every fire by 10 am of the day after it was spotted — was formalised in 1935 and guided federal fire management for decades.

Agee and Skinner (2005, *Forest Ecology and Management*) reviewed the consequences of fire exclusion for fuel structure in western forests, finding that fire suppression had allowed buildup of ladder fuels (shrubs, low-hanging branches, and dead wood that allow ground fires to reach the elevated forest canopy and become stand-replacing crown fires) across large areas of ponderosa pine and mixed conifer forest that historically burned frequently in low- to moderate-intensity surface fires. These forests, which evolved with frequent low-intensity fire, experienced a transformation of their structure under fire suppression, accumulating dead woody material and increasing stem densities far above pre-suppression historical levels.

Collins, Stephens, Roller et al. (2011, *Forest Science*) examined historical fire frequencies in California mixed-conifer forests and found that pre-Euro-American fire return intervals of 5–15 years in many areas were consistent with low-intensity fire regimes maintained by both Indigenous burning and natural ignition. Over 100+ years of suppression, these forests have accumulated fuels that support fire behaviour qualitatively different from historical patterns: where pre-suppression fires would have burned along the ground consuming grasses and shrubs, modern fires under the same ignition conditions can become stand-replacing crown fires with fire behaviour characteristics that make suppression difficult and produce the catastrophic losses documented in recent decades.

**How they interact.** The most important scientific insight, and the one that the binary political debate most often obscures, is that these two drivers interact multiplicatively rather than additively. Fried, Torn and Mills (2004, *Climatic Change*) modelled the interaction between increased fuels and climate-driven aridity, finding that the combination of elevated fuel loads and dry, warm conditions produces fire behaviour that is qualitatively more extreme than either factor alone would produce. Elevated fuel loads create the context in which the increased aridity and temperature produced by climate change can drive extreme fire behaviour; without the fuel accumulation, the same climate conditions would produce fewer and less severe fires; without climate change, the same fuel loads would produce somewhat larger fires than the pre-suppression baseline but not the landscape-scale conflagrations documented since the 1990s.

Keeley, Syphard and Fotheringham (2019, *International Journal of Wildland Fire*) published a systematic review of ignition patterns in California wildfires, finding that human ignitions — utility equipment failures, vehicle sparks, unattended campfires — are the dominant ignition source for the most destructive fires in California's coastal shrublands, and that the increase in destructive fires in these landscapes is primarily driven by the combination of increasing density of human electrical infrastructure and increasing aridity from climate change, rather than fuel accumulation per se. This finding — that fire drivers differ substantially between fire-adapted forests (where fuel accumulation is central) and shrublands (where ignition infrastructure and aridity dominate) — is an important complication to both the simple climate-only and management-only narratives.


## 3. Why People Believe This (And What It Illuminates)

**The political sorting of an empirical claim.** The genuinely novel epistemic dimension of this case is the mechanism by which a well-supported empirical synthesis — both factors matter, they interact — becomes politically sorted into two mutually exclusive single-factor narratives. This is not a case of uncertainty about the evidence producing polarisation. The scientific evidence for both the climate and the management contributions is strong. The polarisation is not epistemic; it is political.

The political mechanics of this sorting have been documented by media researchers. Coverage of wildfire causation by outlets with conservative political leanings has systematically emphasised management failure and de-emphasised or denied climate change; coverage by outlets with progressive political leanings has systematically emphasised climate change and de-emphasised management. Leiserowitz, Maibach, Rosenthal et al. (2019, *Yale Program on Climate Change Communication*) documented that partisan identity is the single strongest predictor of belief about climate change causation in the United States, stronger than education, scientific knowledge, or media consumption. In this environment, factual claims about wildfire are not evaluated primarily on their evidentiary merits but on their correspondence with pre-existing partisan identity.

The political functional value of each single-factor claim is clear. The climate-only framing supports policy attention to emissions reductions, which is a progressive priority that encounters strong opposition from fossil fuel interests. The management-only framing supports policy attention to logging, thinning, and prescribed burning, which is consistent with the economic interests of the timber industry and the political priorities of rural Western communities that have long-standing tensions with federal land management agencies. Each camp has material and ideological incentives to reject the other's preferred explanation.

What this occludes is that the synthesis position — both factors matter, they interact — has specific and defensible policy implications of its own. It implies: (1) aggressive reduction in greenhouse gas emissions is necessary to prevent continued worsening of fire conditions regardless of management intervention; (2) forest management interventions — particularly prescribed fire restoration in fire-adapted forest types — are necessary to reduce fuel hazard and allow the remaining forest to survive the climate conditions already locked in; and (3) development patterns in the wildland-urban interface (WUI), where human infrastructure meets fire-prone landscapes, require reformed planning and building codes to reduce ignition risk and structural losses (Radeloff et al., 2018, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*). A policy portfolio that addressed all three drivers simultaneously is both scientifically indicated and politically intractable, because achieving it requires coalition between constituencies that prefer single-factor narratives.

**The Indigenous burning dimension.** Systematically undercovered in both popular narratives is the role of Indigenous burning practice suppression in the creation of current fuel conditions. Multiple tribes across California and other western states maintained burning practices for centuries that shaped the landscape ecology of western forests. The suppression of these practices was part of the broader colonial project of dispossessing Indigenous peoples of land and sovereignty. Lake (2013, *Ecological Processes*) and Kimmerer (2013, *Braiding Sweetgrass*) have documented both the ecological sophistication of traditional ecological knowledge-based fire management and the institutional and political obstacles to restoring it. Several California tribal nations have worked with state agencies in recent years to conduct cultural burning on ancestral territory; the Karuk and Yurok tribes in northern California have been particularly active in fire management advocacy and practice.

Restoring Indigenous fire stewardship is not simply a restoration ecology question; it is a question of tribal sovereignty, treaty rights, and land relationships that existing forest management policy frameworks are not designed to address. The omission of this dimension from both the climate-only and management-only narratives represents a significant gap in popular coverage of western wildfire causation.


## 4. Verdict

**True.** Both anthropogenic climate change and forest management practices — specifically, decades of fire suppression that allowed fuel accumulation in fire-adapted forests — have contributed to observed increases in western US wildfire extent, severity, and frequency. This is the mainstream position in peer-reviewed fire ecology and climate science literature. The claim is supported by multiple independent lines of evidence, is corroborated by quantitative modelling studies, and represents scientific consensus rather than a contested position.

The nuances that matter are: (a) the relative magnitude of each factor varies by forest type and region — fuel accumulation is most significant in ponderosa pine and mixed conifer forests; climate-driven aridity is significant across nearly all western fire landscapes; (b) the two factors interact multiplicatively, so their combined effect exceeds what either would produce alone; (c) a third factor — the expansion of human development into the wildland-urban interface and the associated increases in ignition risk and structural exposure — contributes substantially to observed losses and is underweighted in both the climate-only and management-only frames; (d) the suppression of Indigenous burning practices is an underacknowledged dimension of the management failure claim that deserves recognition both as ecological history and as a sovereignty and self-determination issue.

The political binary — "it's climate change" vs. "it's forest management" — is not supported by the scientific evidence and actively distorts public understanding of the problem and its solutions.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The western wildfire crisis has intensified dramatically in the twenty-first century in ways that track the predictions of climate science. The 2020 fire season in California broke records set in 2018, which broke records set in 2017. The August Complex fire of 2020 became the first "gigafire" — over one million acres — recorded in California history. Severe fire conditions have extended into the Pacific Northwest, Colorado, New Mexico, and other western states in ways that reflect both the accumulation of decades of fire suppression and the long-term trend of warming temperatures and more frequent and severe drought.

The 2023 and 2024 fire seasons continued this pattern, with significant fires burning in Oregon, Washington, Idaho, and California. Climate projections from the IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (IPCC, 2021) indicate that the warming and aridification trends driving increased fire potential in the western US will continue under all emissions scenarios through at least the 2040s, regardless of near-term mitigation actions, due to greenhouse gases already in the atmosphere. This means that elevated fire risk is locked in for at least the coming generation, and management interventions — fuel reduction, prescribed fire, WUI planning reform — become explicitly the adaptive management challenge for the interim period between current conditions and any future stabilisation of atmospheric forcing.

The debate about forest management and climate change as causes of western wildfires is therefore not merely a historical forensics exercise. It determines how resources, attention, and policy are allocated in the near term. A policy response calibrated to the incorrect belief that climate change is the only driver would invest heavily in emissions advocacy while neglecting the forest management interventions that could reduce near-term fire severity in landscapes already committed to warming. A policy response calibrated to the incorrect belief that management failure is the only driver would invest in logging and thinning while neglecting the emissions trajectory that will continue to worsen fire conditions regardless of management. The scientifically correct synthesis — both drivers are real, they interact, we need both policy streams — is simultaneously more accurate and more demanding to communicate and implement.

Prescribed fire is particularly important as part of the management response and deserves closer examination than it typically receives in popular coverage. Williams and Baker (2012, *Ecosphere*) reviewed evidence for the effectiveness of mechanical thinning versus prescribed burning in reducing high-severity fire risk in western forests, finding that prescribed burning — surface and crown fire that consumes fine fuels and ladder fuels — is generally more effective per dollar invested in reducing subsequent fire severity than mechanical thinning alone. However, prescribed burning faces significant implementation obstacles: smoke management regulations restrict when burns can be conducted; liability rules in some states create disincentives for private landowners to use prescribed fire; and institutional capacity for planned burning in federal agencies was reduced during decades of full-suppression policy orientation. Rebuilding this capacity is a policy problem that is distinct from and complementary to the emissions reduction challenge.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim presents an unusual challenge for conventional fact-checking because the most politically visible versions of it — the climate-only and management-only framings — are both oversimplifications of the well-supported scientific picture, but they are oversimplifications in opposite directions. A fact-checker asked to evaluate the claim "wildfires are caused by climate change" must conclude: partially true, but incomplete. A fact-checker asked to evaluate "wildfires are caused by forest management failure" must reach the same verdict. A fact-checker asked to evaluate "both climate change and forest management failures have contributed to wildfires" should conclude: true, and this is the position supported by the scientific literature.

Major fact-checkers have addressed specific wildfire-causation claims, particularly those made by political figures in the aftermath of major fires. PolitiFact addressed claims made by Trump administration officials minimising the role of climate change in the 2018 California fires and rated several such claims as False or Mostly False. FactCheck.org has addressed related claims about whether California's water management policies contributed to fires. Snopes has addressed a variety of specific fire-related claims. However, the synthesis claim — both factors matter, they interact — has not been the subject of a formalised fact-check on major platforms, perhaps because it is supported by the scientific consensus and does not arise from verifiable political claims in the conventional fact-check format.

The most important media literacy lesson from this case is the asymmetry between the scientific evidence and the political discourse. When scientific evidence robustly supports a nuanced synthesis and political discourse insists on a binary, the fact-checker's role is not to "both sides" the binary — which would imply equal validity for each single-factor position — but to identify where the evidence actually points and to explain why the binary framing persists in political contexts where the evidence supports synthesis. The mechanisms of political sorting — elite cue-taking, motivated reasoning, media ecosystem polarisation — are themselves important parts of the story, and communicating them accurately is part of what good media literacy reporting on this topic requires. Fact-checkers who engage with wildfire causation as a media literacy issue, rather than merely as a claim-by-claim verdict exercise, will produce more durable understanding for their audiences.
