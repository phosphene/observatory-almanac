# Climate change denial arguments examined: the pause, urban heat islands, CO2 lag, and funding bias claims


## 1. The Claim

A cluster of climate change denial arguments circulates across social media, conservative commentary, and contrarian websites, each presenting itself as the overlooked piece of evidence that disproves anthropogenic climate science. Unlike the most elementary denial talking points — "scientists disagree" or "it's just natural variation" — the arguments examined in this entry carry more technical-sounding presentations that give them a surface appearance of sophisticated forensic reasoning. Four such arguments are particularly persistent and merit direct examination.

**The "pause" or "hiatus" argument** holds that global warming effectively stopped after the peak El Niño year of 1998 and did not meaningfully resume until around 2015, demonstrating that the long-term warming trend was exaggerated. Proponents point to cherry-picked datasets and statistical periods to argue that surface temperature records do not show a continuous warming trend, and that climate scientists suppressed or downplayed this finding when presenting climate policy arguments.

**The urban heat island argument** holds that surface temperature station networks are disproportionately located in or near urban areas, and that the documented warming in surface temperature records is largely or entirely an artefact of urban expansion — paved surfaces, waste heat from buildings and vehicles, and reduced vegetation cover around stations — rather than a genuine planetary signal. Some versions assert that scientists have deliberately placed instruments in warm micro-environments to inflate readings.

**The CO2-temperature lag argument** holds that in the Vostok and other Antarctic ice-core records covering the past several glacial-interglacial cycles, CO2 concentrations lagged temperature changes by hundreds to thousands of years. If CO2 follows rather than leads temperature in the palaeoclimate record, the argument goes, then CO2 cannot be the cause of warming — and the entire edifice of attributing current warming to human CO2 emissions is based on an inverted causal relationship.

**The funding bias and institutional groupthink argument** holds that climate scientists are financially dependent on continued research funding that disproportionately rewards alarming findings, and that peer review, career pressure, and institutional incentives have created an intellectual monoculture in which dissenting views are suppressed and the appearance of consensus is manufactured rather than earned by the evidence. The implication is that independent, unfunded thinkers are more reliable judges of the climate evidence than credentialed researchers who are, in the words of one common formulation, "paid to find a problem."

These four arguments appear repeatedly in combination, reinforcing one another to suggest that a comprehensive alternative understanding of the climate system exists and has been deliberately buried by self-interested institutions.


## 2. What's Actually True

### The "pause" claim

The period from roughly 1998 to 2012 did show a slower rate of surface temperature increase in certain datasets compared to the preceding decades — a feature described in the scientific literature itself as the "hiatus" or "slowdown." This is a real statistical observation, not a manufactured controversy. The IPCC Fifth Assessment Report (2013) acknowledged it explicitly in its chapter on observed temperature change. The scientific question was never whether the slowdown existed in some surface records but what caused it, whether it was statistically significant beyond natural decadal variability, and what it implied for long-term trends.

The dominant cause of the apparent slowdown was identified as natural climate variability, particularly enhanced heat uptake by the deep ocean and a sequence of La Niña events following the extreme 1997–98 El Niño. Balmaseda, Trenberth & Källén (2013, *Geophysical Research Letters*, 40(6), pp. 1754–1759) showed that the warming of the 0–700 m and 700–2,000 m ocean layers continued uninterrupted through the supposed "pause" and actually accelerated during the period, with the deep ocean absorbing substantial heat that did not register in surface temperature records. The apparent hiatus in surface temperature was a redistribution of energy within Earth's climate system, not a cessation of planetary heating.

The starting year of 1998 is methodologically dishonest: it represents the highest single year in the pre-hiatus surface record due to the strongest El Niño in the instrumental period, making any subsequent trend look flat relative to that exceptional peak. Karl et al. (2015, *Science*, 348(6242), pp. 1469–1472) identified and corrected biases in sea surface temperature datasets — primarily the transition from ship-intake measurements to buoy-based measurements, which introduced a systematic cold bias into buoy data — and found that once this bias was corrected, the apparent hiatus disappeared entirely, with warming continuing through the period at a rate consistent with prior decades. The subsequent decade proved this decisively: global average temperatures broke successive records in 2016, 2019, 2020, 2023, and 2024, with the last year surpassing 1.5°C above the pre-industrial baseline for the first time.

### The urban heat island argument

Urban heat islands (UHIs) are real and scientifically well-documented: cities are warmer than surrounding rural areas due to heat-absorbing dark surfaces, reduced evapotranspiration, and anthropogenic heat release from buildings, vehicles, and industrial processes. Station siting has also received legitimate scientific attention, with studies examining systematic biases from instrument placement near artificial heat sources. None of this is disputed in mainstream climate science.

What is disputed — and empirically refuted — is whether UHI contamination significantly affects global temperature trend records. Brohan, Kennedy, Harris, Tett & Jones (2006, *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres*, 111(D12), pp. 1–21) assessed systematic station-quality effects on the HadCRUT3 surface temperature record and quantified the UHI contribution to global temperature trends at less than 0.006°C per decade — negligible compared to the observed trend of approximately 0.17–0.20°C per decade over the late twentieth century and 0.26°C per decade in the first two decades of the twenty-first century.

The most decisive evidence against the UHI hypothesis as an explanation for global warming is the independent corroboration from measurement systems entirely unaffected by surface station placement. Satellite-based temperature records of the mid-troposphere — including the UAH and RSS datasets originally developed by John Christy and Roy Spencer, both prominent climate contrarians — show a warming trend consistent with surface records (Mears & Wentz, 2016, *Journal of Climate*, 29(11), pp. 3629–3640). Ocean surface temperatures, measured at sea routes and by autonomous buoys far from any urban infrastructure, show the same warming. Weather balloon radiosonde records show the same warming. Borehole temperature reconstructions preserve thermal signals in the Earth's crust and are entirely unrelated to surface network siting. The convergence of physically independent measurement systems — satellites, oceans, radiosondes, boreholes — producing the same trend eliminates UHI as a plausible explanation. No urban heat island effect contaminates the temperature of the mid-troposphere or the surface of the North Atlantic.

### The CO2-temperature lag argument

This argument embeds a genuine and important palaeoclimate observation: in Antarctic ice-core records covering glacial-interglacial cycles over the past 800,000 years, temperature increases during terminations — transitions from ice ages to warm interglacial periods — often precede the corresponding rise in CO2 concentrations by roughly 200–1,000 years. Caillon, Severinghaus, Jouzel, Barnola, Kang & Lipenkov (2003, *Science*, 299(5613), pp. 1728–1731) documented this lag precisely at the Termination III glacial transition, noting a CO2 lag of 800 ± 200 years relative to the Antarctic temperature signal. This is a real feature of the palaeoclimate record, thoroughly studied and not in dispute.

The argument that this proves CO2 cannot drive warming is a fundamental misapplication of the finding to the current situation. Glacial-interglacial transitions are initiated by changes in Earth's orbital geometry — Milankovitch cycles — that alter the seasonal and latitudinal distribution of solar insolation. These orbital changes produce an initial warming, particularly concentrated in the Southern Hemisphere, which reduces the solubility of CO2 in the deep Southern Ocean and causes outgassing that releases CO2 into the atmosphere. The rising atmospheric CO2 then acts as a global amplifying feedback, driving further warming that substantially exceeds what orbital forcing alone could produce over the timescales involved. Shakun, Clark, He, Marcott, Mix, Liu, Otto-Bliesner, Schmittner & Carlson (2012, *Nature*, 484, pp. 49–54) synthesised temperature records from 80 globally distributed sites and found that globally averaged temperature actually lags CO2 by approximately 460 years during ice age terminations, consistent with CO2 being the primary global amplifier after initial Southern Hemisphere warming. The CO2 lag is an artefact of the geographic structure of orbital forcing, not evidence that CO2 does not warm the planet.

Current anthropogenic emissions occupy a categorically different causal structure: humans are the initiating forcing. CO2 concentrations are rising because fossil fuel combustion is adding approximately 36 billion tonnes of CO2 to the atmosphere annually — at a rate roughly 100 times faster than the natural carbon cycle can process — not as a delayed response to prior warming. To apply the ice-core lag argument to current emissions is to commit a category error: the palaeoclimate situation had CO2 as the response, while our situation has CO2 as the cause. That cause-and-effect relationship is the same in both cases; only the trigger differs.

### The funding bias argument

The claim that climate scientists are uniquely corrupted by funding that requires alarming results inverts the documented financial landscape of climate research and contrarian activity. Brulle (2014, *Climatic Change*, 122(4), pp. 681–694) tracked the funding flows to climate contrarian organisations using IRS 990 forms and found approximately $558 million per year flowing to a network of organisations dedicated to producing doubt about the climate consensus, funded predominantly by fossil fuel companies, associated foundations, and wealthy individuals with contrary economic interests. Farrell (2016, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 113(1), pp. 92–97) demonstrated, using network analysis of 4,556 documents from 164 organisations, that corporate funding was significantly associated with the spread of contrarian framing and created ideological polarisation in public understanding of climate science.

Mainstream climate research is funded by national science agencies — the US National Science Foundation, NASA, NOAA, and equivalent bodies in Europe, Japan, Australia, and China — operating under peer review systems that evaluate research quality, novelty, and rigour, not the political direction of findings. This funding is distributed across thousands of research groups in dozens of countries with vastly different political systems. No coherent mechanism exists for a funding conspiracy to mandate the same false conclusions from researchers in the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Japan, Australia, China, and India simultaneously. By contrast, contrarian research funding has been demonstrably concentrated in a small number of organisations, mostly in the United States, and has produced a documented asymmetry: Anderegg, Prall, Harold & Schneider (2010, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 107(27), pp. 12107–12109) found that researchers who had signed statements opposing the climate consensus had substantially lower expertise as measured by publication records and citation impact in climate-relevant disciplines compared with those who supported the consensus. The institution funding crisis is real, but it runs in the opposite direction from what the argument claims.


## 3. Why People Believe This

Each of these arguments works by identifying a real phenomenon — the hiatus, urban heat islands, CO2 lag in ice cores, research funding — and extracting it from its scientific context to imply a fundamental failure of climate science itself. The arguments derive their persuasive force specifically from genuine partial truths: no competent scientist disputes that UHIs exist, that there was a statistical slowdown in surface warming in the early 2000s, that CO2 follows temperature in Antarctic ice cores, or that research funding can in principle create perverse incentives. The distortion lies entirely in the implied interpretation and the systematically omitted context.

Kahan, Peters, Wittlin, Slovic, Ouellette, Braman & Mandel (2012, *Nature Climate Change*, 2(10), pp. 732–735) demonstrated a troubling finding in motivated reasoning: greater scientific literacy and numeracy were not associated with greater acceptance of the climate consensus. Among people with strong hierarchical or individualist worldviews, higher science literacy was actually correlated with greater resistance to the consensus, because scientifically literate sceptics could better deploy technically-sounding arguments to rationalise culturally-preferred conclusions. These sophisticated framing arguments function as rationalisation infrastructure, giving technically plausible-sounding cover for conclusions already reached on cultural and political grounds rather than representing genuine epistemic reconsideration.

The "pause" and funding arguments additionally carry the psychological weight of exposing a concealed truth — position­ing the person who accepts them as a critical thinker who has seen through institutional deception. This is particularly effective in contemporary media environments that celebrate contrarian positions and treat institutional scepticism as a civic virtue independent of the specific claims being made.

Lewandowsky, Gignac & Vaughan (2013, *Psychological Science*, 24(5), pp. 622–633) examined how the "hiatus" influenced public and political perceptions of climate science and found that emphasising the slowdown — regardless of context — reliably reduced policy support, a finding consistent with the strategic value of deploying such arguments even when the scientific community has itself examined and contextualised them.


## 4. Verdict

**False on each count.** The warming "pause" was a real but statistically cherry-picked feature of surface temperature records that originated in the choice of an extreme El Niño starting year, disappeared with bias correction in sea surface temperature data, was not present in ocean heat content records throughout, and ended definitively with sustained record-breaking warming in subsequent years. Urban heat islands are real phenomena but their contribution to global temperature trends is quantified at under 0.006°C per decade by the studies that have examined them, dwarfed by the observed trend and not replicated by independent measurement systems unaffected by urban proximity. The CO2-temperature lag in Antarctic ice cores is a genuine palaeoclimate observation that reflects CO2's role as an amplifying feedback in orbital-forced glacial cycles, not a refutation of CO2 as a forcing agent in the present situation where human emissions are the initiating cause. The funding-bias claim inverts the documented financial landscape, in which contrarian research has demonstrably greater correlation with industry funding and lower scientific credentials than consensus research. Each argument embeds a genuine partial truth but transforms it into a false wholesale conclusion through omission of context, logical category errors, and misapplication of findings outside their valid domain.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The sophistication of climate denial arguments has evolved in documented response to the progressive strengthening of the scientific consensus. Early scepticism challenged whether warming was occurring at all. When that became untenable, focus shifted to whether warming was human-caused. When that became increasingly difficult to sustain, it shifted to whether consequences would be serious. And when that proved hard to maintain, the strategy shifted to the kind of methodological and institutional arguments examined here: designed to make climate science appear untrustworthy rather than wrong on any specific claim.

This evolutionary pattern, sometimes called the "escalator of denial," was documented by Cook, Lewandowsky & Ecker (2017, *Environmental Research Letters*, 12(2), 024024) through analysis of contrarian argument patterns over time. The pattern closely mirrors what Oreskes & Conway (2010, *Merchants of Doubt*, Bloomsbury Publishing) described in tobacco, lead, and acid rain denial: as evidence accumulates, specific factual claims are progressively abandoned but replaced by methodological and institutional challenges that are harder to refute with any single study because they function as generalised scepticism of scientific institutions rather than falsifiable empirical claims.

The arguments examined here have practical political consequences. Van der Linden, Leiserowitz, Feinberg & Maibach (2015, *Global Environmental Change*, 35, pp. 212–220) demonstrated that perceived scientific uncertainty — even when it does not reflect the actual state of evidence — is among the strongest predictors of public opposition to climate policy. Arguments that appear technically sophisticated are particularly effective at elevating perceived uncertainty among educated audiences. Understanding the structure — genuine partial truth, omitted context, misapplied implication — is therefore a practical prerequisite for informed public engagement with climate policy, not merely an academic exercise in error correction.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The "pause," UHI, and CO2-lag arguments have each been assessed by multiple independent fact-checking organisations and specialist scientific communication resources.

Climate Feedback, which assembles practising climate scientists to evaluate specific media coverage of climate claims, has rated articles promoting the "pause" argument as having "low scientific credibility," citing cherry-picking of 1998 as a start date, failure to account for the continuation of ocean heat content rise throughout the period, and the subsequent record temperatures that definitively closed the claimed hiatus. Climate Feedback's scientist evaluations of UHI articles have consistently distinguished between the real phenomenon of local station siting problems and the false inference that UHI explains global average temperature trends.

Skeptical Science has catalogued and responded to more than 200 distinct climate denial arguments since its founding in 2009, with entries for each of the four arguments examined here. Their CO2-lag entry presents the Shakun et al. (2012) analysis in accessible graphical form. Their UHI entry reproduces the Berkeley Earth project analysis — an independent temperature reconstruction led initially by physicist Richard Muller, a prominent climate sceptic prior to the project — that found UHI correction changes the global temperature trend by approximately 0.01°C since the nineteenth century.

The IPCC Working Group I reports have explicitly addressed the hiatus (AR5, 2013, Chapter 2), the UHI contribution (AR5, Chapter 2, Box 2.2), and CO2-lag questions (AR4, 2007, Chapter 6) in sections reviewed by hundreds of contributing scientists. These assessments represent the appropriate reference standard for evaluating media treatment of all four argument categories and are publicly available without paywall from the IPCC website.
