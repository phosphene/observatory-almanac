# Viral climate change before/after images are misleading or manipulated


## 1. The Claim

A recurring claim in climate-sceptic media and social media comment threads holds that the dramatic before/after photographs used in climate change communication are systematically misleading or outright manipulated. The claim takes several forms: that seasonal differences rather than long-term change explain the contrasts shown; that photographers selectively visit glaciers and coastlines during summer drought versus winter recovery to produce artificial contrasts; that widely circulated images of bleached coral, retreating glaciers, and dried lakebeds have been staged, digitally altered, or lifted from unrelated events; and, in the strongest version, that virtually every iconic before/after image in climate reporting is deceptive and cannot be trusted.

The claim gained significant traction on social media platforms during the run-up to major climate negotiations in the 2010s and has intensified with easier access to basic image forensics tools and reverse image search. Prominent examples that have been repeatedly cited as evidence of manipulation include: photographs of Arctic sea ice decline that were alleged to show the same region photographed at different times of day or season; images of Lake Poopó in Bolivia that were described as deliberately photographed during an extreme drought year; before/after images of Rhône Glacier and other European glaciers that critics alleged used unrepresentative or mismatched vantage points; and coral bleaching photographs from the Great Barrier Reef that were claimed to exaggerate the geographic extent of damage.

The rhetorical function of the claim is significant: by seeding general distrust of visual evidence, it allows those opposed to climate action to dismiss a broad category of compelling evidence without needing to engage with the underlying science. If before/after images are presumptively untrustworthy, the scientific conclusions they are often used to illustrate — which rest on far more than photographs — can be characterised as equally unreliable. The claim imports scepticism about visual presentation and redirects it at the scientific consensus itself.


## 2. What's Actually True

**There are legitimate criticisms of specific images.** The factual core of the claim is that some widely shared climate images have been presented with insufficient context or with framing that overstates what the image demonstrates. This is genuine. Climate scientists and science communicators have themselves documented instances of problematic image use:

Photographic pairings that compare summer-low conditions to historical averages can exaggerate the apparent rate of change. Some glacier before/after photographs have been taken from different angles or distances, making modern retreat appear greater than a strict same-vantage comparison would show. Coral bleaching images have occasionally been captioned with geographic attributions that were either imprecise or sourced from archival stock rather than from the stated location. Images of drought and fire have sometimes been presented as uniquely caused by climate change when complex multi-factor causation — including land use change, hydrological extraction, and multi-decadal climate variability — would be more accurate.

Wardle & Derakhshan (2017, *Council of Europe*) in their framework for understanding information disorder identified this category as "misleading content" — not fabrication but accurate images used in misleading framing or context. This is a real phenomenon in environmental communication, and climate advocates and media organisations have been criticised for it from within the scientific community (Painter et al., 2023, *Environmental Communication*, 17(2), pp. 112–129).

**The much larger reality: the images document real change.** The critical error in the claim is treating the existence of some improperly contextualised images as evidence that the underlying change they attempt to show is not real, or that visual documentation of climate change is generically unreliable.

The retreat of major glaciers globally is among the most thoroughly documented phenomena in modern environmental science. Zemp et al. (2019, *Nature*, 568, pp. 382–386) synthesised data from 19,000 glaciers worldwide and found that ice loss has been accelerating since the early 1990s, losing approximately 335 billion tonnes of ice per year in the most recent period studied. This finding draws on mass balance measurements, satellite altimetry, and gravitational data from the GRACE satellite programme — entirely independent of before/after photographs. The photographs' accuracy or inaccuracy does not affect this conclusion at all.

Arctic sea ice decline is documented through continuous passive microwave satellite data since 1979. Stroeve & Notz (2018, *Nature Climate Change*, 8, pp. 946–953) demonstrated that September sea ice extent declined by approximately 13.1 percent per decade over the 1979–2017 record. This is a physically measured quantity; it has nothing to do with the quality of any particular photograph. Photographs of sea ice conditions serve as illustrative material for a scientifically established finding, not as the primary evidence for that finding.

Similarly, coral bleaching events on the Great Barrier Reef have been documented through systematic dive surveys across thousands of sites. Hughes et al. (2017, *Nature*, 543, pp. 373–377) conducted aerial and underwater surveys of 911 reef sections in 2016 and found that 93 percent showed some bleaching, with 22 percent of coral dead. The accuracy of any photograph chosen to illustrate this event is irrelevant to the empirical finding.

**Image verification has improved substantially.** Major photographic agencies and news organisations now apply documented protocols for climate imagery. Organisations including NASA's Earth Observatory publish before/after satellite comparisons from Landsat and Sentinel missions with full metadata, acquisition dates, sensor specifications, and coordinate information, allowing independent verification of every comparison. Photographic pairings from Extreme Ice Survey (EIS), founded by photographer James Balog, employ fixed camera positions, GPS registration, and time-stamped continuous monitoring specifically to prevent the seasonal and angular confounds that critics identify as sources of manipulation.

Balog et al. (2016, *Global Change Biology*, 22(5), pp. 1755–1763) documented the multi-year photographic record methodology underpinning the EIS project, including quality controls designed to ensure temporal and spatial comparability. The Repeat Photography Project, operated through the US Geological Survey (Hall & Fagre, 2003, *BioScience*, 53(2), pp. 131–140), similarly archives georeferenced historical photographs and precisely replicates the original camera position for contemporary shots, producing verified like-for-like comparisons.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The mechanism: inoculation in reverse — using media literacy against science.** The specific claim-mechanism here is what communication researchers have called the "firehose of falsehood" adapted to a sophisticated media-literate audience. Rather than inventing false images, this strategy works by identifying real cases of imperfect image presentation and then generalising from them to discredit an entire class of evidence. It appeals to audiences who have developed critical media consumption habits and who can be moved by the argument that they are being more rigorous than those who accept visual evidence uncritically.

This mechanism is distinct from ordinary motivated reasoning. It does not require that the audience be credulous; it requires that they be selectively rigorous — applying forensic scepticism to climate images while accepting much less reliable modes of evidence (anecdote, single cold events, unverified counterclaims) when those suit the desired conclusion. Lewandowsky et al. (2017, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 146(12), pp. 1862–1884) documented this asymmetric scepticism in motivated reasoning, finding that people with strong prior beliefs apply more critical evaluation standards selectively to evidence that conflicts with those beliefs.

**Genuine image criticism creates an opening.** Because real improperly framed images have circulated — and because scientists and communicators themselves have documented this — there is a factual entry point for the claim. Once someone has encountered a genuine case of a misleading climate photograph, they have experiential evidence that such manipulation exists, and from that foundation the generalisation to all such imagery is psychologically available even if logically unwarranted.

**The persuasiveness of techno-forensics aesthetics.** Claims that deploy image metadata, pixel-level analysis, or reverse image search carry strong surface credibility in contemporary media environments where image verification tools are widely known. Even when the analysis is superficial or incorrectly applied — for example, interpreting EXIF metadata artefacts as evidence of manipulation when those originate from post-processing export settings — the technical presentation mimics rigorous verification and is difficult for non-specialist audiences to evaluate.

**Political amplification and the manufacturing of doubt.** The strategy of systematically seeding doubt about photographic evidence has documented precedents in science denial methodology. Oreskes & Conway (2010, *Merchants of Doubt*, Bloomsbury Publishing) describe how the tobacco industry and later fossil fuel interests funded campaigns specifically designed to produce public confusion about the state of scientific evidence, without needing to refute the science directly. Manufacturing generalised distrust of the evidence base — including visual evidence — is an effective component of this strategy. Brulle et al. (2021, *Climatic Change*, 167(3–4), article 62) documented the flow of funding to climate contrarian organisations and noted that communications strategies aimed at media credibility were a consistent component.


## 4. Verdict

**Mixture: specific legitimate criticisms exist, but the generalisation is false and strategically deployed.** It is true that some viral before/after climate images have been presented with incorrect or insufficient contextual information, and that a small subset have involved genuine misrepresentation. This warrants ongoing vigilance in science communication practice and supports calls for better image metadata standards and contextual labelling.

It is false that before/after climate imagery is generically untrustworthy or systematically manipulated. The most important documentary evidence for climate change — accelerating glacier mass loss, declining Arctic sea ice, increasing ocean heat content, coral bleaching events, sea level rise — rests on physically measured data from satellites, tide gauges, ocean buoys, and systematic field surveys, entirely independent of photography. The before/after image format, when applied with methodological rigour and proper metadata disclosure, can accurately document real physical change.

The claim that all such imagery is misleading functions primarily as a strategy for producing generalised distrust rather than as a good-faith exercise in media literacy. Genuine media literacy asks which specific images are well-supported and which are not; it does not collapse all images into a single presumptively suspect category.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The manipulation claim participates in a broader pattern sometimes called "epistemological attack" — rather than contesting specific scientific findings on their own terms, contested claims are aimed at the methodology, instruments, and communication practices of the field that produced those findings. If photographs cannot be trusted, satellite data interpretations can be questioned. If satellite data interpretations are suspect, modelling outputs are unreliable. If models are unreliable, projections are speculative. Each level of the challenge is positioned to appear methodologically rigorous while its cumulative effect is to quarantine an entire knowledge domain from productive public engagement.

The appropriate response from science communication is not to abandon visual evidence but to be substantially more rigorous about how it is presented. Best practices developed by institutions including the World Meteorological Organization, NASA's Earth Observatory, and the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Working Group I include: always publishing acquisition metadata with comparative images; never pairing images from non-comparable seasonal conditions without explanation; specifying the spatial scale of before/after comparisons; and labelling the primary evidence base that the image illustrates rather than implying that the image itself constitutes the evidence.

Anderegg & Goldsmith (2014, *Environmental Research Letters*, 9(5), 054002) examined media coverage of climate science and found that accuracy in visual presentation was a significant factor in audience trust calibration. Communication that invests in visual verification rigour produces more durable credibility than communication that prioritises rhetorical impact through dramatic but poorly documented imagery. This suggests that the best defence against the manipulation claim is not denial but genuine improvement in documentation standards.

The claim also reveals something important about how information ecosystems work: in a high-volume media environment where verification is distributed across many actors, errors and approximations in environmental imagery will reliably occur. Those errors will be found, amplified, and generalised by actors with an interest in doubt-production. This creates a structural incentive for science communication organisations to invest in verification infrastructure rather than treating it as a secondary concern.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The claim that climate images are manipulated or misleading has been addressed by multiple fact-checking organisations, typically in response to specific viral claims about particular images rather than as a generalised verdict. Climate Feedback, a network of scientists who review climate coverage accuracy, has produced assessments of dozens of specific before/after images, finding outcomes that range from accurate and well-sourced to misleading due to lack of context to inaccurate. Their corpus demonstrates that the claim has to be assessed image by image, not by category.

PolitiFact and AFP Fact Check have addressed specific viral cases — notably viral claims about Lake Urmia in Iran and various glacier photographs — finding in each case that the images showed real change but that the captions or accompanying text sometimes overstated what the image specifically demonstrated. The characteristic verdict is "mostly accurate" or "missing context" rather than "false," which reflects the mixture nature of the claim.

For media literacy educators, the productive lesson is not that climate images are untrustworthy but that all photographic evidence benefits from contextual metadata — acquisition dates, coordinates, sensor type, and a clear statement of what the image demonstrates versus what the underlying evidence base is. Teaching audiences to ask for this metadata, and to distinguish between images as illustration and images as primary evidence, is more valuable than categorical scepticism. The claim exploits an audience that has learned to question images but has not yet learned to evaluate the independent evidence base that images are used to illustrate. Completing that media literacy training substantially reduces the claim's persuasive force.
