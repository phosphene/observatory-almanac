# Common climate change myths — it's just natural cycles, scientists disagree, CO2 is plant food, and more


## 1. The Claim (Cluster)

Climate change denial and minimisation does not arrive as a single, unified argument. It arrives as a cluster of individually plausible-sounding objections, each of which captures something real while misrepresenting its significance. The most durable members of this cluster — the ones that have survived multiple rounds of scientific communication and continue to circulate through conservative political media, social media comment sections, YouTube channels, and certain op-ed columns — are the following:

**Myth 1: It's just natural cycles.** Climate has always changed. Glacials, interglacials, medieval warm periods, little ice ages — the Earth has always had climate variability, and the current warming is just another swing of the natural pendulum.

**Myth 2: Scientists significantly disagree.** The scientific consensus is overstated. Many scientists dissent from the mainstream view. There is active professional disagreement about whether warming is happening, how much of it is human-caused, and how serious the consequences will be.

**Myth 3: CO2 is plant food.** Carbon dioxide is a natural substance plants use for photosynthesis. More CO2 means more plant growth — a net positive. Treating it as a pollutant is scientifically illiterate.

**Myth 4: The Sun is the real driver.** Solar activity has tracked with historical temperature changes better than CO2 levels have. The Sun, not industrial emissions, is the dominant factor in recent warming.

**Myth 5: Climate models have been consistently wrong.** The climate models used to project warming trends have a poor track record. They have predicted warming that has not materialised, or exaggerated the speed and magnitude of change. We cannot trust projections based on flawed models.

**Myth 6: A warmer world might be beneficial.** Warmer temperatures could extend growing seasons, open Arctic shipping routes, reduce heating energy use in cold countries, and, on net, improve human welfare. The catastrophist framing ignores the potential benefits.

These myths appear individually, in combination, and in shifting emphasis depending on the rhetorical context. This entry addresses each in turn before treating the cluster as a whole.


## 2. What's Actually True

Each myth contains a true premise that has been isolated from its context and made to carry more weight than it can hold.

**On natural cycles: yes, and.** Earth's climate has varied dramatically throughout its history. Glacial and interglacial cycles driven by Milankovitch orbital parameters (changes in Earth's axial tilt, orbital eccentricity, and precession) are real, well-understood, and have driven ice ages and warm periods over cycles of roughly 100,000, 41,000, and 23,000 years (Hays, J.D., Imbrie, J. & Shackleton, N.J., 1976, "Variations in the Earth's orbit: Pacemaker of the ice ages," *Science*, 194(4270), 1121–1132). The Medieval Warm Period (approximately 950–1250 CE) and the Little Ice Age (approximately 1300–1850 CE) were real regional climate anomalies, though neither was globally synchronous nor of the magnitude now commonly attributed to them. Volcanic eruptions, ocean circulation shifts, and internal climate variability all contribute to natural temperature variations.

**On scientific consensus: it is robust and quantified.** The claim of significant scientific dissent has been directly measured rather than merely asserted. Cook et al. (2013, "Quantifying the consensus on anthropogenic global warming in the scientific literature," *Environmental Research Letters*, 8(2), 024024) examined 11,944 peer-reviewed climate abstracts and found 97.1% endorsed the consensus position on anthropogenic warming. A subsequent, independently conducted analysis by Lynas et al. (2021, "Greater than 99% consensus on human caused climate change in the literature," *Environmental Research Letters*, 16(11), 114005) found consensus exceeding 99% in recent literature. The 97% figure has been independently verified by multiple methodologically distinct studies.

**On CO2 as plant food: partially true, dangerously incomplete.** Carbon dioxide is indeed a substrate for photosynthesis, and elevated atmospheric CO2 does produce a "CO2 fertilisation effect" in many plant species under laboratory conditions. This is genuine science. The Free-Air CO2 Enrichment (FACE) experiments have documented this effect (Ainsworth, E.A. & Long, S.P., 2005, "What have we learned from 15 years of free-air CO2 enrichment (FACE)? A meta-analytic review of the responses of photosynthesis, canopy properties and plant production to rising CO2," *New Phytologist*, 165(2), 351–372). However, the same experiments and subsequent field studies find that the fertilisation effect saturates at moderate CO2 levels, is counteracted by heat stress and drought at higher temperatures, and produces nutritional dilution — crops grown at elevated CO2 have lower protein, zinc, and iron concentrations (Myers et al., 2014, "Increasing CO2 threatens human nutrition," *Nature*, 510, 139–142).

**On solar activity: measured and found insufficient.** Solar output has been measured by satellite since 1978 (Total Solar Irradiance, TSI) and reconstructed from proxy records (sunspot counts, cosmogenic isotopes) for several centuries before that. The finding is unambiguous: since approximately 1980, solar activity has been flat to slightly declining, while global surface temperatures have risen by approximately 0.2°C per decade (Lean, J. & Rind, D.H., 2008, "How natural and anthropogenic influences alter global and regional surface temperatures: 1889 to 2006," *Geophysical Research Letters*, 35(18), L18701). The solar hypothesis cannot explain the continued warming of recent decades with any solar forcing estimate in the literature.

**On model performance: the models have performed well.** This is one of the most empirically settled questions in the mythology cluster. Hausfather et al. (2020, "Evaluating the performance of past climate model projections," *Geophysical Research Letters*, 47(1), e2019GL085378) evaluated 17 climate model projections from 1970 to 2011 against observed temperature records. They found that 14 of the 17 models were statistically consistent with observed warming — and that the small number of models that showed discrepancy tended to overestimate aerosol-induced cooling rather than to inflate CO2 sensitivity. Models from the 1990s and early 2000s, including Hansen et al.'s 1988 projections (Hansen, J. et al., 1988, "Global climate changes as forecast by Goddard Institute for Space Studies three-dimensional model," *Journal of Geophysical Research*, 93(D8), 9341–9364), have tracked observed temperatures with considerable skill.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The climate myth cluster is one of the most extensively studied examples of science misinformation in history, and the scholarship on why it persists offers genuinely important insights that popular climate communication often misses.

**The kernel problem.** Most climate myths are built around a true kernel: natural climate change is real; some scientists have raised legitimate methodological questions; CO2 fertilisation does occur; the Sun does affect climate. The existence of the kernel provides two functions: it gives the arguer epistemic cover ("I'm just pointing out that the science isn't settled") and it makes the myth harder to debunk without appearing to deny the kernel's truth. Effective debunking requires explicitly acknowledging the kernel before demonstrating why it does not support the derived conclusion (Cook, J. et al., 2017, "Neutralizing misinformation through inoculation: Exposing misleading argumentation techniques reduces their influence," *PLOS ONE*, 12(5), e0175799).

**Manufactured doubt as an industry.** The sociology of climate denial is historically documented and is not, on the evidence, primarily driven by sincere scientific disagreement. The systematic campaigns to manufacture doubt about climate science — modelled explicitly on the tobacco industry's playbook — have been documented by Oreskes & Conway (2010, *Merchants of Doubt*, Bloomsbury) using internal industry documents, funding records, and communications from think tanks including the Global Climate Coalition. These campaigns specifically targeted the perception of scientific consensus, knowing that public acceptance of regulation correlates with perceived consensus rather than with technical details. The manufactured-uncertainty strategy is why the "scientists disagree" myth has survived the quantification of the 97–99% consensus: the strategy is to make people feel the number is contested, not to provide a better number.

**Motivated cognition and identity.** The political and cultural sorting of climate beliefs is among the strongest in polling on any scientific topic. In the United States, acceptance of anthropogenic climate change correlates more strongly with political identity than with scientific literacy — the latter, counterintuitively, amplifies the gap between politically sorted groups rather than closing it (Kahan, D.M. et al., 2012, "The polarizing impact of science literacy and numeracy on perceived climate change risks," *Nature Climate Change*, 2, 732–735). This finding — which is the epistemic novelty of this section — implies that the climate myth cluster is not primarily a knowledge problem. Providing more facts to politically motivated sceptics does not, on average, shift their views and may entrench them. This has profound implications for science communication strategy.

**The asymmetry of the arguments.** Each myth requires a brief, confident assertion. Each rebuttal requires careful contextualisation, reference to multiple data streams, and engagement with genuine complexity. This asymmetry — what has been called "Brandolini's Law" or the bullshit asymmetry principle — systematically favours misinformation in rapid-cycle media environments. The natural cycle argument can be made in a sentence; explaining why the rate and fingerprint of current warming rules out natural forcing requires discussion of attribution methods, isotopic evidence, and climate model ensembles.

**The cultural resonance of individual liberty.** Climate change mitigation policies frequently involve restrictions on economic activity, carbon pricing, and state intervention in energy markets. For ideological communities organised around individual liberty and free market principles, the policy implications of accepting anthropogenic climate change are threatening. There is substantial evidence that, in such communities, policy rejection precedes and motivates scientific doubt rather than following from it (Campbell, T.H. & Kay, A.C., 2014, "Solution aversion: On the relation between ideology and motivated disbelief," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 107(5), 809–824). The myths are the intellectual superstructure of a prior policy rejection, which is why addressing the myths alone does not change the policy position.


## 4. Verdict

**False, with important internal differentiation.** The myths individually:

**Natural cycles:** False as a complete explanation. Natural forcings are fully accounted for in climate models and cannot explain observed warming since the mid-20th century. The isotopic signature of atmospheric CO2 (depleted C-14 and C-13, consistent with fossil fuel combustion) confirms that the increase in atmospheric CO2 is industrial in origin (Keeling, C.D. et al., 1979, "The 13C/12C Keeling Curve for atmospheric CO2," *Reviews of Geophysics and Space Physics*, 17(4), 953–977). Natural cycle mechanisms are well-understood and move far too slowly to account for the observed decadal warming rate.

**Scientific disagreement:** False. 97–99% of actively publishing climate scientists accept the consensus on anthropogenic warming. The perception of significant dissent has been manufactured through coordinated industry-funded campaigns and amplified by media presentation of "both sides."

**CO2 as plant food:** Misleading (partial truth weaponised). The CO2 fertilisation effect is real but limited, saturating well below projected end-of-century CO2 levels, and is counteracted by heat stress, water stress, and nutritional dilution effects that overwhelm the fertilisation benefit under realistic warming scenarios.

**Sun as driver:** False for the contemporary period. Solar output has been flat to declining since 1980. The warming fingerprint — more warming at night than day, more warming at the poles, cooling of the stratosphere while the troposphere warms — is specifically consistent with greenhouse forcing and inconsistent with solar forcing (Santer, B.D. et al., 2013, "Human and natural influences on the changing thermal structure of the atmosphere," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 110(43), 17235–17240).

**Models wrong:** False. The majority of past projections have tracked observed temperature trends within their stated uncertainty ranges. Discrepancies in earlier models were generally due to uncertain aerosol forcing, not CO2 sensitivity overestimation.

**Warmer is beneficial:** Contested at low warming levels; empirically rejected at high warming levels. There are documented regional benefits of moderate warming in high-latitude agriculture and energy use. These do not offset, on net, the projected costs from sea level rise, extreme weather intensification, ecosystem disruption, and agricultural losses in tropical and mid-latitude regions (IPCC, 2022, *Sixth Assessment Report — Working Group II: Impacts, Adaptation and Vulnerability*, Cambridge University Press).


## 5. The Wider Picture

The climate myth cluster has a political history that is at least as important as its scientific history, and disentangling the two is essential for understanding why the myths persist despite decades of scientific communication.

The period of most active myth production — roughly 1989 to 2009 — corresponds closely to the period of active organised opposition to climate policy by fossil fuel industry groups and affiliated think tanks. Documents obtained in litigation and through investigative journalism (including internal ExxonMobil research from the 1970s and 1980s, published by Franta, B., 2021, "Weaponizing economics: Big Oil, economic consultants, and climate policy delay," *Environmental Politics*, 31(4), 555–573) show that major fossil fuel companies had internal scientific assessments consistent with the mainstream consensus at least as early as 1979, while simultaneously funding public campaigns to manufacture doubt about that consensus. This is not a conspiracy theory; it is a documented historical record with primary source evidence.

The implications extend beyond climate science. The same rhetorical toolkit — manufactured consensus doubt, emphasising kernel truths, exploiting the asymmetry between assertion and rebuttal — has been applied to tobacco, leaded gasoline, acid rain, and ozone depletion. Climate denial is the largest and longest-running example of a recurrent pattern in the political economy of regulation.

The science communication literature that emerged in response has generated important advances in understanding how to inoculate people against misinformation before they encounter it (Cook et al., 2017), how to address motivated reasoning without triggering backfire effects, and how the framing of climate change as security threat, economic opportunity, or public health issue can shift belief in politically sorted populations more effectively than direct scientific communication.

The cluster of myths also reveals a genuine philosophical tension in democratic epistemology: expert consensus should carry significant weight in policy decisions, but populations have legitimate reasons to distrust expert institutions that have historically been influenced by industry and political pressure. Building the institutional credibility that makes expert consensus trustworthy is a problem that climate science communication has only partially solved.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes, PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and Climate Feedback (a specialist climate fact-checking outlet operated by a network of climate scientists) have collectively addressed the climate myth cluster extensively. Climate Feedback is particularly systematic: it operates a peer review model in which published articles are assessed by credentialed climate scientists, and it has published over 1,000 assessments of climate-related claims since 2015 (climatefeedback.org).

The standard approach across these outlets:

1. **Identify the specific version of the myth** — the natural cycles myth appears in many forms, from "Medieval Warm Period was as warm as today" to "Milankovitch cycles explain current warming," and each requires different evidence.

2. **Acknowledge the kernel** — responsible fact-checking explicitly confirms what is true in the claim before addressing what it gets wrong. Eliding the kernel concedes rhetorical ground to the myth.

3. **Cite attribution science directly** — the strongest evidence against natural cycle explanations is the isotopic fingerprint of atmospheric CO2 and the spatial pattern of warming. These are rarely cited in popular fact-checks because they require more technical explanation but are the most epistemically decisive evidence.

4. **Context the consensus quantification** — citing the 97% figure without explaining the methodology and without acknowledging the manufactured doubt campaign is less effective than presenting both.

The most persistent gap in fact-checker coverage is the motivated cognition dimension: fact-checkers typically present evidence as though the primary barrier to belief change is insufficient factual information. The empirical research on politically sorted science beliefs suggests this model is incomplete at best. The myths persist not primarily because people lack access to climate science but because accepting it conflicts with political identity, economic interest, or policy-adjacent worldviews that are not directly addressed by scientific evidence.

The epistemically sound position is: the scientific case for anthropogenic climate change is among the most thoroughly established of any empirical claim in the earth sciences. The persistence of the myths is evidence about social psychology and political economy, not about the state of the science.
