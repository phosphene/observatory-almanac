# Do the Acids in Coca-Cola Make It Harmful to Drink?


## 1. The Claim

The claim arrives packaged as a two-part document. The first part is a genuine, relatively harmless list of household tips attributed to writer Joey Green's 1995 book *Polish Your Furniture with Panty Hose*, featuring alternative uses for Coca-Cola: cleaning toilet bowls, removing rust from chrome bumpers, loosening corroded car battery terminals, removing grease from clothing, and tenderizing hams. These are real uses that reflect the mild cleaning properties of carbonated, slightly acidic liquid—they are noteworthy but not alarming.

The alarming part arrives at the end, presented as a separate section labeled "FYI" or "Important Information." This coda makes three specific claims: first, that Coke's active ingredient, phosphoric acid, renders the beverage's pH at 2.8, a level at which it will dissolve a nail in approximately four days; second, that Coca-Cola syrup concentrate is so corrosive that it must be transported under U.S. Department of Transportation Hazardous Materials placards for "highly corrosive" substances; and third, that a tooth placed in a cup of Coke will dissolve in 24 to 48 hours.

From these three claims, the document draws the conclusion: the acids in Coca-Cola make it dangerous to drink. The implicit argument is that a substance capable of dissolving nails, requiring hazardous materials transport, and destroying teeth within two days cannot be safe for regular human consumption. The document typically ends with the admonition "Drink up!"—a sarcastic form of consumer-awareness rhetoric that uses irony to reinforce the alarm.

This specific version circulated extensively via email in the late 1990s and early 2000s and has persisted in social media as screenshot content. It represents a hybrid genre: part legitimate consumer tips, part fabricated health scare. The hybridization is intentional and effective. Embedding alarming chemistry claims in a document whose first portion is demonstrably accurate creates credibility by association. If the toilet-cleaning tip works, why would the nail-dissolving pH claim be false?


## 2. What's Actually True

Coca-Cola does contain acids. The primary acids are phosphoric acid (H₃PO₄) and citric acid, with carbonic acid present as the result of dissolved CO₂. These are genuine ingredients whose presence in the drink is well-documented and publicly disclosed. The question is not whether acids are present but whether their concentrations make the drink dangerous—and the answer, grounded in basic chemistry and toxicology, is no.

**Phosphoric Acid**: Phosphoric acid is a weak to moderate acid that Coca-Cola uses primarily as a flavoring agent to add a slight tartness that balances the sweetness of sugar or high-fructose corn syrup. Its concentration in finished Coca-Cola is approximately 0.20 to 0.30 percent of the total formula by weight of syrup—equivalent to roughly 11 to 13 grams per gallon of syrup (Allen, 1994, *Secret Formula*, HarperCollins). In the ready-to-drink beverage, the dilution from water further reduces this already low concentration significantly. At these concentrations, phosphoric acid poses no acute corrosive hazard to biological tissue.

**pH Measurement and Its Misinterpretation**: The claim that Coca-Cola has a pH of 2.8 is approximately accurate—independent measurements of Coca-Cola pH range from about 2.5 to 3.4 depending on temperature, age, carbonation level, and measurement method (von Fraunhofer & Rogers, 2004, *General Dentistry*). But the significance of this pH value is routinely misread because of the logarithmic nature of the pH scale. The pH scale, developed by Danish chemist Søren Peder Lauritz Sørensen in 1909, is a negative logarithm of hydrogen ion activity (Sørensen, 1909, *Biochemische Zeitschrift*). Each unit change represents a tenfold change in hydrogen ion concentration, not a linear increment. The difference between pH 2.8 (Coca-Cola) and pH 1.0 (battery acid) represents a hydrogen ion concentration difference of approximately 63 times, not a small additional step. Conversely, the difference between pH 2.8 and pH 7.0 (neutral water) represents only 63,000 times higher hydrogen ion concentration in Coke than in water—a comparison that sounds alarming expressed as "63,000 times more acidic" but which, in absolute chemistry terms, is far from corrosive at the concentrations involved.

For comparison: vinegar has a pH of approximately 2.4 to 3.0—lower than Coca-Cola, or equivalent (Beheshti et al., 2017, *Journal of Food Composition and Analysis*). Lemon juice registers approximately pH 2.0 to 2.5 (Penniston et al., 2008, *Journal of Endourology*). Orange juice sits at pH 3.3 to 4.0 (Lussi & Jaeggi, 2008, *Monographs in Oral Science*). All of these foods and beverages are consumed in large volumes by billions of people daily without causing corrosive injury to the gastrointestinal tract or teeth. Coca-Cola is not markedly different from these in terms of acidity—it is roughly comparable to vinegar and measurably less acidic than lemon juice. If the acids in Coke are dangerous to drink, then so is every citrus fruit and every vinegar-containing food product. This collateral conclusion is not asserted because it is obviously false, revealing the selective alarm in the original claim.

**Gastric Acid Comparison**: The stomach itself continuously produces hydrochloric acid (HCl) for digestion at concentrations that produce a gastric pH of approximately 1.5 to 3.5 in the fasted state (Feldman et al., 2010, *Sleisenger and Fordtran's Gastrointestinal and Liver Disease*, Saunders). This means that Coca-Cola, when it enters the stomach, encounters an environment that is as acidic or more acidic than the drink itself. The gastric acid already present is the strong acid environment; adding Coca-Cola does not meaningfully alter this chemistry. The claim that Coke's acids pose a novel danger to internal tissues ignores the fundamental fact that these tissues evolved in intimate contact with endogenous acids vastly stronger than any dietary acid.

**The Nail-Dissolving Claim**: The claim that phosphoric acid in Coke will dissolve a nail in four days is grossly false as presented. A nail placed in a cup of Coca-Cola will develop surface rust and light corrosion over several days because the mildly acidic, oxygenated liquid creates a favorable environment for iron oxidation—but the nail does not dissolve. Iron oxidation at the pH of Coke is a slow surface process, not bulk dissolution. After four days, one would have a somewhat discolored, slightly corroded nail, not a missing nail. Concentrated phosphoric acid at industrial strength can corrode metals, but the acid in Coke is diluted to concentrations that are chemically incapable of accomplishing this at any realistic timescale.

The tooth-dissolution claim has slightly more nuance but is equally inaccurate as stated. Enamel does erode in contact with acidic beverages over time—this is a genuine dental concern supported by clinical evidence—but a tooth placed in Coca-Cola does not dissolve in 24 to 48 hours. Studies of dental erosion from acidic beverages use in vitro setups with continuous exposure and find measurable enamel microhardness reduction over days or weeks of sustained contact (Larsen & Nyvad, 1999, *European Journal of Oral Sciences*). Real-world dental erosion from Coca-Cola occurs through habitual, repeated oral exposure over years, not overnight dissolution. Regular drinkers who swish Coke around their teeth and sip it constantly throughout the day do show elevated erosion risk; a single daily drink consumed in ordinary fashion presents minimal erosion risk.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The Coke acids claim exploits a specific and underappreciated gap in public scientific literacy: the distinction between *capacity* and *concentration*. This is arguably the most important explanatory gap in food safety and toxicology communication, more consequential than simple factual ignorance, because it systematically derails reasoning about nearly all chemical hazards.

**Concentration as the Invisible Variable**: Paracelsus, the sixteenth-century Swiss physician and alchemist, articulated the foundational principle of toxicology in his dictum: *sola dosis facit venenum* ("the dose alone makes the poison"). This principle—that virtually any substance is harmless at sufficiently low concentrations and toxic at sufficiently high concentrations—is the organizing concept of the entire field of toxicology. Yet it is strikingly absent from popular discourse about food chemicals. The statement "Coca-Cola contains phosphoric acid" carries implicit danger information in common discourse precisely because the dose-dependence of acid hazard is not part of the mental model most people apply when evaluating food safety claims. People reason: "Phosphoric acid is a corrosive industrial chemical → Coke contains phosphoric acid → Coke is corrosive." Each inference is individually sensible given the wrong background knowledge, and the conclusion follows from the premises. The error is not in the logical structure but in the missing premise: "at the concentration present in Coke."

**pH Logarithmic Miscalibration**: The epistemic novelty here, and the key to understanding why pH-based claims are particularly effective in generating false alarms, is what might be termed *logarithmic miscalibration*: the systematic failure of linear intuition when applied to logarithmic scales. The pH scale's logarithmic structure means that acid concentrations at pH values across the edible range—roughly pH 2 to pH 7 for food and beverages—differ by factors of up to 100,000, yet popular representations treat pH values as if they were linear descriptors. The statement "Coke is pH 2.8, battery acid is pH 1, neutral water is pH 7" sounds like Coke is much closer to battery acid than to neutral water on a scale where 1 is dangerous and 7 is safe. In terms of linear spacing on the mental scale, Coke at 2.8 is "close" to 1. In terms of actual chemistry, Coke at pH 2.8 contains roughly 63 times fewer hydrogen ions than battery acid at pH 1—a large chemical difference—and the biological harm potential of dilute phosphoric acid in the stomach is categorically different from concentrated sulfuric acid.

This miscalibration is not merely a failure of mathematical education. Research by Dehaene and colleagues on numerical cognition demonstrates that human intuition about numbers is fundamentally logarithmic in origin, not linear—very young children and individuals in cultures without formal number education map small quantities logarithmically, not linearly (Dehaene et al., 2008, *Science*). Linear representation of quantities is a cultural and educational achievement that has been successfully transmitted for most everyday numerical contexts, but the pH scale—a log-transformed representation of a physical quantity—reverses this: it asks people who have learned to think linearly about numbers to apply logarithmic interpretation to what appears to be an ordinary numerical scale. The result is systematic miscalibration. The number "2.8" on the pH scale communicates far less acid danger than its proximity to "1" suggests to linear-thinking intuition.

**The Dual-Use Chemical Category**: A particularly effective rhetorical device in the Coke acids document is the implication that Coke's industrial uses—listed affirmatively in the "helpful tips" section—constitute evidence of its danger as a food. The document reports that Coke is used to clean car engines, remove road haze from windshields, and loosen rusted bolts, then pivots immediately to the claim about pH 2.8 and nail dissolution. The implicit argument is: "See how aggressive this substance is? Now drink it." This dual-use framing is misleading because mild acids are genuinely useful for non-food cleaning applications—the same acidity that loosens rust from a bolt or removes calcium deposits from a toilet bowl is entirely harmless to human tissue. Vinegar, lemon juice, and dilute citric acid are all effective cleaning agents and are simultaneously consumed in vast quantities without harm. The dual-use character of mild acids does not constitute evidence of food hazard; it reflects the concentration-dependence of chemical effects.

**The Hazardous Materials Transport Claim**: The specific claim about Coke syrup requiring Hazardous Materials transport placards deserves individual scrutiny because it invokes regulatory authority—a particularly effective plausibility anchor. The claim is false. Concentrated Coca-Cola syrup, while moderately acidic and in larger quantity than the final beverage, does not meet the U.S. DOT criteria for Hazardous Materials classification as a "corrosive material." DOT Hazardous Materials regulations define corrosive liquids as those capable of causing visible destruction or irreversible alteration in human skin tissue within a specified test period at concentrations above specific thresholds (49 CFR 173.136). Coca-Cola syrup, at its actual concentration, does not meet this threshold. This claim appears designed to invoke regulatory language to manufacture implied severity without being verifiable by the lay audience.


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: FALSE**

The acids in Coca-Cola do not make it dangerous to drink. The beverage contains phosphoric acid and citric acid at concentrations comparable to common dietary foods including vinegar, fruit juices, and numerous condiments. Its pH of approximately 2.5 to 3.4 falls within the range of normal food and beverage acids. The gastric acid naturally present in the human stomach is equal to or stronger than Coca-Cola's acid content. The specific claims—nail dissolution in four days, tooth dissolution in 24 to 48 hours, hazardous materials transport requirements—are false or grossly exaggerated. The documented real risks associated with regular Coca-Cola consumption relate to its sugar content and the long-term erosive effects of habitual acidic beverage contact with dental enamel, not to any acute corrosive danger from its acid chemistry.

**Gap Category: Concentration-Conflation**

This myth exemplifies the most pervasive and consequential gap in public food safety discourse: the conflation of a chemical's identity with its concentration-dependent hazard properties. Correcting it requires not merely stating "Coke is safe to drink" but communicating the specific explanatory framework—the dose makes the poison, the pH scale is logarithmic, and ordinary dietary acids are not industrial corrosives—that allows people to evaluate future similar claims. Without this framework, the myth recurs in new forms with new substances.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Coke acids myth sits at the intersection of two large-scale communicative phenomena: the viral potential of consumer product health scares, and the vulnerability of public scientific literacy to logarithmically structured claims.

**The Pop Science Health Scare Genre**: The late 1990s and early 2000s saw an explosion of "consumer product hazard" chain emails and forwarded documents that followed a recognizable formula: take a familiar product; find or fabricate alarming facts about its chemical components; build toward a dramatic warning conclusion; distribute as a social act of concern for friends and family. Aspartame, MSG, fluoride, high-fructose corn syrup, microwave ovens, non-stick cookware, and aluminum cookware all became subjects of this genre during this period. The Coca-Cola acids document is a near-perfect specimen because its first portion is genuinely accurate (the cleaning tips are real), its chemical facts are partially accurate (Coke does contain these acids), and its conclusions are false (the concentrations involved are not dangerous).

**Dental Erosion and Legitimate Concerns**: The scientific literature does document real dental health concerns from acidic beverage consumption, though not the acute dissolution claimed by the myth. Systematic review by Lussi and Jaeggi (2008, *Monographs in Oral Science*) found that erosive dental wear is increasing in prevalence in industrialized populations and that regular consumption of acidic drinks—including carbonated soft drinks, citrus juices, and sports drinks—is a contributing factor through prolonged enamel exposure. The mechanism is not chemical burn from corrosive acids but gradual dissolution of hydroxyapatite (the mineral component of enamel) at low pH over extended contact periods. This is a real, evidence-based concern that warrants dental health advice about beverage frequency and oral hygiene. It is distinct from the myth's claim that simply drinking Coke poses acute chemical danger.

**Phosphoric Acid and Bone Health**: Separate from the acute corrosion claim, epidemiological literature has examined associations between heavy cola consumption and bone mineral density. Cross-sectional data from Tucker and colleagues (2006, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*) found that cola consumption—but not other carbonated beverages—was associated with lower bone mineral density in older women, suggesting possible effects of phosphoric acid on calcium metabolism or displacement of dairy consumption. The epidemiological evidence is mixed, mechanistic pathways are contested, and effect sizes are modest. This is a legitimate area of ongoing scientific inquiry that merits nuanced reporting, entirely distinct from the corrosive hazard myth.

**The Calibration Challenge for Science Communication**: The most difficult aspect of correcting the Coke acids myth is preserving legitimate concerns (dental erosion risk from habitual use, the nutritional empty-calorie problem of sugar-sweetened beverages) while decisively rejecting impossible claims (acute corrosion, hazmat transport requirements). Effective correction requires a two-level response: falsify the acute chemical hazard claim using chemistry; acknowledge real dietary concerns using epidemiological evidence. Conflating the two responses—either by dismissing all concerns about Coke as myth, or by accepting the myth as a proxy for real concerns—both misrepresent the science.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published its fact-check of the Coke acids claim on November 20, 2000, authored by David Mikkelson. The verdict was FALSE. The analysis correctly identified the document's hybrid structure—legitimate household tips followed by fabricated health claims—and applied basic chemistry to demonstrate that concentration is the critical variable. The entry correctly noted that Coca-Cola contains less citric acid than orange juice and that phosphoric acid concentrations in Coke are far too low to dissolve nails, teeth, or steaks overnight.

The Snopes entry emphasized the comparison between Coke's acids and the acids in other common foods, which is the most effective corrective frame: if the claim that "Coke's acids are dangerous" were generalized consistently, it would condemn virtually every acidic food in the human diet. The fact that this conclusion is not drawn by the myth's proponents reveals its selectivity—it is targeting a specific commercial product, not making a general claim about dietary acid chemistry.

The entry also correctly raised the gastric acid comparison—the stomach's hydrochloric acid is far stronger than any acid in Coca-Cola—which highlights the fundamental incoherence of the claim about internal harm. If Coke's acids were dangerous to the gastrointestinal tract, they would be overcome by the stomach's own chemistry before causing any damage. The body's digestive system is designed to operate in an acidic environment far more intense than anything an ordinary commercial beverage could create.

For fact-checkers assessing similar product-acid health claims, the analytical checklist should include: What are the actual concentrations of the named acids? How do they compare to other widely consumed acidic foods? What does basic toxicology say about the dose-dependence of this acid type? Is the pH value being reported, and is the logarithmic nature of pH being communicated? What is the comparison to gastric acid? These questions cut through the rhetorical structure of the claim and address its specific chemical content, which is the only level at which it can be responsibly evaluated.
