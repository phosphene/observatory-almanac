# Did Coca-Cola Announce Change to 'More Relevant' Black Santa Claus?


## 1. The Claim

In December 2021, images circulated widely on social media platforms—particularly Facebook—purporting to show a CNN tweet reporting that Coca-Cola had announced it was "changing Santa Claus from a caucasian man to an afro-american man." The purported tweet attributed the following statement to Coca-Cola CEO James Quincey: "The time has come for a Santa Claus that's more relevant to our times." The tweet's graphic showed what appeared to be a painting of an elderly Black man dressed as Santa Claus holding a Coke, along with the tagline "Together the Magic Happens."

The claim arrived in the context of Christmas 2021 and triggered predictable waves of cultural controversy: enthusiastic sharing by those who viewed it positively as a representation change, and outraged sharing by those who viewed it as an inappropriate corporate intrusion into a cultural icon. Both categories of sharing amplified the claim's spread without, in most cases, verifying its accuracy. The specific provenance of the image—whether it represented genuine CNN reporting about a genuine Coca-Cola announcement—was rarely interrogated.

This claim belongs to a well-documented category of misinformation: fabricated social media screenshots from reputable media organizations or verified accounts, attributed to real public figures, designed to provoke cultural controversy. The specific structure—a fabricated tweet from a major news network, quoting a real corporate executive, about a commercially and symbolically loaded announcement—is engineered for maximum viral spread across ideological lines. Supporters and opponents of the claimed change share equally, each group amplifying the fabricated content in opposite emotional registers.


## 2. What's Actually True

The purported CNN tweet was fabricated. Snopes investigators found no record of CNN ever publishing an article or posting a tweet to the effect described. No such announcement was made by Coca-Cola. No record exists of James Quincey—the company's real CEO—writing or uttering the words falsely attributed to him. The independent claim that Coca-Cola was changing Santa Claus's race as a global branding initiative was entirely without factual basis.

Multiple internal features of the fabricated tweet flagged its inauthenticity for careful reviewers, though casual social media scrollers typically did not notice them:

First, the tweet showed an image of what purported to be a Black Santa Claus alongside text that said the official look would be revealed "soon." This is internally inconsistent: a tweet announcing an imminent reveal would not display the object to be revealed. A news organization reporting on an announcement about a future reveal would not typically headline the story with an image that appears to show the already-finalized result.

Second, the tweet used the term "afro-american" to describe Black people—an outdated and by 2021 essentially unused construction in American English that CNN had, according to a Twitter search of CNN's verified account history, never used in any actual tweet. The preferred contemporary journalistic terminology at CNN and nearly all major American news organizations by 2021 was "Black" (capitalized) for the ethnic/racial identity in question. "Afro-American" as a compound term was common in American usage from roughly the 1960s to the 1980s but had largely fallen from use in both popular and journalistic contexts by the 1990s; its presence in a purported 2021 CNN tweet is a strong marker of fabrication.

The factual kernel underlying the false claim is real but substantially different in scope and context. In December 2020—one year before the viral meme—Brazilian television network TV Globo collaborated with Coca-Cola to produce a special Christmas drama featuring an elderly Black man's efforts to play the role of Santa Claus, exploring themes of racial discrimination and stereotyping within Brazilian society. The production was created by advertising firm McCann and was marketed with the tagline "Together the Magic Happens." The artwork from this Brazilian campaign—specifically a painting of the Black Santa figure holding a Coke—appears to be the same image used in the fabricated tweet.

This Brazilian campaign is authentically verified—it was documented in advertising industry publications including Ads of the World, and the McCann production video was publicly available. But it is categorically different from a global corporate announcement about changing the canonical Santa Claus image: it was a single country's television drama, exploring racial diversity and inclusion themes within Brazilian cultural context, produced for a specific cultural moment (the Christmas 2020 season in Brazil during a year marked by global reckonings with racial justice).

The distinction matters both factually and analytically. A nationally specific creative drama exploring racial representation within a local cultural context is a very different thing from a corporate announcement that the company intends to globally replace the traditional white Santa Claus image. The first is an exercise in local cultural creativity and social commentary; the second is an assertion about global brand management with enormous commercial implications and cultural stakes.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The fabricated Coca-Cola Black Santa tweet is a textbook example of content engineered for maximum ideological shareability—what communication scholars sometimes call "partisan misinformation" (Pennycook, G. and Rand, D.G., "Fighting misinformation on social media using crowdsourced judgments of news source quality," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 2019).

Several design features of the fabricated content made it unusually effective at spreading belief:

The claimed source—CNN—carried institutional credibility that pre-authorized the claim for audiences who trusted CNN. For audiences who distrusted CNN, the claim activated a different but equally powerful confirmatory mechanism: evidence that CNN was promoting "woke corporate propaganda." In both directions, the CNN attribution increased the claim's persuasive weight.

The quoted statement attributed to James Quincey was calibrated for maximum controversy. The phrase "more relevant to our times" echoes the language of corporate diversity and inclusion communications, making it feel plausible in the mouth of a Fortune 500 CEO. The phrase also implies that the traditional white Santa image is no longer relevant—which is precisely the kind of statement that generates strong reactions from both supporters (who might see it as a necessary cultural update) and opponents (who might see it as an attack on tradition). Fabricated quotes attributed to real public figures are particularly effective disinformation because they create a sense of direct access to the attributed person's views that is difficult to immediately gainsay.

The visual element—the image of a Black Santa holding a Coke—was real, in the sense that it existed as genuine Coca-Cola marketing material (for the Brazilian campaign). The presence of a genuine Coca-Cola image lent material authenticity to the fabricated text framing it. Misinformation that combines authentic visual material with false textual framing is reliably more difficult to debunk than purely fabricated content, because the visual element provides a real evidentiary anchor that makes the overall claim feel verifiable.

The timing—December 2021, the height of the Christmas season—maximized the claim's emotional resonance. Santa Claus is among the most culturally loaded symbols in the Western Christmas tradition; his visual representation is not merely a commercial question but a point of genuine cultural attachment and contestation. The claim arrived when attention to Christmas imagery was already heightened, and when political polarization in the United States around race and representation had generated intense sensitivity on all sides.

The broader cultural context of corporate "woke" brand positioning is also relevant. By 2021, many major corporations had made explicit public commitments to diversity and racial equity following the 2020 murder of George Floyd and the associated social movement activity. Some of these commitments were substantive; many were widely critiqued as superficial brand positioning. Audience familiarity with real examples of corporations adopting racial equity messaging made a fictitious Coca-Cola racial rebranding announcement plausible within a known pattern of corporate behavior—even though the specific claim was false.


## 4. Verdict

**MOSTLY FALSE**: The specific claim—that Coca-Cola announced a global change to a Black Santa Claus, with CEO James Quincey quoted as saying "the time has come for a Santa Claus that's more relevant to our times"—is false. The purported CNN tweet was fabricated. CNN never published such a story. James Quincey never made the attributed statement. Coca-Cola made no such global announcement.

The claim is rated "Mostly False" rather than simply "False" because a kernel of truth underlies the fabrication: Coca-Cola did collaborate with TV Globo in Brazil in December 2020 on a Christmas drama featuring an elderly Black Santa figure, which was a genuine marketing and social commentary initiative within Brazilian cultural context. The artwork from that campaign appears to be the same image used in the fabricated tweet. The Brazilian campaign was real; the global rebranding announcement was fabricated.

The "Mostly False" verdict appropriately preserves the distinction between a false specific claim and the real but narrower factual reality that informed—and was distorted by—the fabricated content.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Black Santa claim, its fabrication, and the real Brazilian campaign that provided its visual material intersect with several substantive cultural and commercial questions worth examining on their merits.

The question of Santa Claus's racial representation is not trivial in cultural or commercial terms. The canonical modern visual of Santa Claus—portly, bearded, red-suited—is a relatively recent standardization, achieved largely through the influence of Coca-Cola's own advertising campaigns beginning in the 1930s. Artist Haddon Sundblom, commissioned by Coca-Cola, painted a series of Santa illustrations beginning in 1931 that became the defining visual template for the modern Santa figure in American and subsequently global popular culture. The Santa Claus that most people now picture is, in a very direct sense, a Coca-Cola advertising creation.

This history (Pendergrast, M., *For God, Country and Coca-Cola*, Basic Books, 2nd ed., 2000) means that Coca-Cola has a genuine and traceable role in constructing the canonical Santa visual, which makes the question of whether Coca-Cola would or could change that visual representation something other than an absurd hypothetical. The company that created the dominant Santa visual could, in principle, campaign to modify it—though whether a single company retains the cultural authority to modify a now-global symbol that has taken on its own life beyond any commercial origin is a different question.

The Brazilian campaign's existence reflects the genuine complexity of how a global brand manages culturally variable advertising across different national contexts. Santa Claus is not equally culturally embedded in all of Coca-Cola's markets; in Brazil, a country with a more complex racial demographic and a different holiday cultural tradition, questions of racial representation in Christmas iconography have a different valence than in the United States or Northern Europe. The TV Globo/Coca-Cola drama was responding to a specific Brazilian cultural debate about racial representation in a holiday that Brazil adopted under significant colonial cultural influence.

Brazilian scholars have written extensively on the cultural politics of whiteness in Brazilian media and advertising, documenting a historical pattern in which the aspirational ideal projected in advertising imagery has been consistently whiter than the Brazilian population (Sodré, M., *Claros e Escuros: Identidade, Povo e Mídia no Brasil*, Vozes, 1999). The 2020 Black Santa drama can be understood as a response to this critique—a major broadcaster and a multinational consumer brand deploying the Christmas season to produce content that validates Afro-Brazilian identity within a holiday tradition that has historically been represented in ways that exclude or marginalize it.

This context is entirely absent from the fabricated tweet and the controversy it generated. The nuanced question of whether a nationally specific campaign addressing a real racial representation problem in Brazilian media counts as a meaningful representation change is collapsed, in the meme's framing, into a binary American culture-war question about whether corporations are entitled to change a culturally sacred white icon. The specific cultural, geographic, and temporal context of the Brazilian campaign—December 2020, Brazil, TV Globo drama, racial representation in Brazilian media—is erased and replaced with its most controversy-maximizing American equivalent.

The phenomenon of real culturally specific content being decontextualized, stripped of its geography and specificity, and reframed in the terms of American cultural politics is a well-documented pattern in cross-cultural misinformation. Research on "culture war misinformation" (Benkler, Y., Faris, R. and Roberts, H., *Network Propaganda*, Oxford University Press, 2018) finds that material from other national contexts is regularly extracted, reframed, and deployed in American political discourse to manufacture outrage about phenomena that are either not occurring in the United States or are substantially different from their representation.

The Coca-Cola Black Santa case illustrates this pattern with particular clarity. An authentic Brazilian campaign about racial representation in Brazilian Christmas media was photographed, stripped of its identifying context, attached to a fabricated CNN tweet and a fake executive quote, and deployed as evidence in an American culture war debate about corporate "wokeism." Each step of this decontextualization and reframing was an act of misinformation manufacture—the final product bore almost no resemblance to the reality of the original campaign.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's treatment of the Coca-Cola Black Santa claim, authored by Dan MacGuill and published December 15, 2021, is a competent example of the most common type of misinformation debunking: verifying whether a claimed screenshot from a news organization is authentic.

MacGuill's methodology includes standard verification steps for fabricated screenshots: searching CNN's actual tweet history for the claimed content (finding no such tweet), searching for CNN news stories on the claimed announcement (finding none), and identifying internal inconsistencies in the fabricated image (the word "afro-american" not appearing in CNN's documented tweet history; the inconsistency between showing the new look and claiming it will be "revealed soon").

The affirmative identification of the real source material—the TV Globo/Coca-Cola Brazilian campaign—is important for the "Mostly False" verdict. Without identifying what real material the fabricated tweet drew from, the claim would be simply "False"; the identification of the Brazilian campaign as the genuine kernel of truth behind the fabrication justifies the more nuanced verdict.

One analytical gap in the fact-check is reduced engagement with the Haddon Sundblom/Coca-Cola history of creating the modern Santa visual. Given that Coca-Cola's connection to the canonical modern Santa image is itself the underlying reason the company has specific symbolic salience when Santa representation claims are made, this historical context would strengthen the fact-check's framing. An audience that understands Coca-Cola's role in standardizing the modern Santa visual is better equipped to evaluate why a story about Coca-Cola changing Santa felt plausible even without independent verification.

The verdict of "Mostly False" rather than "False" is defensible but worth examining. Some fact-checking organizations apply a purely binary "True/False" framework to claims like this, in which the specific claim—"Coca-Cola announced a global change to Black Santa, as reported by CNN, with this executive quote"—is false in every verifiable particular, and the Brazilian campaign's existence does not change that. The "Mostly False" verdict implies that a meaningful portion of the claim is true, when what is true is narrower than this framing suggests: what is true is that Coca-Cola produced some imagery related to Black Santa representation, in a specific country, for a specific campaign, one year before the viral moment. This is true but is substantially different from the specific claim, and "Mostly False" may inadvertently validate more of the claim than is warranted.

For media literacy education, the Black Santa case offers a productive illustration of how fabricated screenshots can combine real visual material with false textual claims to produce highly shareable misinformation. The methodology of checking claimed screenshots—searching the attributed account's actual history, examining the image for internal anachronisms or inconsistencies with the platform's known design elements, and seeking confirmation from the attributed publisher—is transferable to the large and growing category of fabricated social media post imagery used as misinformation vectors.
