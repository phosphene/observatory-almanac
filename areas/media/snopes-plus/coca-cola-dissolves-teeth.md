# Soaking a Tooth in Coca-Cola Will Dissolve It Overnight


## 1. The Claim

The claim that a tooth submerged in a glass of Coca-Cola will dissolve overnight—or at minimum be severely damaged within a short time period—has circulated as a health warning, a science demonstration suggestion, and a cautionary tale about soda consumption for decades. It appears in health education materials, parental warnings about children's sugar and soda consumption, internet forum discussions, and social media posts. Some versions are precise ("the tooth will be gone by morning"), others more vague ("Coca-Cola will dissolve a tooth"), and still others frame it as an empirical challenge ("try it yourself").

The claim often accompanies warnings about the dangers of regular soda consumption, particularly for children and adolescents, and is frequently deployed by dentists, health educators, and parents seeking to motivate behavior change in soft drink consumption habits. In this sense, the claim serves a health education function—it is not mere recreational misinformation but a piece of messaging deliberately constructed (or at least deliberately circulated) to discourage a behavior that does carry genuine health risks.

The Coca-Cola tooth claim fits into a broader category of health education mythology that uses dramatic and visually compelling claims to motivate behavior change. Similar claims exist about the acidity of energy drinks, the sugar content of fruit juices, and the effects of various foods and beverages on dental, metabolic, or cardiovascular health. These claims often have a true core—soda does affect dental health, energy drinks are highly acidic, fruit juices do contain significant sugar—but the dramatic version significantly exaggerates the magnitude or timeframe of harm.

The challenge posed by this category of claim is epistemic: the claims are partially grounded in real health concerns, the exaggeration is intended to be motivationally useful rather than simply false, and the underlying message (reduce soda consumption, particularly in children) may be health-promoting even when the specific claim is inaccurate. This creates a dilemma for fact-checkers and health communicators: accurately debunking the claim may inadvertently undermine the legitimate health message the claim was designed to convey.

This is an EARC: E (Exaggerated) entry, not a simple refutation—because the health concern underlying the dramatic claim is real and documented, even though the dramatic version of the claim is false.


## 2. What's Actually True

Coca-Cola is acidic. The drink's pH is approximately 2.5—comparable to household vinegar (pH ~2.5-3.0) and significantly more acidic than coffee (pH ~4-5) or orange juice (pH ~3.5-4.0). The primary acids responsible for this acidity are phosphoric acid (added directly as an ingredient for flavor, stabilization, and antimicrobial function) and carbonic acid (produced when carbon dioxide dissolves in water during carbonation). Small quantities of natural fruit acids may also be present in flavored variants.

Tooth enamel—the hard, outermost layer of teeth—is primarily composed of hydroxyapatite, a crystalline calcium phosphate mineral. Hydroxyapatite begins to demineralize (dissolve) at pH levels below approximately 5.5. This threshold, called the critical pH for enamel dissolution, means that Coca-Cola (pH ~2.5) is well within the range that can demineralize enamel.

Clinical and laboratory evidence of soda-related dental erosion is well-established. Studies in dental literature document increased rates of dental erosion in frequent soda consumers, particularly children and adolescents who consume multiple sodas per day. Laboratory studies exposing extracted teeth to continuous submersion in soda solutions do show measurable enamel loss over periods of days to weeks. The real question is the magnitude and timeframe.

A tooth submerged continuously in Coca-Cola will not dissolve overnight. Laboratory studies examining enamel hardness (measured by microhardness testing) and enamel thickness after continuous submersion in cola beverages typically report measurable but modest erosion over periods of 24-72 hours—on the order of microns of surface enamel affected, not gross dissolution of the tooth structure. The tooth remains visibly intact and structurally present after overnight submersion; it shows measurable surface changes but is not dissolved or even dramatically degraded.

The key variables that make in-mouth soda exposure different from laboratory submersion are: (1) saliva, which provides continuous buffering of acid and contains proteins that protect enamel; (2) the temporary nature of soda exposure during drinking (minutes per serving versus continuous submersion); (3) the diluting and clearing effects of saliva flow and swallowing; (4) the presence of fluoride in most modern water supplies, which helps remineralize enamel between acid exposures.

Continuous laboratory submersion, the condition often implied in "try soaking a tooth overnight" demonstrations, bypasses all of these protective factors. It is therefore a worst-case scenario that significantly overstates the real-world exposure during normal soda consumption. A tooth bathed in Coca-Cola for hours without saliva or dilution shows more erosion than a tooth in a mouth consuming Coca-Cola in normal serving amounts.

The dental erosion risk from frequent soda consumption is nonetheless clinically documented and significant. High-frequency soda consumers—defined in research literature as consuming three or more servings per day—show measurably higher rates of dental erosion compared to non-consumers or low-frequency consumers. The mechanism is cumulative: each acidic exposure causes some demineralization, and if acid exposure is frequent enough, demineralization outpaces remineralization, leading to net enamel loss over months and years.

Clinical presentations of soda-related dental erosion are well-characterized. Dentists observe a pattern of smooth, cupped lesions on occlusal (chewing) surfaces, general thinning of enamel leading to increased sensitivity, and in severe cases, loss of vertical dimension as enamel wears away. These findings are associated with chronic heavy soda consumption and represent real, significant dental harm—just not the overnight dissolution suggested by the dramatic claim.

Energy drinks tend to be more erosive than cola beverages due to higher concentrations of organic acids (citric acid is particularly aggressive toward enamel). Lemon juice and vinegar are similarly or more acidic than cola. Interestingly, several sports drinks, often perceived as healthier alternatives to soda, can have comparable or higher erosion potential than colas due to their organic acid content.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The dramatic dissolution claim has several features that make it epistemically compelling despite being false.

First, the causal mechanism is real. Coca-Cola is acidic; acid dissolves enamel; therefore, Coca-Cola dissolves enamel. This syllogism is correct in its components even when wrong about its implied magnitude and timeframe. People who know that acid dissolves calcium minerals (a basic chemistry fact encountered in school) have good reason to believe that highly acidic soda would affect a calcified tooth. The inferential path to the claim is legitimate, even though the specific version of the claim is exaggerated.

Second, the claim is easy to test—or so it appears. "Put a tooth in Coke overnight" is a concrete, simple experiment that seems like it would definitively confirm or refute the claim. The problem is that confirming overnight dissolution requires a dramatic visual change that does not occur, but observing subtle enamel changes (small shifts in surface microhardness, slight discoloration) requires equipment most people do not have. Someone who tries the tooth-in-Coke experiment and sees a tooth that is visibly intact the next morning will correctly conclude that the dramatic dissolution claim is false—but may draw the additional incorrect conclusion that Coke has no effect on teeth, which is also wrong.

Third, health education contexts give the claim credibility. When a dentist, teacher, or parent cites the Coca-Cola tooth claim, it carries professional or parental authority. These are not sources that listeners routinely subject to factual scrutiny. The claim's function in health messaging—discouraging excessive soda consumption—seems aligned with the values of those who disseminate it, further reducing the threshold for critical evaluation.

Fourth, the visual "demonstration" format is powerful. Showing a tooth in a glass of Coke, or describing such a demonstration, activates a laboratory ethos—empirical, show-rather-than-tell, verifiable. The demonstration format mimics the authority of scientific experiment even when the actual outcome of the demonstration does not confirm the claim.


## 4. Verdict

**EXAGGERATED**: A tooth will not dissolve overnight in Coca-Cola. Laboratory studies confirm measurable but modest enamel surface changes after continuous submersion over 24+ hours, not dramatic dissolution. The underlying claim that Coca-Cola is acidic and that this acidity can erode tooth enamel over time is well-supported by clinical and laboratory evidence—but the magnitude and timeframe of the dramatic claim are false.

Coca-Cola (pH ~2.5) is genuinely acidic and does cause cumulative enamel erosion in high-frequency consumers, which is a documented clinical finding with real dental health implications. Chronic heavy soda consumption is a risk factor for dental erosion. These facts are legitimate health messages. The overnight dissolution claim is an exaggeration of those facts that, while perhaps intended to be motivationally effective, misrepresents the actual mechanism and timescale of acid erosion risk.

The accurate message: Coca-Cola is acidic; frequent, chronic consumption creates real risk of enamel erosion over time; moderate consumption in the context of good dental hygiene (including fluoride exposure and not continuously bathing teeth in soda) poses limited acute risk.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Coca-Cola tooth claim sits within a larger landscape of health education communication that uses dramatic or emotionally resonant claims to motivate behavior change, sometimes at the cost of accuracy. This represents a genuine dilemma in public health communication: does accuracy constrain effective messaging, and if so, which should yield?

The evidence base on this question in health communication research is nuanced. Some research suggests that correcting exaggerated health claims—"actually, Coke won't dissolve your tooth overnight"—does reduce fear-based motivation without proportionally increasing accurate concern about cumulative acid effects. Other research suggests that accurate, specific claims ("three or more sodas per day is associated with measurably higher dental erosion over time") are equally or more effective than dramatic exaggerations while preserving audience trust and receptiveness to future health messages.

The trust dimension is important. When audiences discover that health claims they were taught are exaggerated—particularly when they attempt to verify the claim and find it false—they may generalize their skepticism to other health messages from the same sources. Dentists who use the overnight dissolution claim to motivate patients, and whose patients then test it, may find their credibility on other dental health topics undermined. This is a real cost of exaggerated health messaging that is often not considered when the messaging is constructed.

The broader context of beverage acidity and dental health is genuinely complex and worthy of more precise public communication. Cola beverages, energy drinks, sports drinks, citrus juices, and even many sparkling mineral waters all have pH levels below the enamel dissolution threshold of 5.5. The variety of acidic beverages and their variable patterns of consumption create different risk profiles that cannot be captured in a single "Coke dissolves teeth" message.

Research on protective factors provides an important counterpoint to the simple "acid erodes enamel" message. Saliva flow, fluoride availability, rinsing patterns after acidic beverage consumption, and the concentration of calcium and phosphate in saliva all affect individual risk. People with xerostomia (dry mouth), which reduces saliva flow, are at significantly higher risk from acidic beverage consumption because they lack the primary buffering mechanism. This population—often including people on multiple medications that cause dry mouth as a side effect—represents a high-risk group whose risk profile is not captured by generic "Coke dissolves teeth" messaging.

The cultural context of Coca-Cola as a target for health messaging is itself interesting. Coca-Cola is among the world's most recognized brands and has been a target for health advocates, consumer groups, and public health agencies across multiple health domains: sugar content, caffeine, phosphoric acid, dental effects, and associations with obesity. This makes it a reflexive choice for health education demonstrations even when other beverages (energy drinks, citrus juices, sports drinks) may pose equal or greater risk for specific outcomes like dental erosion.

The phosphoric acid in Coca-Cola has also been a subject of research regarding bone health, on the hypothesis that cola consumption might reduce bone mineral density through acid buffering mechanisms or through calcium displacement. The evidence on this question is mixed; some epidemiological studies find associations between cola consumption and reduced bone density in specific populations (particularly women), while mechanistic evidence for the proposed pathway is incomplete. This is a distinct concern from dental erosion that adds another layer to the legitimate health questions around cola consumption.

For dental education specifically, the challenge is communicating genuine cumulative risk in a way that is accurate about timeframe and magnitude while remaining motivationally effective. Oral health researchers have developed more precise messaging about acidic beverage consumption—focusing on frequency of exposure rather than single-serving volume, on the importance of not brushing immediately after acid exposure (which can abrade softened enamel), and on the protective role of rinsing with water or milk after acidic drinks—that is both accurate and actionable.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addressed the Coca-Cola tooth dissolution claim, rating it mostly false and providing an account of both the real acid erosion mechanism and the significant exaggeration involved in the overnight dissolution claim. Their coverage correctly distinguished between the legitimate underlying concern (acid erosion from frequent soda consumption) and the false specific claim (overnight dissolution in a glass of Coke).

The Snopes verdict of "mostly false" rather than simply "false" reflects the epistemic complexity: the claim is not entirely without factual foundation, since Coke is acidic and acids do affect enamel, but the dramatic version is false. This graduated verdict is more accurate than a binary true/false rating but may leave some readers uncertain about what specifically to believe.

Dental professional organizations including the American Dental Association have addressed the claim in patient-facing educational materials, generally choosing to acknowledge the dramatic version's inaccuracy while redirecting to accurate information about cumulative acid erosion risk. This approach models a best practice: accurate debunking combined with redirection to the legitimate health message.

Science education contexts have explored the tooth-in-soda demonstration as a teaching opportunity—examining what the demonstration does and does not show, exploring the difference between laboratory submersion and in-mouth exposure, and discussing why health education sometimes uses exaggerated claims. This meta-level engagement with the claim, beyond simple debunking, is appropriate for educational settings where developing critical thinking about health claims is itself a learning objective.

The case is also regularly cited in discussions of the tension between accuracy and effectiveness in public health messaging, making it a standard example in health communication training and science journalism education. The dilemma it poses—accurate debunking may undermine a health message that is directionally correct—has no clean resolution, but the analysis it provokes is valuable for developing more nuanced approaches to health communication that prioritize both accuracy and effectiveness simultaneously.
