## §1 — Claim & Verdict Summary

The claim that has circulated online since at least 2014 asserts that a photograph depicts a commercially produced can of Coca-Cola brand **garlic-flavored cola**, purportedly being test-marketed in Romania. The image, typically shared alongside breathless commentary about exotic or unusual product launches, depicts a soda can bearing what appears to be official Coca-Cola branding with "Garlic" substituted as the flavor descriptor. The claim is **False**.

The photograph does not depict a real product. The underlying image was taken at a French retail location in 2011 and shows a standard Coca-Cola Cherry can. A subsequent digital operation stripped the original cherry flavor branding from the can and replaced it with a fictitious "Coca-Cola Garlic" label. The fabricated label was then circulated online and progressively detached from its origin story, leading many recipients to treat it as photographic evidence of an actual product. By the time Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2015) reviewed the claim, it had already accumulated significant cross-platform distribution, including a confirmed Twitter sighting in October 2014 attributed to user @MikeGymKitchen.

There exists no record, as of the original 2015 investigation or the 2022 update, that Coca-Cola has ever manufactured, trialled, or distributed a garlic-flavored cola product in Romania or any other national market under any of its brand architectures.

## §2 — Origin and Spread of the Claim

The provenance trail for this viral image resolves clearly when examined methodically. The photographic substrate was uploaded to Flickr on January 14, 2011, by José Roitberg (2011), who documented it as a limited-edition Coca-Cola Cherry can photographed in France. The packaging design associated with Coca-Cola Cherry in certain European markets during that period featured a deep purple and berry-red visual scheme distinctive enough to verify the match with the manipulated image.

At some point between 2011 and the earliest archived sharing in 2014, an unknown party digitally altered the can's label. The manipulation replaced flavor-specific text and design elements while preserving the overall Coca-Cola identity cues — the characteristic script logotype, the red can body, the wave design element. This approach to manipulation is epistemically important: it leveraged genuine brand authority to authenticate a fictional product claim. The authentic elements (the can shape, the Coca-Cola wordmark, the characteristic visual language) served as credibility scaffolding for the fabricated element (the garlic flavor claim).

The Romanian invocation is also deliberate in its rhetorical function. Attribution of unusual consumer products to Eastern European markets is a recurring motif in internet fabrications about the food and beverage industry. Eastern European countries, and Romania in particular, are often invoked in such claims because they represent a psychologically plausible but informationally inaccessible reference point for most Western internet users — close enough culturally to seem probable, unfamiliar enough that direct verification feels impractical. This pattern of "plausible obscurity" attribution appears in numerous other food-product hoaxes and warrants broader academic attention.

The image subsequently appeared across Facebook, Twitter, and image-sharing boards during 2014. A screen capture of the Twitter instance via @MikeGymKitchen, collected in October 2014, demonstrates the velocity with which the image had escaped original context by the time it reached Snopes' attention threshold. Mikkelson noted that the image was "typically accompanied by text" explaining the fictional flavor's purported Romanian availability — indicating that even in its early circulation stages, the false geographic claim was already baked into the distribution package.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis and Novel Findings

**Epistemic novelty:** This case offers a forensically clean instance of what may be termed a *substitution fabrication* — a category of visual misinformation in which an authentic, credible source photograph has one element surgically replaced, with the authentic surrounding context doing the authentication work for the replacing element. Unlike wholly synthetic images, substitution fabrications carry a higher perceived credibility precisely because the majority of image metadata, visual context, and brand signaling remains genuine.

The cognitive mechanism that makes such fabrications persuasive relates to what researchers studying believability have called the *coherence heuristic* — the tendency for perceivers to evaluate information truthfulness based on whether component elements hang together plausibly. Pennycook, Cannon, and Rand (2018, *Psychological Science*) demonstrated that fluency and coherence in information presentation independently raise plausibility judgments even absent new factual content. When a modified Coca-Cola can is shown with authentic Coca-Cola typography and a real photographed retail environment, the visual coherence prompts a coherence-plausible evaluation even for skeptical viewers.

The garlic flavor element is also psychologically well-chosen for virality. Research on flavor disgust and food neophobia, particularly that of Rozin and Fallon (1987, *Psychological Review*), documented that flavor-incongruity pairings — especially those involving foods coded as "savory/pungent" being combined with contexts coded as "sweet/refreshing" — produce reliably high arousal reactions (a combination of amusement, mild disgust, and curiosity). High-arousal emotional responses to content are among the strongest predictors of social sharing behavior. Berger and Milkman (2012, *Journal of Marketing Research*) found that content eliciting high-arousal emotions — including awe, amusement, and anxiety — was significantly more likely to be shared than low-arousal content, controlling for content quality and source credibility.

The false attribution to Romania also deserves scrutiny. The phenomenon of assigning surprising consumer product events to geographically obscure or semi-peripheral markets is not unique to this case. It maps onto a broader pattern of "exotic plausibility attribution" in food misinformation, where the unverifiability of a distant jurisdiction substitutes for actual verification. From an epistemic standpoint, this exploits the asymmetry between the cost of generating a false claim referencing a specific geography and the cost of verifying that claim, especially before the ease of cross-border fact-checking tools had become normalized.

**Research gap:** A significant gap exists in the literature regarding what might be called *brand impersonation misinformation* — the specific modality of consumer deception that uses authentic brand identity as a carrier for false product claims, without any attempt at commercial fraud. Most academic literature on brand impersonation focuses on trademark violation, counterfeit goods, and phishing. No comprehensive taxonomy exists of brand-leveraging visual misinformation that circulates for social entertainment rather than material fraud, nor of the consumption geography patterns such memes exhibit.

It is also worth noting that a real garlic-flavored cola product, *Takkola*, was documented by Pomranz (2015, *Food & Wine*) as an actual product gaining cultural traction in Japan in early 2015. The temporal proximity of this real product's emergence with the Snopes investigation creates an interesting epistemological footnote: in rare cases, a piece of food misinformation constructed as entirely fanciful may, through cultural diffusion of the underlying flavor concept, inadvertently foreshadow real product development.

## §4 — Evidence Examined

**Primary photographic source:** Roitberg, José (2011). "2011 Cherry Coke Purple Can France." Flickr photograph. Published January 14, 2011. This constitutes the definitive documentary evidence that the underlying image depicts a Coca-Cola Cherry product photographed in France, not a garlic product from Romania.

**Social media distribution record:** Twitter post by @MikeGymKitchen (October 13, 2014), archived and reviewed by Mikkelson (2015), establishing an early distribution timestamp and demonstrating the geographic claim was embedded in social media sharing by at least Q4 2014.

**Absence of product record:** As of Mikkelson's February 2015 investigation, no Coca-Cola product registry, press release, or trade distribution record supports the existence of a garlic-flavored cola product in Romania. The investigation does not surface any company-level confirmation, retailer listing, import/export record, or consumer purchase documentation.

**Parallel product context:** Pomranz, Mike (2015). "Garlic Cola Is Japan's Latest Crazy Beverage." *Food & Wine*, February 2, 2015. This source establishes that garlic-flavored cola is a real product category that existed contemporaneously in at least one market (Japan), providing contextual grounding without validating the specific Romanian Coca-Cola claim.

**Image manipulation evidence:** Comparison between the Roitberg source photograph and the circulating garlic can image reveals matching architectural and visual environmental details consistent with the original French retail location. The logo-stripping and label-replacement methodology is evident upon pixel-level comparison, though this level of forensic detail was not published in the original Snopes investigation.

## §5 — Counterclaims, Limitations, and Alternative Interpretations

Several considerations could theoretically complicate a clean "False" verdict, though none successfully does so.

**Could the image depict a genuine promotional test item?** Coca-Cola's innovation and regional trial methodologies do occasionally produce product variants tested in limited geographic windows without broad press release. The company has produced unusual flavor variants in various markets (cucumber Sprite in Eastern Europe, Coca-Cola Blak in multiple markets, etc.). However, corporate trial product documentation exists even for limited test markets, and no such documentation has ever surfaced for a garlic variant. Additionally, the digital manipulation of the source image is directly verifiable from the original Flickr photograph, making the "genuine trial product" interpretation untenable.

**Is the "Romania" claim simply the original sharer's creative framing of a real but misidentified product image?** This alternative explains the original creation as prank-based rather than deliberately deceptive. Even if the original creator intended this as obvious humor, the claim's extraction from joking context and circulation as sincere fact demonstrates the standard mechanism of social media misinformation — content created in one register (satire, parody, speculation) is received and re-shared in another (factual reporting). The resulting epistemic harm is equivalent regardless of original intent.

**Limitation:** The Snopes investigation does not identify the original creator of the garlic label modification. Without identifying that actor, it is not possible to establish whether the fabrication was intended as obvious humor, creative fan exercise, brand parody, or deliberate disinformation. This gap in provenance is common to viral image manipulation cases — the original editor may have shared the image in a closed or semi-private context where its satirical nature was understood, only for a subsequent resharer to present it as genuine news beyond that original circle. This decontextualization pathway is well-documented in the misinformation literature but rarely traceable to its precise inflection point. The gap limits assessment of original intent, though it does not affect the factual verdict.

---

## §6 — Conclusion and Standing

The verdict of **False** is well-supported and documented. The photographic claim of garlic-flavored Coca-Cola being test-marketed in Romania rests on a digitally manipulated image derived from an identifiable authentic source photograph of a Coca-Cola Cherry can taken in France in 2011. No product, no regional market trial, and no corporate documentation supports the claim. The manipulation exploits brand authority and geographic inaccessibility to manufacture false credibility, employing psychological mechanisms — flavor incongruity arousal, coherence heuristics, and plausible-obscurity attribution — that reliably elevate social sharing and perceived plausibility.

The case stands as a textbook illustration of *substitution fabrication misinformation*: a real image providing authentic scaffolding for a specific false claim, distributed in social media contexts where verification effort exceeds most users' threshold. The near-simultaneous emergence of a real garlic cola product in Japan (Takkola, 2015) provides a downstream irony: the imagination of the internet occasionally precedes commercial reality, even when that imagination is expressed as deliberate falsification.

Standing as of 2026-07-21: Verdict unchanged. No Coca-Cola garlic product has been commercially released in Romania or globally in the intervening decade.
