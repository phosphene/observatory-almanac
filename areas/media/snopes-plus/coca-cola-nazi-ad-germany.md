# Did Coca-Cola Run 'Coke Ist Es' Ads in Nazi Germany?


## 1. The Claim

In 2021, a widely shared meme appeared across social media platforms featuring what appeared to be a vintage Nazi-era advertisement for Coca-Cola. The central image displayed a design with the apparent slogan "Ein Volk, Ein Reich, Ein Getränk — Coke Ist Es" — a pastiche of the actual Nazi slogan "Ein Volk, Ein Reich, Ein Führer" ("One People, One Realm, One Leader"), with "Coke Ist Es" serving as a literal German transliteration of the Coca-Cola marketing slogan "Coke Is It." The design, vintage in aesthetic, carried the implication that Coca-Cola had produced advertising in Nazi Germany that deliberately echoed or endorsed Nazi propaganda slogans.

The meme circulated in multiple configurations. One prominent version juxtaposed the fake Nazi Coke ad with a photograph of a Coca-Cola bottle bearing an LGBTQ rainbow motif and the slogan "Love is Love"—a real marketing campaign Coca-Cola ran in Hungary in 2019. In some versions, an image of former U.S. President Donald Trump was also included. The intended rhetorical message appeared to be that large corporations, including Coca-Cola, adapt their branding to whatever cultural or political movement promises the largest customer base—that corporate commitments to diversity and inclusion are as cynical and commercially motivated as a hypothetical alignment with National Socialism would have been. The claim also implicitly made a specific empirical assertion: that Coca-Cola had in fact created advertising in Nazi Germany that co-opted Nazi slogans.

This represents a dual-layered claim worth separating: the specific assertion that the shared image is an authentic historical artifact, and the broader assertion about Coca-Cola's commercial operations in Nazi Germany. The Snopes fact-check correctly focuses on the specific evidentiary artifact and returns a verdict of "False" for the claim that the design was an authentic Nazi-era advertisement.


## 2. What's Actually True

The "Coke Ist Es" image is a fabricated piece of political art, not an authentic advertising artifact from Nazi Germany. Its origin is traceable with precision, which is unusual for viral internet imagery.

The image was created as part of an art installation organized in 2004 by English comedian and political activist Mark Thomas and Welsh artist Tracey Moberly (formerly known as Tracey Sanders-Wood). Early in 2004, Thomas and Moberly solicited submissions for an exhibition called "Coca-Cola's Nazi Adverts," which was explicitly described as a project to *create* fake recreations of what Coca-Cola's advertising in Nazi Germany might have looked like, along with photomontages and original artistic responses to Coca-Cola's corporate history. The exhibition was not presenting itself as an archive of authentic historical artifacts; the early promotional materials explicitly stated that the works were "not the original adverts used by Coke in Nazi Germany" and described the project as producing parodies, imitations, and original works.

More than 400 fake advertisements were submitted to and displayed in the exhibition, which was shown in London (at The Foundry, a bar and venue once operated by Moberly), Moscow, Ecuador, Venezuela, and Colombia between 2004 and 2006. Documentation of the exhibition's real identity was provided to Snopes by photographer Scott van Looy, who confirmed that he photographed the specific "Coke Ist Es" design as displayed on the walls of The Foundry in London on June 10, 2004.

Two anachronisms embedded in the image provide independent confirmation that the design could not be an authentic 1930s-era artifact:

First, "Coke Ist Es" is a German transliteration of the Coca-Cola marketing slogan "Coke Is It." That slogan was not created or used until 1982, when Coca-Cola launched it as part of an advertising campaign. A Nazi-era Coca-Cola advertisement could not have used a slogan that was not coined until four and a half decades later; its presence proves the image's post-1930s origin conclusively.

Second, the Coca-Cola brand name in the image is accompanied by the registered trademark symbol ®. Although Coca-Cola first registered the "Coca-Cola" trademark in 1893, the ® symbol did not appear with the brand's distinctive script design until the 1950s, according to Allen Petretti, an expert in Coca-Cola collectibles and antiques cited in the Snopes investigation. An authentic 1930s advertisement would not have included the ® symbol.

Together, these two anachronisms are sufficient to establish beyond reasonable doubt that the design was created well after the 1930s. The identification of the Mark Thomas/Tracey Moberly exhibition as its specific origin provides an affirmative account of where the image came from.

The factual kernel underlying the broader claim is real: Coca-Cola did operate in Nazi Germany. The German operations of Coca-Cola, led by Max Keith, continued to function during the Third Reich, and Coca-Cola was served at the 1936 Berlin Olympics—a major marketing success for the brand. When World War II cut off supplies of the concentrate needed to produce Coca-Cola, Max Keith developed an alternative beverage using available German ingredients; this beverage was called Fanta, which subsequently became one of Coca-Cola's enduring global product lines after the war. Keith maintained the German operation as a going concern through the war and, notably, kept the business from being absorbed into Nazi state control—subsequently returning it to Coca-Cola's American corporate parent after Germany's defeat.

The history of Coca-Cola in Nazi Germany is therefore real, commercially significant, and not without ethical complexity—it reflects the general pattern of multinational corporations continuing to operate in authoritarian states when commercial calculation favors it. But it is categorically different from the fabricated image's implication of active propaganda collaboration with the Nazi regime.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The rapid virality of the "Coke Ist Es" meme illustrates several dynamics of misinformation spread that extend beyond individual gullibility.

Most immediately, the meme's fabricated image is visually convincing. Whoever created the original design for the 2004 exhibition did skilled work—the aesthetic is plausibly vintage, the typography is appropriate to a stylized period advertisement, and the Coca-Cola script logo gives it the authenticity cue of a real brand mark. In the context of social media scrolling, few users pause to interrogate the authenticity of a visually consistent image that fits a plausible narrative frame.

The narrative frame is the second key driver. The broader story the meme tells—that large corporations have always aligned with power rather than principle, and that contemporary corporate diversity commitments are no more sincere than a hypothetical Nazi collaboration would have been—is not an incoherent argument. It taps into documented patterns of corporate behavior: I.G. Farben and Zyklon B, IBM and the Holocaust, General Motors and Nazi military production, Hugo Boss and SS uniforms. The historical record of corporate complicity with Nazism is real and well-documented; a specific Coca-Cola case would fit an established pattern.

The juxtaposition with the real LGBTQ "Love is Love" Coca-Cola bottle from Hungary adds a contemporary anchor that makes the historical fake seem more plausible by association. If this is a real 2019 Coca-Cola campaign (it is), the argument goes, why wouldn't there be a 1936 German equivalent? The rhetorical logic is internally coherent even though it proves nothing about the fake image's authenticity.

Third, the political context of the 2021 circulation—particularly within discourses skeptical of corporate "wokeism" or progressive marketing—meant the meme was appealing to an audience predisposed to accept evidence supporting a cynicism about corporate virtue signaling. When an image confirms a belief people already hold and want to express, the threshold for accepting it as authentic drops significantly. The meme was shareable not only because it was visually convincing but because it expressed a sentiment that resonated with a significant subset of users.

Finally, the 2004 exhibition context was almost entirely absent from the meme's circulation. Had the image circulated with a caption identifying it as political art from a 2004 exhibition—an explicitly acknowledged fake—it would have had a very different reception. The decontextualization of the image from its origin allowed it to be received as historical documentation rather than contemporary political satire.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE**: The "Coke Ist Es / Ein Volk, Ein Reich, Ein Getränk" image is not an authentic Nazi-era Coca-Cola advertisement. It is a fabricated political artwork created for the "Coca-Cola's Nazi Adverts" exhibition organized by Mark Thomas and Tracey Moberly in 2004 and first displayed publicly at The Foundry in London on or around June 10, 2004. The image's inauthenticity is confirmed by two anachronisms: the "Coke is it" slogan was not coined until 1982, and the ® trademark symbol was not used with Coca-Cola's brand design until the 1950s.

The factual kernel—that Coca-Cola operated commercially in Nazi Germany—is true and historically documented, but does not validate the specific fabricated image, which implies an active Nazi propaganda collaboration that the historical record does not substantiate.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Coca-Cola / Nazi Germany episode, stripped of the fabricated advertisement, raises genuine and important questions about corporate behavior under authoritarian regimes—questions that deserve examination on their own terms, not filtered through forged evidence.

Coca-Cola's German subsidiary, headed by Max Keith, navigated the Third Reich period with a pragmatism typical of multinational commercial operations in fascist states. Coca-Cola was an official sponsor of the 1936 Berlin Olympics and sold extensively at the Games—a fact that has been documented and criticized as providing commercial legitimacy to a regime already engaged in genocidal policies against Jewish Germans and other persecuted groups. The sponsorship arrangement illustrates how international commercial engagement with authoritarian regimes functions as tacit normalization, regardless of the explicit political intentions of corporate actors.

The development of Fanta is the most historically remarkable consequence of Coca-Cola's Nazi Germany presence. Max Keith, when World War II severed the supply chain for Coca-Cola concentrate from U.S. facilities, improvised a new beverage using available German ingredients—primarily whey from cheese production and apple pomace from apple cider processing. The name "Fanta" derived from the German word "Fantasie" (fantasy or imagination), reportedly from a brainstorming instruction Keith gave his team to use their imagination. Fanta was thus literally invented by the German operations of a U.S. corporation during World War II as a product of wartime resource constraints. After the war, when Keith returned the German operations to Coca-Cola's corporate parent, Fanta was absorbed into the Coca-Cola product portfolio and subsequently extended into a globally marketed beverage line.

The Fanta history generates its own periodic misinformation, particularly the claim that "Fanta was literally created by Nazis"—which oversimplifies both the commercial and political context. Keith was not a Nazi official; he was a business executive managing a commercial operation under conditions of wartime scarcity. The beverage was created to sustain a commercial operation, not as a political project. The association with Nazi Germany is real but should be characterized as circumstantial and contextual rather than ideological.

The broader question raised by the meme—whether corporate progressive branding represents genuine values or cynical market positioning—is an empirical question in corporate communications and organizational behavior scholarship that cannot be resolved by a fabricated historical image. Research on corporate social responsibility (CSR) communication consistently finds a significant gap between stated values and operational practices in many large corporations. Studies of corporate advocacy communication (Dodd, M.D. and Supa, D.W., "Understanding the Effect of Corporate Advocacy Communication on Consumer Behavior," *Corporate Communications*, 2014) find that consumer skepticism about corporate sincerity is common and not entirely unjustified—corporations frequently align public communications with prevailing social attitudes for commercial rather than ideological reasons.

This means the meme's underlying argument has empirical traction even though its specific evidentiary artifact is fake. The broader critique of corporate rainbow capitalism—the phenomenon of corporations temporarily deploying LGBTQ symbols and progressive imagery during Pride Month while engaging in practices inconsistent with those values—is the subject of substantive academic and journalistic inquiry. Scholars including Sarah Schulman (*Let the Record Show*, 2021) and corporate responsibility researchers at institutions including the Harvard Kennedy School have engaged seriously with questions about the gap between corporate progressive marketing and corporate political and supply chain behavior.

The fabricated Nazi Coke ad cannot establish this argument empirically, but the argument does not depend on the fabricated ad. Historical examples of corporate accommodation of authoritarian regimes are abundant and documented—they include not just Coca-Cola's presence in Nazi Germany but Standard Oil's deals with I.G. Farben, Ford Motor Company's German subsidiary during the Third Reich (which received Nazi forced labor), and IBM's provision of punch card systems used in organizing the Holocaust (Black, E., *IBM and the Holocaust*, Crown Publishers, 2001). These documented cases provide more than enough material to substantiate critiques of corporate moral opportunism without requiring forged evidence.

The viral success of the fabricated Nazi Coke ad meme, however, highlights a challenge for this type of political argument: the fabricated image was far more shareable and emotionally compelling than the documented historical evidence would have been. The genuine history of corporate complicity in Nazi Germany is complex, contextual, and resistant to reduction to a meme. The fake image offered a viscerally satisfying, simple-to-share image that appeared to confirm the argument in a single glance. The appetite for simple, visual confirmation of complex political arguments creates conditions in which fabricated evidence can spread more virally than accurate historical record.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's treatment of this claim, authored by Dan MacGuill and published in June 2021 (with an update on June 23, 2021 adding photographer Scott van Looy's confirmation), represents a competent example of artifact-level fact-checking that correctly identifies and documents the fabricated nature of the specific image.

MacGuill's methodology demonstrates two best practices for evaluating apparently historical images shared on social media. First, examination for internal anachronisms—features of the image that are inconsistent with its claimed provenance date. The identification of "Coke is it" (launched 1982) and the ® symbol (used with Coca-Cola's brand design from the 1950s) as anachronisms is technically sound and sufficient to establish the image's inauthenticity without relying solely on absence of evidence from archives.

Second, positive identification of the image's actual origin—not merely concluding that it is fake, but tracing it to the 2004 Mark Thomas/Tracey Moberly exhibition. The documentation provided by photographer Scott van Looy (who photographed the image at The Foundry on June 10, 2004) provides direct evidentiary chain from the claimed display location and date to the publication.

The Snopes treatment appropriately separates the specific artifact claim (the image is an authentic Nazi-era ad) from the broader contextual truth (Coca-Cola did operate in Nazi Germany). The verdict of "False" applies specifically to the artifact claim; Snopes does not use the artifact's falseness to deny the broader historical reality of Coca-Cola's Nazi Germany presence.

One notable gap in the Snopes treatment is the absence of detailed engagement with the political context that made the fabricated image meaningful to those who shared it. MacGuill notes that the meme appeared to be arguing that corporate progressive branding is insincere, but does not substantively engage with the documented history of corporate complicity in authoritarian regimes that might have made the fake image feel plausible. This is appropriate for the fact-check's scope but leaves an analytical opportunity on the table. A fuller treatment would distinguish between "this specific image is fake" and "the corporate criticism the image attempted to illustrate has no merit"—because the former is true and the latter is not established by debunking the image.

For media literacy education, the "Coke Ist Es" case is a productive teaching example precisely because the fabricated image was created with explicit political intent (by self-described activist artists) and was displayed honestly in its original exhibition context. The image became misinformation not through its creators' deception but through decontextualization and redistribution stripped of its art-installation framing. This usefully illustrates the mechanism by which deliberately labeled parody or satire can become misinformation when divorced from its original context and redistributed without that context.
