# Risqué Coca-Cola Poster


## 1. The Claim

The claim, which has circulated in various forms since at least the mid-1990s and gained renewed internet circulation in the form of forwarded emails and social media posts, asserts that Coca-Cola once issued a major advertising recall after discovering that a graphic artist had hidden an overtly sexual image within an ice cube in one of its promotional posters. The details in commonly circulated versions of the claim specify:

- The poster was released in South Australia (though contemporaneous news accounts place it in Sydney and the broader Australian market)
- The poster was created in the mid-1980s (the actual date was 1995)
- The recall was complete and total
- The artist responsible lost their job and was sued
- The incident was very rare and hard to document

The circulating text that Snopes collected characterized the event as follows: "This poster was released in the mid 80s and prompted a total recall of all posters because of the picture painted in ice-cubes at bottom right corner — a woman performing an act. The graphic artist who designed the picture put this in as a joke, and it went through unnoticed until someone spotted it on the back of a Coke truck. The artist lost his job and was sued, and all promotional material had to be recalled and destroyed."

The claim is categorized by Snopes as *True*, with corrections to the minor factual details: the correct date is 1995, not the mid-1980s, and the geographic framing is the Sydney metropolitan area and broader South Pacific marketing region rather than exclusively South Australia.


## 2. What's Actually True

The contemporaneous news coverage confirms that the incident occurred substantially as described. Coca-Cola's South Pacific marketing division had commissioned a $200,000 poster campaign to promote the reintroduction of the company's iconic contoured bottle—the hourglass-shaped Coke bottle that had been a brand identity element since its introduction in 1915, whose curves the campaign slogan "Feel the Curves!" was designed to celebrate. The poster depicted a stylized cartoon rendering of a Coke bottle surrounded by ice cubes.

A graphic artist employed by the small contracted design agency concealed an explicit sexual image within one of the ice cubes in the corner of the illustration. The image, which Australian press accounts described as a depiction of a woman performing oral sex, was rendered in sufficiently realistic detail to be recognizable once noticed, but sufficiently integrated into the ice cube's shading and highlight patterns to escape casual detection. The hidden image was not discovered by Coca-Cola management during the design approval process and was not caught at any stage of production or initial distribution. The posters had already been distributed to thousands of venues across Sydney—hotels, bottle shops, and retail locations—before the image was identified.

The discovery is variously attributed in accounts to a retailer who noticed the image and complained, and to a broader pattern of customers calling attention to it. Coca-Cola South Pacific's president, Mike Bascle, responded publicly with condemnation: he characterized the artist's action as "quite irresponsible and not amusing." The company's Sydney-based corporate affairs manager, Ian Brown, described Coca-Cola as "a victim" of the designer's deliberate sabotage.

The recall totaled thousands of glossy posters. The campaign had been intended for 120,000 outlets across Sydney before being aborted by the discovery. The artist lost their employment, and the company pursued legal action. Two contemporaneous news sources independently confirm the event: *The Adelaide Advertiser* ("'Porn' Poster Upsets Coke," 30 August 1995) and the *Auckland Truth* ("Rude Artwork Shames Bosses Into Withdrawal," 19 January 1996).

The claim is therefore essentially true, with two correctable factual errors in the circulating version: the date was 1995, not the mid-1980s, and the distribution was Sydney-focused rather than exclusively South Australian, though news coverage referenced the South Pacific region more broadly.


## 3. Why People Believe This (and Why It Matters That It's True)

Most entries in the Truth Vault address false claims. The Coca-Cola poster recall claim is unusual because it is evaluated as true—and the epistemological challenge it presents is correspondingly different: why do formally true claims circulate with the same uncertain, half-disbelieved quality as invented legends?

**The hidden-image genre framework**: The Snopes fact-check places the Coca-Cola poster in a catalogue of similar claims—phallic imagery allegedly hidden in a Renuzit air freshener, a Star Wars trading card, a Sears catalog underwear advertisement, and the Disney *Little Mermaid* VHS clamshell—most of which are themselves debunked or exaggerated. This genre context creates a credibility problem for the true claims within the genre: because the majority of "hidden sexual imagery in advertising" claims are urban legends, false, or based on pareidolia, a true instance of the phenomenon carries the credibility penalty of its genre neighbors. The poster recall is processed through the same skeptical frame that most hidden-image claims deserve, even though it has contemporaneous news documentation.

**The pareidolia baseline problem**: Human visual cognition is powerfully predisposed to detect faces, figures, and biologically significant forms in ambiguous visual fields. This tendency—which has deep evolutionary roots in predator-detection and social recognition (McKone et al., 2008, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*)—means that people regularly perceive sexual or threatening imagery in neutral visual noise. The majority of "hidden image in advertising" viral claims arise from this natural misperception: someone sees a face or phallic shape in ice cubes, shadows, or product surfaces that was not placed there deliberately. The existence of this baseline pareidolia error makes it genuinely difficult for a viewer to distinguish between cases of true deliberate embedding (the Coca-Cola poster) and false pareidolia claims (many others). Both categories produce the same kind of social sharing behavior and the same quality of witness testimony: "I saw it clearly."

**The epistemic novelty of authenticated advertising espionage**: The interesting philosophical question the Coca-Cola poster incident raises is what it tells us about advertising supply chains and the opportunities for malicious code injection—using that term analogically for deliberate hidden content—at the contractor boundary. The Coca-Cola Company commissioned a design to express a specific brand message. That message was intercepted at the contractor level and altered to include unauthorized content that was diametrically contrary to the company's interests. The Coca-Cola incident is a documented case study in what might be called "creative supply chain sabotage"—a category with few well-documented cases and essentially no theoretical framework in either marketing literature or organizational security research.

This category of risk is distinct from other kinds of advertising controversy (offensive campaigns approved by management, misread cultural signals, inadvertent double entendres) because it involves a deliberate adversarial act by an internal contractor. It is also distinct from cybersecurity supply-chain attacks (such as the insertion of malicious code in software packages by contractors), though structurally analogous. The absence of a formal risk management framework for creative supply chain sabotage means that the advertising industry has no systematic defense against a motivated contractor making a similar insertion into contemporary digital advertising assets, where the "hiding surface" (a video frame, a generated image, a programmatic ad creative) may be large and detection harder than in a static printed poster.

**Date drift as an authenticity signal**: The circulating version of the claim misdates the event to the "mid-1980s" rather than 1995. This kind of temporal degradation is characteristic of authentic events transmitted through informal channels over time: events become associated with earlier periods as they recede in memory and circulate through non-documenting social networks. Paradoxically, the presence of minor factual errors in an oral tradition can—in some contexts—be evidence of authentic transmission rather than fabrication, because fully fabricated claims are typically internally consistent in their stated details. The mid-1980s error reflects the way real events become legendary over repetition.


## 4. Empirical Record

The primary evidentiary basis for the claim's truth is contemporaneous newspaper documentation from two independent sources:

*The Adelaide Advertiser* published an item headlined "'Porn' Poster Upsets Coke" on August 30, 1995. This contemporaneous report establishes the date and the basic facts: the discovery of sexual imagery in an ice cube of a Coca-Cola advertising poster, the company's response, and the recall.

The *Auckland Truth* published a follow-up report titled "Rude Artwork Shames Bosses Into Withdrawal" on January 19, 1996 (page 5). This New Zealand publication confirms that the incident attracted regional press attention across the South Pacific and provides additional details about the executive responses.

The slogan "Feel the Curves!" is independently consistent with documented Coca-Cola marketing campaigns of the mid-1990s, which were focused on the reintroduction and celebration of the contoured bottle shape after a period in which some markets had moved toward straight-sided containers. The bottle reintroduction initiative is independently documented in Coca-Cola corporate history.

The broader phenomenon of hidden sexual imagery in advertising has been studied in the context of the "Ice" controversy in advertising research, most associated with the work of Wilson Bryan Key (1972, *Subliminal Seduction*, Prentice Hall), who claimed extensive evidence of deliberately embedded sexual imagery in mainstream advertising as a psychological manipulation technique. Key's methodology has been substantially criticized in subsequent research—his claims about *subliminal* effects of imperceptible imagery have been largely dismissed as unsupported, and many of his specific "examples" were pareidolia rather than deliberate embedding—but his work established cultural awareness of the hidden-image-in-advertising concept that probably made actual hidden-image incidents more likely to be sought out and reported when they occurred.

The distinction between Key's contested theoretical claims and the documented Coca-Cola incident is important: Key claimed hidden images were intentional corporate policy; the Coca-Cola incident was an individual contractor's deliberate sabotage condemned by the corporation. These are entirely different phenomena that share only the surface feature of sexually explicit content embedded in advertising material.


## 5. Gaps and Open Questions

**The artist's legal fate**: Available sources confirm that the artist "lost his job and was sued," but the legal outcome of the litigation is not documented in publicly available sources. Whether the case proceeded to a judgment, settled, or was otherwise resolved has not been reported in documents accessible to researchers. The legal record, if publicly accessible in Australian court archives from the 1995–1996 period, would provide the most complete account of the incident's consequences.

**The full scope of distribution**: Accounts indicate the posters had been distributed to "thousands" of venues across Sydney before discovery, with a planned reach of 120,000 outlets. The proportion of the total distribution that was successfully recalled, and the proportion that may have remained in circulation at hotels, bottle shops, or private collections after the recall, has not been documented. The circulating claim that the poster is "very rare and hard to get hold of" implies that some copies survived recall, but the actual survival count is unknown.

**Detection methodology in analog advertising**: In 1995, advertising production was partly analog (film-based photography and illustration) and partly transitioning to digital. How the Coca-Cola poster image was originally produced—whether the explicit image was embedded at the drawing or digital composition stage—affects assessments of how easily it could have been detected in quality control review. The question of what detection methodology would have caught the embedded image before distribution has not been publicly analyzed, and the advertising industry has no published standards for "hidden content" review in creative materials.

**Analogous contemporary risk**: The transition to fully digital advertising production and programmatic ad delivery raises a structurally analogous contemporary question: could a malicious contractor embed inappropriate content in digital advertising materials at a resolution or in a context where automated approval systems would not detect it? This question has been discussed in the cybersecurity literature with respect to adversarial examples in machine learning systems (Goodfellow et al., 2015, *Communications of the ACM*), but has not been formally analyzed in the advertising technology context.


## 6. The Takeaway

The Coca-Cola "Feel the Curves!" poster recall is confirmed true by contemporaneous Australian press documentation. A contracted graphic artist deliberately embedded an explicit sexual image in an ice cube in a poster promoting Coca-Cola's contoured bottle reintroduction in 1995. The company discovered the image only after thousands of posters had been distributed across Sydney venues, conducted a full recall, and condemned the artist's conduct. The artist lost their employment and faced legal proceedings.

The minor errors in the circulating version—date placed in the "mid-1980s" instead of 1995, geography attributed exclusively to South Australia rather than Sydney and broader South Pacific—are characteristic of temporal drift and geographic compression in oral legend transmission and do not undermine the essential factual accuracy of the main claim.

The case is epistemically interesting for two reasons beyond its factual confirmation: it demonstrates how a true event's credibility can be discounted by its genre membership (most "hidden image in advertising" claims are false or pareidolia), and it provides a rare documented case study in what might be called creative supply chain sabotage—a category of organizational risk that has no established mitigation framework in contemporary marketing security literature. As advertising production increasingly relies on remote contractor networks and automated creative pipelines, the structural conditions that allowed this incident to occur have become, if anything, more common rather than less.
