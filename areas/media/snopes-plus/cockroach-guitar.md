# Is This a Video of a Cockroach Strumming a Guitar?


## 1. The Claim

In early March 2021, a video circulating across Reddit, TikTok, and other social media platforms showed what was described as a cockroach "strumming" or "playing" a guitar — producing audible musical tones as it climbed along the neck and body of the instrument. The video had originally gone viral in February 2020 after being posted to Barstool Sports' TikTok account, but the 2021 circulation brought it to a new audience accompanied by the caption "I woke up to the sound of my guitar being played and I saw this."

The claim implicit in the viral dissemination was that the cockroach was authentically producing musical sounds through physical contact with the guitar strings — not through video manipulation, looped audio, or any other form of digital fabrication. The framing varied across social media platforms; some shared it purely as an amusing novelty, while others expressed skepticism that a cockroach could genuinely produce string sounds simply by climbing the instrument.

Snopes investigated the video for Alex Kasprak, who noted that while the original source could not be located, the video had first gone viral in February 2020 through Barstool Sports' TikTok account — meaning it had existed online for over a year before the 2021 circulation surge. Kasprak also noted, more intriguingly, that this was not the only footage of cockroach-guitar interaction in existence: a 2017 YouTube video showed what appeared to be a different — "slightly more novice," in Kasprak's words — insect instrumentalist. The existence of multiple independent examples of cockroach-guitar encounters was treated as corroborating evidence that the phenomenon is plausible and that the videos are unlikely to be fabricated.

The final assessment was a "True" rating: the video shows a cockroach producing musical tones on a guitar, there is no evidence of manipulation, and the cockroach-as-inadvertent-musician is not just plausible but apparently reasonably common given the prevalence of related content online and the documented prevalence of cockroaches in musical instrument gear.


## 2. What's Actually True

The Snopes verdict is correct. The video shows genuine string-sound production from cockroach-guitar contact, and no credible alternative explanation for the audio has been proposed. The mechanism is straightforward: guitar strings are tensioned metal or nylon filaments that produce acoustic vibrations when disturbed by physical contact, and a cockroach moving across them would impart contact forces sufficient to induce string vibration. There is no biological, physical, or informational reason to doubt the authenticity of the footage.

Kasprak's additional observation — that guitar gear is apparently a documented habitat for cockroaches, with multiple message board discussions confirming the problem — is epistemically useful. It transforms the video from an improbable isolated event to a data point within a plausible behavioral pattern. Cockroaches in musical instrument cases and equipment are indeed a known phenomenon in pest management literature and musician communities alike, particularly in warmer climates or urban settings with high cockroach pressure. Guitar cases provide warmth, darkness, enclosed spaces, and often food traces (rosin, sweat, food debris), all of which are known cockroach habitat preferences.

However, the Snopes article stops at the verdict. It confirms the basic factual claim — the video is authentic — but does not explore what makes the scenario interesting from an entomological perspective, how the mechanics of the sound production actually work, or what the broader pattern of insect-instrument encounters reveals about pest biology. It also does not engage with the legitimate question of whether "strumming" is an appropriate verb for what the cockroach is doing — a semantic and conceptual question with genuine implications for how the viral claim should be understood.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty: Cockroach Locomotion, String Physics, and Pest Acoustics

**The biomechanics of cockroach movement and contact force.** The reason a cockroach can produce string sounds at all lies in the biomechanics of cockroach locomotion, which is among the most intensively studied in invertebrate biology. Cockroaches are capable of moving at speeds up to 50 body lengths per second — making them among the fastest land animals relative to body size — and achieve this speed through an alternating tripod gait in which three legs (front and back on one side, middle on the other) simultaneously support the body while the other three swing forward (Full, R.J., & Tu, M.S., 1991, "Mechanics of a rapid running insect: two-, four- and six-legged locomotion," *Journal of Experimental Biology*, 156, 215–231).

The key factor for guitar string production is that cockroach tarsi — the distal segments of their legs, functionally analogous to feet — bear specialized pads and claws that provide adhesion on smooth surfaces through van der Waals forces and capillary adhesion from secreted fluids. When a cockroach walks across a tensioned guitar string, each tarsal contact produces a small mechanical impulse. If the string is tensioned appropriately and the contact sufficient to displace the string from its rest position, acoustic vibration will result. The frequency produced corresponds to the string's fundamental mode of vibration (determined by string tension, length, and linear mass density — the standard Mersenne relations), and will decay exponentially after each contact event.

This is not "strumming" in the musical sense — which implies deliberate sweeping motions across multiple strings with controlled attack and release — but it is nonetheless genuine acoustic production from physical contact. The distinction matters: the cockroach is not performing, it is just walking, and its body mechanics happen to produce the contact forces necessary to excite string resonance. The viral framing's use of "strumming" is an anthropomorphism that should be noted without dismissing the underlying factual claim.

**Cockroach acoustic behavior and communication.** There is a separate literature on cockroach acoustics that the Snopes article does not engage which adds an interesting secondary dimension to the story. While cockroaches are not traditionally known as acoustic communicators in the way that crickets or cicadas are, some species — particularly Madagascar hissing cockroaches (*Gromphadorhina portentosa*) — do produce deliberate sound through respiratory spiracle control (Nelson, M.C., 1979, "Sound production in the cockroach, *Gromphadorhina portentosa*: the relationship of acoustic features to behavioral function," *Physiological Entomology*, 4(4), 341–358). More broadly, acoustic signals have been identified across Blattodea in contexts including agonistic interactions and mating.

This is not directly relevant to the Vinny story, which involves a non-hissing species producing sound through inadvertent physical contact rather than behavioral vocalization, but it is worth noting that the framing of cockroaches as silent insects is partially accurate only — and that the broader question of arthropod acoustics is an active and productive research area.

**The pest-instrument interface and guitar gear ecology.** Kasprak's observation that cockroaches in guitar gear is a "somewhat common problem" is confirmed by pest management literature on enclosed musical instrument storage, though this literature is sparse and primarily appears in technical trade publications rather than peer-reviewed entomology journals. What is better documented is the broader phenomenon of cockroach habitat selection in warm, enclosed, food-trace-rich environments generally — wooden resonance chambers, felt-lined cases, and the dense electronics of guitar amplifiers all provide microhabitats consistent with cockroach shelter-seeking behavior (Rust, M.K., Reierson, D.A., & Hansgen, K.H., 1991, "Cockroach infestations: ecological and management considerations," *Pest Control*, 59(1), 1–14).

The 2017 video cited by Kasprak adds independent provenance to the phenomenon, as it shows a different guitar (or at least a different filming context) and a different roach specimen, reducing the probability that the original 2020 video was a single fabricated event. Two independent appearances of the same behavioral pattern in different years is empirically meaningful, even if neither video constitutes a controlled scientific observation.

**Why guitar strings specifically?** The video shows the cockroach producing sounds specifically on guitar strings rather than on the guitar's wooden body or headstock. This is partly a matter of acoustic physics: strings are tensioned oscillators tuned to resonate in the audible range, while the adjacent wood would produce only dull contact sounds from small mass impacts insufficient to excite meaningful resonance. But it is also worth noting that guitar strings present a specific mechanical challenge to cockroach locomotion: parallel thin cylinders spaced at regular intervals. The cockroach's gait mechanics — particularly the spreading characteristics of its tarsal spread and alternating leg placement — may actually make string traversal relatively easy compared to, for example, crossing an equivalent smooth surface with the same spacing. Research on cockroach locomotion across uneven terrains shows that their proprioceptive systems allow for rapid adaptation to unexpected surface geometries (Sponberg, S., & Full, R.J., 2008, "Neuromechanical response of musculo-skeletal structures in cockroaches during rapid running on rough terrain," *Journal of Experimental Biology*, 211(3), 433–446).


## 4. The EARC Assessment

**Empirical status.** Confirmed. The video is authentic; cockroach-guitar acoustic production is physically plausible and mechanically well-understood; multiple independent examples exist; no credible evidence of fabrication has been advanced. The Snopes verdict is correct.

**Adequacy.** Adequate for the binary authenticity question but thin on the scientific dimensions that make the story interesting. The article notes that cockroaches on guitars are "at least somewhat common" without exploring *why*, references the 2017 precedent video without analyzing what it contributes epistemically, and uses the word "strumming" without interrogating the anthropomorphism embedded in that framing.

**Reanalysis required.** No reanalysis of the verdict. The factual verdict is clear and correct.

**Contestation.** Minimal. No significant challenges to the video's authenticity have been advanced. The only genuine interpretive question — whether the cockroach is "playing" or "strumming" as opposed to incidentally producing contact sounds — is a semantic rather than factual dispute, but it is worth naming precisely because it bears on how the claim is understood.

**Gap assigned:** Contextually thin. The verdict is correct and appropriate. The gap is lack of biological and physical depth explaining the mechanism of sound production, the ecology of guitar gear as cockroach habitat, and the relationship between cockroach locomotion biomechanics and the acoustic properties of tensioned strings.


## 5. The Broader Stakes

The viral resonance of the cockroach-guitar video is easy to understand: it activates a distinctly human set of associations around music, creativity, and the comic incongruity of vermin performing culturally elevated activities. The fact that it is funny does not mean it is not interesting. What makes it genuinely interesting, beyond the surface novelty, is what it reveals about the integration of human material culture into arthropod habitat ecologies.

Guitars are wooden resonance chambers with metal string arrays, felt or foam case linings, and pockets of food debris — effectively, from a cockroach's perspective, a somewhat unusual version of the enclosed wooden spaces to which Blattodea have been adapting throughout their evolutionary history alongside human habitation. Cockroaches have cohabited with human architecture for at least 300 million years of evolutionary history (Evangelista, D.A., et al., 2019, "Roaches ran before they walked: facultative crevice dwelling in early Dictyoptera," *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, 286(1914)). Their adaptability to every enclosed, warm, food-trace-containing environment humans create is not incidental — it is among the most successful ecological strategies in animal evolutionary biology.

The guitar is therefore not a strange place for a cockroach. It is precisely the kind of thing cockroaches would find and inhabit. What is unusual is that this particular enclosed human artifact makes noise when cockroaches cross it — and that the noise resembles musical production closely enough to be interesting to humans. The video works comedically and scientifically for the same reason: it reveals the hidden interface between human material culture and the arthropod world that surrounds it.

Understanding that interface matters beyond entertainment. Cockroach infestations of musical instrument storage are genuinely problematic: cockroach frass (fecal matter) and exoskeletons are potent allergens associated with asthma and allergic disease (Rosenstreich, D.L., et al., 1997, "The role of cockroach allergy and exposure to cockroach allergen in causing morbidity among inner-city children with asthma," *New England Journal of Medicine*, 336(19), 1356–1363). Instruments stored in cases that harbor cockroaches become vectors for allergen distribution. This is not a trivial concern for professional musicians working in high-density urban environments with significant cockroach pressure.

The lighter story of Vinny-the-guitar-roach thus points, with appropriate humor, toward a real public health and materials-preservation issue that has not received systematic attention in the pest management literature.


## 6. What This Entry Adds

This Truth Vault entry affirms the Snopes verdict — the video is authentic, the cockroach is genuinely producing string sounds — while layering three dimensions of substantive analysis that the original fact-check omits:

First, a biomechanical account of *how* cockroach locomotion produces guitar string sounds, grounded in the well-developed literature on cockroach gait mechanics and the physics of tensioned string vibration — including a precise clarification of why "strumming" is anthropomorphically inaccurate even though the underlying factual claim is correct.

Second, a contextual account of *why* cockroaches in guitar gear is apparently common, drawing on the literature of cockroach habitat selection and the ecological logic of musical instrument cases as equivalent to the enclosed, warm, food-trace-bearing spaces to which cockroaches have adapted throughout their evolutionary history alongside human habitation.

Third, a note on the public health dimensions of arthropod infestation of instruments — the allergenicity of cockroach frass and its implications for musicians who encounter precisely this problem.

The verdict is True, and the video is what it appears to be. But what it appears to be is more biologically interesting than the Snopes treatment suggests.
