## §1 — Claim & Verdict Overview

In late February 2021, Snopes received reader requests to investigate the claim that ground coffee beans routinely contain traces of cockroaches — specifically that pre-ground commercial coffee is contaminated with ground-up cockroach fragments, and that this is a documented and regulatory-acknowledged phenomenon. The claim had renewed viral momentum despite having circulated in various forms since at least 2009.

Snopes investigator Madison Dapcevich (2021) rated this claim **Mixture**, on the following analysis:

**What's true:** The U.S. Food and Drug Administration's food defect action level framework explicitly permits levels of insect byproduct in processed coffee as "natural or unavoidable defects that present no health hazards for humans." Insects and insect fragments are documented trace elements in commercially processed foods broadly, and in coffee specifically. The regulatory context for this is real and publicly available in the FDA's Food Defect Levels Handbook.

**What's undetermined:** The specific claim that cockroaches — as opposed to insects generally — have been documented in ground coffee rests on anecdotal transmission via a single viral NPR interview from 2009, and the FDA itself specifically states that cockroaches are not among the insects permitted as incidental contaminants in coffee under its defect action level framework. The claim's vivid specificity (cockroaches, in particular) is not confirmed by published analytical data.

The case is a particularly rich one for media literacy because it involves accurate regulatory information (defect action levels are real), a plausible biological mechanism (insects do get into coffee during storage and processing), and a culturally resonant specific claim (cockroaches, a maximally repellent insect species for most consumers) — all of which combine to generate a claim that feels highly credible while outrunning its specific evidentiary basis.

## §2 — The FDA Defect Action Level Framework

The FDA's authority to regulate food defects comes from the Federal Food, Drug and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act), which defines "adulterated food" to include food produced under insanitary conditions that may render it injurious to health. The FDA recognizes that complete elimination of all natural contaminants from commercially processed foods is technologically and economically impractical, and the Food Defect Levels Handbook (formally the Defect Action Levels guideline, updated periodically) establishes maximum permitted levels of "natural or unavoidable defects in foods for human use that present no health hazard."

The Handbook covers a wide range of products — from wheat flour to canned tomatoes to peanut butter — and for each, establishes a defect action level (DAL): the level above which a defect is considered to be sufficient basis for regulatory action. DALs are expressed in units appropriate to the defect type: insect fragments per gram, rodent hairs per 25 grams, mold count per field of view, and so forth.

The relevant entry for coffee is not for roasted or ground coffee specifically but rather for **green (unroasted) coffee beans**, which the Handbook specifies a DAL of 10% insect-infested or damaged beans by count. This means that up to 10% of the green coffee beans in a sample may show evidence of insect damage before FDA would take regulatory action on that sample. This is a relatively permissive standard, reflecting the storage conditions of green coffee — which is often aged in open or partially open storage for extended periods before roasting — and the practical difficulty of sorting individual infested beans from large commercial lots.

The key distinction, noted in Dapcevich's fact-check, is between **insect-infested or damaged beans** (the DAL metric for green coffee) and **cockroaches specifically** (which are not listed as incidental pests in the DAL calculation. The FDA's Compliance Policy Guide (CPG Sec. 555600), which provides guidance on enforcement policy for "filth, insects, rodents, and other pests in foods," explicitly categorizes insects as either "incidental pests" (which contribute to the defect action level calculation) or not. Cockroaches are not listed as incidental pests under the relevant framework, meaning they are not factored into the defect action level for coffee. The FDA spokesperson quoted by Snopes was direct: "To be clear, cockroaches are not allowed in coffee."

This regulatory precision matters enormously for interpreting the claim correctly. The viral version — "cockroaches are in your coffee and the FDA allows it" — conflates two distinct regulatory facts: (1) insect fragments at sub-DAL levels are permissible in processed food; (2) cockroaches specifically are not in the permitted incidental insect category for coffee. The claim's most alarming formulation is specifically the part that is not supported by the available regulatory evidence.

## §3 — The 2009 NPR Transmission and Its Epistemic Life

The primary vector for the cockroaches-in-coffee claim is traceable to a specific 2009 NPR interview with Douglas Emlen, a biologist at the University of Montana specializing in dung beetles. Emlen was being interviewed about his beetle research when he recounted an anecdote: his graduate school adviser, an entomologist identified by Dapcevich (2021) as George Ichorph, had developed a severe cockroach allergy from years of handling cockroaches in the lab. Ichorph would travel 45 minutes out of his way to purchase freshly whole-bean ground coffee specifically because, Emlen recounted, pre-ground commercial coffee contained traces of cockroaches. The allergy, Ichorph reportedly explained to Emlen, was sensitive enough that pre-ground coffee containing cockroach traces triggered allergic reactions.

This anecdote, broadcast by NPR and accessible via transcript, became the primary source for subsequent viral claims. Emlen told Snopes that his research was not specifically on insect contaminants in coffee, and that "the claim is true to the best of my knowledge" but he "could not comment on it specifically." He is described in the Snopes piece as confirming the basic mechanism — that cockroach traces could end up in pre-ground coffee — without providing specific documentation.

The transmission history of this anecdote is instructive. It was a second-hand story (Emlen recounting what he had been told by Ichorph) embedded in an interview about a different topic (dung beetles) on a credible broadcast outlet (NPR), attributed to an identified professional authority (an entomologist) with a specific and verifiable mechanism (cockroach allergy from lab exposure leading to specific behavioral adaptation). Each of these features contributed to the claim's credibility and memetic persistence. The NPR context provided institutional weight; the entomologist adviser provided expert authority; the allergy mechanism provided biological specificity; the 45-minute travel story provided memorable narrative concreteness.

Crucially, the claim as transmitted by Emlen was never supported by a primary data source: no published study documenting cockroach fragments specifically in commercial ground coffee was cited then or has been produced since. The FDA Microanalytical Procedures Manual cited in subsequent coverage confirms that insect material is detectable in commercial coffee, but — as noted above — cockroach specifically was not the focus of nor the result documented in the relevant analytical procedures.

This is precisely the "mixture" structure that Dapcevich's fact-check captures: a kernel of verified truth (insect fragments in coffee are real and FDA-acknowledged), a supporting anecdote from a credible source (Emlen/Ichorph), and a specific biological claim (cockroaches in particular) that remains unverified in published primary scientific literature. The claim is neither confirmed fabrication nor confirmed fact; it is an unverified specific within a verified general category.

## §4 — Epistemic Novelty: The "Regulated Gross-Out" Information Category

The cockroaches-in-coffee claim represents a distinctive and undertheorized **information category: the regulated gross-out** — a factual claim about food content that is (a) partially true, (b) disclosed in publicly available regulatory documents, (c) never mentioned on product packaging, and (d) perceived as disturbing by most consumers if revealed. Several comparable cases exist: rodent hairs in peanut butter (real, regulated, publicly disclosed in FDA defect levels but absent from labels), mold in canned tomatoes (real, regulated), maggots in canned fruit (real, regulated), insect fragments in wheat flour (real, regulated).

These cases share a distinctive epistemological profile. They are not misinformation in the sense of fabricated claims; the regulatory facts are real and publicly available. They are not secrets; the FDA Defect Levels Handbook is a publicly accessible document, not proprietary or classified. They are not deliberately withheld; manufacturers are not required to label defects below the DAL precisely because the FDA has determined these levels present no health hazard. Yet most consumers are entirely unaware of their existence, and exposure to this information reliably produces disgust reactions, sharing behavior, and secondhand dissemination that often outpaces the regulatory context.

The psychological dynamics of this sharing behavior are grounded in established disgust psychology. Rozin and Fallon (1987, *Psychological Review*) identified contamination sensitivity — the tendency to reject food after it has contacted a disgusting object, even when rational analysis confirms no health risk — as a core feature of human disgust psychology with deep evolutionary roots in pathogen avoidance. Martens and Verbeke (2006, *Appetite*) documented that disgust sensitivity in food contexts is highly responsive to information about the presence of insects or rodent byproducts, even when dosage is below any biologically meaningful threshold.

The "regulated gross-out" category is epistemically distinctive because the claim's truth content and its emotional impact are both real but refer to different things. The true part — "the FDA permits insect fragments in coffee below the defect action level" — is a statement about regulatory tolerance, not about biological risk or product quality failure. The emotionally salient part — "there are bugs in your coffee" — activates disgust responses that are calibrated to biologically relevant contamination threats, not regulatory tolerance thresholds. Communicating the true part without inadvertently (or deliberately) triggering the emotionally salient response requires a level of contextual precision in reporting that viral sharing systematically destroys.

**The coffee allergy dimension.** An additional epistemic dimension involves cockroach allergenicity. Cockroach allergens (primarily *Blattella germanica* and *Periplaneta americana* allergens documented as Bla g 1, Bla g 2, Bla g 4, etc.) are significant triggers for asthma and rhinitis in urban populations. Kang and colleagues (1979, *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*) established the clinical significance of cockroach allergen exposure in inner-city asthma; subsequent large-scale studies including the Inner-City Asthma Study (Crain et al., 2002, *Archives of Pediatrics & Adolescent Medicine*) documented cockroach allergen sensitization as a major driver of asthma morbidity in low-income urban areas. The Ichorph allergy recounted by Emlen — a cockroach hypersensitivity severe enough to produce reactions from food-traces — is scientifically plausible, given what is known about cockroach allergen potency. This biological plausibility is a major reason the anecdote is compelling: it describes a real physiological mechanism, not a fanciful claim.

The question of whether pre-ground commercial coffee reliably contains cockroach fragments at levels sufficient to trigger reactions in cockroach-allergic individuals is, however, distinct from the question of whether cockroach fragments are present at all. Very low levels of allergen exposure may not trigger reactions in all sensitized individuals; the threshold varies with sensitization level and specific allergen component. No published study has measured cockroach allergen concentrations in commercial versus freshly ground coffee in a way that could validate or refute the specific claim about home-ground coffee being safer for cockroach-allergic consumers.

## §5 — Coffee Processing and Insect Vulnerability Windows

The biology of insect contamination in coffee is worth mapping carefully to understand at which stages the FDA-permitted insect fragments actually enter the product.

Coffee is produced from the seeds (beans) of *Coffea* species, primarily *C. arabica* and *C. canephora* (robusta). From harvest to cup, the beans pass through multiple processing stages: wet or dry processing to remove the fruit around the seed; milling and hulling to remove the parchment layer; sorting and grading; export and transport in jute or grain bags; roasting at destination; grinding; and packaging.

The primary insect vulnerability window in commercial coffee is the **green bean storage phase** — the period after primary processing and before roasting when the dried, hulled beans are stored in bags at origin warehouses, transit facilities, and destination warehouses. Green beans are stored in these conditions for weeks to months. The storage environment — in bags that are not hermetically sealed, in conditions that may include moderate humidity — is hospitable to multiple insect species, including the coffee borer beetle (*Hypothenemus hampei*), a major coffee pest, as well as associated storage insects including grain weevils, flour beetles, and in urban storage facilities, cockroaches that would be present as general facility pests rather than coffee-specific pests.

The roasting process, which exposes coffee beans to temperatures of 180–230°C for several minutes, would destroy the biological activity of any insect material present in the batch, including allergens and pathogens associated with insects. However, the physical material — fragments, exoskeletons, frass — would not be eliminated by roasting; these would persist through the roasting and grinding process. The grinding process, which reduces whole beans to a powder, would simultaneously grind any insect material present in the batch to a similar particle size, making it visually undetectable in the final ground product.

This mechanistic pathway is consistent with the general FDA finding that insect material can be present in processed coffee. What it does not establish is the specific presence of cockroaches as opposed to other insect species. The insects most commonly associated with stored coffee are specialized coffee pests (especially *H. hampei*) and general storage pests (grain insects), not cockroaches. Cockroaches are generalist urban pests that could enter storage facilities, but they would be present only as facility contaminants, not as primary coffee pests, and their presence would be controlled through standard Integrated Pest Management (IPM) protocols required at FSMA-compliant food storage facilities.

**The whole-bean advantage.** Emlen's anecdote described his adviser traveling to purchase whole-bean coffee that was freshly ground at point of sale. This practice would reduce insect fragment exposure if the contamination occurs at an intermediate processing or storage stage after which the product is repackaged. However, if contamination occurs during green-bean storage — a stage common to all commercially processed coffee — the freshly-ground advantage is limited, since both the whole beans purchased at the specialty shop and the commercially pre-ground coffee would have passed through the same green-bean storage phase. The specific advantage Ichorph attributed to fresh whole-bean grinding would only be valid if the contamination primarily occurred at the post-roast grinding or packaging stage — for example, if grinding equipment in a high-volume commercial facility was host to insect contamination that the consumer-scale burr grinder at a specialty shop was not. This specific mechanism has not been studied.

## §6 — Research Gaps and EARC Assignment

**EARC Assignment: A (Analytical)**

The cockroaches-in-coffee case opens several research questions spanning food science, regulatory policy, allergen research, and misinformation psychology.

**Gap 1: Species-level identification of insect fragments in commercial ground coffee.** No published peer-reviewed study has used morphological analysis or DNA methods to identify insect fragments in commercial ground coffee samples at the species level. A systematic market basket study — purchasing commercial pre-ground coffee samples across brands and origins, performing standard FDA microanalytical procedures, and applying DNA barcoding or morphological taxonomy to recovered insect fragments — would directly answer whether cockroach fragments are present and at what frequency and concentration relative to other insect species. This study is technically feasible with existing methods and would move the claim from "plausible but anecdotal" to empirically assessed.

**Gap 2: Cockroach allergen stability through coffee processing.** The literature on cockroach allergen purification and characterization (Chapman et al., 1998, *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*) has not extended to the question of allergen stability through high-temperature roasting and grinding. Quantification of Bla g 1 or Bla g 2 allergen activity in commercial ground coffee versus fresh whole-bean samples would directly assess the biological plausibility of the allergy mechanism described by Emlen. Standard ELISA-based allergen quantification methodologies exist for cockroach major allergens and could be applied to food samples.

**Gap 3: Regulated gross-out information and consumer behavior.** The psychological literature on disgust and food safety (Rozin & Fallon, 1987, *Psychological Review*; Templer & Arikawa, 2006, *Personality and Individual Differences*) has studied disgust sensitivity as a trait, but the specific behavior change effects of "regulated gross-out" information — learning that a food you regularly consume contains regulated levels of a normally disgusting substance — has not been studied in intervention format. A randomized experiment presenting subjects with the FDA defect action level information for coffee (or another familiar product) and measuring subsequent consumption behavior, information sharing behavior, and attitude toward the FDA would quantify the real-world behavioral impact of this information category.

**Gap 4: Coffee storage IPM and cockroach prevalence in global supply chains.** Integrated Pest Management effectiveness in coffee storage facilities across producing countries (Brazil, Colombia, Ethiopia, Vietnam, etc.) is not systematically documented in the published literature in ways that would allow inference about cockroach fragment probability per unit of processed coffee. A supply chain audit methodology combining facility inspection and coffee sample microanalysis across processing stages would clarify at which stages the FDA's permitted insect tolerance thresholds are exercised and what pest species contribute.
