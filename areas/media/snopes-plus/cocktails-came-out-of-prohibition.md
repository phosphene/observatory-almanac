# "Cocktails Came Out of Prohibition?"

## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: Cocktails — both as a general category of mixed drinks and as the specific genre of spirit mixed with bitters and sugar — are a wholly American invention that originated during, or were substantially created by, the Prohibition era (1920–1933). In this telling, cocktails emerged as a direct response to the poor quality of illicitly produced alcohol: bartenders developed mixed drinks to mask the harsh, fuel-like taste of moonshine and bootleg liquor, giving birth to the cocktail in the speakeasies of Prohibition America.

**Common articulation**: The claim appears in multiple forms across popular history writing, cocktail bar culture, and informal historical discussion. A representative version: "Where beer and wine had previously been the drinks of choice, now alcohol was drunk much more frequently, as it was easier to transport and took up less space, making it cheaper for speakeasy patrons. It was at this time that the 'cocktail' was born, which had virtually been non-existent prior to Prohibition." Another common version credits the Prohibition-era scarcity of good spirits as the mother of mixological invention: bad liquor required good mixing to be palatable.

**The nationalist supplement**: Many versions of the claim include a second layer: not only did cocktails emerge from Prohibition, but they are specifically American — an indigenous contribution of the United States to global drinking culture. This nationalist supplement borrows some authority from the historical figure of Jerry Thomas, a 19th-century American bartender whose 1862 publication *Bar-Tender's Guide* (Thomas, 1862) is the first major American bartending manual. Some interpreters conflate Thomas's influential role in systematizing American cocktail culture with an inventive-priority claim that cocktails themselves originated in America.

**Why this claim has cultural staying power**: The story of the cocktail as Prohibition's child has several attributes that favor narrative persistence. It is emotionally satisfying: oppression begetting creativity, with the speakeasy as a site of both resistance and cultural production. It is nationally flattering for American audiences: a distinctly American contribution to world culture, born of a distinctly American political moment. And it has enough partial truth — Prohibition did influence cocktail culture in documentable ways — that it resists simple falsification while substantially overstating its case.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The first documented use of "cocktail" as a drinking term**: The earliest known printed use of the word "cocktail" in the context of a mixed drink appears not in American sources but in a London newspaper in 1798. This is a crucial chronological fact: the word predates Prohibition by approximately 122 years and predates the United States itself in its modern constitutional form by only about a decade. If the cocktail were a creature of Prohibition, one would need to explain not only why a London newspaper was using the word 120 years before Prohibition, but why American sources before 1920 describe and discuss cocktails extensively.

**Jerry Thomas and early American codification**: The 1862 Thomas Bar-Tender's Guide does represent a landmark in cocktail history — as an early systematic text on American mixed drink preparation. Thomas himself worked in London during part of his career (before returning to the United States), a biographical detail that underscores the trans-Atlantic nature of mixing culture. The book includes recipes for the Mint Julep, the Brandy Smash, the Tom Collins, and classics we would recognize today. In 1867, the term "cocktail" appears in print as referring specifically to a drink of spirits, bitters, and sugar — and by this point the term is already well-established enough to be used casually in popular American writing.

**Colonial American drinking culture**: The narrative that cocktails emerged from Prohibition implicitly assumes a pre-Prohibition era of simple, unmixed beer and wine consumption. Historical evidence contradicts this comprehensively. A 1991 article in the *Organization of American Historians Magazine of History* (Lender & Martin, 1991) documents alcohol consumption in late 18th-century America at approximately 3.5 gallons per person per year — approximately double contemporary consumption rates. By 1770, Americans consumed alcohol regularly with every meal; mixed drinks in various forms — rum toddies, punches, slings, and shrubs — were commonplace. The heavily sugared bottom of a parent's mug of rum toddy was reportedly given to toddlers, a practice indicating that mixed alcoholic drinks were thoroughly integrated into daily colonial life.

**Bitters and the cocktail's defining ingredient**: The cocktail in its specific formulation — spirit plus bitters plus sugar and water — depends critically on bitters, which are highly concentrated botanical extracts. Bitters have a documented history substantially predating American cocktail culture. They were used medicinally for centuries across Europe and the Americas; Angostura bitters, one of the most famous, was developed in Ciudad Bolívar, Venezuela (then Angostura, hence the name), where the German physician Johann Gottlieb Benjamin Siegert developed them in the 1820s to treat digestive complaints in Simón Bolívar's army (Wondrich, D., 2007, *Imbibe!*). Bitters were subsequently mixed with spirits and sugar to improve palatability, transitioning from medicinal to recreational use throughout the 1800s — not in the 1920s.

**Bitters and spirits on both sides of the Atlantic pre-1920**: Cocktail historian David Wondrich documents that by the mid-18th century, gin and bitters, brandy and bitters, and wine and bitters appeared frequently on both sides of the Atlantic. Wondrich quotes an 1844 New Orleans Daily Picayune characterization of "wine-and-bitters" as "one of the most generic terms which the wealth of the English language furnishes" — suggesting the cocktail in its bitters-spirit-sugar form was already generic and familiar by the 1840s.

**George Washington and the first recorded cocktail hour**: Wondrich documents an extraordinary historical vignette: in May 1783, when British officers were meeting with George Washington's staff to arrange the British surrender of New York, Washington "pulled out his watch, and observing that it was near Dinner Time, offered Wine and Bitters" (recorded by Loyalist William Smith in his diary). If interpreting "wine-and-bitters" as a cocktail equivalent (consistent with the 1844 New Orleans usage), this places a documented cocktail moment at the very founding period of the United States — more than 135 years before Prohibition.

**What Prohibition actually did to cocktail culture**: Prohibition from 1920 to 1933 did influence cocktail culture, but through mechanisms distinct from the "invention" narrative. Prohibition drove drinking underground into speakeasies, where the threat of raids created incentive to drink quickly rather than with elegance — the opposite of the refined cocktail culture that preceded it. More significantly, Prohibition degraded the quality of available spirits: bathtub gin was often harsh and impure, and genuinely good spirits required either bootlegging from Canada and Europe or careful home production. In this environment, strong flavors in the form of fruit juices, sweet liqueurs, and syrups were used to mask spirit deficiencies. The more lasting Prohibition effect on cocktail culture was diaspora: skilled American bartenders emigrated to Europe to continue working in legal environments, bringing American cocktail knowledge to London, Paris, and elsewhere — not inventing cocktails, but spreading American versions of a pre-existing tradition.

**Snopes verdict**: **Mixture**. Cocktails substantially predate Prohibition, the word first appears in 1798 London, and mixed-drink culture was well established in both America and Europe throughout the 19th century. Prohibition did influence cocktail culture through quality degradation and bartender diaspora, but it did not invent cocktails.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — Founding Myths, National Identity, and the Invention Fallacy

The cocktail origin myth belongs to a well-documented category of historical claim that might be called the **invention fallacy of national convenience**: the attribution of cultural practices to a specific national-chronological origin point that serves collective identity rather than historical evidence. Understanding why this particular myth is so persistent requires examining the relationship between national mythology and cultural ownership claims.

The invention fallacy typically works through what historians of science and technology call **priority disputes**: claims about who did something first that are simultaneously historical claims and legal/social claims about who owns a cultural achievement. In cultural domains without intellectual property structures, "invention priority" functions as a form of claim to cultural heritage. "Americans invented the cocktail" is not merely a causal historical claim — it is a claim about American cultural contribution to global civilization, and it carries the emotional valence of pride in national achievement.

The claim is particularly susceptible to the invention fallacy because the late 19th century represented an undeniably flourishing period of American cocktail culture. Jerry Thomas, his contemporaries, and the generation of American bartenders who developed the Old Fashioned, the Manhattan, and the Martini in their modern forms were genuinely innovative. The error is in translating "American bartenders made important contributions to cocktail culture" into "cocktails come from America" — a conflation of developmental contribution with inventive origin that systematically underestimates prior European and Latin American mixing traditions.

There is also a specific **temporal compression** effect at work. For many people, American history's emotional highlights cluster around a small number of well-known eras: the Founding, the Civil War, the Gilded Age, and — in popular culture — the Jazz Age / Roaring Twenties, of which Prohibition is an inseparable element. The Prohibition era is emotionally salient in American cultural memory in a way that, say, 1860s bar culture of San Francisco is not. This temporal salience means that when people learn that something culturally interesting is "from that era," the era's vividness can make it feel like an origin point even when it is merely a development point. Speakeasies are glamorous and well-remembered; 1820s Venezuelan bitters-makers are not.

The myth also illustrates what sociologist Barry Barnes and historian David Bloor called the **symmetry principle** in the sociology of knowledge (Barnes & Bloor, 1982, *Rationality and Relativism*): the same social analysis applies to true beliefs and false ones. The persistence of the cocktail Prohibition myth is not explained by its truth; it is explained by its social functions — identity affirmation, narrative satisfaction, and the conversion of mixed drinks from European-shared tradition into an American invention. These social functions are served equally well whether the claim is historically accurate or not, which is why factual correction does not reliably dislodge the myth from circulation.


## 4. The Real Complexity — Alcohol, Temperance, and the Deep History of Drinking

The Prohibition-focused cocktail narrative obscures a genuinely rich and complex alcohol history that predates the 20th century by millennia.

**Archaeological context of fermented beverages**: The human history of fermented and distilled beverages extends to at least 7,000 BCE in China (McGovern, P. E., et al., 2004, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*), with wine documented in ancient Georgia by approximately 6,000 BCE. Fermented beverages were integrated into ancient Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Greek, and Roman culture in ways that blur modern distinctions between food, medicine, and recreation. The idea that mixing flavored additions to spirits to improve palatability — the basic structure of a cocktail — would only emerge in 20th-century America is implausible against this backdrop.

**The temperance movement and its effect on drinking patterns**: The 19th-century U.S. temperance movement is critical context for understanding why the "cocktail born of Prohibition" narrative misdates the actual peak of American drinking culture. Historian W. J. Rorabaugh's research (*The Alcoholic Republic*, 1979, Oxford University Press) documents that American alcohol consumption peaked around 1830 at approximately four gallons per capita per year — not during Prohibition but nearly a century before it. The Prohibition era's drinking, though culturally prominent in popular memory, occurred after decades of successful temperance advocacy had already significantly reduced per-capita consumption from its 1830 peak. The speakeasy culture was the tail of a long drinking tradition, not its birth.

**The cultural trajectory of bitters**: The Angostura bitters story illustrates how thoroughly cocktail culture preceded American Prohibition in geographic and historical terms. Johann Siegert's 1820s Venezuela origin; the product's export to England in the 1850s and subsequent global distribution; the use of bitters-spirit combinations as medicinal and then recreational drinks across Europe and the Americas throughout the 1800s — all of this constitutes a documented trans-Atlantic cocktail culture operating fully a century before 1920.

**The cocktail in Britain**: The claim that cocktails are wholly American contradicts well-attested British cocktail history. The Savoy Cocktail Book (Craddock, 1930) — published in London at the height of Prohibition — documents hundreds of cocktail recipes from the bar of the Savoy Hotel, including classics that had been established in British bar culture for decades. The Harry Craddock who compiled it was an American-born bartender who had emigrated to London — exemplifying the Prohibition-era diaspora that transferred American approaches to the existing British cocktail scene rather than introducing cocktails to Britain for the first time.

**Post-Prohibition rehabilitation narrative**: The claim that cocktails emerged from Prohibition may also reflect a rehabilitation narrative that is characteristically American in its cultural psychology: suffering leads to creativity, constraint generates innovation, adversity produces art. This narrative arc has genuine instances in cultural history — constraint does sometimes produce creative responses — but applied to cocktails it maps the wrong mechanism onto the evidence. Prohibitionists degraded the quality of available spirits; skilled bartenders responded with compensatory masking flavors; the net effect on cocktail quality was largely negative compared to what good pre-Prohibition bartenders with quality spirits could produce. The creative flourishing in American cocktail culture came in the pre-Prohibition Gay Nineties and early 1900s, not in the speakeasies.


## 5. The "Professor" Jerry Thomas Question and American Cultural Contribution

Jerry Thomas deserves more nuanced treatment than either "inventor of the cocktail" or "irrelevant figure." His 1862 *Bar-Tender's Guide* is a genuine landmark for several reasons beyond simple authorship: it was the first major systematic American codification of bar practice; it documented drinks that were genuinely innovative American contributions (the classic American cocktail tradition of the Old Fashioned and its variants is substantially American in development even if not in origin); and it established a genre of bar literature that shaped subsequent global bartending culture.

Thomas's story also complicates the nationalist narrative: he is typically invoked to assert American cocktail primacy, but he worked in London, demonstrating that the culture he codified was already trans-Atlantic. His influence spread, but so did the influence of British and Continental European bar culture on him and his contemporaries. The 19th-century bartender operated in a cosmopolitan cultural environment in which American and European mixing traditions were in continuous exchange, not in isolation.

The broader American contribution to cocktail development is real and should not be dismissed in the course of correcting the Prohibition myth. American bartenders of the 1880–1920 period developed a distinctive approach — precise measurement, systematic technique, consistent flavor profiles — that differed from the more improvised European approach and that spread through the diaspora of Prohibition-era emigrants. American cocktail culture produced genuinely influential canonical drinks: the Manhattan, the Old Fashioned, the Daiquiri (though claimed by Cuba), the Martini. These are genuine contributions. They are simply not inventions in the sense of creating a previously nonexistent category, and they predate Prohibition.

**The mixology revival and historical consciousness**: The 21st-century cocktail revival — roughly 2000–present — has been characterized by a heightened interest in historically accurate drinks, pre-Prohibition recipes, and the recovery of lost or underappreciated ingredients (including historically accurate bitters formulations). This movement has been accompanied by serious historical scholarship on cocktail origins (Wondrich's *Imbibe!*, 2007; his *Punch*, 2010), which has documented the pre-Prohibition origins of cocktail culture in significant detail. The cocktail canon that revival bartenders have drawn on is explicitly pre-Prohibition — suggesting that practitioners of the tradition actively understand its origins to predate the era often credited with its creation.


## 6. Verdict and Takeaway

The claim that cocktails are a wholly American invention born of Prohibition is **Mixture at best, substantially false at worst**. The word "cocktail" appears in 1798 London; bitters were developed in Simón Bolívar's Venezuela in the 1820s; mixed-drink culture flourished in colonial America and on both sides of the Atlantic throughout the 19th century; and George Washington reportedly offered "wine and bitters" in 1783 — before Prohibition by 137 years.

Prohibition did influence cocktail culture in documentable ways: it degraded spirit quality and drove compensatory sweet or citrus masking; it drove skilled American bartenders to emigrate to Europe, spreading American cocktail knowledge to London and Paris; and it left a lasting cultural imprint on American memory that helps explain why the founding-myth attribution sticks. But "influenced" is not "invented," and "popularized in a specific cultural moment" is not "created for the first time."

The persistence of this myth illustrates the invention fallacy of national convenience: the translation of genuine developmental contribution into false inventive priority, driven by the social and identity functions that cultural ownership claims serve. The actual history is richer and more cosmopolitan: cocktails are a multiply-authored, trans-Atlantic, centuries-old tradition to which American bartenders made important but not originary contributions — and in which the desperation of Prohibition played a supporting, not a starring, role.
