# "Drinking Coffee Stunts Children's Growth"

## 1. The Claim

Across several generations of parental guidance, in many countries and languages, children asking for coffee have been told that it will stunt their growth. The rule is stated as confidently as any established nutritional fact: coffee is for adults; it interferes with children's development; it will prevent them from reaching their adult height. Parents who deliver this instruction typically cannot recall where they first heard it and do not feel the need to identify a source. It is one of those pieces of received wisdom so thoroughly embedded in household practice that it has long since been insulated from the need for justification.

The claim operates at multiple levels of specificity depending on the telling. In its simplest form: coffee will make you shorter. In more elaborated versions: the caffeine in coffee depletes calcium from bones and inhibits bone mineralisation, preventing children from reaching their genetically determined height. More dramatic retellings invoke childhood stunting as a permanent consequence—a few cups of coffee in early adolescence, on this account, will shave centimetres off the final height of an adult who might otherwise have grown taller.

The rule has particular force in Western parenting culture but also appears in a range of East Asian, Latin American, and European contexts where coffee consumption is culturally significant and where parents routinely use concerns about children's development to police access to adult beverages. The claim serves a dual purpose: it addresses genuine parental caution about caffeinating children, and it maintains an adult boundary around a widely enjoyed social beverage. This dual function—part nutrition advice, part social norm enforcement—gives it durability that a purely factual claim might not have.

The myth has been reinforced over the decades by well-meaning popular nutrition sources that cited early scientific literature on caffeine and calcium without careful attention to the specific populations studied, the dose-response relationships involved, or the limitations of the methods employed. The result is a claim that sounds scientific ("caffeine affects calcium absorption") attached to a conclusion (therefore children who drink coffee will be shorter) that the underlying research does not actually support.

## 2. What's Actually True

Human height is determined primarily by genetics and, secondarily, by nutritional adequacy and general health during the growth period. The polygenic architecture of height has been characterised in large-scale genome-wide association studies: Wood et al. (2014), writing in *Nature Genetics*, identified 697 independent genetic variants associated with height using a sample of over 250,000 individuals, estimating that common genetic variants together explain approximately 16–20 percent of height variance in the studied population, while the total narrow-sense heritability of height (the proportion of variance attributable to additive genetic factors) is estimated at approximately 80 percent in well-nourished populations. Environmental factors—including nutrition, disease burden, and psychosocial stress—account for the remainder. Coffee drinking is not among the environmental factors that have been found to explain height variation.

The mechanism most frequently cited for the coffee-stunts-growth claim is interference with calcium metabolism. Caffeine does have measurable effects on calcium handling: Massey and Wise (1984), in the *Journal of Nutrition*, reported that caffeine increased urinary calcium excretion in postmenopausal women and suggested that high habitual caffeine intake might be relevant to osteoporosis risk in older women with low baseline calcium intake. This finding attracted media attention and was generalised in popular coverage from "caffeine may affect bone mineral density in elderly women with low calcium intake" to "coffee interferes with calcium metabolism in children," a transformation that lost the specific population (postmenopausal women), the modifier (low calcium intake), the dose qualifier (high habitual intake), and replaced the outcome variable (bone mineral density in ageing adults) with a completely different one (growth in height during childhood). Each lost qualifier represented a different reason why the general claim could not be inferred from the specific finding.

Subsequent research failed to confirm even the original hypothesis. Hallström et al. (2006), in the *Osteoporosis International* journal, conducted a prospective study of 1,009 Swedish women and found that habitual coffee consumption was associated with a modest reduction in bone density only among women with low dietary calcium intake—specifically those consuming fewer than 700 mg per day—and that this association disappeared entirely when calcium intake was adequate. For individuals with normal-to-high calcium intake, coffee consumption had no detectable negative effect on bone mineral density. Given that children in wealthy-country populations tend to have calcium intakes substantially above 700 mg through dairy products and fortified foods, even the limited qualified finding from elderly women cannot be transferred to healthy children.

No longitudinal study has found an association between childhood coffee or caffeine consumption and reduced adult height. Söderqvist et al. (2018), reviewing the epidemiological literature on caffeine intake and growth outcomes in children for *Public Health Nutrition*, identified no studies demonstrating a causal pathway from childhood caffeine consumption to reduced stature, noting the absence of controlled randomised evidence and the failure of observational studies to show significant correlations after controlling for family socioeconomic status, parental height, and dietary composition. The association that occasionally appears in raw data is confounded by the tendency for coffee consumption in children and adolescents to correlate with lower-socioeconomic-status households in some study populations—a variable that is causally relevant to growth outcomes independently of caffeine.

The growth plate hypothesis—that caffeine might interfere with epiphyseal ossification—has been examined in animal models without producing results that support extrapolation to human children at typical consumption dosages. Studies using rodent models and high-dose caffeine administration have occasionally produced effects on bone development, but rodent caffeine metabolism differs substantially from human physiology, and the doses required to produce these effects in animals correspond to far higher human-equivalent exposures than any child would reach through normal coffee or tea consumption.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The specific historical origin of the coffee-stunts-growth claim is more traceable than most dietary myths, and its genealogy reveals an underappreciated pathway by which commercial advertising can generate persistent folk health beliefs: the advertising claim is forgotten while the health message it planted survives.

In the mid-twentieth-century United States, Postum—a caffeine-free grain-based coffee substitute manufactured by Postum Cereal Company, later incorporated into General Foods—ran extensive advertising campaigns positioning coffee as a dangerous beverage for children and promoting Postum as the safe family alternative. C.W. Post, the founder, had a strongly ideological orientation against caffeine; his company's marketing consistently framed coffee as a health hazard. Advertisements in popular magazines and newspapers published from the 1910s through the 1940s repeatedly warned that coffee was "harmful to children" and could interfere with their proper development.

The cultural historian Katherine J. Parkin, analysing American food advertising history, has documented that the Postum campaigns were among the most sustained in linking a mainstream beverage to children's health fears. These advertisements did not invent the coffee-stunts-growth myth from nothing—there were pre-existing cultural traditions of managing children's caffeine access—but they invested heavily in medicalising the concern and circulating it to a mass audience. The commercial origin of the claim has been almost entirely forgotten, but the health message persists in exactly the form in which it was advertised.

This is the key epistemic observation: *advertising-mediated belief laundering is a specific and underappreciated pathway through which commercial claims become incorporated into folk health knowledge, stripped of their commercial origin through the simple passage of time.* The Postum campaigns did not leave a documentary trace in the minds of the people who absorbed their message; the message was encoded as parental common sense and reproduced through domestic transmission entirely independently of its original promotional context. Similar pathways can be traced in the origins of orange juice as a breakfast staple, the creation of "morning sickness" foods, and the promotion of cow's milk as uniquely essential for bone development—all cases where commercial interest and health messaging became so thoroughly entangled that retrospectively separating them is difficult and counterintuitive.

A second mechanism sustaining the mythology is the plausibility scaffolding provided by genuine but limited evidence on caffeine and calcium. When health-conscious parents encounter a debunking article stating "coffee doesn't stunt growth," many are resistant because they have absorbed—correctly—that caffeine has effects on calcium metabolism. The debunking feels like it is denying something they know to be true. But what the evidence actually says is more specific: caffeine affects urinary calcium excretion at high doses, primarily in older adults with low baseline calcium intake, with no demonstrated effects on height outcomes in children. The gap between "caffeine affects calcium excretion in some populations at high doses" and "coffee stunts growth in children" is substantial, but the former statement is true enough to make the latter feel plausible by association.

A third factor is the general precautionary logic that governs parental regulation of children's food access. In contexts where parents are uncertain whether a food substance is safe for children, erring on the side of restriction is cognitively and socially easy. The cost of unnecessarily restricting coffee is low (children don't need it); the imagined cost of allowing it if the myth were true is high (irreversible reduction in height). Asymmetric cost framing combined with plausible-sounding mechanism creates a psychologically stable equilibrium where belief in the stunting claim is maintained even when the underlying evidence is thin, because the behavioural recommendation (don't let children drink coffee) seems prudent regardless of the claim's empirical status.

## 4. Verdict

**REFUTED:** Drinking coffee does not stunt children's growth. No credible scientific evidence supports this claim. The mechanisms most often cited—caffeine's effects on calcium absorption and bone development—do not operate at typical dietary exposures in children with normal calcium intake, and no longitudinal study has demonstrated an association between childhood coffee consumption and reduced adult height.

The claim has a traceable commercial origin in early-to-mid-twentieth century Postum advertising that medicinalised concerns about childhood caffeine access for commercial purposes. The advertising origin has been entirely forgotten while the health message it transmitted has persisted through parental transmission as folk wisdom.

This verdict applies to the specific claim that coffee stunts growth. It explicitly does not apply to other, separately valid concerns about caffeine in children: caffeine is a stimulant that interferes with sleep architecture, and sleep is causally relevant to growth hormone secretion and physical development. Papadopoulou et al. (2021), reviewing caffeine effects in children and adolescents in *Nutrients*, documented that caffeine at doses above approximately 2.5 mg/kg body weight reliably reduces sleep duration and sleep efficiency in children—effects that are independently concerning for neurodevelopment, academic performance, and mood regulation, even if they do not produce the specific outcome (stunted linear growth) claimed in the myth. Parents who restrict children's coffee access for sleep-related reasons are acting on legitimate scientific grounds; those who restrict it because "it will stunt your growth" are repeating a myth.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The coffee-stunts-growth case belongs to a rich category of nutrition myths that can be understood as *commercial knowledge fossils*: beliefs originally generated or amplified by commercial advertising that have outlived their advertising context and now circulate as autonomous folk wisdom, their origin in promotion entirely obscured.

Food and beverage industry history in the twentieth century is dense with such fossils. The eight-glasses-of-water-a-day norm was reinforced by beverage industry interests even if its origin was governmental. The idea that citrus juice is uniquely important at breakfast was promoted by Florida orange growers and the Sunkist brand before it became nutritional common sense. The concept that dairy milk is uniquely essential to childhood bone development—beyond the genuine nutritional value of adequate calcium—was heavily promoted by the dairy industry before it was incorporated into government dietary guidelines in the United States. In each case, the commercial promotion was not simply fabrication: it latched onto genuine nutritional information (hydration is important; vitamin C has value; calcium is needed for bones) and amplified it into a more categorical claim that served the promoter's market position. The amplification is what survives as folk belief; the original fact it exaggerated is what makes the belief feel credible.

The mechanisms by which advertising-generated beliefs enter and persist in popular knowledge are now better understood through work in advertising history and cultural sociology. Marchand (1985), examining American advertising from 1920 to 1940 in *Advertising the American Dream*, documented how interwar campaigns systematically associated commercial products with health and social normalcy, creating a vernacular of domestic health concern that outlasted specific products and campaigns. The Postum health claims about coffee were part of a broad mid-century American discourse about food and contamination in which advertisers repeatedly leveraged genuine anxieties about childhood health to differentiate products.

What makes the coffee-stunts-growth case analytically distinctive within this category is the availability of a specific, traceable advertising agent—the Postum brand campaigns—rather than a diffuse industry effort. This specificity allows the belief's genealogy to be partially reconstructed, demonstrating that folk health wisdom is not simply a reflection of popular misreading of scientific evidence but can be deliberately seeded by commercial actors and will persist independently of its commercial origins once it has entered domestic common sense.

The persistence of the myth also raises questions about the temporal dynamics of belief correction. The Postum campaigns ran primarily from the 1910s through the mid-1940s. Although C.W. Post's ideological anti-coffee stance was well known in his lifetime and the commercial character of his health claims was widely understood by contemporary observers, the health message itself migrated from advertising copy into domestic instruction over approximately two to three decades—roughly one generational cycle. Once encoded in parental practice, the belief required no further advertising support: it was reproduced through normal intergenerational transmission. Correcting such beliefs—once they have cleared the generational threshold and become "what everyone knows"—requires either a dramatic new piece of evidence that attracts popular attention, a sustained counter-campaign with comparable reach to the original promotional effort, or multi-generational educational intervention. None of these is easily available for a myth as benign and as deeply embedded as "coffee stunts growth."

A final consideration concerns what the myth crowds out. By giving parents a false concern about coffee (stunted growth), the myth may crowd out attention to legitimate caffeine-related concerns that are better supported by evidence—particularly the effects of caffeine on adolescent sleep. Sleep disruption through caffeine use in adolescents is not theoretical: the studies reviewed by Papadopoulou et al. (2021) in *Nutrients* show sleep architecture effects at moderate doses, and Cain et al. (2017), in *Acta Paediatrica*, found that self-reported habitual caffeine intake in adolescents was significantly associated with reduced total sleep time and increased sleep onset latency. In a public health communication sense, every mention of the mythological growth-stunting claim is a missed opportunity to communicate these more specific, better-evidenced concerns about adolescent caffeine use and sleep.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not published a dedicated article addressing the coffee-stunts-growth claim as of mid-2026. Given that this is among the most commonly heard childhood dietary prohibitions in North American parenting culture, and that the claim has a specific, traceable commercial origin worth documenting, the absence of a Snopes entry represents a meaningful gap in the popular fact-checking record.

Science journalism has touched on the topic intermittently. Articles in *Scientific American*, *Live Science*, and *Medical News Today* have periodically noted the lack of evidence for a stunting effect, typically referencing the calcium-excretion studies and explaining why they do not support the childhood growth claim. These articles tend to confirm the pattern visible across many health myth corrections: they are published, shared within health-conscious media audiences, and largely fail to dislodge the belief from domestic transmission contexts where parents are unlikely to encounter or seek out science news articles on the topic.

The caffeine-and-children literature has focused primarily on energy drink consumption—a contemporary public health concern attracting considerably more regulatory and media attention than traditional coffee or tea—and on behavioural and sleep outcomes rather than growth. This focus shift means that when consumers do encounter science coverage of "caffeine and children," the coverage they find is likely to be about energy drink risks (cardiovascular effects at high doses, sleep disruption, marketing to minors) rather than the specific growth-stunting claim they grew up hearing. The disconnection between the myth's framing and the contemporary evidence base makes self-directed debunking unlikely even for motivated consumers.

A comprehensive fact-check entry on this claim would benefit from distinguishing between the growth-stunting component (refuted), the calcium-metabolism component (qualified and heavily dose- and context-dependent), and the sleep-disruption component (supported), providing a disaggregated analysis that preserves legitimate concerns while clearly repudiating the unfounded ones. The absence of such analysis in the mainstream fact-checking record leaves the myth with broader cultural freedom to circulate than it arguably deserves, given that at least part of its evidential case can be definitively dismissed.
