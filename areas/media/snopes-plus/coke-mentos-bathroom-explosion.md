# Did a Viral Video Really Show Coke and Mentos Causing a Bathroom Explosion?


## 1. The Claim

In October and December 2020, a short video circulated widely across Facebook, Twitter, and other social media platforms appearing to show a dramatic and catastrophic escalation of the well-known "Coke and Mentos" experiment. In the video's opening moments, an upturned two-liter bottle of Coca-Cola with several Mentos candies attached is placed inside a toilet bowl. The bottle then shoots upward from the toilet, ricochets around the bathroom, and triggers what the video presents as an explosive eruption of sewage and wastewater—surging through the apartment building, flooding multiple floors, and eventually propelling a geyser of toilet water through an exterior window several stories above the street.

The claim embedded in the viral shares was explicit: this was a real incident, a freak accident in which the Mentos effect spiraled far beyond what its creators intended, with calamitous and allegedly hilarious results. Many viewers sharing the video expressed visceral recognition of the Mentos effect as a "real thing" they had personally witnessed or performed, and this familiarity with the underlying phenomenon lent the exaggerated footage a veneer of plausibility. The implicit scientific argument of the viral narrative was: if a small Coke-Mentos interaction can shoot a stream of soda several feet into the air, perhaps a toilet-bowl version could escalate through plumbing infrastructure to catastrophic effect.

The video was shared across ideological and national lines without consistent attribution, making it difficult for many viewers to assess its provenance. At peak circulation, versions of the clip had accumulated hundreds of thousands of views on multiple platforms within a single week.


## 2. What's Actually True

The video is not a documentary record of a real incident. It was created entirely through digital visual effects and animation software by a Russian content creator who operates under the online moniker "Patiffonka," later identified as Sergey Kulishov. Kulishov originally uploaded the video simultaneously on October 27, 2020 to his accounts on Twitter, Reddit, YouTube, and Instagram—the last of which identifies his real name. He made no effort to conceal the video's artificial nature; on the contrary, he discussed the technical methods he used on Reddit in response to curious commenters, explaining that the animation was produced primarily using Houdini and RealFlow, two professional-grade software tools widely used in the film and visual effects industry for simulating fluid dynamics, particle systems, and destruction sequences.

Critically, Kulishov also provided a link to the original source footage he used for the first few seconds of the video—specifically the moment of the Coke bottle initially shooting upward from the toilet bowl. That brief opening clip, which appears to document a genuine household Mentos experiment, was the only segment Kulishov did not animate himself; every subsequent scene of cascading floods, erupting plumbing, and the exterior geyser was entirely computer-generated. The provision of raw footage, the simultaneous multi-platform upload, the creator's track record of producing similar animated absurdities, and his candid technical discussions all converge to confirm that the video was a creative work passed off as real through organic viral spread rather than any deliberate promotional strategy.

The actual Mentos effect—the physical process the video satirically exaggerates—is a genuine and well-studied phenomenon. When Mentos candies are dropped into carbonated beverages, the rough textured surface of the candy shell provides an enormous number of nucleation sites: microscopic surface irregularities and trapped gas pockets where dissolved carbon dioxide can rapidly transition from solution to gas phase. This rapid nucleation releases dissolved CO₂ much faster than the liquid surface alone could, producing the characteristic high-velocity foam column. The effect is real, reproducible, and scientifically interesting, but it is also sharply bounded by the physical quantity of dissolved gas in the beverage and the surface area of the nucleation medium. A standard two-liter bottle of Coke contains roughly 8.2 grams of dissolved CO₂; releasing all of it instantaneously would produce approximately 4.2 liters of gas at atmospheric pressure—nowhere near the energy density required to flood an apartment building or drive a hydraulic geyser through a structural wall.

The Mentos effect is spectacular in proportion to the small domestic scale at which it is performed; it is incapable by any physical mechanism of the catastrophic escalation depicted in Kulishov's animation.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The credibility of Kulishov's animation rests on several interlocking epistemological vulnerabilities that are worth mapping carefully, because they recur across many categories of manipulated viral video.

**Experiential anchoring**: The Mentos effect is uniquely memorable among household demonstrations precisely because it appears to violate ordinary expectations. A child who has watched foam shoot from a Coke bottle experiences a genuine surprise—an apparently domestic object producing a dramatic response with minimal input. This vivid experiential memory creates what cognitive scientists call a "familiarity anchor" for subsequent claims. When viewers encountered the viral video, the opening seconds showing a genuine upward bottle discharge activated this memory, causing the brain to encode the entire sequence under the umbrella of "verified Mentos behavior." The subsequent escalation to flooding was processed not as physics to be evaluated but as an extension of something familiar. This is a known feature of misinformation transmission: true elements embedded in false content dramatically increase credibility ratings (Pennycook & Rand, 2019, *Psychological Science*).

**The plausibility gradient problem**: Human intuitive physics is poorly calibrated for exponential and catastrophic scaling effects. Viewers who correctly understand that a two-liter Coke bottle can generate significant upward force tend to reason by analogy rather than by calculation: if a small bottle causes a spectacular reaction, a larger one in an enclosed space might cause a much larger reaction. This reasoning pattern—valid in some contexts, invalid in others—maps onto a genuine cognitive bias documented in research on risk perception and proportional scaling. Studies by Tversky and Kahneman (1974, *Science*) on representativeness heuristics demonstrate that people systematically overestimate the likelihood of events whose causes seem proportional, in kind if not in degree, to the effect presented.

**Epistemic novelty in viral video authenticity**: The Kulishov video occupies a particularly sharp edge case in the media literacy problem space because it is neither deepfake (no real human faces are manipulated), nor straightforward fabrication (it correctly renders the initial seconds of a genuine demonstration), nor a misrepresentation of a real event (there was no real event to misrepresent). It is instead what might best be called a *physical extrapolation hoax*: a genuine scientific phenomenon is digitally extended far beyond its actual parameters. This category of manipulation is less studied than face-swapping or political deepfakes, but it is arguably more insidious for scientific credibility: it borrows the credibility of a real phenomenon to launder imaginary consequences. Existing media literacy frameworks, which focus heavily on identifying manipulated faces or detecting geolocation inconsistencies, are poorly equipped to address physical extrapolation hoaxes, because the authentication question is not "did this person say X?" but "can X do Y?," a question requiring scientific domain knowledge that most viewers lack.

**Platform incentives for emotional content**: Research by Berger and Milkman (2012, *Journal of Marketing Research*) demonstrated that content eliciting high-arousal positive emotions—particularly awe and amusement—spreads significantly more widely than neutral or low-arousal content. The video generates exactly this response: it is funny, spectacular, and kinetically satisfying in a way that rewards multiple viewings. The emotional reward was decoupled from any concern about authenticity; for most sharers, the truthfulness of the clip was irrelevant to its entertainment value.


## 4. Empirical Record

The chemistry and physics of the Coke-Mentos reaction have been studied with formal rigor. Coffey (2008, *American Journal of Physics*) published a detailed analysis of the mechanism, confirming that the primary driver of foam generation is heterogeneous nucleation rather than any chemical reaction between the candy ingredients and the beverage. The candy shell provides nucleation sites; the CO₂ vaporizes; the gas-rich foam column accelerates upward. Coffey's measurements showed that the rate of foam ejection is principally controlled by the density of nucleation sites and the degree of carbonation in the beverage, placing an upper physical bound on the volume and velocity of ejected material.

Liger-Belair and colleagues (2004, *Langmuir*) had previously conducted high-resolution imaging studies of CO₂ nucleation in carbonated beverages, establishing the microscopic physics of bubble formation and coalescence. Their work illuminates why Mentos are such effective nucleators: scanning electron microscopy reveals that the candy shell consists of a densely packed polycrystalline matrix with an enormous surface-to-volume ratio, its surface populated with tiny cellulose fibres that trap air pockets from manufacture—ideal nucleation seeds. Liger-Belair et al. estimated nucleation site densities on Mentos surfaces in the range of 10⁴–10⁵ per square centimeter, orders of magnitude greater than the smooth interior surface of a glass bottle.

Denny (2017, *American Journal of Physics*) further formalized the fluid mechanics of the mentos experiment as a teaching problem in applied hydrodynamics, confirming that peak volumetric flow rates during the foam eruption are consistent with the modest energy stored in dissolved gas rather than any combustion or pressurizaiton analogy.

None of these foundational studies—nor any subsequent physical modeling—supports the energy density required for the explosive structural failures depicted in Kulishov's video. The exaggeration is not marginal; it is many orders of magnitude outside any physical plausibility window.

From a media forensics standpoint, Kulishov's disclosure of his methods on Reddit serves as collateral authentication. His description of using Houdini (a node-based 3D simulation and animation package widely used in film visual effects) and RealFlow (a specialized fluid simulation tool) is consistent with the visual appearance of the animated fluid dynamics in the video. Fluid simulations in these tools characteristically produce the smooth, energetic, somewhat stylized water behavior visible in the video's later scenes—behavior that real wastewater and building plumbing would not produce—but which is only apparent to viewers familiar with high-end CGI rendering.


## 5. Gaps and Open Questions

Several questions remain genuinely open at the intersection of the Mentos effect, plumbing hydraulics, and viral video authenticity:

First, while the physical bounds on the Mentos reaction in an open system are well established, the behavior of a carbonation-driven foam burst within a sealed or partially-sealed plumbing system has received less formal study. Residential toilet trap seals and waste lines are not designed to be pressure vessels, and a sufficiently forceful gas discharge in a sealed drain stack could theoretically propagate pressure waves through a plumbing system, though far short of anything approaching structural damage. This edge case has not been formally modeled.

Second, the media ecosystem dynamics of physical extrapolation hoaxes remain understudied. The majority of deepfake research focuses on face manipulation and political speech (Chesney & Citron, 2019, *Foreign Affairs*); content that extrapolates real physical phenomena into false disasters occupies a gap in both academic literature and platform content moderation policy. There are no established detection methodologies comparable to those developed for face-swap deepfakes.

Third, the role of platform content moderation in the spread of demonstrably false physics-based viral content is unclear. The Kulishov video remained accessible and widely shared across platforms even after fact-checking debunks were published, suggesting that existing misinformation labeling systems that target false political claims may not be calibrated to address entertaining-but-scientifically-false entertainment content.


## 6. The Takeaway

The Coke-and-Mentos bathroom explosion video is false: a technically accomplished digital animation created by a Russian entertainer, which spread virally because its opening seconds depicted a genuine phenomenon and because the subsequent absurdist escalation was entertaining enough that numerous viewers chose to share it without concern for its truth value.

The Mentos effect is chemically and physically real—a product of nucleation chemistry that has been rigorously characterized in peer-reviewed physics literature—but it is bounded by the quantity of dissolved gas in the beverage and is incapable of causing plumbing system explosions, structural flooding, or high-velocity exterior geysers. The gap between the reaction's real capabilities and the video's depiction is not ambiguous or contested; it is physically decisive.

The broader lesson is about a specific category of convincing false content: physical extrapolation hoaxes that borrow the credibility of a genuine scientific phenomenon to lend plausibility to impossible consequences. Defending against this form of viral misinformation requires not just media literacy about source verification but a baseline literacy in physical scale and scientific constraint—the kind of quantitative intuition that allows a viewer to ask not just "is this real?" but "could this even be real?" That second question, grounded in actual physics rather than familiarity, would have immediately resolved the puzzle the video posed.
