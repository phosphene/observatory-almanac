## The Claim

Going out in the cold — or sitting in a draft, or getting your feet wet, or failing to wear a coat — will give you a cold. It is one of the oldest and most universally transmitted pieces of parental advice in the world. The name of the illness encodes the belief: "cold." The folk wisdom is so ingrained that adults who should know better still feel the pull of it: come inside out of the cold, you will catch something.

The standard scientific debunking has been crisp and dismissive: viruses cause colds. Rhinoviruses are responsible for roughly 30 to 50 percent of common cold cases, coronaviruses (other than SARS-CoV-2) for another 10 to 15 percent, with adenoviruses, enteroviruses, and parainfluenza viruses accounting for the rest. You cannot get a cold from cold temperatures alone. You can only get a cold from a virus.

This is correct as far as it goes. But recent research has substantially complicated the simple dismissal, revealing that cold temperatures and cold viruses interact in ways that make "cold weather gives you colds" a much more accurate folk heuristic than the debunking narrative allowed. The mechanism is wrong. The directional prediction is surprisingly right.

## What's Actually True

**Viruses cause colds. This part is settled.** No virus, no cold, regardless of how cold you get. Cold exposure experiments conducted in the 1950s and 1960s — most famously at the Common Cold Research Unit in Salisbury, England — confirmed that subjects who were chilled but not exposed to cold viruses did not develop colds, while subjects who were exposed to cold viruses but kept warm did. Temperature alone does not produce a cold.

**Cold weather nevertheless genuinely increases your likelihood of getting a cold**, and the mechanisms by which it does so are multiple, overlapping, and now much better understood than they were twenty years ago.

**The rhinovirus temperature preference is real.** A landmark 2015 study by Foxman and colleagues, published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, provided the first molecular explanation for why rhinoviruses thrive in nasal passages and struggle to replicate in the warmer environment of the lungs. The nasal passages, which are directly exposed to ambient air, typically run at around 33 to 35 degrees Celsius — several degrees cooler than core body temperature at 37 degrees Celsius. Rhinovirus replicates more efficiently and produces more viral particles at 33 degrees than at 37 degrees. Crucially, the antiviral innate immune response in nasal epithelial cells is also temperature-dependent: cells at 37 degrees mount a more robust interferon response to rhinovirus infection than cells at 33 degrees. When ambient temperatures drop, nasal cavity temperature drops further, creating conditions that simultaneously favor viral replication and impair the innate immune response mounted against it.

This means that breathing cold air does not give you a cold — you still need the virus — but it does shift the competitive balance between virus and immune defense in favor of the virus. Cold weather is not a cause of colds but a vulnerability amplifier that operates through a well-characterized molecular mechanism.

**Cold exposure and mucosal immune function.** Mourtzoukou and Falagas, reviewing the evidence in a 2007 paper in the *International Journal of Tuberculosis and Lung Disease*, found consistent evidence that cold exposure modulates immune function in ways relevant to respiratory infection susceptibility. Cold temperatures reduce ciliary function in the respiratory tract — the cilia that sweep mucus and trapped particles out of the airways operate less effectively at lower temperatures, allowing viruses to persist longer at the mucosal surface. Cold also induces peripheral vasoconstriction, which reduces blood flow to mucosal surfaces and may impair immune cell delivery to the site of infection.

**The behavioral transmission pathway** is perhaps the most powerful mechanistic link between cold weather and cold incidence. When temperatures drop, people move indoors. They spend more time in enclosed, poorly ventilated spaces — schools, offices, homes — in closer proximity to other people. This dramatically increases the likelihood of exposure to respiratory viruses. Heating systems that recirculate dry air further reduce mucosal hydration, affecting the mucus layer that traps virions and the humidity conditions under which virus particles survive. Cold, dry air favors the aerosolization and persistence of respiratory virus particles compared to warm, humid air.

A study by Shaman and Kahn (2008, *PLoS Pathogens*) found that absolute humidity — not temperature per se — was the strongest predictor of influenza transmission, with low-humidity conditions characteristic of cold winter air dramatically increasing viral survival and transmission efficiency. The same physics likely applies to rhinoviruses and other cold-causing pathogens. Indoor heating that produces warm but very dry air may create near-optimal conditions for respiratory virus survival and spread while paradoxically being perceived as warm and protective.

**Vitamin D as a seasonal immune factor.** Reduced sunlight exposure in winter months leads to lower endogenous vitamin D production. Vitamin D plays established roles in innate and adaptive immune function, and deficiency is associated with increased susceptibility to respiratory infections. Multiple meta-analyses have found that vitamin D supplementation reduces the incidence of acute respiratory tract infections, particularly in individuals with low baseline vitamin D levels. This represents another pathway through which winter conditions — rather than cold temperature directly — increase cold susceptibility through a genuine immunological mechanism.

**The full picture** is that cold weather does not cause colds, but it creates a cluster of conditions that together substantially increase risk: impaired nasal antiviral defenses through direct temperature effects on interferon signaling; enhanced rhinovirus replication efficiency at nasal cavity temperatures; reduced ciliary clearance and mucosal protection; behavioral crowding into high-transmission indoor environments; reduced vitamin D status; and air humidity conditions that favor virus persistence and aerosolization. The folk belief "cold weather gives you colds" is a rough but remarkably accurate heuristic for this complex causal cluster. The mechanism attributed in folk belief — direct harm from cold exposure — is wrong. The directional prediction is right.

## Why People Believe This

The folk belief about cold weather and colds has survived so long for reasons that are more than simple ignorance. It encodes several real observations about the world that were available long before the virology was understood.

**The seasonal correlation is obvious.** Colds are more common in winter. This is true across virtually all temperate climates and has been recorded across centuries of medical observation. Before virology existed as a science, this pattern was real and undeniable. The association between cold and colds is not a mistake — it is a valid observation of a genuine epidemiological pattern. The mistake is in the causal inference, not in the observation.

**Temporal proximity creates causal narrative.** Getting cold and then getting sick within a day or two creates a compelling temporal sequence that cognitive psychology has documented as the dominant heuristic for causal attribution. Humans are wired to infer causation from temporal proximity, especially when the correlation is reliable (colds really are more frequent in winter) and the mechanism seems obvious (cold disrupts the body's equilibrium). The folk belief is cognitively sticky because it matches a real pattern and a plausible narrative.

**Partial biological truth reinforces the narrative.** Before the Foxman (2015) research, the standard dismissal of the cold-weather-causes-colds belief treated it as purely correlational. But as we now know, getting cold does affect nasal immune function, does reduce ciliary clearance, and does create a more hospitable nasal environment for rhinovirus replication. The folk belief was not entirely wrong about the mechanism — it correctly identified that cold exposure affects the body's vulnerability to respiratory illness. It was wrong to omit the virus from the story, but it was not wrong that cold exposure matters.

**The Salisbury experiments were inconclusive in a subtler sense.** The Common Cold Unit experiments that supposedly disproved the cold-causes-colds belief had methodological limitations that are instructive. They tested whether extreme cold exposure caused colds in virus-free subjects — it did not. But they did not definitively test whether cold exposure increased susceptibility among subjects exposed to cold viruses under realistic transmission conditions. The experiments were interpreted as a clean dismissal, but the question they actually answered was narrower.

**Cultural universality entrenches the belief.** Across enormously different cultures, from medieval Europe to traditional Chinese medicine to indigenous North American traditions, advice about protecting yourself from cold air to avoid respiratory illness appears with remarkable consistency. Cultural universality is not proof of truth — many universally held beliefs are false — but it is evidence that the belief is tracking something real in human experience, even if the causal model is incomplete.

## Verdict

**The claim that cold weather directly causes colds is false** in the strict causal sense. Cold temperature alone, without viral exposure, does not produce a cold. This is established by experimental evidence going back decades and is not in serious dispute among virologists or immunologists.

**The claim that cold weather increases your risk of getting a cold is true**, through a now well-characterized set of mechanisms: rhinovirus replication is more efficient at nasal cavity temperatures; interferon antiviral response is reduced at cooler nasal temperatures; ciliary clearance is impaired; behavioral crowding into poorly ventilated indoor spaces increases exposure; and dry winter air favors viral survival and transmission.

**The folk advice** — wear your coat, come inside from the cold, protect yourself in winter — is not scientifically wrong as a practical risk management strategy, even though the folk explanation of why it works is mechanistically incorrect. Managing cold exposure does reduce your risk of getting a cold, not because cold alone causes illness, but because it modifies several of the biological conditions that determine your susceptibility and exposure when you encounter cold viruses.

**EARC Mode: A — Accurate directional observation, distorted causal account.** The folk belief correctly predicts that cold weather is associated with higher cold risk. It incorrectly identifies direct cold exposure as the cause while omitting the virus and the complex immune-behavioral-environmental mechanisms through which weather affects susceptibility.

## The Wider Picture

The cold-weather-causes-colds story is scientifically interesting because it is one of the cases where folk medicine was tracking a real signal — just with the wrong model for why the signal existed.

**The Foxman (2015) PNAS paper** represents a genuine advance in understanding, not merely a confirmation of common sense. The molecular mechanism it identified — temperature-dependent interferon signaling in nasal epithelial cells, combined with temperature-dependent rhinovirus replication efficiency — was not obvious from prior research and required sophisticated cell culture and mouse model experiments to demonstrate. The paper helped reconcile decades of apparent contradiction between the folk belief (cold weather causes colds) and the experimental dismissal (cold alone doesn't cause colds without virus) by showing that both are capturing something real, just at different levels of analysis.

**The humidity physics** is a reminder that common cultural concepts like "cold weather" are bundles of correlated variables rather than single factors. Cold weather means lower absolute humidity, reduced sunlight, behavioral changes, vitamin D changes, indoor crowding, and changed air circulation patterns. Attributing outcomes to "cold weather" as a single cause conflates all of these, and disentangling them requires holding many variables constant simultaneously — something everyday experience cannot do but epidemiological studies are designed to attempt.

**Rhinovirus seasonality in tropical climates** provides an interesting test case. In tropical regions without marked seasonal temperature variation, rhinovirus and other cold-causing viruses still show seasonal variation, but the pattern differs from temperate climates and is more strongly associated with rainy-season humidity and crowding changes than with temperature. This suggests that the behavioral crowding pathway — the most culturally mediated of the mechanisms — operates relatively independently of the temperature-immune pathway and can produce seasonality even without cold weather.

**SARS-CoV-2 and COVID-19** added a new chapter to this story. The pandemic provided natural experiments about how seasonal patterns in respiratory virus transmission interact with behavioral factors, ventilation, and population immunity at a scale never previously observed. The dramatic variation in COVID-19 incidence across seasons and climates, often consistent with the humidity-and-crowding model, has reinvigorated research into environmental determinants of respiratory virus transmission that can inform both cold-prevention and pandemic-prevention strategies.

**For the individual**, the practical implications of the updated science are: increased vigilance about cold virus exposure during winter months is genuinely warranted; indoor air quality and ventilation matter; nasal passage warming (such as wearing a mask in cold air) has a theoretical basis for reducing rhinovirus replication efficiency; and vitamin D sufficiency throughout winter months is a reasonable precaution with evidence support. These recommendations are more mechanistically sophisticated than "wear your coat," but they are not entirely different in spirit from the folk advice that humans have been giving each other for centuries.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The cold-weather-causes-colds claim is a case where the categorical verdict is technically correct but the fuller story is more interesting and more useful.

**The categorical response is: False** in the strict causal sense. Cold weather does not cause colds. This is accurate and defensible. Fact-checkers who rate this False are not wrong.

**The limitation of the flat False verdict** is that it invites a dismissive conclusion — cold weather has nothing to do with colds — that the Foxman (2015) research has substantially undermined. Telling someone "cold weather has nothing to do with your cold risk" is now scientifically inaccurate, even though the folk mechanism (direct cold exposure causes illness) is wrong.

**The better fact-check structure** acknowledges: (1) Viruses cause colds, and cold temperature alone cannot produce a cold; (2) Cold weather is associated with higher cold incidence through multiple genuine mechanisms including temperature effects on nasal immune function, behavioral crowding, and reduced humidity; (3) The practical folk advice to protect yourself from cold in winter has a real basis in risk reduction, even though the causal story behind it is incomplete.

**Fact-checkers should distinguish** between the strong causal claim ("cold weather causes colds") — which is false — and the weaker but accurate claim ("cold weather conditions increase your risk of catching a cold") — which is true. The difference matters for public communication: dismissing the folk belief entirely may lead people to underestimate genuinely relevant cold-season risk factors.

**Primary sources worth consulting:** Foxman et al. (2015) in *PNAS* for the temperature-immune mechanism; Shaman and Kahn (2008) in *PLoS Pathogens* for the humidity-transmission relationship; Mourtzoukou and Falagas (2007) for the broader review of cold exposure and immunity. These are accessible papers that directly address the question at a mechanistic level and provide the evidence base for the updated scientific picture that the simple "False" verdict misses.
