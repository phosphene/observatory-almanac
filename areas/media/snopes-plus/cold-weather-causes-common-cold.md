# Cold weather causes colds and flu

## 1. The Claim

Go out in the cold without a coat and you'll catch a cold. Get your hair wet in winter and you'll come down with something. Stand near an air conditioning vent too long and you'll be reaching for the tissues. The association between cold weather and respiratory illness is one of the oldest and most universally held beliefs in folk medicine. Parents in virtually every culture with cold winters transmit it to children. The language we use reflects it: we call respiratory illness a "cold," we say someone has "caught a chill," we describe illness as something that came from the weather rather than from another person.

At the core of this belief is a causal claim: cold temperatures make people sick. When the weather turns cold, respiratory illness increases, and the cold itself is held responsible. This claim exists in multiple forms, from the naive version (going outside in cold weather causes illness) to more sophisticated folk theories (cold temperatures lower immune function, making people more vulnerable to what's already in their environment). The belief is so widespread that it generates genuine friction in families, offices, and public health discourse—people who believe in the cold-causes-cold connection make decisions about outdoor activity, clothing, and personal behavior based on it.

The scientific consensus has long held that colds and flu are caused by viruses, not temperatures. Rhinovirus causes most common colds. Influenza A and B viruses cause flu. These viruses are transmitted from person to person through respiratory droplets, aerosols, and contact with contaminated surfaces. You cannot get a cold from a cold draft if no virus is present. The mechanism the folk belief implies—cold temperature acting directly on the body to produce illness—is not supported.

And yet, respiratory illness does increase dramatically in winter. The correlation between cold weather and more colds is real and robust. The question is whether the causal chain is as simple as the folk belief implies, or whether cold weather facilitates viral illness through more indirect but still genuine mechanisms. The answer is more interesting than a simple "false."

## 2. What's Actually True

**Viruses, not temperatures, cause colds and flu.** This remains the foundational truth against which the popular belief must be assessed. The germ theory of disease, established in the 19th century and refined throughout the 20th, is not in dispute. Respiratory illness requires a pathogen—primarily rhinoviruses (>100 serotypes), coronaviruses, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), adenoviruses, and influenza viruses. One cannot develop a cold without viral exposure. Experiments designed to test the direct cold-causes-cold hypothesis—in which subjects are exposed to cold temperatures without viral exposure—consistently fail to produce illness. This particular claim at the folk theory's core is false.

**But the correlation between cold weather and respiratory illness has real mechanistic grounding.** Several independently plausible mechanisms explain why winter produces more respiratory illness, and the evidence for each has strengthened over the past two decades.

**Behavioral crowding.** The most straightforward mechanism is that cold weather drives people indoors, into enclosed spaces with recirculated air and closer proximity to other people. School children who were previously playing outdoors return to classrooms. Adults spend more time in offices, mass transit, and enclosed public spaces. Holiday gatherings bring extended family members, including children from different school cohorts, into prolonged close contact. More indoor proximity means more viral exposure opportunities, regardless of any direct immunological effect of cold temperature. This is the mechanism with the clearest evidence and the most explanatory power for seasonal respiratory illness patterns.

**Nasal temperature and viral replication.** A 2015 study by Foxman, Storer, Fitzgerald, Wasik, Hou, Bhatt, Bhatt, Bhatt, Thomas, and Bhatt—published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*—provided direct experimental evidence for a temperature-dependent mechanism in viral replication. The study examined how rhinovirus replicates in human airway cells at different temperatures: 37°C (core body temperature) versus 33°C (approximately the temperature inside the nasal cavity when breathing cold air). Rhinovirus replicated significantly more efficiently at 33°C. Additionally, the innate immune response of nasal cells was more robust at 37°C than at 33°C—cells exposed to rhinovirus at the warmer temperature mounted a stronger interferon response. The combination—more viral replication and weaker immune defense at colder nasal temperatures—provides a plausible direct mechanism for why cold air inhalation might facilitate rhinoviral infection. This is not the folk belief's mechanism (cold makes you sick directly) but it is a genuine biological pathway through which cold air contributes to infection susceptibility.

**Rhinologist Ronald Eccles and cold-induced susceptibility.** Ronald Eccles, a rhinologist at Cardiff University's Common Cold Centre, conducted research and published commentary exploring the relationship between chilling and cold susceptibility. In a 2002 review in *Rhinology* and related work, Eccles argued that chilling the body—particularly the feet—can trigger vasoconstriction in nasal mucosa as a reflex response, reducing blood flow to the nasal lining and potentially impairing mucosal immune function. A key study involved exposing subjects to chilling of their feet and found that those chilled were more likely to develop cold symptoms in the following days compared to unchilled controls—presumably because a subclinical viral infection they were already carrying was able to progress to symptomatic illness when immune defense in the nasal mucosa was compromised. This finding remains somewhat contested but points toward the possibility that cold exposure can convert subclinical infection to symptomatic illness even without increasing initial viral transmission.

**Absolute humidity and airborne viral survival.** Research by Shaman and Kahn (2009) in *PLoS Biology*, and subsequent work, demonstrated that absolute humidity is a key determinant of influenza virus survival in aerosols. At low absolute humidity—characteristic of cold, dry winter air—influenza viruses in aerosols remain viable longer than at higher absolute humidity. Cold winter air holds less water vapor; when this air is heated indoors, relative humidity drops further. The resulting low-humidity indoor environments in winter allow viral aerosols to persist longer and travel further before settling or degrading, increasing the likelihood that an exhaled viral particle will reach another person's respiratory tract. This mechanism applies particularly to influenza and other respiratory viruses transmitted as aerosols or droplets. It offers another genuine pathway through which cold weather conditions—via low humidity—facilitate transmission.

**Vitamin D deficiency hypothesis.** An additional proposed mechanism involves vitamin D. Sunlight exposure drives vitamin D synthesis in skin, and vitamin D plays a role in immune function including antiviral innate immunity. Winter in temperate latitudes means less sunlight exposure and declining vitamin D levels. The vitamin D deficiency hypothesis proposes that lower vitamin D in winter contributes to greater infection susceptibility. This mechanism is plausible and has observational support (lower vitamin D levels are associated with more respiratory infections), but controlled supplementation trials have not consistently shown that vitamin D supplementation prevents respiratory illness in generally healthy adults, tempering enthusiasm for this as a primary explanatory mechanism. It may contribute additively to the seasonal pattern without being the dominant driver.

**What the experimental challenge studies showed.** Classic experiments in which subjects were deliberately exposed to cold temperatures and then monitored for cold development—without deliberately introducing virus—generally failed to produce elevated rates of illness. These studies have been cited extensively to argue that cold weather doesn't cause colds. They are correct in demonstrating the absence of direct cold-to-illness causation. But they cannot exclude that cold weather facilitates illness when virus is present, through the mechanisms described above. The challenge study design (controlled exposure without virus) tests the wrong hypothesis for the more nuanced mechanistic claims.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The cold-causes-cold belief is one of the most ancient and cross-culturally consistent in folk medicine, and its persistence reflects a genuine correlation, plausible intuitive mechanisms, and the nature of human causal attribution under conditions of uncertainty.

**The correlation is real.** Respiratory illness does increase in winter. This is not a cognitive illusion—it is an empirical fact. The folk belief captures a genuine pattern in the world. The error is not in observing that pattern but in incorrectly inferring direct causation from cold temperature to illness when the actual causal pathway is more indirect. Folk theories often track genuine correlations even when they misidentify mechanisms. The cold-causes-cold belief is a paradigm case: the pattern is right, the mechanism is wrong, but the pattern is strong enough that the belief survives despite the mechanistic error.

**Temporal coincidence and attribution.** When someone goes out in cold weather, then develops cold symptoms 2-4 days later (the typical rhinovirus incubation period), the temporal sequence is compelling evidence for causation to an untrained observer. The viral exposure that actually caused the illness almost certainly occurred within 24-48 hours before symptom onset, often from an infected family member or colleague in an enclosed space. But the memorable event that preceded illness is the cold weather exposure, not the invisible viral transmission. Human causal reasoning is heavily biased toward temporally proximate, visible, and memorable events—cold weather qualifies on all dimensions; invisible viral transmission does not.

**The pre-germ-theory medical framework.** For most of human history, the germ theory of disease simply did not exist. Pre-germ-theory medicine had to explain disease patterns with available conceptual tools, and environmental exposure—including exposure to cold, wet, and damp—was a natural candidate for explaining respiratory illness that increased with winter weather. Miasma theory, humoral medicine, and various folk frameworks all incorporated environmental cold as a cause of illness. This explanatory tradition is extraordinarily ancient, cross-cultural, and deeply embedded in language, ritual, and medical practice. The germ theory is a scientific achievement of the 19th century; the cold-causes-cold belief is a folk theory with thousands of years of cultural momentum behind it.

**Partial mechanistic validity.** Unlike many folk beliefs where the mechanism is entirely wrong, the cold-causes-cold belief has partial mechanistic validity: cold does affect nasal temperature and immune function in ways that can facilitate infection. Parents who insist children dress warmly in cold weather are not entirely wrong. The advice to dress warmly is partially justified—not because cold air directly causes illness, but because cold exposure may impair nasal mucosal immunity and facilitate progression of subclinical infections to symptomatic illness. The folk belief is wrong about direct causation but points toward a real, if more complicated, mechanism. Beliefs with partial mechanistic validity are particularly resistant to correction because selective experiences can always find confirmation.

**Maternal transmission and authority.** The belief is transmitted by parents to children in contexts of care, concern, and authority. These transmission contexts give the belief psychological weight that is difficult to dislodge through abstract information. When a child who went out without a coat gets sick, the parental explanation ("I told you so—you caught a cold") is emotionally powerful and situationally immediate in ways that a public health communication about airborne viral transmission is not.

## 4. Verdict

**EMPIRICALLY GROUNDED BUT MECHANISTICALLY DISTORTED.** Cold weather does not directly cause colds or flu—viruses do, and cold exposure without viral exposure does not produce illness. The causal claim at the core of the folk belief is false. However, cold weather genuinely facilitates respiratory viral transmission and susceptibility through multiple documented mechanisms: behavioral crowding indoors, cold-air effects on nasal temperature and viral replication efficiency, low absolute humidity prolonging viral survival in aerosols, and possibly cold-induced vasoconstriction impairing nasal mucosal immunity. The winter spike in respiratory illness is real, and cold weather's contribution to it through these indirect pathways is real. The folk belief gets the correlation right and the mechanism wrong—not a negligible error, but a belief with more genuine grounding than many myths.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The cold-weather-causes-colds question illuminates several important themes in public health communication and folk medicine.

**The gap between mechanism and correlation in popular health understanding.** Most people do not have the conceptual tools to distinguish "cold weather is associated with more colds" from "cold weather causes colds." The correlation is the observed reality; the causal mechanism requires knowledge of virology, epidemiology, and immunology that is not part of general education. This gap is not a failure of individual intelligence—it is a gap between what everyday observation can reveal and what scientific methodology is required to establish. Folk beliefs fill this gap with available conceptual tools, and when the available tools predate germ theory, they reliably produce miasmatic or environmental explanations.

**Seasonal respiratory illness and public health preparedness.** The mechanistic understanding of why winter produces respiratory illness spikes is not merely academic—it has direct implications for public health intervention. If behavioral crowding is a key driver, ventilation and air filtration in schools and public buildings become primary targets. If low absolute humidity is a significant factor for influenza transmission, humidifying indoor environments in winter becomes a plausible intervention (there is preliminary evidence supporting this). If cold-induced immunosuppression in nasal mucosa matters, strategies for warming inspired air in vulnerable populations become relevant. Understanding the actual mechanisms opens up intervention strategies that the folk belief's direct-causation model would not suggest.

**Rhinovirus and the expanding scientific picture.** The Foxman et al. (2015) *PNAS* study provided a mechanistic account of temperature-dependent viral replication and immune response that partially rehabilitated the folk belief. The research showed that the relationship between cold temperatures and rhinoviral infection is not simply correlational but involves a genuine biological mechanism—just not the one the folk belief implies. This kind of finding represents the productive interaction between folk observation and scientific investigation at its best: the folk belief identified a real pattern, and science has gradually elucidated the actual mechanisms responsible for it.

**Influenza and humidity: practical seasonal implications.** The humidity-influenza relationship is particularly significant given the global burden of influenza. Influenza causes hundreds of thousands of deaths annually and economic losses in the billions. The finding that low absolute humidity promotes influenza transmission has generated interest in humidity control as a public health intervention. Research by Noti et al. (2013) in *PLOS Pathogens* examined influenza virus survival and transmission in relation to humidity and found that raising relative humidity significantly reduced viral transmission in simulated environments. If maintaining higher indoor humidity during winter months reduces influenza transmission, the practical implication is significant—and it is one that would never have been identified if the folk belief had simply been dismissed as entirely wrong rather than investigated for genuine mechanisms.

**The COVID-19 pandemic and seasonal respiratory illness.** The emergence of SARS-CoV-2 brought renewed public attention to the mechanisms of respiratory viral transmission, including the role of aerosols, humidity, and indoor versus outdoor exposure. Evidence accumulated rapidly that outdoor transmission was substantially lower than indoor transmission—consistent with the behavioral crowding mechanism underlying seasonal respiratory illness patterns. The public discussion of ventilation, filtration, and humidity as mitigation strategies during COVID-19 essentially popularized the mechanistic understanding of why winter is a high-transmission season for respiratory viruses. In this sense, the pandemic may have done more to educate the public about actual respiratory transmission mechanisms than decades of prior public health communication.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The "cold weather causes colds" claim is a recurring target for fact-checkers and health communication outlets, particularly at the start of cold and flu season each year. Snopes, while not addressing this specific formulation directly, has covered related claims about cold exposure and illness susceptibility. *Scientific American*, *WebMD*, *Harvard Health*, and the NIH's MedlinePlus have all published accessible explanations noting that viruses, not cold temperatures, cause illness.

The standard fact-checking treatment of this claim falls into a relatively simple "False/Mostly False" category: cold temperatures don't cause colds, viruses do. This verdict is accurate as far as it goes but often elides the genuine mechanistic connections between cold weather and respiratory illness rates. More sophisticated health journalism—found in outlets like *Vox*, *The Atlantic's* health coverage, and *The Washington Post*'s health section—has engaged with the more nuanced picture, acknowledging both the mechanistic falsity of the folk belief and the genuine mechanisms connecting cold weather to higher infection rates.

The communicative challenge is familiar to science communicators working in this space: a simple "false" verdict is incomplete in ways that may backfire. If audiences learn that cold weather doesn't cause colds, they may conclude that cold-weather behaviors (avoiding crowds, dressing warmly, humidifying indoor spaces) are entirely irrelevant to respiratory health—an overcorrection that ignores genuine mechanistic connections. The better message—cold doesn't directly cause illness, but cold weather creates conditions that facilitate viral transmission through several real mechanisms, so prudent winter health behavior is still warranted—is more accurate and more actionable, but takes more space and nuance to convey than most brief fact-check formats allow.

Public health agencies including the CDC and WHO have updated their public communications to reflect the viral etiology of respiratory illness clearly while noting the genuine seasonal patterns and their behavioral and environmental contributors. The CDC's seasonal flu information explicitly notes the behavioral crowding mechanism and does not claim cold weather is irrelevant to disease patterns—a sophisticated, accurate position that acknowledges the genuine winter effect while correctly attributing it to viral exposure rather than temperature directly.
