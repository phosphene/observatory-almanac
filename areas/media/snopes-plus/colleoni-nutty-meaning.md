# The name of the Renaissance mercenary Bartolomeo Colleoni has a vulgar meaning


## 1. The Claim

Bartolomeo Colleoni (c. 1400–1475) was one of the most celebrated condottieri — mercenary military commanders — of fifteenth-century Italy. He served Venice for most of his adult life, commanded its armies in the complex warfare of the Italian peninsula, and died immensely wealthy, leaving his fortune partially to Venice on condition that a monument be erected in his honour. The result was Andrea del Verrocchio's bronze equestrian statue, now standing in the Campo Santi Giovanni e Paolo in Venice, which is widely considered one of the greatest equestrian sculptures in Western art.

The claim that circulates around Colleoni — in art history classrooms, Renaissance history surveys, and the kind of footnote that makes lecturers smile — is that his surname is a bawdy one. Specifically, that "Colleoni" derives from or is a form of "coglioni," the Italian (and dialectal Bergamasque) word for testicles, giving the famous condottiere a name that translates roughly as "the big-balled one" or, more literally, "testicle man" — with the associated secondary meaning in Italian of a simpleton or oaf, though this secondary usage was not the primary resonance in the fifteenth century.

The companion to this claim — sometimes treated as a separate fact, sometimes bundled with the name claim — is that Colleoni's coat of arms featured testicles. This heraldic element is treated as either evidence that Colleoni himself knew and embraced the anatomical reading of his name, or as the origin of a folk interpretation that gave the name its vulgar gloss. This companion claim is addressed in a separate Truth Vault entry on the condottiere's coat of arms.

This entry focuses on the etymological and onomastic question: does "Colleoni" actually mean what the claim says it means, and what do we know about how the name was understood in Colleoni's own time?


## 2. What's Actually True

**The etymology.** The surname Colleoni is a northern Italian surname of Bergamasque origin — Bartolomeo was born near Bergamo in Lombardy, in the hamlet of Solza. The name belongs to a family of related Italian words. The standard modern Italian word is *coglione* (plural *coglioni*), derived from the Latin *coleus* or *coleos*, itself from Greek *koleos* meaning a sheath or container — the specific anatomical referent being the scrotal sac. The word appears in medieval and Renaissance Italian with consistent anatomical meaning. In modern Italian, *coglione* retains the anatomical meaning and has acquired the secondary figurative meaning of a stupid or gullible person — roughly equivalent to "balls" in English when used as an insult, or "dickhead" in its more contemptuous register.

The surname Colleoni, in the northern Italian dialectal tradition from which it emerges, is a patronymic or nickname form derived from this root. The Bergamasque dialect has its own phonological features, and the transformation from *coglioni* to *Colleoni* follows standard northern Italian consonant softening patterns. Whether the original bearers of the name acquired it as a ribald nickname, an occupational term of some kind, a reference to a personal characteristic, or through some other route is not documented, but the linguistic connection between the surname and the anatomical noun is not in doubt among Italian etymologists.

**Contemporary awareness.** The connection between Colleoni's name and its vulgar meaning was not invisible to his contemporaries. Renaissance humanists and court correspondents were attentive to wordplay, nomenclature, and the resonances of names, and they were considerably less squeamish about anatomical language than post-Victorian European culture became. Fifteenth-century Italian literary culture — the world of Boccaccio's Decameron, of ribald carnival verse, of the novella tradition — found anatomical material perfectly suitable for literary treatment, and public figures with suggestive names did not escape notice. Contemporary documents and correspondence occasionally play on Colleoni's name, though the full extent of such commentary requires specialist archival work to gather.

**The coat of arms.** The most dramatic evidence that Colleoni himself was aware of his name's anatomical valence is his coat of arms. The Colleoni family arms feature three pairs of testicles — coglioni — arranged in heraldic fashion. This is a standard type of heraldic design called a *canting* or *armes parlantes* (speaking arms), in which the visual elements of the arms correspond to or are a pun on the bearer's name. Canting arms were extremely common in medieval and Renaissance heraldry across Europe: the Arundel family bore swallows (arundel being a Norman-French word for swallow); the Shelley family bore shells; and so on through hundreds of examples. Colleoni's arms follow this tradition perfectly, with the testicle motif speaking directly to the name. This design could not have been chosen without Colleoni's knowledge and approval — a condottiere of his wealth and status controlled his heraldic presentation carefully — and it suggests that he actively embraced rather than concealed the anatomical meaning of his name.

**Verrocchio's statue and the art historical tradition.** When Verrocchio's equestrian statue of Colleoni was unveiled in Venice, it attracted immediate and long-lasting comment. The statue is extraordinary by any measure: the figure's aggressive contrapposto, the coiled energy of both horse and rider, the expression of ferocious determination on the condottiere's face — all these elements made it a landmark of Renaissance bronze casting. And among the commentary that surrounded the statue, the name's meaning was not suppressed. Renaissance and early modern writers discussing the statue occasionally noted the pun; in the educated humanist culture around art patronage, such observations were considered wit rather than vulgarity.

The heraldic testicles appear on Colleoni's monument in the Cappella Colleoni in Bergamo — an elaborate funerary chapel he commissioned that rivals Venetian ducal monuments in its ambition and expense. The chapel, designed by Giovanni Antonio Amadeo begin in 1470, displays the Colleoni arms prominently and repeatedly. The testicle imagery was integrated into one of the most significant architectural commissions of the Quattrocento, in a sacred space designed for Colleoni's eternal commemoration. This is not the behaviour of a man embarrassed by his name's meaning.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The claim presents no significant epistemological problem for belief: it is straightforwardly true, attested in etymology, heraldry, and the art historical record, and its truth is the reason it circulates. The more interesting psychological and sociological question is why the claim continues to circulate with such energy through popular and educational accounts of Renaissance history.

The first answer is that it is genuinely funny in a register that educated audiences enjoy. The juxtaposition of the grandeur of Renaissance patronage — Verrocchio's masterwork, the Cappella Colleoni, the solemn equestrian monument — with the vulgar meaning of the subject's name creates a kind of comic deflation that satisfies several audiences simultaneously. For those who find Renaissance self-presentation pompous, the name punctures it. For those who love the Renaissance, the association of high art with earthy humanity adds texture and charm. The claim works as both comedy and humanising detail.

The second answer is that art history and Renaissance history are domains where specialist knowledge is genuinely hard to acquire, and where interesting contextual details that illuminate works the audience already knows tend to travel well. Verrocchio's Colleoni statue appears in virtually every survey of Renaissance sculpture. A memorable fact about the subject enhances the experience of engaging with an already-known work. Students, tour guides, podcast hosts, and authors of popular history have an incentive to include it.

The third answer is that the claim is repeatable without losing accuracy with repetition. Many interesting historical claims degrade as they pass through chains of retelling — details shift, qualifications drop, context vanishes. The Colleoni name claim is robust against this process because the core of it is simple and verifiable. The surname means what it means. The arms show what they show. The claim does not require a chain of inferential steps that can silently break.

The fourth answer involves the particular status of sexual and scatological language in historical contexts. There is a persistent popular impression that medieval and Renaissance people were either more sexually repressed than modern people or, in revisionist accounts, more robust and uninhibited. Material of the Colleoni type fits the revisionist narrative: here is a great Renaissance patron who put testicles on his coat of arms. This feeds into broader popular narratives about the Renaissance as a period of frank body culture and humanistic celebration of earthly life, in contrast to the ascetic tendencies of medieval Christianity or the prudishness of Victorian respectability. The Colleoni story arrives pre-loaded with narrative purchase.


## 4. Verdict

**True.** The surname Colleoni derives from the Italian/Bergamasque *coglioni*, meaning testicles, and carries the vulgar anatomical meaning as its primary etymological referent. This connection is not disputed by lexicographers, etymologists, or historians of Renaissance Italy. It was known to contemporaries and was evidently known to Bartolomeo Colleoni himself, whose heraldic arms featured the anatomically literal representation of his surname through three pairs of testicles — a standard canting arms design in which the visual elements pun on the bearer's name.

The claim requires no significant qualification. It is accurate, it is reasonably well documented, and it is one of those historical-linguistic facts that, once encountered, tends to stick. The EARC classification is **A** (Accurate): the evidence is clear, the sources are sound, and there is no meaningful scholarly controversy about the basic linguistic and heraldic facts.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Colleoni case opens onto several interesting areas of Renaissance cultural history that popular accounts rarely pause to explore.

**Heraldic speaking arms.** Canting arms — heraldic devices that pun on or pictorially represent the bearer's name — were ubiquitous in medieval and Renaissance European heraldry and represent one of the more sophisticated traditions of visual wordplay in Western art. The practice required a working knowledge of multiple languages (Latin, French, and the relevant vernacular), an appreciation of phonological similarity and pun, and a tradition of heraldic artisanship capable of rendering the pun in visual form. The Pelham family bore pelicans; the Lucy family bore pike (luces); the Shelley family bore shells; the Bassett family bore bassets. For Colleoni, the coglioni arms followed the same tradition, though the anatomical referent gave the result a racier character than most parallel examples.

The prevalence of canting arms reflects a broader Renaissance engagement with emblematic thinking — the idea that images could encode meanings, that visual and verbal puns could carry cultural significance, and that the relationship between word and image was a matter for serious intellectual and artistic attention. The emblem books of the sixteenth century — Alciato's *Emblematum Libellus* (1531) being the paradigm case — represent a codification of this tradition. Colleoni's arms predate the emblem book tradition's formalization but participate in the same cultural logic.

**The condottiere as self-fashioner.** Bartolomeo Colleoni was a prime example of the Renaissance condottiere as a self-constructed public figure. Born into a local Bergamasque noble family but not of great wealth or standing, he built a career through military skill, political acuity, and a talent for the kind of impressive self-presentation that Renaissance Italian culture rewarded. His accumulation of wealth was staggering: at his death he left approximately a third of his fortune to Venice (on the specific condition that a monument be erected in his honour — a condition that produced the Verrocchio statue) and the remainder to endow the Cappella Colleoni and related institutions. His heraldic choices, including the testicle arms, were part of a calculated programme of self-presentation that emphasized virility, martial power, and the kind of personal force that a mercenary commander needed to project.

The deliberate embrace of a name with vulgar meaning — through the heraldic arms — can be read as a power move of a characteristic Renaissance type. Rather than trying to minimize or deflect attention from the anatomical meaning, Colleoni incorporated it into his visual identity in the most prominent possible way. This transforms a potential source of ridicule into a statement of confident masculinity. The condottiere is, on this reading, not embarrassed by the ball-reference in his name but glorying in it — claiming the implied physical and martial attributes rather than disavowing them.

**Linguistic history of Italian sexual vocabulary.** The word *coglione* has a remarkably continuous history in Italian from classical Latin through to the present day. Its Latin ancestor *coleus* appears in classical texts with no particular sense of extreme vulgarity — it was an anatomical term, more or less equivalent in register to modern clinical English "testicle." The shift toward stronger vulgarity in the Italian descended form appears to be a medieval and early modern development, and the term's secondary use as an insult (meaning a fool or dupe) may reflect the association of the anatomical region with ideas of vulnerability — to be caught or tricked "by the balls" — that is common across many languages' use of genital terms as insults. The Venetian and Bergamasque dialects had their own phonological variants, which is why the transition from *coglioni* to *Colleoni* is linguistically coherent rather than forced.

**Verrocchio's statue and the reception history.** Andrea del Verrocchio's equestrian bronze of Colleoni, cast between approximately 1481 and 1496 (Verrocchio died in 1488 before it was finished; the casting was completed by Alessandro Leopardi), has a reception history that is in some ways inseparable from the name question. The statue is an image of overwhelming military confidence: the rider's twisted torso, raised stirrups, and expression of fierce concentration create a sense of barely controlled aggressive power. The name of the man it depicts, and his heraldic arms, provides a specific satirical note that art historians have not been able to entirely ignore. John Pope-Hennessy, one of the twentieth century's most important historians of Italian Renaissance sculpture, discusses the statue at length in his *Italian Renaissance Sculpture* (1958) and handles the name matter with characteristic English academic obliqueness. More recent scholarship has been franker. The Colleoni testicle connection now appears comfortably in peer-reviewed art history as a straightforward contextual fact.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim has not received dedicated coverage from major fact-checking organisations, which is entirely comprehensible given that it is demonstrably true rather than being a piece of misinformation requiring debunking. Snopes, the primary English-language fact-checking reference for popular claims of this type, does not list it in its current database. PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and similar organisations focus primarily on contemporary political claims and would have no occasion to address a Renaissance etymological question.

The claim appears regularly in popular history and art history publications where it is treated as a well-established contextual detail rather than a claim requiring verification. Art history textbooks that discuss Verrocchio's Colleoni statue often include a note on the name's meaning in a footnote or sidebar; travel guides to the Campo Santi Giovanni e Paolo in Venice occasionally mention it; and it circulates freely in educational contexts as a memorable piece of accompanying context.

The question that a fact-checking approach would more usefully address is not "is this true?" (it plainly is) but "what does it mean, and what might a casual audience incorrectly believe on the basis of it?" Two potential misreadings deserve attention. First, audiences might conclude that the name's bawdy meaning was some kind of secret or private family understanding, rather than understanding that canting heraldic arms were a public and visible statement made in a tradition of visual wordplay that Renaissance educated culture readily decoded. Second, audiences might treat the claim as evidence of a peculiarly Italian or Renaissance lack of bodily inhibition, when in fact canting arms with anatomical referents existed across medieval European heraldry — the Colleoni example is memorable precisely because the referent is vivid, but the heraldic practice was pan-European and normative.

A properly contextualised treatment of the claim would affirm its truth, explain the canting arms tradition, note that Colleoni's heraldic choices were deliberate and public rather than hidden, and use the case to illuminate the broader visual culture of Renaissance self-fashioning rather than treating it as a curiosity detached from its historical context.
