# Did Colonel Sanders Steal the KFC Original Recipe From a Black Woman?

**Verdict: Unproven — lacking documentary evidence, though the broader historical context the claim invokes is accurate and important.**

The "Miss Childress" story began circulating on Facebook and Instagram around 2019, finding eager audiences in African American communities and broader social justice networks: Colonel Harland Sanders, the white entrepreneur whose face is plastered on more than 22,000 fast-food restaurants globally, built his empire on a recipe he stole from a Black woman who died in poverty while he became a billionaire. Snopes investigated in September 2019 and rated the claim **Unproven** — a verdict that requires careful unpacking, because what the investigation revealed is not that the claim is false but that it cannot be verified, and that its viral spread is entangled with legitimate grievances that pre-existing evidence does not support in this specific instance but which are materially accurate in the wider landscape.

---

## 1. The Claim

The claim circulated in several formulations, sharing a common core:

- Colonel Sanders obtained the famous 11-herbs-and-spices recipe from a Black woman named "Miss Childress" (or "Mrs. Childress")
- He later paid her a one-time sum of $1,200 after her family complained
- KFC is now worth approximately $15 billion
- Miss Childress died in poverty

The African Diaspora Facebook page formulated it as: *"Meet Mrs. Childress. Colonel Sanders Stole His Famous Fried Chicken Recipe From A Black Woman Named Mrs. Childress. He later paid her $1,200 for her recipe. KFC is worth 15 Billion Dollars today."*

A separate Facebook post by Glenn Dickens added: *"In case you didn't know. Meet Miss Childress, she died in poverty. She is the woman behind the original #KFC recipe. He took all her profits and made us think it was his recipe."*

Nearly all versions of the meme were accompanied by the same photograph of a Black woman preparing fried chicken.

---

## 2. What the Investigation Revealed

### The image is not Miss Childress

The most immediately falsifiable element of the meme is the image itself. Snopes identified the photograph — or more likely, painting — as being taken from a 1920s magazine advertisement for Snowdrift shortening. The figure in the image is identified only as "Sarah," and she is depicted in the style of the racist "mammy" caricature that was ubiquitous in American commercial imagery of the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: a cheerful, large Black domestic servant associated with cooking for white families.

The use of this particular image — which is not a photograph of "Miss Childress," who may not exist as a documented person — does significant epistemic and ethical damage to any historical claim the meme might otherwise advance. The mammy caricature was itself a mechanism of racial subordination, deployed in advertising to simultaneously exploit the labour of Black women and render it as natural, invisible servitude. Using such an image to represent a supposed victim of recipe theft layers contemporary racial justice rhetoric over imagery that was originally designed to perpetuate the same dynamics.

Whether the creators of the meme were aware of the image's origin is unclear. What is clear is that the image is not evidence of any "Miss Childress."

### The Ron Douglas attribution is false

A 2010 blog post claimed that food writer Ron Douglas, in his book *America's Most Wanted Recipes* (2009, Simon & Schuster), stated that "Sanders took his secret recipe from a black woman, one Miss Childress of Kentucky, whose family he paid $1,200 when they complained." This attribution became the quasi-evidentiary anchor for the Miss Childress claim — bloggers and meme creators cited Douglas' book as their source.

Snopes examined the book. It contains no such claim. The Miss Childress story is not in Douglas' book in any form. The attribution was fabricated by the 2010 blog post and subsequently propagated as though it were a documented citation — a form of **phantom sourcing** in which a secondary reference implies a primary source that does not contain the claimed information.

### No archival evidence

Snopes' search of newspaper archives spanning more than a century found no contemporaneous reporting of a dispute between Sanders and a woman named Miss Childress, no legal proceedings, no family complaint, and no $1,200 payment. Food writer Joshua Ozersky's 2003 biography *Colonel Sanders and the American Dream* (University of Texas Press), the most comprehensive scholarly account of Sanders' life and career, makes no mention of Miss Childress.

This absence is not, by itself, conclusive. Suppression, erasure, and invisibilisation of Black women's labour in historical records is extensively documented, and the silence of white-authored records about a Black woman's contribution to Southern cuisine would not be surprising. But absence of evidence is still the relevant fact; the claim makes a specific, verifiable assertion — a payment, a family complaint, a named individual — and none of these are traceable to any documented source.

## 3. What Sanders Actually Did, and Why Context Matters

### The conventional account

The conventionally accepted narrative of Kentucky Fried Chicken's origins, as documented by Ozersky (2003) and corroborated by independent food history sources, runs as follows:

Harland Sanders was born in 1890 in Henryville, Indiana, and spent most of his working life in a series of moderately successful business ventures: labour contracting, a ferry boat operation, an acetylene lighting company, a filling station in Corbin, Kentucky. At his Corbin filling station in the early 1930s, he began serving meals to highway travellers — first at his own table, then in a dedicated dining space, and eventually in a restaurant-motel operation known eventually as Sanders' Court & Café.

Sanders was a serious cook with a genuine interest in flavour, and his fried chicken developed a regional reputation. The key technical innovation Sanders introduced — using a pressure cooker to fry chicken rather than a conventional pan or deep fryer — dramatically reduced cooking time, making the product viable as "fast food" before that category existed. A pressure cooker reduces the time to fry a piece of chicken from over half an hour to under fifteen minutes while maintaining moisture, and it allowed Sanders to produce a consistent product at scale.

The specific blend of eleven herbs and spices was refined over years. Sanders began franchising the recipe in the late 1950s, driving to potential franchise partners himself, collecting a five-cent royalty on every piece of chicken sold, and growing the network to over six hundred locations by 1963 when he sold the company for two million dollars.

### The pressure cooker is the story

Ozersky's argument — accepted by most food historians — is that the *recipe* was not, strictly speaking, what made KFC viable or valuable. Southern fried chicken using seasoned flour dredges and various herb-and-spice combinations existed widely in Black and white Southern cooking traditions. The herbs and spices contributed to flavour, but they were not technically revolutionary in the way Sanders' pressure-cooking method was. The scaling innovation was mechanical and procedural, not culinary.

This distinction matters for evaluating the Miss Childress claim. The meme treats the recipe as the sole source of value — the thing that made KFC worth $15 billion — and therefore treats theft of the recipe as straightforwardly equivalent to theft of the entire commercial outcome. This overstates the recipe's independent contribution to KFC's success.

### What the claim gets right in aggregate

The specific Miss Childress claim is unproven. But the broader pattern it invokes — white entrepreneurs extracting economic value from Black culinary knowledge and labour in the American South — is historically well-documented and not in serious scholarly dispute.

Adrian Miller, in his authoritative *Soul Food: The Surprising Story of an American Cuisine, One Plate at a Time* (2013, UNC Press), documents extensively how Black Southern cooks — overwhelmingly women — developed and maintained the techniques that white-owned restaurants, food businesses, and cookbooks then monetised without attribution or compensation. The plantation kitchen as culinary laboratory, the Black domestic cook as unpaid or underpaid innovator, and the systematic exclusion of Black women from the economic fruits of their own culinary expertise are historical facts.

Psyche Williams-Forson, in *Building Houses Out of Chicken Legs: Black Women, Food, and Power* (2006, UNC Press), gives particular attention to fried chicken as a technology of Black women's economic agency and cultural expression, and to the ways in which that agency was constrained and appropriated within structures of racial capitalism. The "mammy" caricature in the Snowdrift advertisement used in the Miss Childress meme appears — and is extensively analysed — in Williams-Forson's book. Its use in the KFC meme thus inadvertently recapitulates the dynamic Williams-Forson describes: a Black woman's culinary identity reduced to a servile, nameless image.

## 4. The Epistemics of Narrative Justice

The Miss Childress story presents a distinctive epistemological challenge that the "Unproven" verdict captures but does not fully explore: what epistemic standards should govern claims about historical injustice in racially asymmetric evidentiary environments?

Helen Longino (2002), writing in *The Fate of Knowledge* (Princeton University Press), argues that the standards of evidence applied in scholarly practice are not socially neutral: they reflect the interests and perspectives of those who constructed them, and their application systematically advantages groups whose activities and existence were already documented by institutional record-keeping. A claim about what a wealthy white entrepreneur did is abundant in historical evidence by default; a claim about what an impoverished Black woman contributed faces an uphill evidentiary challenge not because her contribution was less real but because she occupied a social position that institutions did not document.

This is the precise situation the Miss Childress claim finds itself in. If Sanders paid a Black woman $1,200 for a recipe sometime in the 1930s or 1940s, the documentation of that transaction — if any existed — would have been on terms she did not control, potentially private, and likely to have disappeared without the kind of preservation efforts that record, say, Sanders' incorporation documents. "No evidence found" is not the same as "it didn't happen," and the Unproven verdict acknowledges this.

At the same time, Longino's framework does not endorse abandoning evidential standards: claims without evidence can still be acted on, but they should be held with appropriate epistemic humility. The Miss Childress meme presents the claim as established fact — "she is the woman behind the original #KFC recipe" — which it is not. The epistemically honest position combines acknowledgement of the plausibility of unnamed, unrecorded recipe attribution with a clear statement that the specific Miss Childress narrative is not documented.

---

## 5. Verdict

**Unproven.** No documentary evidence supports the specific claim that Colonel Sanders obtained his recipe from a Black woman named Miss Childress, paid her $1,200, or was confronted by her family. The image circulated with the meme is not of any "Miss Childress" but of a figure from a racist 1920s advertisement. The secondary source cited — Ron Douglas' cookbook — contains no such claim. No newspaper archives, no biography, and no contemporaneous accounts substantiate the story.

The broader historical claim — that Black culinary labour and knowledge has been systematically extracted and monetised by white entrepreneurs in American food history, without credit or adequate compensation — is extensively documented. Colonel Sanders and KFC represent a legitimate flashpoint for that analysis even without the Miss Childress narrative, given the role of Black Southern cooking traditions in the broader cuisine from which Kentucky fried chicken emerged.
