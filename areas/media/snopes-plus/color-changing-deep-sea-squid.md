## §1 — The Viral Video and Its Moment of Virality

On May 16, 2022, a video posted to the subreddit r/WTF under the title "This Incredible Color-Changing Deep Sea Squid" achieved a velocity of engagement that briefly overwhelmed the forum's moderation capacity before being removed by r/WTF moderators — not for falsity but apparently for misclassification of content. By the time of removal the post had accumulated nearly 9,000 upvotes, and the video had spread to other platforms in copied form.

The footage itself is brief and striking. A pair of human hands can be seen lifting a dark-black squid from what appears to be a holding tank of water. As the animal is held in air it transitions with remarkable speed from deep, near-opaque black to nearly complete transparency — appearing to become glasslike, with internal organs faintly visible. Upon being returned to the water the squid shifts back to dark coloration as rapidly as it lost it. The visual effect is genuinely astonishing, which explains both the initial viral velocity and the widespread uncertainty about whether the video could be authentic.

Snopes journalist Madison Dapcevich published an explanatory news piece on May 18, 2022, reporting her contact with the California Academy of Sciences for expert verification. Steinhart Aquarium Curator Charles Delbeek confirmed that the video appeared authentic and provided a species identification — probable *Sepioteuthis lessoniana*, the oval squid or bigfin reef squid — while noting that accurate species confirmation requires knowledge of the animal's provenance and the circumstances of capture. He attributed the colour change to a stress-induced fright response, characterising it as one expression of cephalopod chromatic communication.

This entry develops the biology behind what the video shows, with particular attention to the three cellular systems that together give cephalopods their chromatic capabilities, and the specific neuroethological question of why stress triggers transparent-lightening in this species rather than darkening or patterning.

## §2 — Chromatophores: The Primary Colour Mechanism

Cephalopod colour change rests on three distinct but interacting cellular systems. The first and most studied is the **chromatophore**, a complex multicellular organ that functions as the primary pigment-delivery mechanism.

A key early misconception to dispense with: cephalopod chromatophores are not analogous to vertebrate chromatophores or to the melanophores of fish. In vertebrates, chromatophores typically change the *distribution* of pigment granules within a cell — granules disperse to show colour or aggregate to hide it. In cephalopods, the mechanism is fundamentally different and importantly faster.

As described by Delbeek in his statement to Snopes, and documented in detail by Mäthger, Denton, Marshall & Hanlon (2009, *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*), each cephalopod chromatophore unit is composed of a central chromophore cell accompanied by radial muscle cells, nerve cells, glial cells, and sheath cells. Inside the chromatophore cell, pigment granules are enclosed within an elastic membrane called the **cytoelastic sacculus**. When the surrounding radial muscles contract, the sacculus is stretched into a flat disk, dramatically expanding its surface area and making the pigment visible as colour. When the muscles relax, the sacculus springs back to a small sphere, and the pigment essentially disappears from view.

This means cephalopod colour change is a **muscular** process, driven by rapid contraction and relaxation of attached radial musculature, not a molecular diffusion process. The neurological implication is profound: colour changes can be as fast as a single muscle twitch — milliseconds — and can be controlled with the spatial precision of individual chromatophore fields. Hanlon and Messenger (1988, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society of London B*, 300: 437–487) demonstrated that the chromatophore patterns in cephalopod skin are topographically mapped in the brain in a pattern similar to the pattern of chromatophores they control — a dedicated neural representation for colour output that has no direct parallel in vertebrate neuroscience.

In the viral video, the rapid loss of dark coloration when the squid is removed from water is explained by sudden chromatophore sacculus relaxation — the radial muscles surrounding the dark-pigment-containing sacculi relax under acute stress, removing the tensioned expansion that normally makes dark pigment visible. What remains is the transparency of the muscle and organ tissue without overlying pigment expression.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Iridophores, Reflectin, and Structural Colour Under Neural Control

The second colour system — iridophores — is epistemically more surprising than chromatophores and has been the subject of intensive research spanning molecular biology, materials science, and neuroscience over the last decade.

Iridophores are cells containing stacked platelets of membrane and protein that create structural colour through thin-film interference — the same optical principle responsible for the rainbow colours seen in soap bubbles or oil films. In cephalopods, the protein responsible for building these reflective plates is **reflectin**, a class of proteins unique to cephalopods and first characterised by Crookes et al. (2004, *Science*, 303: 235–238). Reflectin proteins are intrinsically disordered in their natural state, meaning they lack stable secondary structure — a property that makes them unlike virtually all other structural proteins in biology and allows them to undergo dramatic, rapid changes in optical properties in response to phosphorylation signals.

The critical finding from Mäthger and Hanlon (2007, *Journal of Experimental Biology*, 210: 3624–3634) using marine cephalopods is that iridophores in at least some squid species can be actively controlled through the nervous system via neurotransmitter release. When acetylcholine binds to receptors on iridophore cells, it triggers phosphorylation cascades that alter the packing density of reflectin proteins, changing the optical path length in the stack of reflective plates and therefore shifting the wavelength of reflected light. This means a squid can, through neuronal signalling alone, change the specific colour of its iridomorphic patches — not just whether they are visible or not.

This is deeply counterintuitive from a materials science perspective. Structural colour in most biological and synthetic systems is a static property — the physical structure determines the colour and it cannot be dynamically changed without physically rebuilding the structure. Cephalopod iridophores sidestep this by using a protein whose packing geometry is proteomically adjustable in real time. The reflectin discovery has since inspired biomimetic materials research: Lin et al. (2013, *Advanced Materials*, 25: 5050–5056) used reflectin films to create electrically tunable structural-colour devices, directly borrowing the cephalopod mechanism for human engineering applications.

For the viral video, iridophores provide secondary and tertiary contributions to the colour shift seen. The dramatic transparent appearance of the squid in air reflects not only the absence of chromatophore pigmentation but the altered optical state of the iridophore layer, which under stress transitions away from the reflective configuration that gives the dark-coloured animal its visual depth. What the camera captures is the combined output of three cellular systems in a state of rapid coordinated transition — a system so dynamic and so precisely integrated that Hanlon (2007, *Current Biology*, 17: R400–R404) called it "the fastest active camouflage and communication system in the animal kingdom."

## §4 — Leucophores and the Third Layer: Environmental Mirroring

The third chromatic-cell system is leucophores, which are less familiar outside cephalopod specialist literature but are essential to understanding the full range of colour behaviour visible in the video.

Leucophores, documented in detail by Mäthger et al. (2003, *Journal of Experimental Biology*, 206: 3607–3613), are broadband reflector cells that contain crystalline spheres of reflectin (or related proteins) arranged to scatter and mirror incoming light across the full visible spectrum. Unlike iridophores, which create iridescent structural colour, leucophores function as near-perfect diffuse white reflectors under some illumination conditions, and as environmental mirrors under others — they reflect whatever wavelengths of light happen to be abundant in the local environment.

This gives cephalopods a passive background-matching capability on top of the active chromatic control provided by chromatophores and iridophores. When a squid hovers against a white sandy substrate, leucophores contribute to the paleness of its ventral surface; when it moves against the dark water column, the leucophores reflect the dark blue-green ambient light. From a camouflage-engineering perspective, leucophores represent a passive, low-metabolic-cost layer of environmental matching that supplements but does not replace the active, neural-controlled layer.

In the transparent-appearing squid of the viral video, leucophores are visible as faint internal light scattering within the transparent tissue. The body does not become truly glass-transparent — it becomes pale and translucent because the leucophores are still present and still reflecting ambient light, even as chromatophore sacculi have relaxed and iridophore output has shifted.

## §5 — Species Identification, Distribution, and Ecological Context

The species tentatively identified by Steinhart Aquarium Curator Charles Delbeek as *Sepioteuthis lessoniana* — the bigfin reef squid, also known as the oval squid — is the most commonly encountered squid in tropical and subtropical Indo-Pacific coastal waters. Classified by Lesson (1830) and now represented in the literature as a species complex (several morphologically similar forms previously grouped under *S. lessoniana* may represent distinct species; see Izuka et al., 1994, *Bolletino Malacologico*, for early molecular evidence), it is distributed from East Africa through the Indian Ocean to the western Pacific.

*Sepioteuthis lessoniana* is a neritic species — it inhabits shallow coastal and reef environments rather than the deep sea, making the video's description as featuring a "deep-sea squid" a common-name inaccuracy. The Reddit post title included "deep-sea," and this label was widely repeated in share text and news coverage including the Snopes report's headline, but deep sea typically refers to depths below 200 metres where sunlight fails to penetrate. *S. lessoniana* occupies the photic zone and frequently enters very shallow water around reefs.

This taxonomic imprecision matters for science communication. "Deep-sea squid" as a label invokes the extreme adaptations of truly deep-sea cephalopods — species like the glass squid (*Cranchia scabra*) or the colossal squid (*Mesonychoteuthis hamiltoni*) — and may contribute to confusion between distinct ecological and physiological contexts. The colour-change performance seen in the video is characteristic of neritic squid with full chromatophore repertoires; truly deep-sea species that live below the photic zone have reduced chromatophore expression, since producing visible colour provides no adaptive benefit in an environment with no ambient light.

The ecological significance of colour change in *Sepioteuthis lessoniana* specifically has been studied in the context of mating behaviour. Mäthger et al. (2012, *Journal of Experimental Biology*, 215: 3630–3635) documented that male *S. lessoniana* simultaneously display male courtship signalling on the side of their body facing a female and female-mimicking camouflage on the opposite side facing a rival male — a binocular duality of chromatophore display that exploits the fact that cephalopod eyes have full panoramic vision but limited cross-eye neural integration.

## §6 — Verdict, Residual Gaps, and Lines for Further Inquiry

**Snopes classification: Real and explained (news report).** The video shows an authentic biological event. The species is tentatively identified as *Sepioteuthis lessoniana*. The colour-change behaviour is a well-documented fright response driven by relaxation of chromatophore sacculi under acute stress. The science of cephalopod chromatic biology is extensively peer-reviewed.

**EARC assignment: A (Accepted/Verified).** The biological mechanism is documented in peer-reviewed literature across multiple independent research groups. Expert verification was provided directly to Snopes for this specific video. The event is confirmed, and the explanation is well-supported.

**Residual gaps for future entries:**

1. *Species resolution.* The *S. lessoniana* species complex remains taxonomically unresolved; molecular phylogenetic work (e.g., Izuka et al., 1994) suggests multiple cryptic species may be conflated under the single name, with potentially different chromatophore behaviours across clades.
2. *Neural mapping of chromatophore control.* Hanlon and Messenger (1988) established the topographic brain-skin mapping in *Sepia officinalis* (cuttlefish); equivalent mapping in squid species including *S. lessoniana* is less thoroughly documented.
3. *Reflectin protein diversity.* The reflectin gene family in cephalopods is more diverse than initially recognised — Morse et al. (2015, *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*) identified multiple paralogue groups with different optical and biochemical properties; the functional mapping across species is incomplete.
4. *Conservation and trade status.* *Sepioteuthis lessoniana* is commercially important in Southeast Asian and East Asian fisheries; the individual in the viral video appears to be a captive specimen, but provenance is unknown, and trade-related regulations would apply if the animal was held privately without permits.

The viral video captured something real, beautiful, and biologically sophisticated. The label "incredible" in the Reddit post title turns out to be factually accurate — what the video shows is the output of one of the most impressive integrated biological systems in the animal kingdom, operating at full speed under stress. That the underlying mechanism involves protein-level materials engineering, real-time neural colour computation, and evolutionary history stretching back 300 million years of cephalopod evolution makes the thirty-second clip considerably more interesting than the word "incredible" can contain.
