# What's Up With This 'Incredible' Color-Changing, Deep-Sea Squid?

## The Claim

In May 2022, a video began circulating across social media platforms — Twitter (then still pre-X), Facebook, Reddit, and TikTok — showing a deep-sea squid apparently changing color in real time. The captions and voiceovers variously described the animal as "the incredible color-changing deep-sea squid," "a squid that can become any color in the rainbow," and, in one particularly shareable iteration, "the only squid in the ocean that can change color on command." The video footage, which appeared to originate from an NOAA (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration) deep-sea exploration mission, showed a squid — later identified as a species of *Wenus* or possibly a closely related deep-sea oegopsid — cycling through a sequence of hues: reddish-brown, pale white, and a striking iridescent pattern that rippled across its mantle and arms. The viral framing was unequivocal: this was a marvel of nature, a squid performing what amounted to a light show in the permanent darkness of the bathypelagic zone, and viewers should be amazed.

Snopes published a fact-check on May 18, 2022, under a headline that asked whether the video showed a "color-changing deep-sea squid." Their verdict acknowledged that the footage was genuine, the squid was real, and the color changes were not fabricated or digitally enhanced. However, as we will see, the story's viral packaging introduced several layers of distortion — not outright falsehood, but a pattern of exaggeration, context-stripping, and implicit over-claiming that is endemic to how nature content travels online. The gap between what the video shows and what the viral framing implies is the kind of space where media literacy matters most: not debunking a hoax, but understanding how truth gets stretched in transit.

The central claims embedded in the viral content can be summarized as follows:

1. **The squid changes color.** This is true, in the sense that the animal's appearance shifts across hues during the footage.
2. **This is exceptional or unique among squid.** This is misleading — many cephalopods change color, and deep-sea squid are no exception to this broader clade-level capability.
3. **The squid is "performing" on command or for display.** This is speculative — the color changes are likely stress responses, camouflage attempts, or physiological processes unrelated to deliberate "display."
4. **The footage is from a deep-sea mission.** This is accurate, though the specific depth, location, and mission details were frequently omitted or garbled in the viral iterations.

What makes this case interesting for the Truth Vault is not that someone lied. Nobody did, at least not in a prosecutable sense. What makes it interesting is how a real animal, real footage, and a real phenomenon can be packaged into something that leaves viewers with a set of impressions that are collectively less accurate than any single component. This is the signature of the "distorted-but-grounded" gap category: the building blocks are solid, but the mortar is wrong.

## What's Actually True

Let us establish the biological facts before examining the distortions.

**Cephalopods change color.** This is one of the most well-documented phenomena in marine biology. Coleoid cephalopods — the subclass that includes squids, octopuses, and cuttlefish — possess sophisticated color-changing systems that are among the most complex in the animal kingdom. These systems operate through three primary mechanisms:

**Chromatophores** are small, pigment-filled sacs controlled by radial muscles innervated directly by the nervous system. When the muscles contract, the pigment sac expands and becomes visible; when they relax, the sac retracts into a small dot. Chromatophores typically contain one of three pigment classes: reddish-brown (erythrophores/pheomelanin), yellow (xanthophores), or black/brown (melanophores). By modulating the combination and intensity of these overlapping sacs, a cephalopod can produce a wide spectrum of colors and patterns — not instantaneously "any color in the rainbow," but a substantial range within the warm-toned and neutral spectrum.

**Iridophores** are structural color-producing elements that operate through thin-film interference rather than pigment. These are layered stacks of reflective platelets (often containing guanine crystals) that produce metallic, iridescent hues — blues, silvers, and greens — depending on the angle of incident light and the spacing of the layers. Unlike chromatophores, iridophores are not neurally controlled in most species; their appearance shifts with viewing geometry rather than active modulation, though recent research has shown some species can actively tune iridophore reflectance.

**Leucophores** are white, light-scattering elements that produce bright white patches, often used in combination with chromatophores to create high-contrast displays or to blend with light-splattered backgrounds.

A deep-sea squid, like the one in the video, possesses a combination of these structures. The color changes observed in the footage are consistent with chromatophore activity — the expansion and contraction of pigment sacs visible as waves of color sweeping across the animal's body. This sweeping pattern is characteristic of cephalopod color change: because each chromatophore is individually innervated, the nervous system can produce traveling waves, pulses, and complex mottled patterns across the skin in fractions of a second.

**The footage is genuine.** NOAA's Ocean Exploration program regularly conducts deep-sea missions using remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) equipped with high-definition cameras. These ROVs capture footage of animals in their habitat at depths where no sunlight penetrates — typically below 1,000 meters. The ROV's lights illuminate the animal, and the footage is accurate documentation of the squid's appearance under those specific lighting conditions. There is no evidence of digital manipulation, color enhancement, or artificial injection of color-changing effects.

**The squid was likely stressed.** When an ROV approaches a deep-sea organism, the vehicle's lights, thrusters, and electromagnetic signature represent an alien stimulus in an environment that has been stable for millions of years. Cephalopods respond to stress, threat, or disturbance with rapid, sometimes chaotic chromatophore activity — the so-called "blanching" or "flushing" response. This is well-documented in laboratory settings: an octopus startled by a stimulus will produce a rapid color change that is not a deliberate display but a physiological stress response mediated by the sympathetic nervous system. The color changes in the viral video are almost certainly this kind of response — the squid is reacting to the ROV, not performing.

So, to be precise: the squid is real, the footage is real, the color change is real, and the biological mechanism is well understood. What is not real is the framing — the suggestion that this is a unique squid performing a voluntary display in a natural, undisturbed state.

## What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

**What Snopes Gets Right**

Snopes correctly identified the footage as authentic NOAA footage, confirmed the squid's identification as a deep-sea species, and validated that the color-changing behavior was genuine and not digitally fabricated. Their fact-check struck the appropriate cautionary note: the video is real, but the viral packaging adds layers of sensationalism that exceed the evidence. Snopes also correctly noted that cephalopod color change is a well-known phenomenon, not a unique capability of this particular species, which undercuts the "incredible" and "only squid" framings found in social media captions.

The Snopes verdict — that the video is mostly accurate but that some viral descriptions exaggerate the mechanism and novelty — is a fair characterization. It reflects a nuanced reality: the underlying claim is true (the squid changes color), but the surrounding rhetoric inflates and distorts the specifics of that truth. This is the kind of careful distinction that fact-checking is supposed to make, and Snopes made it.

**Where It Falls Short**

However, the Snopes article operates within the inherent limitations of the fact-check format. These limitations are not failures of the specific writer or editor — they are structural constraints of the genre.

First, **the fact-check does not explore the biological mechanisms in depth.** A reader of the Snopes piece learns that the squid changes color and that this is not unique, but does not learn about chromatophores, iridophores, or the neurophysiology of cephalopod skin. The result is a reader who knows the claim is "mostly true" but cannot articulate *why* or *how* the squid changes color, which limits the educational value of the correction. Fact-checking that confirms a claim without explaining the underlying science risks leaving readers with a hollowed-out understanding: they know the what but not the why.

Second, **the fact-check does not address the ROV-stress confound.** The Snopes article does not discuss whether the color changes observed in the footage might be a stress response rather than a natural behavior. This is a significant omission because the viral framing implicitly suggests the squid is "performing" — a voluntary, natural display — when the evidence more strongly supports an involuntary reaction to disturbance. Without this context, the fact-check implicitly validates the "display" interpretation even while noting the exaggeration of novelty.

Third, **the fact-check does not examine the sociology of the content's spread.** How did this particular video go viral? What accounts amplified it? What caption variants traveled fastest? The fact-check treats the video as a single claim to verify, but in practice, the viral content was a cluster of differently-captioned iterations, each with its own degree of distortion. Understanding the gap between truth and virality requires understanding the transmission dynamics, not just the biological facts.

Fourth, **the fact-check does not contextualize the broader pattern.** This is not an isolated case. Nature videos routinely go viral with captions that overstate novelty, anthropomorphize animal behavior, or strip away critical context. The "color-changing squid" is one instance of a systemic pattern in how science content travels online. A fact-check that treats this as a single claim to verify misses the opportunity to point readers toward the broader media-literacy lesson.

The Snopes article is, in short, a competent piece of verification work that stops short of being a piece of science communication or media analysis. It answers the narrow question — "Is this video real?" — but leaves the richer questions — "What does this video tell us about how nature content moves through social media?" — largely unasked. ## Why This Matters

This case matters for several intersecting reasons that together illuminate the gap between scientific reality and public perception.

**The Anthropomorphism Trap**

When viewers watch a squid cycling through colors on their phone screen, they naturally project intentionality onto the animal. The color changes look deliberate — the squid appears to be "showing off," "performing," or "reacting" like a human performer might. This is the anthropomorphism trap: we interpret animal behavior through the lens of human emotional and volitional categories. The squid, of course, has no such intentions. Its chromatophore system is responding to neurological signals triggered by environmental stimuli — in this case, almost certainly the stress of the ROV's presence. The color changes are about as voluntary as blushing in humans, which is to say: not very.

This matters because anthropomorphism is not merely a harmless interpretive error. When we frame animal behavior as intentional performance, we implicitly extend moral and emotional expectations to the animal that it cannot meet. We create a relationship that does not exist. And when the framing enters the public consciousness — when millions of people come away thinking "squids perform light shows" — we build a collective understanding of marine biology that is structured around entertainment rather than ecology. This can distort public support for conservation: a squid valued for being "entertaining" is a squid valued for the wrong reasons, and when the entertainment proves illusory, the value proposition collapses.

**The Deep-Sea Distortion Layer**

The deep sea is, for most people, entirely mediated. We will never visit it. We will never see it with our own eyes. Our entire understanding of the bathypelagic zone comes through screens — documentaries, YouTube videos, social media posts, and the occasional museum exhibit. This means that every layer of mediation between the deep-sea reality and the viewer's screen is a point where distortion can enter. The ROV camera captures the squid under artificial lighting that no natural viewer would ever see. The video editor trims the footage to its most dramatic moments. The social media captioner adds framing language — "incredible," "unique," "on command." The platform's recommendation algorithm amplifies the most engaging variants. By the time the video reaches the viewer's phone, it has passed through four or five layers of curation and interpretation, each of which adds a degree of separation from the animal's actual life.

This is not unique to the deep sea, but it is most acute there. A video of a hummingbird in a garden can be cross-checked against the viewer's own experience — "I've seen hummingbirds, and they do move like that." No such check exists for a deep-sea squid. The viewer has no independent referent. They must trust the chain of mediation entirely, which makes the chain's integrity paramount — and social media's chain of mediation is notoriously low-integrity, optimized for engagement rather than accuracy.

**Science Communication and Marine Biology**

Marine biology has a peculiar position in public science engagement. It is simultaneously one of the most popular and one of the most misunderstood scientific domains. Documentaries like *Blue Planet* and *The Octopus Teacher* have created enormous public enthusiasm for ocean life, but they have also created expectations — that marine animals will behave dramatically, that every encounter will be revelatory, that the ocean is full of "incredible" things waiting to be discovered. This expectation creates pressure on content creators to frame every encounter as extraordinary, even when the underlying behavior is routine — for the animal, if not for the viewer.

A squid changing color is, for a cephalopod biologist, about as surprising as a dog wagging its tail. It is a fundamental, well-documented behavior observed across the entire coleoid clade. But for a public whose engagement with marine biology is mediated through highlight reels, the behavior appears miraculous — and the content creator who frames it as miraculous gets the engagement. This creates a selection pressure that systematically inflates the extraordinariness of routine biological phenomena. The result is a public that is simultaneously excited about marine biology and systematically miseducated about what is routine versus what is genuinely novel.

**Media Literacy Around Nature Videos**

The broader lesson here is about media literacy — specifically, the kind of media literacy that applies to nature and science content. Traditional media literacy training focuses on textual claims: is this headline accurate? Is this statistic correct? Is this quote taken out of context? But nature videos present a different challenge because the primary medium is visual, and the distortion is in the framing rather than the pixels. The video is not manipulated; the interpretation is manipulated. The squid really does change color; the color change really is captured on camera; the camera really is recording a real animal in a real habitat. Every individual element is true, and yet the aggregate impression — that this is a unique squid performing a voluntary display — is false.

This is the hardest kind of misinformation to combat because it does not contain a single false statement that can be isolated and debunked. Instead, it works through aggregation: a collection of true facts, arranged and framed in a way that produces a false understanding. Fact-checking, which is oriented toward verifying or falsifying specific claims, struggles with this kind of distortion because there is no single claim to mark "false." The Snopes verdict — "mostly accurate" — is technically correct but functionally inadequate because the real problem is not in any single claim but in the *gestalt*, the overall impression created by the combination of footage, caption, and context.

Media literacy for nature content needs to go beyond fact-checking and teach viewers to ask different questions: not just "is this true?" but "what is the animal actually experiencing?" "what is the context that this footage omits?" "what framing choices are being made?" "what would a specialist in this field say about this encounter?" These are harder questions, and they require more than a fact-check can provide. They require the kind of extended analysis that the Truth Vault is designed to offer.

## Research Notes

**Species Identification**

The squid in the viral footage has been variously identified in popular and semi-technical sources. NOAA's own species identification during the mission was provisional — deep-sea squids are notoriously difficult to identify from video alone because key diagnostic characters (beak morphology, tentacular club structure, photophore arrangement) often require specimen collection. The animal appears to be an oegopsid squid, possibly in the family Enoploteuthidae or Ancistrocheiridae, both of which are known for prominent chromatophore activity and bioluminescent photophores. The viral content frequently omitted species-level identification entirely, referring to the animal simply as "a squid," which contributed to the impression that this was a unique individual rather than a representative of a well-studied clade.

**Chromatophore vs. Bioluminescence Confusion**

A persistent confusion in popular coverage of deep-sea cephalopods is the conflation of two distinct phenomena: color change via chromatophores and light production via bioluminescence. Deep-sea squid frequently possess both systems, and they serve different functions. Chromatophores, as described above, are pigment-based, neurally controlled, and operate under the animal's skin surface. Bioluminescence, by contrast, is light production through chemical reactions — typically involving a luciferin substrate and a luciferase enzyme — that occurs in specialized organs called photophores. Many deep-sea squid use bioluminescence for counter-illumination (matching the downwelling light from above to eliminate their silhouette when viewed from below), for mating displays, or for predator avoidance.

The viral video of the color-changing squid shows chromatophore activity, not bioluminescence. The distinction matters because the two mechanisms imply different things: chromatophore activity in a deep-sea squid is most likely a stress or disturbance response, whereas bioluminescence would more naturally suggest a display or camouflage function. Conflating the two — which several viral iterations did, by referring to the squid "glowing" or "lighting up" — creates a misleading impression of the behavior's function and context.

**NOAA Mission Context**

The footage was captured during one of NOAA's Ocean Exploration missions, which systematically survey deep-sea habitats using ROVs. These missions are scientific in purpose — they document biodiversity, map seafloor geology, and identify potential conservation priorities. The footage is recorded under controlled conditions, with timestamp, depth, location, and species observations logged. The raw footage is publicly available through NOAA's video archive, though the specific mission and dive number associated with this viral clip were frequently omitted in social media sharing, making it difficult for interested viewers to access the source material — a common failure point in the viral content pipeline.

**Cephalopod Color Change Research**

The scientific literature on cephalopod color change is extensive. Key references include the foundational work of Roger Hanlon and colleagues at the Marine Biological Laboratory in Woods Hole, who have documented the neuroethology of chromatophore control across multiple cephalopod species. Their research has established that the chromatophore system is directly innervated by the brain, with motor neurons originating in the sub-esophageal mass, and that the system can produce thousands of distinct patterns through combinatorial control of individual chromatophore organs. The neural architecture underlying this capability has been mapped in detail in several species, including *Octopus vulgaris*, *Doryteuthis pealeii*, and *Sepioteuthis lessoniana*.

Of particular relevance to the deep-sea context is the question of why a squid living in perpetual darkness would retain a sophisticated color-change system. In the absence of light, chromatophores would appear to have no function — there is nothing to see and nothing to camouflage against. Several hypotheses have been proposed: that the chromatophore system is an ancestral trait retained from shallow-water ancestors and maintained because it imposes no significant metabolic cost in the deep sea; that deep-sea squid use chromatophores in conjunction with bioluminescent photophores to modulate the appearance of emitted light; or that the system serves a physiological function unrelated to visual display, such as UV protection or metabolic regulation of pigment byproducts. This is an area of active research and genuine scientific uncertainty, which the viral content's confident framing entirely elided.

**The Viral Content Pipeline**

Analysis of how the video traveled through social media reveals a pattern common to nature content. The original NOAA footage was posted to the agency's website and YouTube channel with scientifically accurate but low-engagement captions. A secondary content creator — likely a nature-focused aggregator account — extracted the most dramatic segment, added a sensationalized caption, and posted it to Twitter. From there, the video was re-shared across platforms, with each iteration accumulating caption mutations: the "incredible" framing became "the only squid that can change color" in some variants and "a squid that glows in the dark" in others. Each mutation was a small step further from the original footage's context, and each step accumulated engagement that further amplified the distorted variants over the accurate ones. This is the viral content pipeline in miniature: a real thing, stripped of context, mutated by engagement pressure, and delivered to millions of viewers as something other than what it is.

## Verdict

The video is real. The squid is real. The color change is real. The biological mechanism — chromatophore activity under neural control — is one of the best-documented phenomena in cephalopod biology, and it is neither unique to this species nor indicative of voluntary "performance." The footage was captured during a legitimate NOAA deep-sea mission, and the animal's behavior is almost certainly a stress response to the ROV's presence rather than a natural display.

The viral packaging, however, introduced multiple distortions: it implied uniqueness where there was none, suggested volition where there was stress, conflated chromatophore activity with bioluminescence in some iterations, and omitted the mission context that would have allowed viewers to access the source material. These distortions were not fabricated falsehoods — each was a small exaggeration or omission — but together they produced a public impression that was meaningfully less accurate than the underlying footage warranted.

Snopes correctly identified the core claim as mostly accurate and noted the exaggeration of mechanism and novelty. Their fact-check was competent but incomplete: it did not explore the biological mechanisms, address the stress-response interpretation, or situate the case within the broader pattern of nature-content distortion online. The Truth Vault fills these gaps.

**Final Assessment: Distorted-but-Grounded.** The underlying phenomenon is real and well-documented. The viral framing exaggerates novelty, implies volition, and omits context. The gap between the claim and the reality is not one of fabrication but of framing — and framing is where most misinformation in the nature-content space actually lives. The squid is not performing for us. It is responding to us. The difference matters, both for our understanding of the animal and for our understanding of how media shapes what we think we know about the ocean's depths.
