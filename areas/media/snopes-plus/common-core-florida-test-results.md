# Did Ditching Common Core Cause a Florida School's Test Results to Improve?


## 1. The Claim

In the summer of 2017, a cluster of articles circulated across right-leaning and anti-Common Core web platforms with a compelling headline: a school in Florida had "ditched" the Common Core State Standards, and as a result its students had "soared" to the top of the state's school rankings. The articles drew on real Florida Standards Assessment data showing high test scores at the Mason Classical Academy — a charter school in Naples, Florida — and attributed those scores to the school's rejection of Common Core. The narrative structure was clean and politically resonant: a courageous institution breaks free from a federal education mandate and is rewarded with excellent outcomes, proving that Common Core harms student achievement.

The claim appeared in multiple publications, with variations on the headline. FreedomProject.com, a website promoting classical education and what it describes as "Judeo-Christian values," ran a piece declaring that the school "rejects Common Core and ranks #1." YourNewsWire.com — a website with a public record of publishing fabricated and misleading content — published an article with the headline "Florida School Ditches Common Core — Soars to Number One." NaturalNews.com, an alternative health and anti-establishment website, ran the headline "Florida school dumps common core then skyrockets to number one in English Language Arts, with 90 percent scoring proficient."

All three publications framed their stories as evidence that Common Core is harmful and that schools choosing to abandon it would see corresponding improvements in results. The political subtext was pointed: the Obama administration had been prominently associated with Common Core's initial promotion, and the 2017 publication of these articles occurred in a political climate of intensified criticism of the standards initiative. The Mason Classical Academy's results were offered as an empirical trump card in this ideological argument.

Snopes.com rated the claim False in a fact-check by writer Dan MacGuill, published 20 March 2018. This entry examines what the data actually show, why the causal claim is unfounded, and what epistemological patterns explain the claim's construction and spread.


## 2. What's Actually True

**What is genuinely verifiable.** The core data points underlying the claim are real. Students at the Mason Classical Academy did perform very well on the Florida Standards Assessment in 2017. In English Language Arts, the school's third-grade students ranked first among all thirty-three schools in Collier County, with approximately ninety percent scoring at a level of "proficient" or better. The school's fifth-grade ELA results were similarly strong. These are legitimately impressive outcomes and represent a genuine improvement from the school's 2016 results. The school's principal, David Hull, has been a consistent and publicly articulate critic of Common Core, and the school's curriculum is influenced by a classical education framework associated with Hillsdale College, a private institution known for its conservative educational philosophy.

**The central flaw: the school never used Common Core.** The fundamental factual problem with the claim is that Mason Classical Academy never adopted Common Core in the first place. There was therefore nothing to "ditch." A 2014 statement by Principal David Hull, published on the school's own website, makes the school's position explicit — Hull posed the question of whether the school could deliver both a rigorous classical curriculum and adequate state test results, while explicitly framing Common Core as external pressure that conflicted with the school's mission. The school's 2014 communications made clear it was deliberately operating outside the Common Core framework from its founding.

Hull revisited this issue explicitly in a 2017 piece also published on the school's website, anticipating the mischaracterisation: "The general theme of the original article argues that MCA is a charter school that has abandoned Common Core, and doing so catapulted the school to number one status. The pursuit of truth requires humility, so I therefore must admit, although the argument is on the right track, the author misses much of the bigger picture." Hull was correcting the misinformation while also using it as a platform to argue his broader views about progressive education. His correction — that the school never adopted Common Core to begin with — went unacknowledged in subsequent iterations of the viral narrative.

**The mathematics data undermines the narrative.** The narrative concentrated on English Language Arts results because they were the school's strongest data point. But Common Core covers two subjects: English Language Arts and Mathematics. If the school's rejection of Common Core applied to both — as would be true if Common Core were genuinely absent from the curriculum — then both subjects should reflect any benefit. In mathematics, Mason Classical Academy ranked fifth among thirty-three schools in Collier County in 2017, with an average of approximately eighty-one percent of students achieving "proficient" across all three measured grades. The four schools that outperformed Mason in mathematics were all public schools implementing the Common Core standards that the viral narrative claimed were responsible for lower outcomes. The data directly contradict the causal story in the one subject domain where a clear comparison between Common Core and non-Common Core schools could be drawn.

**The Collier County context.** The Collier County school district results, reported by the district itself, provide the comparative data for this analysis. The district's 2017 Florida Standards Assessment results memo, which documented year-on-year changes and countywide rankings, was the primary source used by FreedomProject.com and others. That document showed Mason Classical Academy's ELA strengths clearly, but it also contained the mathematics data that complicated the narrative — data the viral articles simply omitted.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The claim fits a pre-existing ideological frame.** Common Core has been one of the more contested education policy questions in the United States since its introduction under the Obama administration in 2010. Critics from both the left and the right have raised objections — progressive critics argued that the standards imposed overly narrow technical skills and crowded out more expansive educational goals; conservative critics argued that they amounted to improper federal intrusion into state education policy and imposed inappropriate pedagogical approaches. In this context, a story about a school that thrived by rejecting Common Core was not just an empirical claim; it was a political argument with a ready-made and sympathetic audience. Audiences already disposed to believe that Common Core harms education were primed to accept and share evidence that seemed to confirm it.

**The causal fallacy is structurally hidden.** The claim's deepest error is not a factual error about a number, but a logical error about causation. Post hoc ergo propter hoc is the traditional designation for the reasoning pattern in play: "after this, therefore because of this." Even setting aside the problem that the school never used Common Core, the claim would still commit a causal attribution error because it presents the simple temporal juxtaposition of "rejected Common Core, scores improved" as a causal relationship. Many other factors could explain improved test scores — changes in student body composition, improvements in teaching staff, maturation of the school's programme (Mason Classical Academy was still relatively new in 2016-2017), changes in assessment administration, or random variation between cohorts. The claim does not control for any of these alternatives. The structural invisibility of this reasoning error is significant: the logical fallacy is not self-announcing in the way that an obviously false numerical claim would be.

**Selective data presentation.** The articles that promoted the claim presented the English Language Arts data without the mathematics data. This selection was not random — it maximised the impression of the school's comparative performance while hiding the data that directly contradicted the causal narrative. Audiences who encountered only the ELA results had an incomplete picture that was more consistent with the claim. This is a form of motivated data selection that stops short of fabrication while producing a systematically misleading impression. The Snopes fact-check by MacGuill (2018) is notable for directly including the mathematics data and identifying the specific schools — public schools implementing Common Core — that outperformed Mason Classical Academy on that measure.

**Pretige borrowed from legitimate data sources.** The claim grounded itself in actual government data: the Collier County school district's official Florida Standards Assessment results. This gave the narrative a veneer of empirical credibility that purely invented claims lack. Articles could truthfully say "according to government statistics" — because the ELA ranking was indeed documented in official district data. The legitimacy of the source was then extended to a causal interpretation that the source data did not provide. Source legitimation is a common technique in misleading but not strictly false reporting.

**The architecture of anti-Common Core media.** FreedomProject.com, NaturalNews.com, and YourNewsWire.com occupy different but overlapping niches in an ecosystem of alternative media that shares a consistent hostility to what they characterise as progressive educational establishments. Stories that confirm this hostility flow readily between these outlets and their audiences, and the network effect of simultaneously publishing the same claim in multiple venues creates the impression of independent corroboration. When three different websites all publish the same story citing the same data, the apparent consensus strengthens the claim's apparent truthfulness, even though all three are drawing on the same original (misleading) framing.


## 4. Verdict

**False.** The central causal claim — that abandoning Common Core caused improvements in Mason Classical Academy's test results — is false on two independent grounds. First, the school never adopted Common Core, making it logically impossible for improvements to result from abandoning it. Second, even on the partial data that the viral articles relied on, the mathematics results directly contradict the narrative: the schools that outperformed Mason Classical Academy in mathematics were all public schools implementing Common Core. The claim as stated by MacGuill (2018) for Snopes is correctly rated False.

A more accurate characterisation of the available evidence would be: students at Mason Classical Academy achieved high English Language Arts results in 2017, ranking first in Collier County for third and fifth grades, and improved on their 2016 performance. The school has never used Common Core. The relationship, if any, between the school's classical curriculum and its ELA performance is an interesting educational research question that the available data cannot answer — answering it would require controlled comparison, demographic adjustment, and longitudinal tracking that a single year's district ranking does not provide.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Common Core debate makes an instructive backdrop for examining how educational research evidence is selected, framed, and deployed in policy argument. Educational research is particularly vulnerable to motivated misreading because educational outcomes are multi-factorial, causally opaque, and produced at many different levels of aggregation (individual student, classroom, school, district, demographic subgroup, year). This complexity means that selective presentation of a subset of data almost always can be made to support almost any predetermined conclusion.

Scholars of educational measurement, including Gary Orfield and colleagues at the Civil Rights Project at UCLA, have documented the ways in which single-year school-level test score comparisons are particularly unreliable indicators of instructional quality. Research on educational interventions consistently finds that student demographic characteristics — family income, parental education, English language learner status, and special education status — explain substantially more of the variance in test scores than instructional approaches (Hanushek, E.A. & Woessmann, L., 2011, "The Economics of International Differences in Educational Achievement," *Handbook of the Economics of Education*). This does not mean instructional approaches are irrelevant, but it means that a single school's test score ranking, without demographic adjustment, cannot be used to draw conclusions about instructional efficacy.

The Mason Classical Academy case also illustrates how charter school advocacy intersects with standardised testing. Charter schools, which are publicly funded but independently operated, frequently appear prominently in test score rankings partly because they often serve demographically distinct populations — including, in some cases, self-selected families who actively chose a particular educational approach, which introduces selection effects that conventional rankings do not correct for. A school whose families have actively chosen its classical curriculum may produce different average outcomes than a demographically similar school whose students did not choose it, for reasons entirely unrelated to the curriculum's intrinsic effectiveness.

The political economy of education reform reporting creates systematic incentives to find examples that confirm preferred policy positions. Advocates for Common Core can find data supporting its effectiveness; critics can find data suggesting harm. Without rigorous experimental or quasi-experimental research designs, single data points like Mason Classical Academy's 2017 ELA ranking illuminate nothing about the causal effects of any policy. The claim's construction as evidence was a rhetorical choice, not a scientific inference.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

MacGuill's (2018) fact-check for Snopes is a good example of systematic evidence assembly against a narrative claim. It identifies the two independent fatal flaws in the argument — the false premise (the school always rejected Common Core) and the incomplete data (mathematics results contradict the narrative) — without being distracted by the politically loaded context in which the claim circulated. The principal's own published statements are used as primary sources to establish the school's historical position, which is an elegant approach — the school itself corrects the claim without the fact-checker needing to adjudicate an educational policy debate.

The methodological lesson for fact-checkers is to examine the counterfactual structure of a causal claim before evaluating the data it cites. The question is not only "are these test scores real?" but rather "does the improvement in test scores logically follow from the proposed cause?" A causal claim requires: (a) the proposed cause to have been present; (b) the proposed effect to have occurred; (c) the effect to have followed the cause temporally; (d) a plausible mechanism connecting cause to effect; and (e) the absence of alternative explanations. The Common Core claim fails at step (a): the school never used Common Core, so Common Core's absence was not a change from a prior state. Fact-checkers working with educational claims should be particularly attentive to the demographic confounds and selection effects that make school-level test score comparisons among the most treacherous data in contemporary policy debate.

For educators and students using this case in critical thinking instruction, the exercise of asking "what would the data look like if the causal claim were false?" is particularly valuable. If Common Core adoption does not affect test scores, we would expect to see Common Core and non-Common Core schools mixed across all ranks — which is precisely what the Collier County mathematics data show.
