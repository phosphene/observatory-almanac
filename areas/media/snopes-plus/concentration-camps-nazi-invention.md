# "Concentration Camps Were Invented by the Nazis"


## 1. The Claim

The assertion that "concentration camps were invented by the Nazis" appears frequently in educational materials, popular history discussions, and political rhetoric about mass detention. This claim treats the Nazi concentration camp system as a unique historical innovation, positioning the Holocaust as different from previous forms of mass civilian internment and systematic persecution.

The belief operates on the assumption that the Nazis introduced both the concept and practice of concentrating civilian populations in guarded camps for political, racial, or military purposes. This framing treats Nazi camps as historically unprecedented rather than as an extreme evolution of existing colonial and wartime detention practices.

This misconception has significant implications for historical understanding, as it obscures the longer trajectory of mass detention systems and potentially minimizes earlier atrocities by treating them as categorically different from Nazi genocide. The claim also affects contemporary discussions about detention practices by establishing the Nazi system as the sole historical referent for "concentration camps."


## 2. What's Actually True

**Spanish precedents in Cuba (1896-1898):** General Valeriano Weyler's "reconcentración" policy forcibly relocated Cuban rural populations into heavily guarded camps to prevent support for independence fighters. Spanish authorities explicitly used the term "reconcentración" (reconcentration) to describe this strategy. An estimated 170,000-400,000 Cuban civilians died in these camps from disease, starvation, and poor sanitation. Contemporary American and European press coverage used the term "concentration camps" to describe Weyler's facilities.

**British camps during the Second Boer War (1899-1902):** British forces established camps to house Boer civilians displaced during their "scorched earth" campaigns in South Africa. British authorities and press explicitly called these "concentration camps," introducing the English-language term. Official British records document approximately 28,000 Boer civilian deaths and 20,000 Black African deaths in these camps. The mortality rate reached 34% for children under sixteen in some camps. British concentration camps were not intended as extermination facilities but became sites of mass death through overcrowding, inadequate food supplies, and disease outbreaks.

**German Southwest Africa (1904-1908):** During the Herero and Nama genocide, German colonial authorities established concentration camps to intern surviving members of these populations. General Lothar von Trotha's "extermination order" explicitly called for the destruction of the Herero people. Sharks Island camp and other facilities served as sites of forced labor and systematic killing. An estimated 65,000-80,000 Herero and 10,000 Nama died during this genocide, with camps playing a central role in the killing process.

**Ottoman camps during the Armenian Genocide (1915-1916):** The Ottoman Empire established concentration camps in Syria and Mesopotamia to house Armenians deported from Anatolia. These camps, such as those at Deir ez-Zor, were deliberately designed as sites of mass death through starvation, exposure, and systematic killing. Contemporary German allies and American missionaries documented conditions in these camps and used the term "concentration camps" in their reports.

**Russian Civil War and early Soviet camps (1918-1921):** Both White and Bolshevik forces established concentration camps during the Russian Civil War. Lenin's government created the Cheka concentration camp system beginning in 1918. These camps predated the Nazi rise to power by more than a decade and established administrative and logistical precedents for mass civilian detention in Eastern Europe.

**The Nazi innovation: extermination camps:** What the Nazis created that was genuinely unprecedented was the industrial extermination camp system—facilities like Treblinka, Sobibor, and Belzec designed specifically and solely for mass murder. These were distinct from concentration camps in their single-purpose design for killing rather than detention, forced labor, or political control. The gas chamber technology, railroad coordination, and bureaucratic systems for processing millions of victims represented genuine innovations in the technology of genocide.


**Historical continuity versus exceptionalism:** Nazi concentration camps represented an evolution and intensification of existing mass detention practices rather than a complete innovation. The administrative techniques, logistical challenges, and ideological justifications had been developed and tested in colonial contexts before being applied in Nazi Europe. Recognizing this continuity does not minimize Nazi crimes but places them within a longer history of systematic oppression.

**Definitional precision matters:** The distinction between concentration camps (facilities for mass civilian detention) and extermination camps (facilities designed specifically for industrial killing) is crucial for historical accuracy. Nazi Germany operated both types of facilities, but only the extermination camp system represented a genuine historical innovation. Concentration camps as sites of detention, forced labor, and mass death had multiple precedents.

**Colonial origins of modern atrocity:** The techniques of mass civilian detention, forced relocation, and systematic dehumanization were developed primarily in colonial contexts where they attracted less international attention and documentation. The Spanish reconcentración policy, British Boer War camps, and German colonial genocide established administrative and logistical precedents that were later applied in Nazi Europe with greater scale and efficiency.


## 3. Why People Believe This

This claim reflects a common pattern in historical memory where the most extreme example of a phenomenon becomes treated as its origin point. The Nazi concentration camp system was indeed the largest and most systematically murderous, but treating it as the historical beginning obscures important continuities with earlier colonial and wartime practices.

The epistemological problem involves conflating temporal prominence with historical precedence. Because Nazi camps were the most documented, most discussed, and most systematically organized, they dominate historical consciousness about mass detention. This recency bias makes it seem as if earlier examples were different phenomena rather than precedents in a longer historical development.

The claim also reflects nationalist and colonial blindness in historical education. European and American educational systems often treat Nazi atrocities as exceptional while minimizing earlier colonial genocides. This selective emphasis creates the false impression that mass civilian detention was a Nazi innovation rather than a technique refined through decades of colonial practice.

**The "Peak Event" Cognitive Distortion:** Psychologists studying collective memory have identified a consistent pattern where the most extreme instance of a phenomenon retroactively absorbs the history of all prior instances. This operates similarly to how many people believe the atomic bomb was "invented" for Hiroshima—ignoring the Manhattan Project's developmental timeline and the Trinity test—because the most devastating use becomes the definitional event. For concentration camps, the Holocaust's overwhelming scale and moral gravity make it the cognitive anchor around which all understanding of mass detention organizes. Earlier instances are not so much forgotten as reclassified: they become "something else" in popular imagination, even when contemporaries used the identical terminology.

**Documentary Asymmetry:** The sheer volume of documentation from Nazi camps—survivor testimonies, photographic evidence, trial transcripts, administrative records captured by Allied forces—creates a documentary weight that earlier camp systems cannot match. Spanish reconcentración camps in Cuba were documented primarily through newspaper dispatches and diplomatic correspondence. British Boer War camps were better documented but still relied on limited official reports and the observations of reformers like Emily Hobhouse. The Herero and Nama camps in German Southwest Africa were documented almost entirely by perpetrators, with survivor accounts only systematically collected decades later. This documentary asymmetry means that when people search for historical information about concentration camps, Nazi-era material overwhelms earlier examples by sheer volume, creating the false impression that the history begins in 1933.

**Curricular Architecture:** The structure of history education in most Western countries reinforces this belief through its organization. World War II and the Holocaust typically receive dedicated units in secondary school curricula, often spanning weeks of instruction. Colonial history, when covered at all, rarely includes detailed treatment of colonial camp systems. A student who completes standard American or British secondary education will have encountered extensive material on Auschwitz, Dachau, and Treblinka but may never have heard of Weyler's reconcentración, the British camps at Bloemfontein and Mafeking, or the Shark Island camp in Namibia. This curricular architecture doesn't merely fail to teach pre-Nazi camp history—it actively constructs a narrative framework in which the concept of the concentration camp originates with the Third Reich.

**National Self-Image and Selective Memory:** For the major colonial powers—Britain, Spain, Germany, and the United States—acknowledging that their own governments operated concentration camps before the Nazis complicates cherished narratives about national identity and moral authority. Britain's self-image as a defender of liberty is harder to maintain alongside frank acknowledgment that British forces killed 48,000 civilians in concentration camps during the Boer War. Spain's historical narrative about its colonial mission in Cuba, Germany's long silence about the Herero genocide, and the United States' reluctance to frame its own internment of Japanese Americans as concentration camps all reflect the same dynamic: nations prefer to locate the invention of concentration camps with a regime universally recognized as evil rather than within their own historical experience.


## 4. Verdict

**CONTESTED: Historically false but politically convenient**

Concentration camps predate Nazi Germany by several decades, with documented examples from Spanish Cuba (1896), British South Africa (1899-1902), German Southwest Africa (1904-1908), and Ottoman Armenia (1915-1916). Nazi Germany's innovation lay in extermination camps—facilities designed specifically for industrial killing rather than detention.

**The contested reality:** The claim serves important political and educational functions by emphasizing the exceptional nature of Nazi atrocities, but it achieves this emphasis through historical inaccuracy that obscures earlier colonial genocides. The Nazi concentration camp system was indeed unprecedented in scale and bureaucratic organization, but not in concept or basic implementation.

**Historical accuracy:** Spanish "reconcentración" camps in Cuba introduced both the practice and terminology of concentrating civilian populations under guard. British camps during the Boer War established the English-language term "concentration camp" and demonstrated the mass mortality potential of such facilities. German colonial camps in Southwest Africa provided direct institutional precedents for Nazi practices, sometimes involving the same administrators and military units.

**The definitional distinction:** Nazi extermination camps like Treblinka and Sobibor represented genuine historical innovation—facilities designed solely for mass murder rather than detention, labor, or population control. These differ categorically from concentration camps, which serve multiple functions including detention, forced labor, and political intimidation, though they often become sites of mass death.

**Why the confusion persists:** Nazi camps dominate historical memory due to superior documentation, larger scale, systematic organization, and central role in World War II narratives. Earlier colonial camps were less documented, occurred in peripheral regions, and have been systematically minimized in European and American historical education.

**Implications for historical understanding:** Recognizing the pre-Nazi history of concentration camps places the Holocaust within a longer trajectory of colonial violence while maintaining its distinctive features. This broader historical context enables better understanding of how mass detention systems develop and operate across different political contexts.

**Contemporary relevance:** Debates about current detention facilities often invoke Nazi precedents as the sole historical standard, creating artificially high thresholds for recognizing systematic persecution. Understanding concentration camps as a technique with multiple historical applications provides broader analytical frameworks for evaluating contemporary mass detention practices.

This case demonstrates how historical memory can be shaped by political and educational priorities that emphasize certain atrocities while obscuring others. The result is historical narratives that serve important functions—emphasizing the severity of Nazi crimes—while sacrificing accuracy about the longer development of systematic persecution techniques.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**Memory politics and Holocaust uniqueness:** The claim that Nazis invented concentration camps supports arguments for Holocaust uniqueness that can inadvertently minimize other genocides. While the Holocaust had distinctive features—its industrial scale, bureaucratic organization, and ideological completeness—treating it as historically unprecedented disconnects it from the longer trajectory of colonial violence and systematic persecution.

The "uniqueness debate" in Holocaust studies has produced a vast scholarly literature, with historians like Yehuda Bauer arguing for the Holocaust's unprecedented character while scholars like A. Dirk Moses and Dan Stone emphasize continuities with colonial violence. This is not merely an academic dispute: the framing directly affects how genocide is recognized and prevented. If the Holocaust is treated as a singular, unrepeatable event, then anything short of its specific features—industrial gas chambers, explicit racial ideology, bureaucratic precision—can be dismissed as "not really genocide." If instead the Holocaust is understood as the most extreme manifestation of a longer pattern, then earlier warning signs in contemporary events become recognizable and actionable.

**Contemporary political implications:** Debates about whether current detention facilities constitute "concentration camps" often invoke Nazi precedents as the sole historical standard. This narrow historical framework limits understanding of how mass detention systems operate and evolve. Recognition that concentration camps predate Nazi Germany provides broader historical context for evaluating contemporary practices.

The definitional gatekeeping around the term "concentration camp" has real political consequences. When U.S. Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez described migrant detention facilities at the southern border as "concentration camps" in 2019, the resulting debate focused almost entirely on whether the comparison to Nazi death camps was appropriate—obscuring the fact that the term has a longer history that includes British, Spanish, and other colonial precedents that are far more directly comparable to contemporary detention systems. The insistence that "concentration camp" can only mean "Nazi extermination camp" effectively renders the broader historical category unusable for describing ongoing practices, which is itself a form of historical distortion.

**Decolonizing genocide studies:** Scholarly work in genocide studies increasingly emphasizes connections between colonial violence and European genocide. Scholars like David Olusoga, Jürgen Zimmerer, and Isabel Hull have documented how techniques developed in Africa, Asia, and the Americas were later applied in Nazi Europe. This research challenges narratives that treat Nazi atrocities as disconnected from colonial precedents.

The personnel connections between colonial and Nazi camps are particularly revealing. Eugen Fischer, who conducted racial experiments on Herero prisoners in German Southwest Africa, later became a leading racial scientist in Nazi Germany and trained Josef Mengele. Franz Ritter von Epp, who served in the colonial genocide, later became the Nazi governor of Bavaria. These biographical links are not coincidental—they represent direct transmission of ideology and practice from colonial to European contexts. The administrative techniques of population registration, racial classification, forced labor management, and camp logistics developed in colonial settings provided a practical toolkit that the Nazi regime refined and scaled.

**Educational curriculum implications:** Textbooks that present Nazi camps as historical innovations miss opportunities to teach about the longer development of mass detention systems. A more complete historical education would trace the evolution from Spanish reconcentración through British Boer War camps to Nazi facilities, showing how techniques of mass civilian detention developed over time.

**International law development:** The legal frameworks prohibiting concentration camps and genocide emerged partly in response to Nazi atrocities but also drew on earlier documentation of colonial violence. The Genocide Convention and international humanitarian law reflect accumulated international awareness of systematic persecution techniques that predated Nazi Germany. Raphael Lemkin, who coined the term "genocide" and championed the Genocide Convention, explicitly studied the Armenian Genocide and colonial violence alongside the Holocaust in developing his legal framework. His unpublished manuscripts reveal extensive analysis of colonial-era mass killing—work that was marginalized in favor of a Holocaust-centric framing during the Convention's adoption but is now being recovered by scholars like Douglas Irvin-Erickson.

**Archaeological and historical evidence:** Recent archaeological work at colonial-era camps has revealed systematic patterns of mass death and deliberate killing that were previously minimized in historical accounts. Excavations at Shark Island (Namibia) and documentation of British Boer War camps have provided physical evidence for the systematic nature of colonial-era mass detention. Archaeological teams working at Shark Island between 2015 and 2020 uncovered evidence of organized mass burial, systematic nutritional deprivation, and architectural features designed to maximize exposure to lethal environmental conditions—findings that challenge narratives framing colonial camp deaths as unintended consequences of poor administration rather than deliberate policy.

**Comparative genocide analysis:** Understanding concentration camps as a technique with multiple historical applications enables better comparative analysis of different genocide processes. The Armenian Genocide, Herero and Nama genocide, Holocaust, Cambodian genocide, and other cases all employed mass detention systems with varying organizational structures and killing methods.

**The Technology of Mass Detention:** The evolution of concentration camp technology reveals a pattern of innovation transfer that crosses political and ideological boundaries. Barbed wire, invented for American cattle ranching in the 1870s, became the defining material technology of mass detention within two decades. Railroad networks designed for commercial transport were repurposed for deportation logistics in both Ottoman and Nazi contexts. Registration and classification systems developed for colonial population management evolved into the bureaucratic machinery of the Holocaust. Each genocidal regime did not invent its technology from scratch but adapted existing infrastructure and techniques to new purposes—a pattern that has profound implications for how we monitor the potential misuse of contemporary surveillance, logistics, and data management technologies.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed this historical claim, likely because it falls into the category of historical interpretation rather than discrete factual verification. The statement involves complex definitional questions about what constitutes a "concentration camp" versus other forms of mass detention, making it challenging to address through traditional fact-checking frameworks.
