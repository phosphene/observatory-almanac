## §1 — Claim and Verdict

In May 2018, amid viral social media attention to Capriccio Bubbly Sangria — a bottled high-alcohol sangria that users on Twitter and Facebook claimed caused unusually intense intoxication — a hoax emerged claiming that the beverage was contaminated with HIV. The specific mechanism of the claim was a screenshot of what appeared to be a chyron from Chicago television station WFLD (Fox 32 Chicago), stating that Capriccio Sangria was "spreading HIV worldwide."

Snopes rated this claim **False**.

The falseness of the claim operates at two distinct levels. The first and most immediate level is the fabricated news image itself: no such report was broadcast by WFLD or by any other legitimate news organization. The image was a digitally manipulated screenshot in which a false text overlay was inserted onto a genuine Fox News/WFLD segment about the drink's popularity. The second and more foundational level is biological: HIV cannot be transmitted through food or beverages under any realistic scenario, and the scientific consensus on this point is not in dispute.

The Capriccio Sangria HIV claim is not an isolated incident. Snopes noted in its coverage that it belongs to a long series of hoaxes making identical structural claims about food products supposedly contaminated with HIV. These have appeared with sufficient regularity that both the World Health Organization (WHO) and the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) have issued standing public health guidance specifically addressing them.

## §2 — Origin and Propagation

The claim's propagation followed a recognizable pattern that exploits the proximity of unrelated viral content to inject a secondary false claim. In May 2018, Capriccio Bubbly Sangria became the subject of extensive organic social media viral attention for an entirely unrelated reason: community members reported experiencing extreme intoxication from the beverage, sharing accounts of blackouts and unexpected potency. The sangria was positioned by media comparison as "the new Four Loko" — a reference to the 2010 controversy over caffeinated alcoholic beverages. A single tweet from user @StateofThe__ on May 12, 2018, aggregating Facebook posts about the drink's effects, generated the initial viral spread.

The HIV hoax entered this viral stream as parasitic content. The Capriccio sangria conversation was already generating high engagement volume, and an image falsely claiming HIV contamination required minimal effort to fabricate and insert into that existing conversation. Fabricated news screenshots — particularly those mimicking local television chyrons, which are familiar to broad audiences as a format associated with "breaking news" urgency — function as a trusted-source spoofing mechanism. A chyron-formatted claim carries the implicit authority of a broadcast newsroom even when it is entirely fabricated.

The geographic specificity of the fabrication is notable: the choice of WFLD (Fox 32 Chicago) grounds the claim in a particular place and institution, increasing plausibility because viewers may verify that WFLD is indeed a real station covering the Chicago market, without taking the additional step of verifying whether the specific story aired. This "verified institution, fabricated claim" structure is a common feature of sophisticated media hoaxes.

## §3 — Evidence Assessment and Epistemic Novelty

**EARC Rating: R (Refuted — biologically impossible claim)**

The epistemic novelty of this entry engages with a question of medical misinformation persistence: why does the HIV food-contamination hoax genre recur across decades, despite unambiguous scientific consensus that HIV cannot survive in food?

The virology of HIV transmission is well established. HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) is an enveloped retrovirus with a lipid bilayer membrane that is highly susceptible to environmental degradation. World Health Organization guidance, confirmed by CDC and consistent with the broader peer-reviewed literature, states that HIV cannot be transmitted through food or water. The survival of HIV outside the human body is extremely brief — minutes in most ambient conditions. Exposure to air initiates rapid degradation of the viral envelope; heat conditions (including body temperature, cooking temperatures, and the stomach's natural acidity) destroy viral infectivity entirely. Even in the event that a food product contained trace quantities of HIV-infected biological material, the virus would not remain viable through the gastrointestinal tract.

Lifson (1988, *Lancet*) provided early systematic documentation of HIV transmission routes in a large case-series review, confirming no documented food-borne transmission. Piot & Carael (1988, *British Medical Bulletin*) similarly reviewed epidemiological evidence and found no food or water routes in the documented transmission spectrum. Subsequent decades of epidemiological surveillance have not altered this conclusion; HIV's known transmission routes remain consistent: unprotected sexual contact, sharing of intravenous drug injection equipment, mother-to-child transmission during birth or breastfeeding, and receipt of contaminated blood products.

The epistemic novelty specific to this entry concerns what might be called the "viral paranoia cycle" of HIV food hoaxes. These hoaxes have attached to orange juice, bananas, needles in cinema seats, restaurant food tampering, and now commercial beverages. Across these iterations, the structural claim is identical: a product handled by anonymous workers in an industrial supply chain has been contaminated with HIV by someone within that chain. The fear is about the industrial food system itself — its opacity, its handoffs, its distance from the consumer — projected specifically onto an already emotionally charged pathogen. The hoax thus functions less as misinformation about HIV and more as misinformation about industrial food trust.

Brunvand (1993, *The Baby Train and Other Lusty Urban Legends*) documented early versions of food contamination folklore that predate the HIV era, suggesting that the category of "anonymous and malicious food adulteration" is a pre-existing narrative slot into which each generation's most feared pathogen is inserted. HIV, as the dominant fear pathogen of the late 20th century and continuing into the 21st, fills this slot with particular effectiveness because of the social stigma attached to HIV transmission routes, which makes the contamination claim feel both threatening and transgressive.

## §4 — Scientific and Expert Context

The scientific case against this claim is absolute, and it is worth elaborating the mechanisms precisely, because the claim's persistence suggests that the brevity of standard debunking responses ("HIV dies outside the body") does not fully satisfy the informational needs of people encountering it.

**HIV virology**: HIV-1 and HIV-2, the two major types of human immunodeficiency virus, are classified as lentiviruses within the retrovirus family. Their outer envelope is derived from the host cell membrane and is rich in glycoproteins (gp120 and gp41) essential to cell entry. This lipid envelope is the primary target of environmental degradation. Contact with air, soap, alcohol, bleach, or temperature extremes below 0°C or above 60°C disrupts the envelope irreversibly (WHO, 2016). In ambient dry conditions, HIV infectivity falls below detectable levels within two to four hours. In liquid media, viral decay depends on pH, temperature, and the presence of other chemicals; standard commercial beverages — including wine and sangria with alcohol concentrations of 6 to 13 percent — are hostile environments for viral stability (Resnick et al., 1986, *Journal of the American Medical Association*).

**Stomach acid and gastrointestinal pH**: Even in the hypothetical scenario in which viable HIV entered a food product and survived to the point of consumption, gastric acid (pH approximately 1.5 to 3.5 in the fasting stomach) would destroy the viral envelope before any infectious material could reach the intestinal mucosa. HIV requires direct access to receptors on CD4+ T cells or dendritic cells, which are present in the intestinal mucosa but not accessible via intact gastric epithelium to a lumen-dwelling virus already inactivated by acid exposure.

**Epidemiological null finding**: No documented case of food-borne HIV transmission exists in the peer-reviewed literature, in public health surveillance data, or in WHO or CDC case registries, despite the virus affecting an estimated 38 million people globally as of 2020 (UNAIDS, 2021). This absence represents a strong epidemiological refutation in addition to the mechanistic biological arguments above: if food-borne HIV transmission were possible, even at very low efficiency, the scale of the global epidemic would have produced observable cases by now.

**Alcohol context**: The Capriccio sangria's high alcohol content — noted in the viral social media discussion that preceded the hoax — is itself a powerful antiviral agent. Alcohol at concentrations above approximately 20 percent is virucidal; ethanol disrupts lipid envelopes and destroys HIV infectivity with high efficiency (Hernandez et al., 2008, *Journal of Infectious Diseases*). The same property that makes hand sanitizer effective against enveloped viruses applies in principle to an alcoholic beverage. While the concentration of ethanol in Capriccio sangria (approximately 13 percent) is lower than in clinical antiviral applications, the combined effects of ethanol, low pH (wine pH typically 3.0–3.6), and absence of any medium capable of sustaining viral viability render the contamination scenario biologically implausible at every stage.

## §5 — Comparative Misinformation Analysis

The Capriccio sangria HIV claim is a member of one of the most persistent misinformation families in documented fact-checking history. Snopes's own archive contains numerous entries addressing HIV food-contamination claims across multiple decades and multiple product types. Understanding why this genre persists despite repeated refutation is as important as understanding why any individual instance of it is false.

Several structural features distinguish persistent misinformation families from isolated hoaxes. First, persistent families address claims about topics where fear is high and baseline scientific literacy is low. HIV transmission routes are not well understood by the general public; surveys have repeatedly found significant percentages of respondents believing transmission is possible through casual contact (Perloff, 2014, *Journal of Health Communication*). Uncertainty about what HIV can and cannot do creates epistemic space that hoaxes can fill.

Second, persistent families use narrative frames that are emotionally compelling and socially contagious. The food-contamination narrative evokes disgust, the most easily transmissible of negative emotions in viral content research. Tybur et al. (2013, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*) documented disgust's role in pathogen avoidance and found that disgust-coded information spreads more reliably in social networks than neutral or even fear-coded information. A story about something in the food supply that could transmit a feared disease is maximally disgust-coded.

Third, each new iteration of the genre gains false plausibility from the previous iterations. Someone who encountered an earlier HIV food-contamination story might remember not having thorough information about why it was false, and apply that residual uncertainty to the current version. This is the mechanism Nyhan & Reifler (2010, *Political Behavior*) studied in the context of corrections: corrections can leave residual trace memories of the original false claim that retain some influence on subsequent evaluations of related claims.

The specific targeting of Capriccio sangria rather than another food product is not arbitrary. The drink's viral cultural moment — specifically the context of mysterious unexplained intoxication that participants attributing to "secret ingredients" or supernatural agency — pre-positioned the product for conspiratorial extension. Allegations that a product has hidden ingredients that affect consumers adversely, combined with a beverage format (a container with a sealed label, where contents are opaque), created exactly the right conditions for a contamination claim to spread.

## §6 — Verdict Summary and Guidance

Snopes's **False** rating is unambiguously correct and supported by both immediate evidentiary analysis (the WFLD screenshot is fabricated) and foundational scientific knowledge (HIV cannot survive in food or beverage environments).

The claim's falsehood is not probabilistic — it does not depend on uncertainties in ongoing research or contested interpretations of evidence. HIV's inability to transmit via food is not a hypothesis awaiting further testing. It is a well-established biological fact with mechanistic, epidemiological, and clinical support spanning more than four decades of HIV research since the virus was first characterized in 1983.

**For readers encountering this claim:** a claim of this type should be evaluated with extreme skepticism when it: (1) originates from a social media screenshot of a news image rather than a link to verifiable broadcast content; (2) attaches a new pathogen claim to a product already experiencing unrelated viral attention; and (3) makes claims directly refuted by standing WHO and CDC public guidance. The HIV-in-food hoax genre is one of the most thoroughly documented recurring misinformation categories, and any new iteration of it should be presumed false absent extraordinary evidence.

The lasting public health harm from claims of this type is twofold: they spread unfounded fear about a specific product or category, and they undermine public understanding of actual HIV transmission and prevention. When people believe HIV can spread through food, they may simultaneously overestimate risks from food and underestimate risks from documented transmission routes, distorting their actual public health behavior.

**Epistemic gap flagged:** The psychological and sociological mechanisms that sustain the multi-decade persistence of the HIV food-contamination hoax genre merit further systematic study. Why do corrections fail to permanently reduce this genre's recurrence? Research on "genre-level" versus "claim-level" misinformation correction is limited, and the HIV food hoax family would be a productive subject for longitudinal study of correction persistence.
