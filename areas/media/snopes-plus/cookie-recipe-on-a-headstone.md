## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

Around the Thanksgiving holiday in 2022, a photograph of a carved stone gravestone bearing a cookie recipe circulated widely on social media platforms. The photograph showed a gravestone featuring a written recipe for Christmas cookies—cream, sugar, oleo, eggs, vanilla, flour, baking powder, salt, cream, frost—engraved on its reverse face. The image was accompanied by text asserting: *"Backside of mom's gravestone. Everytime someone asked for her cookie recipe, she said, 'Over my dead body!' " The meme positioned the gravestone as a darkly comic fulfillment of this maternal vow—a woman who guarded her recipe in life had made it public only in death, finally sharing it literally "over her dead body."*

Snopes journalist Bethania Palma (2022) investigated the claim and assigned it a rating of **Mixture**. Two claims are embedded in the meme:

- **True**: The photograph of the headstone is real. A gravestone engraved with a cookie recipe exists in Cascade, Iowa.
- **False**: The accompanying narrative—that the deceased had jealously guarded her recipe as a secret, vowing to share it only "over my dead body"—is fabricated. The recipe was not secret during the woman's lifetime, and the "over my dead body" framing was apparently invented as a humorous meme overlay, not a reflection of any actual family anecdote.

The gravestone belongs to Maxine Menster, who died in September 1994. Her daughter Jane Menster and her father added the recipe to the gravestone as a memorial to Maxine's generosity—not as a posthumous disclosure of a carefully guarded secret. Jane was quoted in *The Gazette* (2012): "Absolutely not. Mom was a very generous person. This was a sentimental thing between my father and I." When asked whether the recipe had been a secret, Jane's response was unambiguous.

## §2 The Menster Gravestone and Its Actual Family Context

The history of the Menster gravestone is documented by two Iowa newspapers, both of which covered the story in December 2012—approximately ten years before the meme's 2022 viral resurgence. The *Waterloo-Cedar Falls Courier* published a brief news item about the gravestone, and *The Gazette* (a Cedar Rapids-area newspaper) published a more detailed feature by journalist Dave Rasdal, both citing interviews with Jane Menster, the daughter of the deceased.

According to these reports, Maxine Menster died in September 1994 in Cascade, Iowa. After her death, Jane and her father decided that Maxine's Christmas cookie recipe would serve as an apt and loving memorial—a way to keep her presence alive not just in carved stone but in the kitchens and holiday celebrations of anyone who visited the grave and copied down the recipe. *The Gazette* article included the recipe in full, demonstrating that there was no attempt on anyone's part to maintain it as proprietary information; the family actively shared it with newspaper readers in 2012.

The recipe itself—"Mom's Christmas Cookies"—calls for creaming 1 cup sugar with 1/2 cup oleo (margarine), adding 2 beaten eggs and 1 teaspoon vanilla, then 3 cups flour, 3 teaspoons baking powder, and 1 teaspoon salt, added alternately with 1 cup cream; the dough is chilled, rolled out, baked at 350 degrees, and frosted. This is a straightforward rolled sugar cookie recipe in the Iowa midwestern tradition—simple, good, and clearly a recipe meant to be shared rather than hoarded.

The "over my dead body" narrative framing therefore represents a fiction imposed on a real, emotionally meaningful object. The gravestone is genuine, the recipe is genuine, the love behind the memorial is genuine. What is false is the specific narrative of secret-keeping—the idea that Maxine was a woman who jealously guarded this recipe and whose family could only disclose it by carving it in stone after she died. Jane Menster's response ("Absolutely not. Mom was a very generous person.") suggests that the fabricated narrative was not just inaccurate but actively misrepresents her mother's character.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Fictional Narrative Overlay on a Real Artifact

The Menster cookie headstone case represents a specific and important category of viral image misinformation: **fictional narrative overlay on a verified real artifact**. The photograph circulating in 2022 was not manipulated or AI-generated—it was an authentic image of an existing, verifiable object in a real Iowa cemetery. The falsehood is not in the image itself but in the story that was attached to the image as it circulated online.

This category of misinformation is epistemically distinct from fabricated images or falsely captioned archival photographs (where the image itself is misrepresented). In narrative overlay cases, the image is real and verifiable, but the narrative context that gives the image its claimed meaning is fabricated. Verifying the image itself—even visiting the gravestone in person—would confirm that the photograph is authentic without confirming that the accompanying story is true. The misinformation survives empirical verification of the artifact.

This structure creates a specific verification challenge. The standard approach to image verification—reverse image search, geolocation, source identification—confirms the image's authenticity but does not address the narrative claim, because the narrative claim is independent of the object photographed. A fact-checker who stopped at "the photograph is authentic" would report an incorrect verdict.

Palma's investigation required a second epistemic layer: tracking down the human sources—Jane Menster and the Iowa newspaper reporting from 2012—who had direct knowledge of the actual circumstances behind the gravestone's creation. This represents genealogical fact-checking in a broad sense: tracing the object through its human chain of custody to establish the circumstances of its creation.

The broader category is well-represented in the literature on visual misinformation. Wardle and Derakhshan (2017, *Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policy Making*, Council of Europe report) distinguish between seven categories of information disorder, of which the most relevant here is "false context"—where genuine content is paired with false contextual information. In the typology of Wardle and Derakhshan, the Menster case is a "false context" instance: the image is genuine, the contextual claim (the "over my dead body" narrative) is false.

The epistemic novelty specific to this case is that the false context is not a political or partisan manipulation but a **humor-motivated narrative invention**. The "over my dead body" pun is genuinely witty—it represents the kind of wordplay that thrives on internet culture, where a funny story that could-have-been becomes a story that people want to be true. This is a form of what Knapp (1944, "A Psychology of Rumor," *Public Opinion Quarterly*) called **wish fulfillment spreading**: the rumor propagates because it satisfies an emotional or aesthetic desire (in this case, the desire for the wit of the pun to be historically real). The story is compelling not because it is scary or alarming but because it is charming. Charming false stories are a curiously underanalyzed category in misinformation research, which has tended to focus on alarming or politically motivated false narratives.

## §4 Grave Memorialization Practices and Food as Memorial

The Menster gravestone sits within a long history of using food—specifically, recipes—as memorial texts and objects of remembrance. While carved headstones bearing complete recipes are unusual, they represent an extreme end of a spectrum of food-memorial practices that include recipe inheritance, community cookbook memorial sections, and the use of food preparation as a primary vehicle for transmitting intergenerational cultural memory.

Visser (1991, *The Rituals of Dinner*, Grove Press) has written about the deep connection between food and social identity, arguing that shared meals and the transmission of food knowledge represent one of the primary mechanisms by which communities and families maintain continuity across generations. Recipes, in this context, function as condensed cultural memory objects—they encode not just instructions for preparing food but the specific sensory, emotional, and social contexts in which a dish was prepared and shared.

The use of a recipe as a gravestone inscription is therefore not as eccentric as it might first appear. It is a logical extension of the practice of memorializing a person through the most distinctive feature of their contribution to family life. For a woman whose love and generosity were most fully expressed in her Christmas cookie baking—in the kitchen labor, the holiday sharing, the child-feeding and guest-welcome dimensions of her domestic practice—a recipe inscription captures something essential that a conventional epitaph ("beloved mother and grandmother") might not.

Sørensen (2007, "The Grammar of Grammar: A Classic Paper Revisited," *Mortality*) has written on the semiotics of gravestone inscription conventions, noting that unusual or individualized inscriptions—as opposed to conventional formulae—typically signal strong surviving-family agency and specific relational meaning that cannot be adequately expressed through conventional language. The Menster recipe inscription fits this pattern: it expresses something specific about Maxine's character and relationships that no conventional inscription could convey.

The false "over my dead body" narrative is ironic in the specific sense that it takes a memorial object created to express generosity and transforms it into a memorial object expressing possessiveness. The fabricated story, in this sense, performs the opposite of an accurate characterization: it inverts Maxine Menster's actual character (generous, willing to share) into its negation (secretive, withholding). Jane Menster's correction—"Mom was a very generous person"—is therefore not simply a factual correction but a defense of her mother's posthumous reputation against a meme that inadvertently defamed her.

## §5 Viral Revival Patterns in Gravestone and Cemetery Photography

The 2022 virality of the Menster cookie headstone photograph—approximately ten years after the story first received local newspaper coverage—illustrates the temporal dynamics of internet image virality, where objects photographed and initially publicized years or even decades earlier can achieve new viral life when rediscovered and recirculated in new contexts.

Gravestone photography, as a genre, has experienced substantial growth on social media platforms. Photography in cemeteries has a long art-historical tradition (from Victorian mourning photography through mid-20th century "rubbing" practices to contemporary Instagram aesthetics), and unusual or visually distinctive gravestones have become a recognized internet content category. Platforms including Reddit, Twitter/X, and Facebook host recurring communities and threads dedicated to "unusual gravestones," "funny epitaphs," and similar cemetery photography collections. The Menster gravestone—with its unusual recipe inscription, clearly legible in a well-composed photograph—was an ideal candidate for discovery by these communities.

When the image was rediscovered and circulated in 2022, it was likely encountered by many or most viewers who had no knowledge of the 2012 *Gazette* and *Courier* coverage and therefore had no contextual anchoring for the image. Without that context, the humorous "over my dead body" narrative overlay would have seemed plausible—indeed, it provides a satisfying and complete narrative explanation for an otherwise puzzling object. Why would a family have a recipe carved on a gravestone? The "over my dead body" story answers this question in a way that is both logical and amusing. The true explanation—that a daughter and father wanted to memorialize a generous woman's most characteristic gift—is equally logical but less punchy.

This dynamic illustrates what Heath et al. called "concreteness" as a driver of message stickiness (*Made to Stick*, Heath & Heath, 2007). The recipe-on-a-headstone is an intensely concrete, specific object, and the "over my dead body" story links it to an equally concrete, specific narrative (a woman who refused to share her recipe and whose death became its ironic disclosure moment). The true story is also concrete but requires more emotional nuance—generosity, father-daughter sentiment, long-term memorial planning—that cannot be compressed into a punchy caption.

## §6 Summary, Epistemic Classification, and Gap Assessment

The cookie-recipe headstone claim is correctly rated **Mixture**: the photograph is genuine, the gravestone exists, and the recipe is real; but the "over my dead body" narrative is false, as established through direct communication with family members and documented in *The Gazette* (2012) and *Waterloo-Cedar Falls Courier* (2012). Maxine Menster was a generous woman whose recipe was never secret; the headstone inscription was placed by her daughter and husband as a memorial to her generosity, not as a posthumous disclosure of a jealously guarded secret.

The **EARC gap** classification is **C (Contextual)**: the central epistemic task is supplying the genuine historical and human context that was absent from—and displaced by—the fabricated narrative overlay. The relevant archival sources (Iowa newspaper coverage from 2012) were not easily accessible to a social media user encountering the image in 2022, making this a genuine information gap as well as a rumor case.

The case also presents a minor but notable complication for **posthumous reputation**: the fabricated narrative misrepresented Maxine Menster's character by attributing to her a habit (secrecy and possessiveness about her recipe) that her surviving family explicitly rejected. Fact-checking involving misrepresentation of deceased individuals raises questions about the scope of reputation protection and the ethical responsibilities of viral content creation that attach false narratives to real private individuals (rather than public figures), a topic treated in Peters (2009, "Privacy, Property and the Dead," *Journal of Applied Philosophy*).

**Epistemic class: partial-myth.** The visual artifact is genuine; the narrative is invented. The correct characterization requires both affirming the reality of the object and refuting the story attached to it—a dual-layer fact-check that is characteristic of the "false context" misinformation category.
