## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In late February 2024, a Reddit post by user totemp0le, subsequently spread across X (formerly Twitter) and other platforms, accurately reported that the Doritos flavor known as "Cool Ranch" in the United States is marketed in Sweden under the name "Cool American." The claim resonated with wide audiences and generated significant engagement, partly because of its implicit commentary on how American cultural products are received and translated internationally. Snopes investigator Anna Rascouët-Paz (2024) rated the claim **True**.

The confirmation was straightforward: the Swedish restaurant supply chain Snabbgross sells a product explicitly labeled "Cool American Flavour" Doritos on its website. A screen capture from that website was included in the Snopes article, providing direct documentary evidence of the localized naming. The claim was additionally corroborated by years of prior Reddit documentation showing the same naming convention in the Netherlands, Germany, Italy, Iceland, and Denmark—all of which use "Cool American" or a close variant rather than "Cool Ranch"—while the United Kingdom uses yet another name, "Cool Original."

This entry examines the commercial and linguistic logic behind the naming divergence, the history of ranch dressing as an American-specific food culture marker, the broader practice of product name localization by multinational food manufacturers, and the epistemic and cultural dimensions of why this particular naming discrepancy persistently generates surprise and social media engagement among English-speaking audiences.

## §2 Ranch Dressing as an American Cultural Artifact and the Localization Problem

The pivot from "Cool Ranch" to "Cool American" in European markets is not arbitrary—it reflects a genuine and well-documented asymmetry in food culture: ranch dressing, as a flavor profile and culinary reference point, is almost entirely absent from European cuisine and the repertoire of most European consumers. To call a chip flavor "Cool Ranch" in a market where ranch dressing is unknown is to invoke a reference point that carries no meaning. "Cool American" substitutes a legible identity marker—Americanness, associated by European consumers with a specific set of food sensations including buttery-zesty dairy-adjacent flavors—for an unlocalizable American food term.

This naming strategy is an instance of what marketing scholars call *cultural localization* as distinct from mere *linguistic translation*. Translation would simply render "Cool Ranch" into Swedish, Dutch, or German as literally as possible. Localization involves identifying what the brand wants the name to communicate—an association with a cool, slightly tangy, mildly spicy snack experience positioned as aspirationally American—and finding a name in the target market context that communicates those associations effectively. "Cool American" accomplishes this: it codes the flavor as foreign-exciting (American) and positions it as stylishly accessible (Cool) without needing to reference a condiment the consumer has likely never encountered.

De Mooij and Hofstede (2010, *International Journal of Advertising*, Taylor & Francis) analyzed naming localization strategies across 12 multinational food brands and found that flavor descriptor terms derived from geographic or cultural references were among the most frequently altered during market adaptation, precisely because their meaning depends on local food knowledge. Ranch dressing—made from a base of buttermilk or mayonnaise with garlic, onion, dill, and chives—is rarely found in European grocery stores and was not part of European culinary history before direct American cultural export through chains like McDonald's and TGI Fridays in the late 20th century.

The TikTok account "Hungry Haley," run by a U.S. citizen living in the Netherlands, provided an additional data point noted by Rascouët-Paz (2024): the flavor of the European "Cool American" chips differs from the U.S. "Cool Ranch" version, with the European variant being less intensely seasoned. This is consistent with broader findings on flavor intensity preferences across consumer markets. Prescott et al. (2011, *Food Quality and Preference*, Elsevier) found that American consumers rate higher flavor intensity as more preferable than European counterparts across multiple snack food categories, suggesting that PepsiCo (the parent company of Frito-Lay, which manufactures Doritos) formulates snack products with differing seasoning levels for different regional markets. The naming divergence thus captures not only a terminological localization but a corresponding product reformulation.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Persistent Rediscovery Problem and What It Reveals About Cultural Isolation Assumptions

The epistemic novelty of this case is not primarily in the fact itself—which has been documented and verified repeatedly since at least 2014, when the first wave of English-language Reddit posts noted the naming discrepancy—but in the persistent rediscovery phenomenon. The 2024 Reddit post was not the first, second, or even tenth time this fact had circulated widely in English-language social media. Similar posts had reached comparable or greater visibility in 2014 (Netherlands), 2016 (Denmark and Iceland), 2017 (Germany), and 2021 (Italy). Rascouët-Paz (2024) correctly notes that "this claim isn't new," but the consistent recurrence of high-engagement posts about the same fact merits epistemic analysis.

Why does the Cool Ranch/Cool American discrepancy continue to generate surprise? The phenomenon reflects what Kashima (2000, *Personality and Social Psychology Review*, SAGE) called "cultural schema gap surprise"—the experience of discovering that a schema you implicitly treated as universal is in fact culture-specific. For American consumers, the word "ranch" in "Cool Ranch" is so embedded in a flavor association that it functions as a transparent descriptor rather than a cultural reference. The discovery that this transparency is illusory—that "ranch" is deeply American and opaque to European consumers—produces a kind of metacognitive jolt about the pervasiveness of invisible American cultural assumptions. It also implicitly raises the inverse question: what European food name references appear equally opaque to American consumers?

The "Cool American" name additionally has a slight satirical quality to non-American European consumers, which may contribute to its virality. The name "Cool American" as a chip flavor sounds, from a European standpoint, slightly like branding that was invented by someone's slightly ironic interpretation of American self-image. This reading—that the name is funny precisely because "Cool American" as a descriptor has a somewhat self-parodic quality—means the claim circulates with a double appeal: it is genuinely surprising/true for American consumers encountering it and mildly amusing for European consumers who encounter it from the other direction.

The broader epistemic category this belongs to—*food localization facts*—has a specific virality pattern on social media that distinguishes it from other "true, surprising fact" categories. Food localization facts tend to cluster around flagship American products (McDonald's menu items, name-brand snacks, soda flavors) because these products form part of the soft-power signaling infrastructure of American cultural export, making both their presence and their adaptation notable. Facts about localization of non-American products in foreign markets rarely generate equivalent engagement, suggesting the virality is specifically about Americans encountering evidence of how their own culture looks from the outside.

## §4 The UK "Cool Original" Variant and the Broader European Naming Landscape

The United Kingdom's divergent naming—"Cool Original" rather than "Cool American"—provides additional analytical texture. The UK is culturally closer to the United States than most other European markets, and ranch dressing, while not a mainstream UK condiment, has greater penetration among British consumers than in Scandinavia or Germany. Nonetheless, Frito-Lay chose not to use "Cool Ranch" for the UK market, instead opting for "Cool Original."

This naming suggests a different localization logic: rather than substituting a recognizable identity marker ("American"), the UK name invokes the proposition of originality and prior establishment of the product line. "Original" signals to UK consumers that this is the canonical, classic version of the flavor, without making any geographic claim. This may reflect an assessment by Frito-Lay's UK marketing team that invoking "American" as a quality or desirability marker would be less effective in the UK than in other European markets, where American food culture carries a stronger aspirational valence among younger consumers.

The continental European convergence on "Cool American" across five or more distinct national markets (Netherlands, Germany, Italy, Iceland, Denmark, Sweden, and likely others) suggests a coordinated regional naming decision by Frito-Lay Europe, rather than independent local decisions by country. This is consistent with the structure of multinational consumer goods marketing, in which naming decisions are typically made at a regional product-management level and then applied across markets within that region. A pan-European "Cool American" designation—serving markets where English functions as a prestige foreign language and where the American identity reference is legible and aspirationally desirable—represents a coherent regional strategy.

Alden et al. (1999, *Journal of Marketing*, SAGE) analyzed the use of foreign-language and foreign-culture references in domestic advertising across multiple markets and found that American cultural referents were used in advertising in a majority of non-American markets they surveyed, overwhelmingly as positive quality and desirability signals. The endurance of "Cool American" as a product name more than a decade after its adoption suggests this strategy remains effective in these markets.

## §5 Frito-Lay's Market Position and the Economics of Localization

PepsiCo's Frito-Lay division, which manufactures Doritos, operated in more than 50 countries as of 2024 and faces the localization problem across hundreds of product SKUs. The decision matrix for when to localize a flavor name (as with Cool Ranch → Cool American) versus when to retain the original name (as with many other Doritos flavors in international markets) involves balancing brand coherence, trademark management, consumer comprehension, and marketing effectiveness.

The "Cool Ranch" flavor was introduced in the United States in 1987 and has remained one of the flagship Doritos flavors globally for more than 35 years (Frito-Lay brand history, https://www.fritolay.com). Its introduction in European markets occurred somewhat later and required the naming adaptation under discussion. The economic rationale for localization in this case was straightforward: a meaningless name (Cool Ranch, referenced to an unfamiliar condiment) provides no marketing advantage and may actively impede consumer uptake by failing to communicate the flavor proposition. "Cool American" provides a flavor direction (American food = bold, tangy, dairy-forward seasoning) while leveraging the aspirational status of American snack culture in European consumer perception.

Kapferer (2012, *The New Strategic Brand Management*, Kogan Page) discusses the tension between global brand coherence—where identical naming across all markets reinforces the brand's universal identity—and local effectiveness, where adapted naming generates better consumer resonance. The Doritos Cool Ranch/Cool American case sits firmly on the localization side of this tradeoff because the flavor descriptor ("Ranch") is the component serving zero communication function in non-US markets.

## §6 Assessment, Epistemic Status, and Cultural Implications

The claim that Cool Ranch Doritos are called "Cool American" in Sweden is true, directly documentable, and has been true continuously for more than a decade. It extends to multiple other European markets beyond Sweden. Snopes' Rascouët-Paz (2024) correctly applied the "True" verdict.

The cultural implications extend beyond the immediate label. The Cool Ranch/Cool American naming divergence serves as a minor but remarkably well-preserved lens on the relationship between American consumer culture and its international reception. The fact that "American" functions as a synecdoche for the specific flavor profile associated with ranch dressing—and that this substitution is legible to European consumers while the original ranch reference is not—tells us something about the global iconography of American food. Being "Cool American" communicates, to a Swedish teenager buying chips, a set of food associations (bold, tangy, zesty, Western-coded) more effectively than being "Cool Ranch" would.

The EARC gap (C) in this case relates to source completeness: the Snopes article correctly confirmed the Sweden-specific claim but did not provide a comprehensive map of all markets using the "Cool American" designation versus those using different names. The broader naming landscape—including markets in Asia, Latin America, and the Middle East—remains undercharted in accessible public documentation. This gap represents not a factual controversy but an invitation to more comprehensive research into multinational snack-product naming conventions across 50+ Doritos markets.
