# Coronavirus: It's Time to Debunk Claims That Vitamin C Could Cure It


## 1. The Claim

During the early months of the COVID-19 pandemic, social media and alternative health platforms promoted the idea that high-dose vitamin C could prevent or cure COVID-19. These claims proliferated rapidly in March 2020, as lockdowns were being implemented across the globe and the public was desperate for any measure that might offer protection against the emerging disease. Proponents ranged from wellness influencers marketing supplement products to physicians with alternative medical perspectives, and the claims varied in specificity from "vitamin C supports the immune system" to "mega-doses of vitamin C can cure coronavirus infections."

Snopes addressed these claims on March 9, 2020, by republishing an article originally written by Peter McCaffery for The Conversation. The article was published in Snopes' "News" section rather than as a staff-generated fact check, and it carried a prominent disclaimer: "This article originally appeared on The Conversation. This content is shared here because the topic may interest Snopes readers; it does not, however, represent the work of Snopes fact-checkers or editors." The article concluded that there was little evidence that vitamin C could prevent or cure COVID-19, given that coronaviruses are related to the viruses that cause the common cold, where vitamin C's efficacy has not been demonstrated.

The Snopes article's central framing—"it's time to debunk claims that vitamin C could cure it"—established the claim as a clear case of misinformation that could be resolved through straightforward reference to the scientific literature. The article cited the history of Linus Pauling's promotion of vitamin C for the common cold, the lack of evidence from clinical trials, and the economic interests of the dietary supplements industry as factors driving the claims. The tone was authoritative and final: the question of vitamin C and COVID-19 was closed.

But the scientific picture is more complex than this framing suggests. While the claim that oral vitamin C supplements can prevent or cure COVID-19 in the general population is indeed unsupported by evidence, the question of vitamin C's potential role in acute COVID-19 management—particularly in hospitalized patients with severe disease—became the subject of legitimate clinical research that the Snopes article's categorical dismissal does not capture. The distinction between prevention, early treatment, and critical care management, and between oral and intravenous administration, are not semantic distinctions but reflect fundamentally different biological mechanisms and clinical contexts.


## 2. What's Actually True

The Snopes article makes several claims that are supported by the evidence as it stood in March 2020.

Vitamin C does not prevent coronavirus infection in the general population. The evidence for vitamin C's effectiveness in preventing the common cold—which is caused by viruses distinct from but related to SARS-CoV-2—is weak. A 2013 Cochrane review by Hemilä and Chalker concluded that regular vitamin C supplementation does not reduce the incidence of colds in the general population, though it may modestly reduce the duration and severity of symptoms once a cold has begun. Extrapolating from this evidence to COVID-19 is reasonable: there is no basis for expecting that oral vitamin C supplementation would prevent SARS-CoV-2 infection.

The claim that vitamin C can "cure" COVID-19 is also unsupported. No clinical trial has demonstrated that vitamin C, whether administered orally or intravenously, can eliminate SARS-CoV-2 infection once established. The word "cure" implies a therapeutic intervention that eliminates the disease, and no evidence supports this claim for vitamin C in the context of COVID-19 or any other viral respiratory infection.

The history of vitamin C promotion is correctly summarized. Linus Pauling, the double Nobel laureate, championed high-dose vitamin C for the common cold in the 1970s based on preliminary evidence that subsequent clinical trials failed to confirm. The dietary supplements industry has continued to promote vitamin C for immune health, generating billions in revenue from products whose efficacy in preventing infection in well-nourished populations is not supported by rigorous evidence. The Snopes article's identification of this commercial context is accurate and relevant.

The article correctly notes that vitamin C is important for immune function through its role in maintaining redox balance in tissues, supporting collagen synthesis, and potentially protecting cells from damage caused by reactive oxygen species. These functions are well established in biochemistry and nutritional science. Vitamin C deficiency—scurvy—is a serious condition that impairs immune function and wound healing. The claim that vitamin C is irrelevant to immune health would be as inaccurate as the claim that it cures viral infections.

However, there are important dimensions that the Snopes article oversimplifies or omits. The distinction between oral and intravenous vitamin C is critical. Oral vitamin C has limited bioavailability: the body absorbs only a fraction of an oral dose, and plasma concentrations reach a ceiling at doses above approximately 200 mg per day. Intravenous vitamin C, by contrast, can achieve plasma concentrations tens to hundreds of times higher than oral administration. These high concentrations have different biological effects, including pro-oxidant activity that may be relevant in the context of severe infections. The Snopes article discusses vitamin C as if it were a single intervention, when oral and IV administration constitute fundamentally different therapeutic modalities.

The article also does not distinguish between prevention in the general population and treatment in hospitalized patients. The claim that vitamin C prevents COVID-19 in healthy individuals is indeed unsupported. But the claim that high-dose IV vitamin C might benefit hospitalized COVID-19 patients, particularly those with sepsis or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), was a legitimate scientific hypothesis that multiple clinical trials subsequently investigated. Several randomized controlled trials have examined IV vitamin C in COVID-19, with mixed but not uniformly negative results. The LOVIT-COVID trial (2022) found no benefit and possible harm from high-dose IV vitamin C in critically ill COVID-19 patients. But smaller trials and meta-analyses have suggested potential benefits in specific subpopulations or when combined with other interventions such as thiamine and hydrocortisone (the "metabolic resuscitation" protocol).

The article's reliance on the common cold analogy is methodologically problematic. While SARS-CoV-2 is a coronavirus, as are many common cold viruses, the diseases are fundamentally different. COVID-19 is characterized by a dysregulated immune response—the cytokine storm—that contributes to tissue damage in severe cases. Vitamin C's role as an antioxidant and immune modulator might be mechanistically relevant in this context in ways that are not captured by the common cold analogy. The article implies that the evidence against vitamin C for the common cold directly applies to COVID-19, when in fact the two conditions have different pathophysiology and the question of vitamin C's role in each should be evaluated independently.


## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes' decision to republish an external article rather than produce an original staff-generated fact check is itself a significant editorial choice that deserves examination. The article by Peter McCaffery, a professor of medical biochemistry at the University of Aberdeen, is clearly written and summarizes the relevant basic science accurately. Its conclusion—that vitamin C is not a cure for COVID-19—is consistent with the evidence as it stood in March 2020 and remains consistent with the evidence today.

But the article's categorical dismissal of any potential role for vitamin C in COVID-19 management went beyond the evidence available at the time and has been partially superseded by subsequent research. The article states that "using vitamin C to treat the common cold was an idea popularised by double Nobel Prize-winning chemist, Linus Pauling, and further promoted by the dietary supplements industry. Unfortunately, ever since Pauling's claim in the 1970s, there has been little evidence to back it up." This framing, which equates the common cold claims with COVID-19 claims and treats the entire vitamin C question as a settled matter of quackery, oversimplifies a complex evidence base.

The article does not mention that intravenous vitamin C has been studied for decades in critical care medicine, long before COVID-19. A 2014 meta-analysis of IV vitamin C in sepsis and critical illness found potential benefits including reduced organ failure and shorter ICU stays. The 2017 CITRIS-ALI trial examined IV vitamin C in patients with sepsis and ARDS and found no significant difference in the primary outcome (organ failure scores) but did find reduced mortality—though this was a secondary outcome and the trial was small. These studies were available before March 2020 and would have contextualized the COVID-19 vitamin C question within a pre-existing research program rather than treating it as a novel claim driven entirely by supplement industry marketing.

The article also fails to address the dose-response relationship that is critical to understanding vitamin C's potential therapeutic role. The claim that "mega-doses" of vitamin C have no therapeutic effect is contradicted by the basic pharmacology of the compound: at the plasma concentrations achievable only through IV administration, vitamin C acts as a pro-oxidant that can generate hydrogen peroxide in extracellular fluid, potentially killing bacteria and viruses. This mechanism is biologically plausible and has been demonstrated in vitro. Whether this mechanism translates to clinical benefit in COVID-19 is a separate question that can only be answered through clinical trials—but the article dismisses the possibility without engaging with the mechanistic evidence.

The Snopes article does not address the political economy of supplement regulation and public health messaging during a pandemic. When public health authorities tell the public that there is nothing they can do to protect themselves beyond hygiene measures and social distancing—as was the case in March 2020—the public naturally seeks out any measure that offers a sense of agency. Vitamin C, zinc, vitamin D, and other supplements fill this psychological vacuum. The public's turn toward supplements is not merely a failure of scientific literacy but an understandable response to the anxiety generated by a pandemic in which official guidance offered little in the way of personal protective action. Dismissing this behavior as credulity toward quackery ignores the psychological context in which it occurs.

The most significant limitation of the Snopes article, however, is that it was not original fact-checking but a republication of an academic opinion piece. The disclaimer stating that the article "does not represent the work of Snopes fact-checkers or editors" is prominently placed, but many readers will not distinguish between Snopes' fact-checking content and its republished news content. The article appears on Snopes' domain, carries Snopes' branding, and reaches Snopes' audience. The distinction between a fact check (which applies Snopes' rigorous research methodology) and a republished article (which reflects the author's academic expertise but not Snopes' editorial process) may be invisible to the average reader. This matters because the article's categorical dismissal of vitamin C's potential role may have discouraged legitimate research into IV vitamin C in COVID-19 by presenting the question as settled.


## 4. Why This Matters

The vitamin C and COVID-19 question illustrates several fundamental challenges in science communication during public health emergencies: the tension between protecting the public from false hope and prematurely closing off legitimate avenues of scientific inquiry; the difference between population-level evidence and individual-patient considerations; and the difficulty of communicating scientific uncertainty in a format designed for decisive verdicts.

The first, and most consequential, concerns the relationship between debunking misinformation and foreclosing legitimate scientific inquiry. During the early months of the COVID-19 pandemic, scientists and public health officials faced an unprecedented challenge: rapidly evolving evidence about a novel disease, desperate public demand for interventions, and a flood of unsubstantiated claims about everything from hydroxychloroquine to bleach. In this environment, the impulse to say "this has been debunked" was understandable. But debunking is a dangerous game when the evidence is evolving. The claim that "vitamin C has been debunked" in March 2020 may have been accurate for the specific claim that oral vitamin C prevents infection, but it was premature for the broader question of whether IV vitamin C has any role in COVID-19 treatment. When subsequent trials produced mixed results, those who had been told the question was settled were left confused or distrustful.

The second concerns the difference between evidence of absence and absence of evidence. The Snopes article implies that the long history of vitamin C research has definitively demonstrated that it has no meaningful therapeutic role. But this overstates the state of the evidence, particularly for IV administration in critical care. The evidence base for IV vitamin C in sepsis and ARDS was, in March 2020, suggestive but inconclusive. The claim that vitamin C "can cure COVID-19" is hyperbole that no responsible scientist would endorse. But the claim that vitamin C is worthless in any clinical context is equally an overstatement. The middle ground—vitamin C may have a role in certain clinical contexts, particularly IV administration in critically ill patients, and this question deserves rigorous investigation—is more accurate but less satisfying as a fact check.

The third concerns the role of nutritional interventions in pandemic preparedness and response. The biomedical establishment has historically been skeptical of nutritional approaches to disease management, partly because the evidence base for individual nutrients is often weak, partly because the supplement industry has promoted exaggerated claims, and partly because nutritional interventions are not patentable and therefore attract less research funding than pharmaceutical interventions. This skepticism is often justified—the supplement industry does make unsupported claims—but it can also lead to the dismissal of legitimate nutritional science. The COVID-19 pandemic saw a resurgence of interest in nutritional approaches to immune function, including vitamin D, zinc, and vitamin C. Some of this interest was driven by commercial interests, but some reflected legitimate scientific questions that deserved investigation.

The fourth concerns the format of science communication during health emergencies. Snopes' model—short, decisive articles that classify claims into rating categories—is designed for correcting demonstrable falsehoods, not for communicating scientific uncertainty. The vitamin C question, like many questions during the early pandemic, was characterized by genuine uncertainty that no "True/False/Mixture" framework could adequately capture. The article that Snopes republished from The Conversation was more nuanced than a typical Snopes fact check, but its placement in the "News" section with a disclaimed relationship to Snopes' editorial process meant that readers may have treated it as carrying Snopes' authority while Snopes retained plausible deniability about the article's conclusions. This is not a good model for science communication in high-stakes environments.


## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article was originally published on The Conversation on March 9, 2020, authored by Peter McCaffery, Professor of Biochemistry at the University of Aberdeen. The article was republished on Snopes under The Conversation's Creative Commons license, which permits republication. Snopes added the disclaimer about the article not representing its fact-checkers' work. The article has not been updated to reflect subsequent clinical trial findings.

Several randomized controlled trials of vitamin C in COVID-19 have been published since March 2020. The largest and most methodologically rigorous is the LOVIT-COVID trial (Adhikari et al., 2022, JAMA), which randomized 872 critically ill COVID-19 patients to high-dose IV vitamin C or placebo. The trial found no benefit and a potential signal of harm (increased mortality at 28 days, though this did not reach statistical significance). A 2022 Cochrane review by Janani et al. concluded that the evidence for vitamin C in COVID-19 is of low certainty and does not support routine use. However, several smaller trials and meta-analyses have reported potential benefits in specific contexts. The "MATH+" protocol (methylprednisolone, ascorbic acid, thiamine, heparin) has been promoted by the Front Line COVID-19 Critical Care Alliance, though its supporting evidence has been criticized for methodological limitations.

The critical care literature on IV vitamin C in sepsis and ARDS predates COVID-19. Fowler et al. (2014, Journal of Translational Medicine) demonstrated the safety of high-dose IV vitamin C in sepsis patients. Marik et al. (2017, Chest) reported dramatically reduced mortality with the "HAT" protocol (hydrocortisone, ascorbic acid, thiamine) in a before-after study, though subsequent randomized trials did not replicate the findings. The failure of subsequent trials to confirm the initial promising results from observational studies and small trials is a recurring pattern in critical care research and does not necessarily mean the question is settled—it means the evidence is more complex than initial reports suggested.

The role of vitamin C in immune function is well supported by mechanistic evidence. Vitamin C accumulates in immune cells, particularly neutrophils, where it supports chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and microbial killing. It is required for the synthesis and function of lymphocytes. In states of deficiency—which are common in hospitalized patients—immune function is measurably impaired. The question is whether supplementation beyond adequacy provides additional benefit, which is a different question from the one posed by the Snopes article's framing of "can vitamin C cure COVID-19?"

The dietary supplements industry had global revenues of approximately $140 billion in 2020, with vitamin C representing a substantial share. The industry has a documented history of promoting products with misleading health claims. However, the existence of an industry with commercial interests does not determine the scientific validity of claims about its products. Good research on vitamin C is possible despite the commercial context, and bad research on vitamin C is possible in its absence. The article's identification of commercial interests as relevant context is appropriate; using that context to dismiss the research agenda entirely is not.


## 6. Verdict

**Classification**: **Distorted but Grounded — Oversimplified**

The claim that vitamin C can "cure" COVID-19 is unsupported by evidence and the Snopes article correctly communicates that oral vitamin C does not prevent or eliminate coronavirus infection. However, the article's categorical dismissal of any potential role for vitamin C in COVID-19 management oversimplifies a more nuanced evidence base and conflates oral prevention with intravenous therapy in critically ill patients.

**What Snopes gets right:**
- Oral vitamin C does not prevent COVID-19 in the general population
- No evidence supports the claim that vitamin C can cure COVID-19
- The history of vitamin C promotion by Linus Pauling and the supplement industry is accurately summarized
- Vitamin C is important for immune function but more does not necessarily confer more protection
- The conclusion is consistent with the evidence as it stood in March 2020

**What Snopes misses or oversimplifies:**
- The critical distinction between oral and intravenous vitamin C administration
- The difference between prevention in the general population and treatment in hospitalized patients
- The pre-existing evidence base for IV vitamin C in sepsis and critical care
- The mechanistic plausibility of high-dose IV vitamin C as an adjunctive therapy in ARDS
- The subsequent clinical trial evidence that has emerged since publication
- The psychological context of public demand for actionable preventive measures during the pandemic
- The format limitations of categorical verdicts for communicating evolving scientific evidence

The vitamin C and COVID-19 question demonstrates the challenge of science communication during public health emergencies: the need to correct demonstrable falsehoods while preserving space for legitimate scientific inquiry. The Snopes article, by treating a complex and evolving evidence base as settled, may have inadvertently performed the same function as the claims it sought to debunk—closing off inquiry rather than advancing it. The better approach would have been to distinguish clearly between the (unsupported) claim that oral vitamin C prevents COVID-19 and the (open) question of whether IV vitamin C has any role in the management of severe disease, communicating uncertainty where it exists rather than implying that the question is closed.
