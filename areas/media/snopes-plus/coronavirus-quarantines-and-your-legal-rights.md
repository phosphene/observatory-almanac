# Your Legal Rights During COVID-19 Quarantines and Lockdowns

## 1. The Claim

During the COVID-19 pandemic, and especially in the spring and autumn of 2020, a cluster of claims circulated widely on social media and in alternative media describing — or purporting to describe — the legal rights of American citizens subject to government-imposed quarantines, stay-at-home orders, business closures, and later, vaccine mandates. These claims ranged from clearly accurate summaries of existing constitutional doctrine to fabricated assertions about presidential emergency powers, to good-faith but oversimplified descriptions of complex and genuinely contested legal terrain.

Representative examples included: claims that the federal government could legally detain any citizen who refused a quarantine order; claims that no government in the United States could legally require anyone to stay home against their will; claims that governors were acting unconstitutionally in closing churches; claims that wearing masks could not be legally required; and claims that the entire lockdown framework amounted to unconstitutional martial law. The Snopes entry for this topic was developed as a consolidated resource in mid-2020 to address the most common of these claims with reference to the controlling legal framework.

The underlying legal question — what *are* your rights during a public health emergency? — is real, consequential, and considerably harder to answer simply than most popular coverage suggested. The pandemic generated genuine constitutional litigation at every level of the court system, and several significant cases produced rulings that modified or clarified pre-existing doctrine. The complete picture is one of legitimate government authority bounded by constitutional constraints that were actively being tested in real time.

## 2. What's Actually True

### The Baseline Legal Framework

American public health law vests the primary authority for managing disease outbreaks in state governments, not the federal executive. This arrangement derives from the traditional police powers of states — the broad constitutional authority recognised since the nineteenth century for states to regulate for the protection of public health, safety, and welfare. The controlling Supreme Court precedent remains *Jacobson v. Massachusetts* (1905), in which the Court upheld compulsory smallpox vaccination laws and established that individual liberty must yield to the common welfare in genuine public health emergencies, provided government action is not "arbitrary, oppressive, or unreasonable."

The federal government has separate but more limited public health authority. The Public Health Service Act authorises the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention to apprehend, detain, and conditionally release individuals reasonably believed to be infected with communicable diseases when arriving from foreign countries or moving between states. This authority, codified at 42 U.S.C. § 264, was the statutory basis for various federal quarantine orders during the pandemic. Domestic quarantine authority within states, however, belongs to state governments under their police powers.

State quarantine statutes vary substantially in their scope, procedures, and penalties. Most states have frameworks authorising health departments to isolate individuals who pose an ongoing public health risk, require reporting of communicable diseases, and order the closure of facilities posing contagion risks. The procedures under which these powers must be exercised — including notice requirements, hearing rights, and the right to seek judicial review — also vary and are frequently underdeveloped in state statutes that were not designed for the scale of a pandemic.

### Stay-at-Home Orders

The stay-at-home orders issued by governors across the United States in March and April 2020 were exercises of executive authority under state emergency management statutes rather than direct applications of quarantine law, which technically applies to individuals rather than populations. The legal basis for population-wide mobility restrictions was therefore somewhat novel in its specific application, even though the underlying police powers are well-established.

During the first wave of restrictions, very little serious constitutional litigation had been decided. Courts generally applied a "rational basis" standard of review to economic restrictions — the most deferential standard of constitutional review, which almost always results in upholding government action — while noting that stricter standards would apply to restrictions affecting fundamental rights or suspect classifications. This distinction became critical in challenges to restrictions on religious gatherings.

### Religious Gathering Restrictions

The most constitutionally significant litigation of the pandemic concerned restrictions on religious services. Early church closure orders and capacity limits on houses of worship were upheld by lower courts under *Jacobson*, with judges reasoning that government had broad latitude during a declared emergency. However, in *South Bay United Pentecostal Church v. Newsom* (2020), the Supreme Court initially declined to block California's church attendance limits — with Chief Justice Roberts writing a concurrence endorsing *Jacobson*'s deferential framework.

The jurisprudence shifted substantially in *Roman Catholic Diocese of Brooklyn v. Cuomo* (2020), decided by the Supreme Court in November 2020 on an emergency shadow docket application. A 5-4 majority granted an injunction against New York's restrictions on religious gatherings, with the majority reasoning that New York had imposed stricter limits on houses of worship than on comparable secular establishments such as acupiscine facilities, bike shops, and liquor stores — a distinction the Court found constitutionally impermissible under the *Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v. Hialeah* (1993) principle that laws specifically burdening religion receive heightened scrutiny.

*Roman Catholic Diocese of Brooklyn* effectively overruled the more deferential approach of *South Bay* and signalled that the Court would not continue to apply *Jacobson*'s emergency exception to First Amendment religious exercise claims where the government had treated comparable secular activity more favourably. This was a significant doctrinal development that substantially modified the legal rights of religious organisations during public health restrictions — a modification that occurred not before or at the outset of the pandemic but in its second wave.

### Vaccine and Mask Mandates

The legal framework for vaccine mandates draws directly on *Jacobson*, which was explicitly decided in the context of compulsory vaccination, and on *Zucht v. King* (1922), which upheld mandatory school vaccination requirements. Courts applying this framework consistently held that state vaccine requirements were legally permissible exercises of police powers.

The Biden administration's federal vaccine mandates for large employers (100+ employees), implemented through OSHA's emergency temporary standard, generated the most significant federal litigation. In *National Federation of Independent Business v. OSHA* (2022), the Supreme Court struck down the OSHA broad-employer mandate, ruling 6-3 that the mandate exceeded OSHA's statutory authority as an agency charged with occupational safety — the Court reasoning that a broadly applicable public health mandate governing all aspects of daily life was not the kind of "occupational" hazard Congress had authorised OSHA to regulate. The Court simultaneously upheld a narrower vaccine requirement for healthcare workers at facilities receiving federal Medicare or Medicaid funding in *Biden v. Missouri* (2022), reasoning that Congress had unambiguously given the relevant federal agency authority to regulate conditions of participation in federal programmes.

Mask requirements generally fared well in constitutional litigation under *Jacobson*, with courts declining to find a constitutional right to be maskless in public or commercial spaces. Federal mask mandates for public transportation were struck down in *Health Freedom Defense Fund v. Biden* (2022) by a federal district court in Florida, on the statutory ground that the CDC had exceeded its statutory authority under the Public Health Service Act — a ruling later appealed but with the government abandoning the mandate before the appeal concluded.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The circulating claims about COVID-19 quarantine rights included many fabrications but also much good-faith error — and the harder epistemic problem concerns the latter. The pandemic created conditions in which the legal landscape was genuinely uncertain, changing rapidly, and being shaped in real time by emergency orders, agency guidance documents, and shadow docket Supreme Court decisions that moved faster than anyone's understanding could track. The social environment of error was not one of simple ignorance versus simple truth, but of genuine legal uncertainty being resolved under unprecedented time pressure.

### The Jacobson Problem

The *Jacobson* framework that courts repeatedly cited is nearly 120 years old, was decided in a context without meaningful equal protection doctrine, and does not clearly specify the degree of deference courts owe government during declared emergencies as opposed to the normal standard of review. Legal scholars disagree substantially about what *Jacobson* requires.

One camp, represented by Mark Hall (2009, *Louisiana Law Review*) and others, argues that *Jacobson*'s reasonableness standard is essentially rational basis review and that courts should be extremely deferential to governmental health judgments. A competing camp, represented by Lindsay Wiley and Stephen Vladeck (2020, *Harvard Law Review Forum*), argued during the pandemic that *Jacobson* was never meant to create an emergency exception to normal constitutional scrutiny and that courts were wrong to stop examining whether pandemic restrictions were proportionate to the documented risk in each specific restriction. The *Roman Catholic Diocese* ruling sided with the latter camp in the religiousexercise context.

This doctrinal uncertainty meant that lawyers giving advice in 2020 were genuinely uncertain what the law required — and the confidently stated "you have no rights" claims on one end of the spectrum and the equally confident "they cannot make you do anything" claims on the other were both oversimplifications of real uncertainty. The epistemic novelty here is the **contested-precedent problem**: when the controlling legal framework is genuinely unclear, popular simplifications of that framework will inevitably be wrong in ways that are not easily corrected, because the correct answer is "it depends on contested questions courts haven't resolved yet."

### The Federalism Complexity

A persistent source of misinformation was the failure to distinguish between federal and state authority. Claims that "the government" had or did not have particular powers routinely failed to specify which government — a critical omission in a federal system where states, counties, and municipalities all have distinct authority. The stay-at-home order in California was a state executive order; the mask requirement on a commercial airline was under federal authority; a county-level public health order closing bars was under county authority. The rights available to challenge each were different, the applicable courts were different, and the sources of authority were different.

Social media flattened this complexity into a single question about "the government," which cannot be answered at that level of generality. Many claims that were technically accurate in one jurisdictional context were false in another — a problem compound by the fact that the individuals making and sharing claims were themselves frequently unaware of the federalism distinction.

Popular media coverage, including from mainstream outlets, contributed to this confusion. Stanton and Liu (2021, *Columbia Journalism Review*) documented that television coverage of pandemic legal orders frequently failed to distinguish between federal, state, and local authority, contributing to public misunderstanding of the scope and source of restrictions in their specific locations.

### The "Martial Law" Misframe

A specific and particularly prevalent mischaracterisation was the claim that pandemic restrictions constituted "martial law" and that this had specific constitutional implications — such as the suspension of civilian courts, the right of military forces to police streets, or the authorisation of detention without charge. None of these characteristics apply to pandemic public health orders; martial law is a specific legal condition declared in response to domestic insurrection or military necessity, not disease. The two legal regimes share surface-level features (restrictions on movement, government authority over civilian behaviour) without substantive constitutional similarity.

The martial law misframe was persistent partly because it is evocative and historically loaded, and partly because it enabled the attachment of rights claims that exist in the martial law context but not in the public health context — the right to habeas corpus, for instance, applies forcefully to detention without criminal charge, but pandemic quarantine was not criminal detention and courts consistently declined to apply habeas corpus doctrine directly to public health isolation orders.

## 4. Verdict

**The claim that individuals have legal rights during public health quarantines and lockdowns is true, but the nature, scope, and enforceability of those rights is genuinely complex and was evolving in real time during the pandemic.**

Government at all levels in the United States has broad but bounded authority to impose public health restrictions on individuals and businesses during declared emergencies. That authority operates under constitutional limits that include due process, equal protection, the First Amendment (especially regarding religious gatherings), and the limits of specific statutory authority. The scope of those limits was being clarified by courts throughout 2020–2022 in ways that both expanded and contracted government authority depending on the specific right and restriction at issue. Standard fact-checker ratings of "True" or "False" for individual specific claims are accurate; the overall landscape of popular understanding was riddled with fabrications, simplifications, and good-faith errors across the political spectrum.

Snopes verdict: **Mostly True** for the core claim that citizens retain constitutional rights during public health emergencies, with substantial qualification about the complexity and evolution of those rights.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### The Shadow Docket Question

The Supreme Court's *Roman Catholic Diocese of Brooklyn* decision — which substantially changed the legal landscape for religious gathering restrictions — was issued without full briefing, oral argument, or a signed majority opinion, via the Court's "shadow docket" emergency application process. Legal scholars William Baude (2015, *Harvard Law Review*) and Steve Vladeck (*The Shadow Docket*, 2023) have documented the Court's increasingly expansive use of this mechanism to make substantive legal rulings that previously would have required plenary review.

The pandemic accelerated this tendency. Multiple significant changes to public health legal doctrine were accomplished not through signed majority opinions following full argument but through unsigned, unexplained emergency orders that left lower courts uncertain about their reasoning and scope. This created a legal landscape in which the "actual law" was genuinely difficult to state with confidence, because the authoritative statements came from a process that does not produce the kind of reasoned opinion courts normally use to interpret prior rulings.

### Public Health Law's Pre-Pandemic Gaps

The pandemic exposed dramatic inadequacies in the pre-existing public health law framework. State quarantine statutes, many substantially unchanged since the mid-twentieth century, were not designed for pandemic-scale deployment, had poorly developed procedural protections, and contained ambiguities about the authority for population-wide orders as opposed to individual quarantine. The federal quarantine authority at 42 U.S.C. § 264 had never been operationalised at scale and generated its own legal uncertainty when invoked.

Lawrence Gostin, probably the leading American public health law scholar, argued before and during the pandemic (Gostin & Wiley, *Public Health Law*, 4th ed., 2016) that the United States was systematically unprepared for the legal and institutional dimensions of a major pandemic — an assessment the pandemic confirmed. The mismatch between the scale of the emergency and the adequacy of the legal framework was itself a source of genuine legal uncertainty, because courts were being asked to rule on the limits of authority that had never previously been tested at this scale.

### Rights and Their Real Costs

The COVID-19 pandemic generated a genuine values conflict that legal analysis alone cannot resolve: between the rights of individuals to move freely and conduct their lives as they choose, and the rights of vulnerable individuals not to be subjected to preventable transmission of a life-threatening disease by others' behaviour. Legal rights are not zero-sum in principle, but in practice the assertion of individual liberty claims against public health measures does have consequences for population health outcomes, and those consequences fall disproportionately on immunocompromised individuals, elderly populations, and communities with reduced access to healthcare.

Public health law scholars including Wendy Mariner (2007, *Journal of Health Politics, Policy and Law*) had long argued that American public health law's dominant framing in individual rights terms — inherited from the libertarian turn in constitutional law of the 1970s and 1980s — was inadequate for the collectivist demands of pandemic management, and that a framework centred on the right to health rather than the right against government interference would produce more coherent legal doctrine. The pandemic did not resolve this tension; if anything it sharpened it.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's entry on this topic, developed specifically for the COVID-19 context, offers one of the more thorough treatments available, explaining the *Jacobson* framework, distinguishing between federal and state authority, and noting that rights exist but are subject to genuine limitations in genuine emergencies. The limitation of the Snopes treatment is that it was necessarily written at a particular moment in evolving doctrine and represents a snapshot rather than a continuously updated legal analysis.

PolitiFact rated multiple specific COVID-19 legal rights claims across its spectrum, finding both genuine misinformation about government powers (including fabricated claims about presidential detention authority and false assertions about the illegality of all lockdowns) and legitimate criticisms of government overreach where evidence of proportionality failures existed. FactCheck.org provided detailed breakdowns of specific claims about mask mandates, vaccine requirements, and quarantine authority with appropriate citation to statutory and constitutional sources.

The most consistently useful approach for individual citizens navigating these questions was not single-verdict fact-checking but legal referral — the ACLU produced extensive jurisdiction-specific guides during the pandemic, and the National Health Law Program maintained a running analysis of pandemic legal developments. These resources recognised that the correct answer to most rights questions was jurisdiction-specific and time-specific, a level of specificity that broadcast fact-checking cannot achieve.
