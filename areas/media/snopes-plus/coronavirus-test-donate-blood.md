# You can find out if you've had COVID-19 by donating blood

## 1. The Claim

In the early weeks of March 2020, as the COVID-19 pandemic began its rapid spread through the United States and other Western nations, a post circulated widely on Twitter that offered what it described as a "loophole." The message was simple: if you donated blood, the agency accepting your donation would be required to test it for coronavirus — and they would have to tell you the result for free. Here, the post suggested, was a way to access testing at a moment when commercial testing was scarce, expensive, and difficult to arrange.

The appeal was obvious. In those early weeks the United States was experiencing a testing catastrophe. The CDC's initial test rollout had been beset by manufacturing errors; commercial labs were overwhelmed; private testing was expensive and inconsistently available. For millions of people wondering whether their recent cough, fatigue, or fever had been COVID-19, the prospect of obtaining a free result through an act that also benefited others — blood donation — was attractive.

The claim spread quickly. It was shared thousands of times. It arrived in a climate of extreme anxiety and information scarcity, which meant it received significantly less sceptical scrutiny than it deserved.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

At the time the claim first circulated — early March 2020 — it was straightforwardly false. Blood donation centres were not testing donor blood for coronavirus. They did not have the diagnostic test kits. The FDA-required testing panel for donated blood covered a range of infectious diseases — HIV, hepatitis B and C, West Nile virus, syphilis, and others — but COVID-19 was not among them, and no regulatory requirement mandated it. There was at that time no scientific basis for adding it: the CDC, FDA, and major blood bank organisations had confirmed there was no evidence that COVID-19 could be transmitted through blood transfusion, so testing for it was neither a donor safety measure nor a recipient protection measure from the perspective of blood supply integrity.

The American Red Cross was explicit about this in a March 2020 statement: "there is no data or evidence that COVID-19 can be transmitted by blood transfusion, and there have been no reported cases of transmissions for any respiratory virus, including this coronavirus, worldwide." Kate Fry, chief executive officer of America's Blood Centers — a network overseeing more than 600 collection sites — told the Associated Press directly: "We do a whole range of testing on blood donations as required by the FDA, but screening or testing for coronavirus is not happening."

Additionally, the Red Cross and other organisations were actively discouraging sick people from presenting for donations precisely because of the claim. Dr. Pampee Young, chief medical officer of the Red Cross, told AP: "The last thing we would want is for people who are not healthy coming just to try to get tested." Groups at elevated risk of infection — including those who had recently travelled to heavily affected countries — were being asked to defer donation.

The situation changed materially in June 2020. On June 15, the American Red Cross announced that *all* donated blood would henceforth be tested for COVID-19 antibodies. This was offered as a free public service to donors, who would receive their results. Other major blood bank organisations — including OneBlood and Vitalant — made similar announcements around the same period.

However, this development introduced a new set of important caveats. The tests being conducted were **antibody tests**, not **diagnostic tests**. The distinction matters significantly:

- A **diagnostic test** (PCR or antigen) detects the presence of the virus itself or its genetic material. It tells you whether you are currently infected.
- An **antibody test** detects the presence of antibodies that your immune system produced in response to a past infection. It tells you whether your immune system has previously encountered the virus.

These are not the same thing, and they are not interchangeable. An antibody test performed on donated blood in June 2020 could not tell a donor whether they currently had COVID-19. It could not serve as a substitute for a diagnostic test if someone was symptomatic or had recently been exposed. And — critically — a positive antibody result could not be interpreted as confirmation of immunity. At that stage in the pandemic, the durability and protective threshold of antibody responses to COVID-19 were not yet understood; public health authorities were emphatic that a positive antibody test should not change individual behaviour in any way.

So the truth at the point of Snopes' update in July 2020 was a partial: donating blood could now yield a free antibody test as a public service from some major organisations, but it was not and had never been a route to diagnostic testing for COVID-19, and the test available was not equivalent to what the original claim had promised.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The COVID-19 testing shortage in early 2020 was a genuine crisis that made misinformation of this kind attractive to a degree that would have been difficult to achieve in ordinary circumstances. Several factors specific to this moment explain the claim's spread.

First, there was **wishful thinking at scale**. People wanted to know if they'd had the disease. Access to that information felt important for practical and psychological reasons alike. The blood donation claim offered a solution that was simultaneously altruistic (you'd be helping someone who needed blood) and self-interested (you'd get information in return). The dual benefit added to its believability; claims that offer win-win scenarios tend to spread more readily than ones that require sacrifice without reward.

Second, there was **plausible mechanism confusion**. It is true that blood donations are tested for many diseases. Most people are vaguely aware that the blood supply is screened. The inferential leap from "they test blood for diseases" to "they would test it for COVID-19 if there was a pandemic" is not inherently unreasonable — it just happened to be wrong about regulatory requirements, institutional priorities, and the logistical realities of test kit availability.

Third, the claim arrived at a moment of **profound institutional mistrust and information chaos**. Official guidance in those early weeks changed frequently, sometimes contradicted itself, and was delivered through fractured communication channels. In an environment where the official line seemed unreliable, a credible-seeming informal tip gained traction it would not have earned in calmer times.

Fourth, the **later partial truth** created a specific kind of persistent confusion. Once the Red Cross actually *did* begin offering antibody testing in June 2020, earlier corrections became harder to navigate. People who encountered a fact-check saying the original claim was false, and then saw news reports about blood banks offering COVID-19 antibody tests, may have concluded that the fact-checkers were wrong — that the "loophole" had turned out to be real. The distinction between antibody testing and diagnostic testing, between "you may have been exposed" and "you currently have COVID-19," is one that requires careful explanation and was frequently collapsed in popular framings.

Fifth, the **novelty of antibody testing as a public health tool** meant that ordinary audiences had little framework for understanding what an antibody test result could and could not tell them. The public understanding of immunology had been developed primarily through decades of vaccine communication, which had generally proceeded on the premise that if you had antibodies, you were protected. The more complex message — that COVID-19 antibodies existed along a spectrum of durability and protective efficacy that was not yet characterised — was both accurate and deeply uncomfortable to communicate.

---

## 4. Verdict

**Mixture.** The original claim — that donating blood would yield a free diagnostic test for COVID-19 — was **false** when it first circulated in March 2020. No blood banks were performing coronavirus testing on donor samples at that time, and no regulatory requirement obliged them to. From June 2020, the situation became more complex: the Red Cross and other organisations did begin offering free antibody tests to all donors as a public service. However, antibody tests are not diagnostic tests, cannot confirm current infection, and at the time could not confirm immunity. The claim as originally stated remained misleading even after that development. Snopes updated its rating from False to **Mixture** in July 2020.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The blood donation COVID testing claim is one of a category of misinformation stories that are best understood not as deliberate disinformation but as **plausible-but-broken inferences** that emerge spontaneously from a strained information environment. The person who first tweeted the "loophole" may not have been attempting to deceive anyone; they may simply have reasoned from what they knew about blood testing to an incorrect conclusion about what was actually happening in March 2020.

This category of misinformation is in some ways harder to counter than deliberate disinformation, because it does not have a single point of origin that can be discredited. It is distributed, organic, and often spread by people acting in good faith. Corrections that say "this person was lying" fail to engage with the actual mechanism of spread. Corrections that say "this was a reasonable inference that turns out to be wrong" are more accurate but require more nuance than fits easily in a social media post.

The episode also illustrates a recurring feature of pandemic information dynamics: the way that **partial truths and corrections interact**. Once the Red Cross began offering antibody testing, the landscape of claims became genuinely complicated. A fact-checker who had rated the original claim False in March 2020 had to update in July 2020 — which created an opening for those who wanted to argue that fact-checkers were unreliable or had been wrong. The fact that the update was itself accurate and transparent was less legible to a general audience than the headline-level impression that the original verdict had been reversed.

This matters for how we think about the temporal dimension of fact-checking. Claims are often treated as static objects — true or false once, permanently. In fact, pandemic claims existed in an evolving factual landscape in which the truth could change as policy and practice changed. Blood banks *did not* test for COVID-19. Then they *did*. A single verdict label cannot capture that arc without detailed contextual explanation, and contextual explanation does not travel well at platform speed.

The blood supply dimension of this story is also worthy of attention in its own right. The fear that COVID-19 patients might contaminate the blood supply was scientifically unfounded from early in the pandemic, and blood banks worked to communicate this while simultaneously managing genuine anxieties. The combination of those two things — reassurance that the blood supply was safe, and new restrictions on who could donate — created interpretive tension. For people primed to look for contradictions in official messaging, the coexistence of "COVID-19 can't be transmitted in blood" and "please don't donate if you've been exposed to COVID-19" looked like a contradiction even though it had a logical explanation rooted in donor health rather than blood safety.

Finally, the episode is a reminder of how **access inequity shapes pandemic misinformation**. The "blood donation as testing loophole" claim was essentially a response to the failure of systematic testing. If adequate diagnostic testing had been available at reasonable cost from the outset of the pandemic, there would have been no market for the claim. Misinformation often fills genuine gaps in institutional provision; addressing it requires not just correction but remediation of the underlying gap.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published its initial assessment of this claim on March 13, 2020, authored by David Mikkelson — the site's founder — rating the claim **False**. The piece drew on statements from the American Red Cross, America's Blood Centers, and an Associated Press report featuring interviews with blood bank executives and medical officers. The rebuttal was direct: no testing was occurring, no testing was planned, and the organisations soliciting blood donations were actively concerned that the claim might deter healthy donors while encouraging sick ones to present.

On July 29, 2020, the piece was updated to reflect the June 2020 developments, with the rating changed to **Mixture**. The update explained the antibody testing programmes at the Red Cross, OneBlood, and Vitalant, while clearly distinguishing between antibody testing and diagnostic testing and reiterating that a positive antibody result did not confirm immunity.

The AP had published its own debunking in March 2020, citing Kate Fry and Pampee Young directly — a useful factual anchor that gave authority to the correction by putting named, credentialled sources on record.

The handling of this claim by fact-checkers illustrates the value of rate-updating — the willingness to revise published assessments as factual circumstances change. This is a strength of format that Snopes exemplifies and that distinguishes responsible fact-checking from static journalism: the record is treated as living, and corrections are published conspicuously. Critics of fact-checking sometimes cite rating changes as evidence of inconsistency or political manipulation; the blood donation case demonstrates that they are instead evidence of fidelity to truth as it evolves.

For media literacy educators, this case offers a useful illustration of the **antibody vs. diagnostic test distinction** — a conceptual split that proved relevant throughout the pandemic and remains relevant for any future infectious disease outbreak. The difference between a test that detects current infection and one that detects past exposure is not intuitively obvious to non-specialist audiences, and effective health communication requires building that understanding proactively rather than waiting for confusion to manifest as misinformation. News organisations that covered the Red Cross antibody testing announcement in June 2020 without adequately explaining the distinction between antibody and diagnostic testing inadvertently contributed to the confusion they were attempting to resolve.
