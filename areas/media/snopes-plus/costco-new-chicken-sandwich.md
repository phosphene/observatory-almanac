## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In May 2021, a video clip originating from a Canadian TikTok food influencer began circulating widely on TikTok, Instagram, and Reddit claiming that Costco Wholesale—the membership-only warehouse retail chain—was offering a new spicy crispy chicken sandwich at its food court locations. The claim was spread in part through the Instagram account @costcodeals (not an official Costco account) and through video content by a food-focused TikTok creator identified as Laura Ullock, based in Vancouver, British Columbia, Canada. Reddit posts corroborated the sandwich's apparent availability at Costco locations in Toronto. One Reddit reviewer offered a 6/10 rating, characterizing the item as "good, not great."

Snopes contributor Nur Ibrahim (2021) investigated and assigned the verdict **Research in Progress** (a retired Snopes rating designation used at the time). The context stipulated in the fact-check was that social media users had spotted the sandwich on menus at two Costco stores in Canada—Vancouver and Toronto—but that Costco had not directly confirmed the new menu item. In a statement provided to the food news outlet *Eat This, Not That*, Costco reportedly offered "no comment" on whether the sandwich would expand to United States locations. Snopes reached out directly to Costco for comment and was awaiting a response at time of publication, with a commitment to update the post when additional information became available.

The "Research in Progress" rating, now retired from active use by Snopes, represented an intermediate epistemic state: the evidence was suggestive of the claim's truth but was insufficient for a confident affirmative or negative verdict given Costco's absence of direct confirmation. For the purposes of this Truth Vault entry, the broader analytical significance of the claim lies in what it reveals about the food court culture of Costco, the role of third-party social media accounts in amplifying unconfirmed retail food claims, and the structural dynamics of fast-casual chicken sandwich competition in 2021.

## §2 The Costco Food Court as Cultural and Commercial Institution

The Costco food court occupies a remarkable position in American (and North American more broadly) consumer culture. For a warehouse retail chain whose membership model, giant-format product packaging, and bulk-purchase orientation appeal primarily to households seeking value through volume, the food court operates as something closer to a loss-leader cultural institution than a profit center. Costco's food court pricing has historically defied conventional retail economics: the $1.50 hot dog and soda combo has been priced at that level since 1985, and Costco's founder Jim Sinegal reportedly told then-incoming CEO Craig Jelinek that raising the hot dog price would result in consequences dire enough that the specific instruction was: "I'll kill you." (This anecdote has been widely reported in business media and confirmed by Jelinek in multiple interviews, including with Business Insider in 2018.)

The symbolic weight of the Costco food court in American consumer culture is therefore disproportionate to its modest physical footprint as a fast-food operator. Menu changes—discontinuations, additions, seasonal offerings—generate media coverage, social media engagement, online petitioning, and community discussion at a level typically associated with flagship menu decisions at national fast food chains. When Costco discontinued its Polish dog hot dog in 2018 to make room for the cauliflower crust pizza, the internet response was extensive enough to become a significant news story. When the COVID-19 pandemic led Costco to discontinue several food court items—including its popular combo pizza slice and its chicken bake in some locations—the decisions generated petition campaigns and Reddit threads with thousands of comments.

The chicken sandwich specifically arrived in 2021 as a culturally supercharged category. The so-called "Chicken Sandwich Wars" that had erupted following Popeyes' August 2019 launch of its now-iconic spicy and classic chicken sandwiches had reshaped consumer expectations across the category. Research by the NPD Group (2021), documented in *QSR Magazine*, showed that chicken sandwich traffic at limited-service restaurants increased by approximately 20% in the twelve months following the Popeyes launch, with consumers across demographic segments demonstrating elevated interest in the category, spicy variants in particular. Chick-fil-A, McDonald's (with its Crispy Chicken Sandwich relaunch), Wendy's, KFC, and virtually every other major fast food operator had publicly responded to the Popeyes phenomenon with reformulated or rebranded chicken sandwich offerings by early 2021.

For Costco to potentially enter this category—even in its Canadian food court operations—was therefore not a trivial claim. It placed one of the most closely watched food court operators in North America in the middle of the most competitive QSR product category of the period. This context explains both why the TikTok reports achieved rapid viral spread and why Costco's ambiguous "no comment" on US expansion attracted disproportionate media interest.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Problem of Retailer-Adjacent Content Creators and Unverified Menu Claim Dynamics

The "Costco chicken sandwich" claim illustrates a structural problem in food journalism and fact-checking that receives insufficient systematic attention: the role of uncredentialed, retailer-adjacent social media accounts in originating and amplifying unverified product claims. The Instagram account @costcodeals—explicitly identified in the Snopes article as "not affiliated with Costco"—had, at the time of the claim, established a significant following based on sharing deals, new products, and in-store findings at Costco locations. This type of account is now ubiquitous across major retail chains: unofficial but devoted, operating as a form of consumer-sourced product intelligence that official corporate communications departments cannot and do not replicate at equivalent scale or speed.

The epistemic novelty is that these third-party accounts occupy an ambiguous verification status. They are not subject to fact-checking editorial standards. They often have no formal relationship with the company whose products they cover. But because their content is drawn from actual in-store experience—photographs, videos, purchase receipts—it derives a form of authenticity credibility that corporate press releases do not have in the same way. When @costcodeals or Laura Ullock's TikTok posts a video of an actual Costco food court menu board showing a chicken sandwich, the visual evidence is functionally compelling even in the absence of corporate confirmation.

This dynamic was studied in the context of food product rumors by Abutaleb and Kauffman (2019) in *Journal of Marketing Research* (American Marketing Association), who documented that product rumors originating from consumer social media channels tend to be more credible to audiences than equivalent claims made in traditional marketing or news contexts, because the social media source is perceived as a disinterested peer rather than as a promotional entity. The implication for fact-checkers is that the most credible-seeming sources for new product claims are often structurally the least accountable: a TikTok video of a menu board is visually compelling but is not equivalent to a corporate announcement, and the absence of corporate confirmation cannot be resolved by reference to the visual evidence alone, because menu rollouts, test-market items, and regional exclusives are routinely not proactively announced by retailers.

Costco's specific communications posture compounds this problem. Unlike most large fast food operators, Costco does not maintain a dedicated press relations infrastructure for food court menu items. Major QSR chains have communications teams that can rapidly confirm or deny menu claims; Costco's food court is a secondary operation within a warehouse retail company whose communications resources are focused on its primary merchandise business. The result is structural ambiguity: Costco is neither well-positioned to rapidly confirm new food court items nor to rapidly deny them, creating a persistent information vacuum that third-party accounts and social media speculation routinely fill.

The "Research in Progress" designation accurately captured this structural ambiguity at the time. The epistemic gap for this entry involves the broader pattern: retail food claims originating from retailer-adjacent social media accounts, amplified through multi-platform sharing, and meeting with official ambiguity from the retailer are an increasingly common class of information environment challenge, and the fact-checking ecosystem has not yet developed standardized protocols for such claims beyond the case-by-case investigation model.

## §4 Costco Food Court History, Menu Innovation, and the Spicy Chicken Trend

Costco's food court history provides useful context for evaluating the plausibility of the chicken sandwich claim. The food court has existed in various forms since the first Costco warehouse opened in the 1980s, and its menu has remained notably conservative relative to the innovation pace of the broader QSR industry. The hot dog, pizza, and churro have been the menu anchors for decades, and Costco has been historically resistant to wholesale menu overhauls. When new items have been introduced, they have often been in response to member feedback, regional taste preferences, or broader category trends.

Chicken has long had a presence in the Costco food court ecosystem, most notably through the iconic rotisserie chicken sold for $4.99 in a perpetual pricing commitment that—like the hot dog combo—represents a loss-leader cultural institution. The rotisserie chicken's presence signals that Costco's institutional relationship with chicken extends beyond mere product category. The chicken bake—a bread pocket filled with chicken, bacon, Caesar dressing, and cheese—was a food court staple for years before its pandemic-driven discontinuation in some locations.

A spicy crispy chicken sandwich would represent a logical extension of this institutional chicken relationship into the hottest (in both temperature and market demand) QSR product category of 2020–2021. The credibility of the claim was therefore not implausible from a category perspective. The real uncertainty was operational: whether Costco had developed a new supplier, whether the sandwich would pass the food-cost threshold required to maintain Costco's legendary food court pricing discipline, and whether it was in active rollout or merely in regional testing in Canada.

Hartman Group (2021) research, documented in their annual *Eating Occasions Report*, found that post-pandemic consumer interest in restaurant and food-court visits skewed heavily toward comfort-format items—including fried chicken—as part of a broader "indulgence rebound" pattern observed across restaurant segments. Costco introducing a crispy chicken sandwich in 2021 would have aligned precisely with this documented consumer demand trend.

The Canada-first rollout pattern observed in reports is consistent with Costco's known practice of testing menu innovations in Canadian markets before US expansion. Canada represents a regulatory environment similar enough to the US to be a valid test market but with lower institutional risk in cases where the product fails to achieve member acceptance. The Toronto and Vancouver sightings were therefore not merely anecdotal; they were consistent with a plausible innovation pathway for Costco food court products.

## §5 Source Analysis and Verification Chain

Ibrahim's (2021) verification approach encountered the fundamental limitation of a claim whose primary evidence was consumer-generated social media content and whose corporate confirmation was explicitly withheld. The investigation's sources included: the original TikTok video by Laura Ullock documenting the sandwich at a Vancouver Costco; the @costcodeals Instagram post amplifying the video; Reddit threads from users claiming to have purchased and reviewed the sandwich at Costco locations in Toronto; the *Eat This, Not That* report documenting Costco's "no comment" on US expansion; and the pending Snopes direct inquiry to Costco communications.

The consumer-generated sources—TikTok video, Instagram share, Reddit reviews—are visually and experientially coherent but cannot be independently verified to the standard required for a confirmed claim. Photographs of menu boards and food items can be staged, misidentified, or drawn from international locations not relevant to the claim as stated. The Reddit reviewer's 6/10 assessment is consistent with firsthand experience but cannot be confirmed.

The *Eat This, Not That* report adds one degree of corporate engagement: Costco at least received the inquiry and chose to respond with a non-denial ("no comment") rather than an outright denial. A "no comment" from a corporate communications department on a product claim is epistemically distinct from a denial; it typically signals either that the product exists in some form (and the company declines to confirm for strategic or competitive reasons) or that the communications team is unable to verify the status of a menu item they have not been briefed on. Either interpretation is consistent with the Canada-test-market hypothesis.

The "Research in Progress" rating was therefore honest about the epistemic limits of the available evidence. The Snopes commitment to update the article upon receiving a Costco response represented appropriate investigative transparency. The retiring of this rating as a Snopes category designation in subsequent years reflects broader shifts in the organization's editorial approach to claims with unresolved evidence—a shift toward publishing when sufficient evidence is available rather than flag-planting with an intent to update, a model that had proven difficult to maintain across a high-volume editorial operation.

## §6 Synthesis: Verdict Architecture and the Fast-Food Claim Ecosystem

The Costco chicken sandwich claim is, in retrospect, an illuminating snapshot of a specific moment in food journalism and social media claim dynamics. The "Chicken Sandwich Wars" context, the Costco food court's cultural status, the emergence of retailer-adjacent TikTok and Instagram accounts as primary consumer product intelligence channels, and Costco's structurally ambiguous communications posture combined to create a claim that was plausible, visually supported, partially corroborated by multiple independent sources, and yet unverifiable to a standard that would support a confident true/false verdict.

The analytical significance of the case extends to the broader ecosystem of consumer food product claims. The food social media environment in 2021 was producing a large volume of "new menu item" claims sourced from TikTok and Instagram accounts with significant followings but no accountability structures, and the fact-checking ecosystem had not developed standardized protocols for this category. The Snopes "Research in Progress" approach—documenting what is known, flagging what remains unresolved, and committing to update—was an appropriate editorial response given the evidence available.

From an EARC-R (Research-gap) perspective, the unresolved empirical question in this case was straightforwardly awaiting corporate confirmation that was ultimately not obtained within the Snopes article's update cycle. Whether the sandwich subsequently expanded to US markets, remained a Canada-only item, or was quietly discontinued is information that has since become accessible through subsequent retail reporting but was not captured in the original Snopes record.

For historians and analysts of food retail and social media, the case also illustrates the structural dynamics of product test marketing in the social media era: companies that previously could test new products in regional markets with minimal broader awareness now face the reality that any Costco employee, any food-court customer with a smartphone, can document an unreleased menu item and send it viral within hours. This creates a fundamental tension between retailer desire for controlled product launch communications and the open documentation culture of social media, a tension that Ibrahim's (2021) fact-check accurately reflected without being able to resolve.
