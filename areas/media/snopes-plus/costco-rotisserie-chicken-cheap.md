# Does Costco Lose Millions Annually for Keeping Rotisserie Chicken Cheap?


## 1. The Claim

Costco's $4.99 rotisserie chicken has become something of a cultural institution—a beloved fixture of the American shopping experience and a subject of recurring social media discussion precisely because of its price. The claim circulating widely, particularly through a viral Uber Facts tweet and subsequent TikTok videos, holds that Costco deliberately sacrifices tens of millions of dollars annually in gross margin to maintain the $4.99 retail price, operating the chicken not as a profitable product but as a "loss leader" designed to attract shoppers who then purchase far more profitable merchandise.

The claim quantifies the loss at "$30 million to $40 million a year" and attributes this willingness to absorb losses to Costco's long-term strategic commitment to member satisfaction and foot traffic generation. The $4.99 price has been maintained through periods of rising input costs including chicken feed prices, energy costs, and labor expenses that would have pushed any conventional pricing model toward a price increase. The fact that the price has held—and has been explicitly defended by Costco leadership—makes the claim feel intuitively compelling even before examining the evidence.

Embedded in this claim is a broader assertion about retail strategy: that some products are priced below cost deliberately, that this below-cost pricing serves a coherent commercial purpose, and that the resulting consumer benefit (a $4.99 rotisserie chicken when competing retailers charge $7–$12) is a kind of corporate gift made possible by the membership-fee business model that allows Costco to absorb such losses. This is not merely a claim about one poultry product. It is a claim about how large-scale retail operates, how membership models redistribute value between companies and consumers, and how low-cost anchor items shape consumer behavior and brand loyalty at scale.

Snopes correctly identifies the 2015 CFO disclosure as the source of the loss figures and notes that more recent data has not been publicly released. However, the analytical architecture behind why Costco pursues this strategy—and how the economics have evolved since 2015, including through Costco's own vertical integration into chicken supply—receives no treatment in the fact-check.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rates the claim as a **Mixture**, and this rating is defensible given the available evidence. The core factual claim traces to a 2015 statement by Costco's chief financial officer (CFO, identified as Richard Galanti in some sources), who said the company was willing to sacrifice "$30 million, $40 million a year on gross margin" to maintain the $4.99 price point. This is a direct public statement from a senior corporate officer, not an estimate or third-party analysis, and it constitutes strong evidence for the loss-leader characterization as of 2015.

The "Undetermined" component of the Mixture rating reflects the fact that by 2019, Jeff Lyons, Costco's Senior Vice President of Fresh Foods, declined to confirm to CNN whether the company still loses money on the chicken at $4.99. This is not the same as a statement that losses have ended; it is corporate reticence. However, Snopes correctly notes that the absence of updated figures makes the specific loss claim unverifiable for the current period.

What is unambiguously true is that Costco has sold approximately 100 million rotisserie chickens annually in recent years—a figure that makes the chicken one of the most consumed individual grocery items in the United States. In 2018, the company reportedly generated $91 million in revenue from rotisserie chicken sales alone. Revenue, of course, is not profit; the underlying cost structure remains undisclosed. The $4.99 price has remained fixed while commodity costs, particularly corn and soybean used as chicken feed, have experienced substantial volatility over the same period.

Snopes also correctly identifies the parallel of the $1.50 Costco hot dog and soda combo, citing the famous anecdote in which Costco CEO Craig Jelinek reports that co-founder Jim Sinegal threatened to fire or kill him if he raised the hot dog price. This anecdote, regardless of its literal accuracy, crystallizes a genuine corporate philosophy: certain low-price anchor items function not primarily as profit centers but as loyalty signals, communicating to members that Costco's pricing philosophy privileges value over margin.


## 3. Epistemic Analysis: What the Verdict Leaves Unexplored

The most significant analytical omission in Snopes' account is the absence of any engagement with the economic theory and empirical research on loss-leader pricing—a well-studied phenomena in retail economics with a substantial literature.

**Loss-Leader Economics and Their Empirical Validation**

Loss-leader pricing is a retail strategy studied extensively in industrial organization economics. The canonical insight is that retailers can profitably price certain high-visibility items below cost if the resulting increase in store traffic generates sufficient complementary purchases of high-margin items. Hess, J.D. & Gerstner, E. (1987, "Loss Leader Pricing and Rain Check Policy," Marketing Science) formalized this as a strategic rationale under specific market conditions: the loss leader must be highly price-visible (so consumers notice and respond to the low price), the complementary purchases must have sufficient margin spread, and the retailer's cost structure must support the resulting basket economics.

Costco's membership fee model creates an unusual refinement of this logic. Unlike a conventional retailer that depends entirely on merchandise margin for profitability, Costco generates a substantial portion of its earnings directly from membership fees. This structural feature means Costco can afford to operate merchandise categories at near-zero or negative margins, because its economic model effectively pre-collects a portion of the profit through the annual fee. Shilony, Y. (1977, "Mixed Pricing in Oligopoly," Journal of Economic Theory) and subsequent work have noted that retailers with loyalty programs or membership fees face different margin requirements than fee-free competitors—an insight directly applicable to understanding why Costco's rotisserie chicken economics work when no traditional grocery store could sustain the same pricing.

Costco's annual report for fiscal year 2022 documents that membership fee revenue represented approximately $4.2 billion, accounting for the large majority of the company's operating income. This means that in a structural sense, Costco's merchandise operations—across all categories—are run approximately at cost or slight loss in aggregate, with profit extracted through membership fees rather than merchandise margin. The rotisserie chicken is an extreme case within this broader pattern rather than an anomaly.

**Vertical Integration as the Hidden Story**

Perhaps the most significant analytical gap in Snopes' account is the complete absence of Costco's vertical integration strategy for chicken supply, which is directly relevant to understanding the current economics of the $4.99 price.

In 2019, Costco opened its own chicken processing facility in Fremont, Nebraska—a $450 million investment in a fully integrated poultry processing and production operation, including hatchery, feed mill, and processing plant. The facility, operated under the Lincoln Premium Poultry brand, was designed to supply Costco with approximately 100 million chickens annually, making it one of the largest single poultry facilities in the United States. The explicit corporate rationale was to reduce dependence on external chicken suppliers whose pricing was subject to commodity market volatility—the same volatility that made maintaining the $4.99 price increasingly difficult through the 2010s.

This vertical integration fundamentally changes the calculus of the loss-leader claim. A company that produces its own chickens at integrated cost (controlling feed, hatchery, processing, and transportation) faces a very different cost structure than one purchasing from external suppliers at market rates. The loss, if it exists, is measured differently when the "cost" of the chicken includes fixed capital depreciation on a $450 million facility and internal transfer pricing between Costco business units rather than a transparent external market price.

The $450 million capital investment also signals the depth of strategic commitment: Costco built an entire vertically integrated meat processing enterprise not to make money selling chickens but to maintain control over a $4.99 retail price point that it had determined was worth several hundred million dollars of infrastructure investment to preserve. This is a remarkable corporate decision whose implications exceed anything in the Snopes account.

**Behavioral Economics and Anchor Pricing**

The rotisserie chicken functions not only as a foot traffic driver but as an epistemic anchor for the Costco value proposition. Behavioral economics research by Ariely, D. and colleagues (Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec, 2003, "Coherent Arbitrariness," Quarterly Journal of Economics) documents how initial price exposures create psychological reference points that shape subsequent willingness to pay across unrelated product categories. A shopper who has just paid $4.99 for a fully cooked rotisserie chicken—a product that signals cooking skill, time investment, and quality—has their sense of "reasonable pricing" calibrated in ways that make subsequent purchases of Costco's private-label products, bulk foods, and discretionary items feel more attractive relative to the value signal the chicken already provided.

The $4.99 chicken is thus not merely a loss leader in the mechanical sense—it is a trust signal, a proof of concept for the entire Costco value proposition, and an anchor that affects the consumer's evaluation of every other price in the warehouse. This is the empirical behavioral economics dimension of the story that does not appear in Snopes' account.


## 4. The Scientific and Historical Record

The loss-leader strategy in American retail has deep historical roots. Supermarkets and department stores have used below-cost items to drive traffic since at least the 1930s, and the Federal Trade Commission has periodically investigated loss-leader pricing for potential predatory pricing violations (though these investigations have rarely resulted in enforcement in the retail context). Costco's policy can be traced to the corporate philosophy of its co-founder Jim Sinegal, who consistently maintained that retailers should seek to earn the lowest possible margin consistent with business sustainability, passing savings to consumers as a mechanism for building loyalty.

Costco's warehouse model—which requires membership fees, sells in bulk, offers fewer SKUs than conventional grocery stores, and focuses on high-volume, low-margin operations—creates a distinctive economic environment where individual product economics can be discussed separately from overall business viability. The company's fiscal year 2022 total revenue was approximately $229 billion with an operating income of approximately $6.3 billion, of which membership fees contributed roughly two-thirds. This structural context is essential to understanding how a company can voluntarily absorb product-level losses as a deliberate strategy while remaining highly profitable overall.

Rotisserie chickens as a grocery category were popularized by Sam's Club (Walmart's competing warehouse chain) in the early 1990s, and Costco adopted the category to match a direct competitor in a flagship traffic-driving product. The price competition between these two chains—and BJ's Wholesale Club in the northeastern US—has maintained downward pressure on rotisserie chicken prices well below conventional supermarket pricing for three decades. The Costco CFO's 2015 statement about $30–40 million in annual margin sacrifice reflected this competitive context as much as it reflected a unilateral strategic commitment.

The Lincoln Premium Poultry facility in Nebraska generated significant controversy during its development and opening, including concerns from existing poultry suppliers who lost business and from labor advocates who raised questions about working conditions. These dynamics are part of the full story of how the $4.99 chicken is sustained, though they fall outside the scope of the Snopes fact-check's specific claim.


## 5. EARC Assessment: Where Snopes Adds Value and Where the Gap Lies

**Epistemic Value Added:** The Snopes article by Nur Ibrahim (2020, updated 2023) correctly identifies the primary evidential source (the 2015 CFO statement) and correctly applies appropriate epistemic caution about more recent figures. The Mixture rating is justified: the historical loss is documented, and the current loss is unknown. The article also provides useful context about the hot dog anecdote and the broader loss-leader framing, and correctly identifies that Costco's strategic motive is member retention and basket economics rather than altruism.

**Epistemic Gap — Analytically Incomplete (A):** The account does not engage with: the economic theory of loss-leader pricing and its conditions; the distinctive structure of membership-fee retail economics; Costco's vertical integration into chicken supply as a structural response to production cost volatility; or the behavioral economics of anchor pricing. These are not peripheral additions—they are the substance of why the $4.99 chicken story is interesting and what it reveals about how retail strategy actually works.

**Gap Category: Economically Underexplored** — The fact-check establishes the loss claim as historically documented and currently indeterminate, which is accurate, but locates the discussion in a thin anecdotal frame (hot dog story, CFO quote) rather than the economic and organizational analysis that would make the claim meaningful.


## 6. Verdict and Summary

The claim that Costco loses millions annually to maintain the $4.99 rotisserie chicken price is **True as of 2015** and **Undetermined but architecturally plausible for subsequent years**. Snopes' Mixture rating is accurate, and the reasoning behind it is correct: we have documentary evidence for the specific loss figure from 2015 but no confirmed current data.

The deeper story—which the Snopes rating acknowledges without analyzing—is that Costco built an entire $450 million vertically integrated chicken processing enterprise precisely to maintain control over this pricing, which is a more dramatic and consequential demonstration of commitment to the $4.99 price than any annual margin sacrifice figure. The company effectively decided that owning its own chicken supply chain was worth half a billion dollars of capital investment rather than yield on price.

For the consumer, the practical reality is simply that the $4.99 rotisserie chicken is a genuinely exceptional value, subsidized by membership fee economics and deliberate strategic pricing. Eating it is not a corporate gift—it is the delivery mechanism for the Costco business model, which profits from building the kind of deep member loyalty that only comes from reliably and implausibly low prices on highly visible anchor items. The chicken is not cheap because Costco is generous. The chicken is cheap because Costco is brilliant.

The Mixture verdict is correct. The full story is considerably larger.
