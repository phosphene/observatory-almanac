# "Cough CPR" Can Save Your Life During a Heart Attack


## 1. The Claim

The claim originates from an email chain that began circulating on the internet in June 1999 and has been periodically resurrected across two decades of viral media. The email, usually formatted as urgent health advice with a header like "HOW TO SURVIVE A HEART ATTACK WHEN ALONE," instructs recipients as follows:

> *Since many people are alone when they suffer a heart attack, without help the person whose heart stops beating properly and who begins to feel faint, has only about 10 seconds left before losing consciousness. However, these victims can help themselves by coughing repeatedly and very vigorously. A deep breath should be taken before each cough, and the cough must be deep and prolonged, as when producing sputum from deep inside the chest. A breath and a cough must be repeated about every two seconds without let up until help arrives, or until the heart is felt to be beating normally again. Deep breaths get oxygen into the lungs and coughing movements squeeze the heart and keep the blood circulating.*

The email was often attributed to Rochester General Hospital and to *Mended Hearts*, a support organization for cardiac patients. Both organizations have explicitly disavowed the email and its claims.

The claim presents cough CPR — a real technique used in supervised clinical settings — as a broadly applicable self-rescue maneuver for any person who believes they are having a heart attack while alone. The implied message is: if you have chest pain and no one is with you, cough aggressively and you may survive.

This claim achieved extraordinary reach. It was forwarded by millions of well-intentioned people, reproduced in community newsletters, shared by faith communities and workplace wellness programs, and translated across multiple languages. It continues to resurface periodically in social media contexts. Its authors almost certainly had no harmful intentions. The problem is that the advice, applied without medical supervision, could kill the person following it.


## 2. What's Actually True

There is a real medical procedure called cough CPR. There is also a real cardiac event in which it might appropriately be attempted. And there is a much more common cardiac event in which attempting it could make things dramatically worse. The viral email instruction does not distinguish between these scenarios, and most laypeople receiving a cardiac event notice from their own bodies cannot distinguish between them either.

**What "cough CPR" actually is.** Cough CPR refers to a technique in which the patient repeatedly, forcefully coughs during a specific type of cardiac arrhythmia — one that causes the heart to stop beating effectively and threatens immediate loss of consciousness. The mechanism is genuine: a vigorous cough generates a significant pressure change in the thoracic cavity, briefly squeezing the heart and transiently increasing arterial blood pressure enough to maintain cerebral perfusion (blood flow to the brain) for a short period. This can buy seconds to minutes of consciousness during certain arrhythmias, providing time for physicians or defibrillation equipment to intervene.

The technique has documented use in cardiac catheterization laboratories, where patients in close proximity to trained cardiologists and emergency equipment experience arrhythmias that can be managed with physician-directed coughing while other interventions are prepared. The context of the catheterization laboratory is essential: the physician has real-time ECG monitoring, knows exactly what arrhythmia is occurring, can instruct the patient moment by moment, and has a crash cart immediately available if coughing does not maintain sufficient perfusion.

**The crucial distinction: cardiac arrest versus heart attack.** The viral email consistently blurs a distinction that is clinically essential. These two events are not the same thing, and appropriate responses to them differ significantly.

A *heart attack* (myocardial infarction) involves a blockage in a coronary artery that cuts off blood flow to a section of heart muscle. The heart continues to beat — typically irregularly, but it continues. The immediate danger is permanent muscle damage from ischemia and, in severe cases, a secondary arrhythmia. The first-line treatment is to minimize physical exertion (which increases oxygen demand on the damaged heart), call emergency services immediately, and, if appropriate, chew an aspirin (which inhibits platelet aggregation and helps prevent the clot from expanding). The heart is still pumping; it does not need coughing to pump for it. Vigorous coughing during an uncomplicated myocardial infarction increases oxygen demand, raises blood pressure, and stresses the ischemic myocardium — all potentially harmful.

*Cardiac arrest* involves the heart ceasing to pump effectively due to a lethal arrhythmia, typically ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia. The person loses consciousness within seconds. Without defibrillation and CPR, survival rates plummet approximately 10% per minute. This is the scenario where cough CPR has limited documented utility — but only in the seconds before loss of consciousness, only with precise execution, and only when the person can recognize the pre-arrest arrhythmia as it is occurring. Patients experiencing cardiac arrest are rarely able to self-administer any intervention because unconsciousness follows so rapidly.

**The email's fatal instruction.** The email tells readers that symptoms of a heart attack — chest pain, radiating pain to the arm, pain into the jaw — are cues to begin vigorous coughing. But these are primarily symptoms of myocardial infarction, not the pre-arrest arrhythmias in which cough CPR is indicated. Following the email's instructions during a heart attack without cardiac arrest means subjecting a damaged, oxygen-deprived heart to repeated high-pressure stress. Dr. Richard O. Cummins, Seattle's director of emergency cardiac care, stated directly: cough CPR can be dangerous for someone having a heart attack that does not result in cardiac arrest.

**What the American Heart Association actually recommends.** The AHA does not recommend cough CPR for public use. Its official guidance is: call emergency services immediately, chew (not swallow whole) an aspirin if available and not contraindicated, and sit or lie down to reduce physical exertion. These instructions reflect the primary mechanism of most cardiac deaths — treatable myocardial infarction that progresses to fatal arrhythmia, or fatal arrhythmia, when timely medical care is delayed. Every minute of delay in calling 911 is the most dangerous interval in most cardiac emergencies.

**The aspirin action, briefly.** The recommendation to chew an aspirin at the onset of suspected cardiac symptoms dates to 1991, when Dr. Michael Vance, president of the American Board of Emergency Medicine, recommended it as a first-line action during a suspected heart attack. A 1993 American Heart Association guideline incorporated a 325 mg aspirin recommendation for chest pain presenting as possible myocardial infarction. Follow-up analyses suggested that failing to take aspirin during early heart attack symptoms may contribute to as many as 10,000 preventable American deaths annually. Chewing achieves faster systemic absorption than swallowing — significant in a time-sensitive event. This recommendation is evidence-grounded, generalizable, and does not carry the "wrong cardiac event" risk that cough CPR does.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Emotional appeal of agency.** Among the most powerful psychological drives in medical emergency scenarios is the desire to act — to do something rather than wait helplessly. A person experiencing chest pain alone faces a terrifying loss of agency. The offer of a physical maneuver they can perform restores a sense of action, of self-rescue. This is psychologically compelling regardless of the maneuver's actual efficacy.

**A real technique misapplied.** The claim is not entirely fabricated, and that partial truth is precisely what makes it dangerous. Cough CPR has a real clinical basis; it has been used in real hospitals; it has been the subject of real medical publications. The June 1999 email chain appears to have derived its core content from a passage in an emergency cardiac care textbook that described the technique in its appropriate professional context. When stripped of that context and reframed as lay advice, the specific indication and the supervision requirements disappeared. What remained was a real technique with its constraints removed.

**Institutional credibility grafting.** The email attributed its advice to Rochester General Hospital and to the *Mended Hearts* newsletter. Both are real, credible institutions. Rochester General is a legitimate academic medical center; *Mended Hearts* is a legitimate cardiac patient support organization. Neither had anything to do with the email's contents. The email reached *Mended Hearts'* newsletter through a reader submission process with insufficient editorial oversight, and the newsletter's chapter-distribution network then amplified it to a much wider audience than any individual email chain could reach. By the time organizations tried to disavow the content, it had become institutional-feeling by association.

The mechanism here is important: fake medical advice often achieves apparent legitimacy not through any single institutional endorsement, but through the accretion of legitimate-seeming sources over multiple forwarding generations. By the time a recipient sees an email that has been forwarded several times and attributes itself to a hospital, a nonprofit, and a medical publication, the provenance feels authoritative even if every attribution is false or misrepresented.

**The self-sufficient narrative.** The email explicitly addresses the vulnerability of being alone during a heart attack — "since many people are alone when they suffer a heart attack." This framing resonates with genuine human experience. People do have heart attacks while alone. The fantasy of a technique that can bridge the window between symptom onset and emergency services arrival is understandable and emotionally resonant. It also plays to cultural distrust of dependency on emergency systems — concerns about response times, ambulance costs, accessibility in rural areas, fears of being helpless.

**Confirmation from the principle of mechanism.** Lay audiences told that "coughing squeezes the heart and keeps the blood circulating" can mentally simulate the mechanism and find it intuitively plausible. The thoracic pump function has a logical basis. A person without clinical training cannot easily distinguish between "this mechanism has limited application in supervised clinical settings" and "this mechanism will help me at home during any cardiac event." The mechanistic plausibility acts as confirmation for those who do not know what questions to ask about appropriate indications.


## 4. Verdict

**MIXTURE** — corresponding to Snopes' own verdict on this fact-check.

The claim is a mixture because cough CPR exists as a real medical technique with documented use in appropriate clinical settings, and the underlying mechanism is physiologically real. However, as advice for laypeople experiencing cardiac symptoms alone, the claim is dangerous: it conflates distinct cardiac events with dramatically different appropriate responses, cannot be safely executed without medical supervision and real-time ECG monitoring to identify the correct arrhythmia type, and could worsen or precipitate death in someone experiencing a standard myocardial infarction.

The American Heart Association does not recommend cough CPR for public self-administration. The organizations falsely credited as the email's source have explicitly disavowed it. The technique's appropriate role is in supervised clinical contexts where a physician can verify indication and oversee execution.

**If you believe you are having a heart attack:**
1. Call emergency services immediately (911 in the US)
2. Chew (do not swallow whole) a 325 mg aspirin if available and not allergic or contraindicated by medication
3. Sit or lie down — do not exert yourself
4. Unlock your door if able, so emergency responders can enter
5. Do not attempt cough CPR unless instructed by a physician with real-time monitoring capability

The specific danger of the viral email instruction is not merely that it offers bad advice — it is that following it could convert a survivable heart attack into a fatal one. That warrants genuine public health attention rather than treatment as a benign folk remedy.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The heart attack survival landscape.** Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death in the United States and globally. Approximately 805,000 Americans have a heart attack each year; about 1 in 5 are silent myocardial infarctions, occurring without recognized symptoms. The determinants of survival in myocardial infarction are dominated by two variables: time from symptom onset to first medical contact, and time from first medical contact to coronary reperfusion (restoration of blood flow). Every minute of delay in receiving thrombolytic therapy or percutaneous coronary intervention after symptom onset measurably increases infarct size and reduces survival probability.

In this context, any recommendation that delays the call to emergency services — by occupying the patient with a self-rescue maneuver, by creating the impression that a solo action may be sufficient, by distracting from the one action that is known to materially improve outcomes — is directly harmful. The viral email does not explicitly tell people not to call 911, but by offering an alternative action that seems to address the problem, it implicitly competes with the 911 call for the patient's attention in the first critical minutes of symptom onset.

**Viral medical misinformation's structural features.** The cough CPR email exemplifies structural features that characterize durable medical misinformation:

*Real mechanism, wrong context:* The technique has legitimate clinical application, lending plausibility to the lay version.

*Institutional credibility grafting:* False or appropriated attribution to trusted medical institutions.

*Emotional appeal to self-sufficiency:* Addressing a real vulnerability (isolation during cardiac emergency) with a proposed solution.

*Unsupervised simplification:* Stripping a procedure of its prerequisites and presenting the action without the context that defines when it is appropriate.

*Good-faith forwarding:* Well-intentioned senders forwarding what they genuinely believe is helpful information, bypassing the critical evaluation that might accompany a more obviously commercial or ideological message.

These features allow the email to persist across decades of internet culture in ways that overtly false claims cannot.

**The aspirin recommendation in context.** The aspirin recommendation embedded in the Snopes analysis of this claim represents an example of evidence-based emergency first aid that genuinely is applicable broadly, does not require clinical supervision to execute safely, and has significant mortality benefit when adopted. The contrast between the aspirin recommendation (simple, generalizable, evidence-supported, endorsed by cardiac professional organizations) and the cough CPR recommendation (complicated, non-generalizable, supervision-dependent, not endorsed by cardiac professional organizations) is instructive for how to evaluate emergency medical advice found in informal communication channels.

**CPR training and AED access.** The genuine public health intervention available to laypeople witnessing cardiac arrest — not the person experiencing it, but witnesses — is standard CPR and the use of automated external defibrillators (AEDs). Hands-only CPR (chest compressions without rescue breathing) is now recommended by the AHA for untrained bystanders and has become widely disseminated through public awareness campaigns. AED programs in public spaces provide a real intervention for out-of-hospital cardiac arrest. Investment in these genuine evidence-based interventions — which require training but which are genuinely effective — represents the appropriate public health response to the problem the cough CPR email was attempting to solve.

**Medical misinformation and emergency preparedness.** The persistence of the cough CPR email illustrates the broader challenge of medical misinformation in emergency preparedness contexts. People desire practical, actionable knowledge about how to respond to medical emergencies, and this demand is legitimate. When official public health messaging does not satisfy that desire with sufficient clarity and practical detail, the gap is filled by well-intentioned but potentially harmful informal advice. Effective emergency preparedness communication must directly address the scenarios people fear — including being alone during a cardiac event — with genuinely applicable guidance.

**The distinction between cardiac arrest and heart attack in public communication.** One of the practical legacies of the cough CPR email's circulation is that it has muddied public understanding of the difference between cardiac arrest and myocardial infarction. These terms are frequently used interchangeably in lay communication but refer to meaningfully different events with different appropriate responses. Heart attack is a plumbing problem (blockage); cardiac arrest is an electrical problem (failure of rhythm coordination). Public health education that clarifies this distinction — and that provides clear response guidance for each — has genuine preventive value independent of the cough CPR claim specifically.

**The Polish research cited by Snopes.** The Snopes fact-check references 2003 research by Dr. Tadeusz Petelenz of the Silesian Medical Academy in Katowice, Poland, who claimed to have instructed cardiac patients in cough CPR with positive results. As Snopes noted, these results were not independently confirmed, and the methodology — specifically, whether patients were performing the technique under medical supervision or independently, and whether their arrhythmia diagnoses were confirmed — was questioned by reviewing cardiologists including Dr. Marten Rosenquist of the Karolinska Institute. The citation of this research in forwarded versions of the claim represents a form of credibility laundering through academic-seeming reference that does not actually support the claim being made.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addressed this claim in a fact-check originally published September 2, 2003, authored by Barbara Mikkelson. The verdict is **Mixture**, and the analysis is detailed and medically well-grounded. The fact-check clearly explains the distinction between the cardiac events in which cough CPR has documented clinical use and the broader category of cardiac events to which the viral email applies the technique. It quotes Dr. Richard O. Cummins and incorporates the statement from Mended Hearts' executive director Darla Bonham, who investigated the American Heart Association's position and conveyed it clearly: the AHA does not recommend public use of cough CPR without medical supervision.

The fact-check also directly quotes Rochester General Hospital's disavowal of the email attribution, notes that the hospital has a page on its website specifically addressing the misinformation, and records Mended Hearts' acknowledgment that the newsletter publication happened through insufficient editorial oversight.

Notably, Snopes positions the aspirin recommendation as the appropriate countervailing advice — practical first aid that genuinely does have broad applicability, is endorsed by cardiac professional organizations, and does not carry the risk of worsening a myocardial infarction. This framing is effective: it does not leave the reader without any self-care advice, but directs that desire for agency toward a genuinely helpful intervention.

The Snopes fact-check has served as a durable reference since 2003, periodically cited in public health communication when the email resurfaces. Its limitation is the one intrinsic to any 2003 text: it predates the social media era in which this kind of email-chain content found new distribution mechanisms and new audiences. The underlying medical guidance has not substantially changed, but the distribution environment has, and periodic updating of the fact-check's framing to address social media dissemination would strengthen its practical utility.

This Truth Vault entry extends the Snopes analysis by situating the claim within the broader framework of cardiac emergency epidemiology, the structural features of medical misinformation, and the public health case for evidence-based emergency preparedness communication — context that complements the fact-check's core verdict.
