# "Counting Sheep Helps You Fall Asleep"

## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: Counting imaginary sheep — visualizing a pastoral scene and mentally tallying white sheep as they leap one by one over a fence — is an effective method for inducing sleep onset. The practice is held to quiet the racing mind, provide a sufficiently monotonous mental occupation to prevent intrusive wakefulness thoughts, and gradually allow the brain to transition into sleep. When you cannot sleep, count sheep.

**Cultural establishment**: Few sleep recommendations are as culturally entrenched as counting sheep. The practice appears in nursery rhymes, children's books, cartoons, and adult humor as the canonical response to insomnia. Characters who cannot sleep in film and television are depicted counting sheep almost reflexively. Advice columns, parenting guides, and casual conversation produce the recommendation as if its efficacy were self-evident. The sheep leaping over a fence has become such an archetypal symbol of the experience of trying to sleep that it functions as visual shorthand for insomnia even outside any literal recommendation context.

**The plausibility argument**: The claim is not implausible a priori. Cognitive psychology and behavioral sleep medicine recognize that intrusive thoughts — rumination about worries, planning about tomorrow, replaying of the day's events — are among the primary cognitive factors that maintain arousal and impede sleep onset in insomniacs (Harvey, 2000, *Behaviour Research and Therapy*). If an intruding task can displace these arousing thoughts without itself generating arousal, it could in principle facilitate sleep onset. Counting sheep might work by occupying attentional resources with a neutral, repetitive task that competes successfully enough with worrying to allow arousal to wind down.

**Behavioral sleep medicine context**: The general category of cognitive distraction as a sleep aid is scientifically legitimate. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), the gold-standard evidence-based treatment for chronic insomnia, includes a component called stimulus control and another called cognitive restructuring, both of which involve managing the thought content of pre-sleep wakefulness. The question of whether counting sheep specifically is an effective cognitive-distraction technique within this general framework is an empirical one that has received direct investigation.

**Why it matters**: The counting-sheep recommendation is widely given to children and adults who have trouble sleeping, and its continued popularity despite available evidence about its (lack of) effectiveness illustrates broader questions about folk-remedy persistence and the gap between popular sleep advice and sleep medicine evidence. For the substantial minority of adults who experience regular insomnia — estimated at 10–30% of the population depending on the definition used (Ohayon, 2002, *Sleep Medicine Reviews*) — the difference between an effective and an ineffective sleep intervention is clinically meaningful.

**Variants of the approach**: Not all versions of the counting-sheep recommendation are identical. Some specify visualization of sheep jumping over a fence. Others prescribe simply counting backward from 300 by threes — a different cognitive demand. Some versions emphasize the pastoral imagery rather than the counting, treating the green meadow and white sheep as the effective element rather than the numerical tallying. And some advisors recommend counting breaths rather than sheep, which is closer to the mindfulness-based breathing techniques that do have evidence support. These variants differ in their proposed mechanism and their evidence base, and conflating them under the general heading of "counting sheep" obscures important distinctions.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Oxford study: direct experimental testing**: The most cited direct empirical test of counting sheep as a sleep-onset technique is Allison Harvey and Suzanna Payne's study published in *Behaviour Research and Therapy* (Harvey and Payne, 2002, *Behaviour Research and Therapy*, 40:267–274). Harvey and Payne recruited insomniacs and randomly assigned them to one of three conditions on different nights: (1) counting sheep, (2) engaging in mentally engaging and emotionally pleasant imagery (imagining a relaxing holiday scene in vivid detail), and (3) doing nothing (the control, reflective of the participants' usual experience).

The key outcome variable was sleep onset latency — the time from lights-out to falling asleep — measured by self-report across multiple nights. The results were clear and remarkable: participants in the imagining-a-relaxing-scene condition fell asleep approximately 20 minutes faster than in the control (doing-nothing) condition. Participants in the **counting-sheep condition fell asleep no faster than in the control condition** — and numerically fell asleep slightly more slowly, though this difference was not statistically significant. The counting sheep condition offered essentially no benefit over simply lying in the dark with an uninstructed mind.

Harvey and Payne's interpretation emphasized the distinction between two types of cognitive-distraction techniques: those that are sufficiently engaging and sufficiently pleasant to displace arousing worry thoughts (such as rich imagined holiday scenes), and those that are insufficiently engaging to do so (such as the repetitive and relatively content-sparse activity of counting sheep). Counting sheep apparently fails to generate enough mental content to effectively occupy the attentional system and competitively inhibit intrusive worry. The pastoral imagery is too sparse; the counting is too automatic and low-demand; the combination does not successfully compete for the cognitive resources that rumination uses.

**The attentional resource account**: The Harvey-Payne finding integrates with a broader model of pre-sleep cognitive activity developed by work on the cognitive neuropsychological model of insomnia (Harvey, 2002, *Psychological Review*). Under this model, chronic insomnia is maintained by a cycle in which arousal produces attentional bias toward sleep-related threat (perceived failure to sleep, consequences of insufficient rest), which generates additional arousal in a self-sustaining loop. The intrusive thoughts that keep insomniacs awake are not random; they are biased toward worry, threat, and performance monitoring.

For a distraction technique to interrupt this loop, it must command enough attentional resources to displace threat-monitoring. Vivid, emotionally positive mental imagery does this effectively because it recruits the spatial, narrative, and affective processing systems of working memory simultaneously. Counting sheep provides a thin serial task that leaves the vast majority of attentional capacity available for threat monitoring to continue unimpeded. It is too dimensionally sparse to compete.

**Counting backward and related numerical tasks**: Counting backward from 300 by threes, sometimes recommended as a variation on counting sheep, imposes greater cognitive demand than simple serial counting — it requires active arithmetic subtraction — and may be somewhat more effective at occupying working memory resources. However, this increased demand creates its own problem: the difficulty of the task can be frustrating for someone in a state of sleep-related anxiety, and failures at the arithmetic (losing count, making errors) can themselves become arousal-generating events. More challenging arithmetic tasks have been found in some clinical reports to increase rather than decrease presleep arousal for anxious insomniacs.

**Breathing-based techniques and their distinct evidence base**: Breath counting — counting each exhale from one to ten and repeating — is categorically different from counting sheep and has a better evidence basis. Breath counting is the foundation of many mindfulness-based relaxation techniques, and there is evidence that it reduces physiological arousal, may redirect attentional focus from worry to bodily sensation, and supports sleep onset in some populations. A study by Levenson et al. (2014, *Frontiers in Psychology*) found that breath-counting accuracy correlated with sleep quality measures and that people with better breath-counting performance showed reduced pre-sleep worry. The mechanism here is related to, but distinct from, the counting-sheep approach: breath counting uses an external focus (actual breathed exhalation) and a short reliable cycle that is harder to automate than sheep-counting, and is integrated with physiological relaxation through its link to respiration rate.

**Relaxation imagery techniques with positive evidence**: Multiple well-constructed studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of relaxation imagery for sleep onset. Guided imagery techniques — visualizing peaceful scenes, beaches, forest walks, or other personally calming environments — consistently show effects on sleep onset latency when compared to no-intervention controls. The Harvey-Payne study result, in which the imagery condition outperformed the sheep-counting condition by approximately 20 minutes, is consistent with this broader literature. Stimulus-specific imagery approaches developed within the framework of Image Rehearsal Therapy (originally for nightmare disorder; Krakow and Zadra, 2006, *Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine*) have demonstrated that mental imagery is a substantively powerful tool for sleep intervention — just not in the thin, repetitive form that sheep-counting instantiates.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the phenomenology of boring tasks and the paradox of effort**:

Counting sheep is held to work through a seemingly reasonable psychology: boring tasks make you sleepy, and the only thing less exciting than counting sheep is nothing at all. This mundane-task-induces-sleep intuition is rooted in a real phenomenon: monotonous, low-stimulation environments do facilitate sleep in normal sleepers in normal circumstances. Long car rides, repetitive lectures, and uneventful afternoons reliably produce drowsiness in people who are already sleep-inclined. The counting-sheep claim generalizes from this genuine pattern — boring things are soporific — to the insomniac's bedroom, where the fundamental problem is not insufficient boring stimuli but active autonomic arousal that overrides sleep drive.

This is the key epistemic error: generalizing from normal sleep facilitation (reduced stimulation leads to sleep in someone who is not aroused) to insomnia management (reducing stimulation leads to sleep in someone who is aroused). Insomnia by definition involves a decoupling between sleep drive and actual sleep onset, typically maintained by arousal (cognitive, physiological, or both). The tools that help normal sleepers transition into sleep faster do not necessarily help insomniacs, and may even be counterproductive if they provide just enough cognitive engagement to sustain wakefulness without enough engagement to displace worry.

There is also what might be called the paradox of intentional sleep effort. Research on sleep-onset ironic processes, drawing on Wegner's ironic process theory of mental control (Wegner et al., 1987, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*), suggests that trying too hard to sleep can itself produce insomnia. The act of monitoring whether one has fallen asleep requires not being asleep, so intensive sleep monitoring maintains arousal. Counting sheep is a way of doing something without directly trying to sleep, which might seem to bypass the ironic-monitoring problem — but because sheep-counting is so low-demand, it tends to alternate with monitoring rather than replacing it.

Additionally, counting sheep is one of the most culturally transmitted pieces of sleep advice, and the transmission channel is primarily childhood — the recommendation is given to children by parents and depicted in children's media. Because many children do not have clinical insomnia (their difficulty sleeping is typically situational rather than chronic), occasional sheep-counting may coincide with eventual sleep onset and be credited with causing it, when the actual cause was simply time passing until arousal resolved naturally. This childhood-origin positive reinforcement creates a community of adult believers who remember sheep-counting working in childhood without recognizing that the childhood context was not comparably arousing.

The persistence of the myth is also supported by the absence of systematic self-experiment. Most people who try counting sheep in a bad night's sleep are not running a controlled trial. They count for a while, eventually fall asleep, and attribute the outcome to the counting. Selection effects further bias this: on nights when arousal is extremely high and sleep is very delayed, people may not count sheep at all, or may give up on counting before eventually sleeping. On nights when arousal is moderate and sleep comes within an hour or two, counting sheep may be tried and credited. The practical self-report error rate is high.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE as a general sleep recommendation**: The experimental evidence is clear that counting sheep does not improve sleep onset latency for insomniacs compared to doing nothing, and that it is substantially inferior to more engaging mental imagery techniques. The Harvey and Payne (2002) finding has not been substantially challenged by replications, and the cognitive mechanism it identifies — that sheep-counting is too dimensionally spare to displace worry effectively — is theoretically coherent and well-grounded.

**Nuance for non-insomniacs**: The verdict applied here is specifically for the insomniac population, which is the population for whom the advice is most commonly offered and most consequential. For a non-insomniac — a person with normal sleep architecture who simply has a delayed sleep onset on a particular night — even ineffective distraction techniques may do no harm, and any form of quiet attention direction might transiently help. The recommendation is false in the context where it matters most: people with chronic insomnia, for whom it is not only ineffective but potentially actively counterproductive.

**What actually works instead**: Evidence-based alternatives for improving sleep onset include: (1) vivid relaxation imagery involving emotionally pleasant and spatially rich scenes (Harvey and Payne, 2002); (2) progressive muscle relaxation, which combines cognitive distraction with direct physiological arousal reduction (Bernstein and Borkovec, 1973, *Progressive Relaxation Training*); (3) paradoxical intention, in which the insomniac attempts to stay awake as long as possible without engaging in stimulating activity (Ascher and Turner, 1979, *Behaviour Research and Therapy*); (4) stimulus control therapy (Bootzin, 1972), which restricts bed use to sleep and sex only to strengthen the associative conditioning between bed and sleep. All of these techniques are components of CBT-I, which has demonstrated superiority over pharmacological sleep aids in long-term outcomes (Morin et al., 2009, *JAMA Internal Medicine*).

**The real sheep**: Actual sheep have no role in sleep physiology. Sheep were probably recruited into the counting-for-sleep tradition because they are quintessentially docile, visually similar to one another (supporting the monotony effect), and pre-industrially associated with pastoral settings conducive to rest. The symbolic pastoral landscape — meadow, fence, identical woolly sheep hopping in sequence — packages soporific visual imagery in a form that is culturally stable and easily transmitted. But the imagery itself is insufficiently rich and emotionally engaging to work as the kind of vivid, immersive relaxation scene that Harvey and Payne found effective. Ironically, if you replaced sheep-counting with rich, vivid, emotionally positive sheep-related imagery — imagining yourself in the middle of a warm foggy meadow, feeling the grass under your feet, hearing the distant bells on a flock, feeling the cool evening air — this might approach the kind of relaxation imagery that actually does facilitate sleep. The problem is the counting and the thinness of the associated imagery, not the sheep.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The history of counting as a sleep induction technique**: The specific recommendation to count sheep has been traced by linguistic historians to at least the fourteenth century in British literary sources, with variant forms appearing in manuscript literature describing pastoral scenes of shepherds counting their flocks as a late-night mental occupation (Ekirch, 2005, *At Day's Close: Night in Times Past*). The recommendation may reflect pre-industrial patterns of sleep that were quite different from modern consolidated eight-hour sleep. Historian Roger Ekirch argues extensively in *At Day's Close* that before artificial lighting, pre-industrial Europeans and other populations commonly experienced segmented sleep — a first sleep of three to four hours, then a period of wakefulness of one to two hours, then a second sleep. During the mid-night waking period, people engaged in a range of quiet activities including prayer, mild conversation, sex, and reflection. Counting — as a form of quiet mental occupation during the waking interval of segmented sleep — may have a history rooted in managing the in-between state of segmented sleep, not the pathological state of insomnia.

**Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I)**: CBT-I is the primary evidence-based psychological treatment for chronic insomnia disorder. It includes several components: sleep restriction therapy (temporarily limiting time in bed to consolidate sleep drive); stimulus control (reserving the bed for sleep and sex only); sleep hygiene education; relaxation techniques; and cognitive components (addressing dysfunctional beliefs about sleep and sleep consequences). A comprehensive meta-analysis by Morin et al. (2006, *Sleep*) found that CBT-I produced significant and durable improvements in sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset, and total sleep time, with effect sizes comparable to or exceeding those of pharmacological sleep aids and with advantages in the maintenance of gains after treatment ends. Counting sheep is not a component of CBT-I.

**The paradox of effort in sleep**: The sleep-effort paradox — that trying harder to sleep makes it harder to sleep — has a robust empirical basis established through Wegner's ironic process paradigm (Wegner et al., 1987) and its sleep-specific implications (Espie et al., 2006, *Sleep Medicine Reviews*). Espie and colleagues proposed an attentional control model of insomnia in which sleep onset normally requires the disengagement of intentional attention from the sleep process, allowing automatic sleep-onset processes to operate without interference. Chronic insomniacs are hypothesized to have disrupted this automaticity, with attentional monitoring of the sleep process creating a sustained vigilance loop that prevents the disengagement required. Counting sheep, by providing a nominal task, might seem to facilitate disengagement — but because the task is so cognitively thin, monitoring intrudes continuously.

**Placebo effects and sleep**: A complicating factor in evaluating any sleep recommendation is the role of placebo effects in sleep improvement. Research has documented robust placebo effects in sleep studies: participants told they are receiving an effective sleep treatment show real improvements in subjectively measured sleep quality, and some improvements in objective actigraphy measures, even when the treatment is inert (Guardia et al., 2019, *Journal of Sleep Research*). Counting sheep, believed to be effective, might produce a placebo sleep improvement through expectancy mechanisms. This possibility does not rescue the recommendation — placebo effects require the recipient to believe in the treatment, and in an era when the debunking is widely available, belief is not guaranteed — but it means that the Harvey-Payne null result for sheep-counting may represent a lower bound on the effect in highly credulous users.

**Sleep folklore across cultures**: The counting-sheep technique is specifically Northern European and North American in cultural origin and distribution. Many other cultures have distinct folk remedies for sleep difficulties that also make claims to efficacy without strong evidentiary support. South Asian traditions including Ayurvedic medicine prescribe warm milk with specific additives (including turmeric and nutmeg); Chinese traditional medicine includes herbal preparations including jujube and lily bulb; Japanese traditional practice emphasizes specific breathing exercises. The universal cross-cultural existence of sleep-onset folk practices speaks both to the universality of insomnia-like experiences and to the universal tendency to generate explanatory frameworks and action recommendations for them, most of which have not been rigorously evaluated.

**The technology of sleep improvement**: Contemporary commercial interest in sleep improvement has generated a large market in sleep-enhancing technologies, apps, supplements, and accessories, most of which have limited evidence of efficacy. Technology-assisted sleep interventions share a structural problem with counting sheep: they provide a nominal intervention that users can believe in, often without the mechanism or dose that the evidence base would require. Weighted blankets, white noise machines, specific sleep-preparation apps, and many supplements are sold on the basis of limited evidence that they improve sleep quality. Counting sheep fits into this broader ecosystem of plausible-seeming sleep interventions whose evidence basis is weak — and serves as a historical prototype for the commercial sleep-improvement category.

**The evidence gap in popular sleep advice**: A meta-level observation about counting sheep is that its persistence illustrates a broader gap between popular sleep advice and evidence-based sleep medicine. Survey research has found that common sleep recommendations given by general practitioners, parenting guides, and health advice columns include several interventions with little or no supporting evidence (Lacks and Morin, 1992, *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology*), alongside genuinely effective recommendations like maintaining a regular sleep schedule and limiting caffeine. The counting-sheep recommendation is not uniquely bad by the standards of popular sleep advice; it is representative of a category of folk-wisdom recommendations that have never been rigorously evaluated (or, in this case, have been evaluated and found wanting).


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes verdict**: Snopes rates the claim as false, noting the Harvey-Payne study directly and pointing out that the experimental evidence found no benefit for sheep-counting over doing nothing, while finding robust benefit for relaxation imagery. The Snopes article emphasizes the specific experimental design — insomniacs assigned to different cognitive conditions across different nights — as the kind of controlled evidence that distinguishes this entry from folk belief.

**Science media coverage**: Sleep science coverage in science journalism has broadly sided with the Harvey-Payne finding. Popular-science treatments in *New Scientist*, *Scientific American Mind*, *The Guardian's* science section, and similar publications have used the counting-sheep study as an illustration of the gap between intuitive sleep remedies and evidence-based sleep management. The study has been in circulation for long enough that it has reached secondary and tertiary science journalism coverage, meaning the debunking is available in reasonable search inquiries.

**The gap between awareness of debunking and behavior change**: As with many debunked home remedies, awareness that counting sheep doesn't work has not fully translated into a cessation of its recommendation. The recommendation continues to appear in parenting content, children's media, and casual conversation even among people who are aware of the Harvey-Payne finding, because its cultural function as a dismissive response to "I can't sleep" is so well-established that it survives its falsification as a useful cultural shorthand. Telling a child to count sheep when they can't sleep performs a social function (acknowledging the problem, offering comfort, modeling that there are strategies to try) regardless of its pharmacological efficacy.

**What sleep medicine recommends instead**: Clinicians specializing in behavioral sleep medicine would broadly recommend: CBT-I for chronic insomnia; basic sleep hygiene (consistent sleep and wake times, dark and cool sleep environment, avoidance of screens before sleep); relaxation imagery techniques; and, for those with difficulty due to thought intrusion, scheduled worry time earlier in the day followed by a dedicated cognitive defusion exercise before bed. None of these involve counting sheep. Several require working with a trained sleep therapist or following a structured multi-week program. The evidence-based alternatives are generally more effortful and less intuitively satisfying than counting sheep, which may be part of why folk alternatives like sheep-counting persist despite being ineffective.

**Calibrated confidence**: The counting-sheep-helps-sleep claim is false for the population where it matters most (insomniacs with chronic sleep difficulty), based on direct experimental evidence. The mechanism proposed by the claim — that boring repetitive counting displaces arousal-sustaining thought — is both theoretically poorly motivated and empirically disconfirmed. The false claim is likely behavioral neutral for most non-insomniacs (it doesn't hurt, it just doesn't help), but its persistence displaces recommendation of better-evidenced alternatives, constituting a mild but real opportunity cost in public health terms.
