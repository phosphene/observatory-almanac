## 1. The Claim

In fall 2025, as the Northern Hemisphere entered another respiratory virus season four years after the World Health Organization declared the end of the COVID-19 public health emergency, a new rumor began circulating on social media. The claim took the form of a viral video posted by a man identifying himself as "Dr. Tony" on TikTok: that a new strain of the virus causing COVID-19, referred to as "COVID-25," was circulating with "very different signs" compared to earlier strains. The video, which accumulated over 3.1 million views and 114,500 likes, listed a specific set of symptoms: extreme fatigue described as feeling like one had not slept for days; sore throat and dry cough lasting for weeks; mild fever around 100 to 101 degrees Fahrenheit that came and went for no clear reason; intense headache resembling a migraine; and loss of appetite, nausea, and sometimes stomach pain. The video encouraged viewers to search for "latest covid symptoms 2025." Similar iterations of the claim appeared on other TikTok accounts, on Substack, and on X (formerly Twitter), spreading across platforms with the kind of velocity that characterizes pandemic-era health misinformation.

The Snopes article by Anna Rascouët-Paz, published November 11, 2025, addressed these claims with a "False" rating. The piece argued that there was no "COVID-25" strain in circulation; that the symptoms listed by "Dr. Tony" were actually common to many COVID-19 variants and not new at all; that the real circulating strains in fall 2025 were Omicron subvariants known colloquially as "Nimbus" (NB.1.8.1) and "Stratus" (XFG); and that while these strains had some distinctive symptoms — Nimbus's characteristic "razor blade throat" and Stratus's persistent dry cough — these symptoms did not match the list in the viral video.

The article was well-sourced and methodologically sound. It cited the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's symptom page, Stony Brook Medicine's overviews of both Nimbus and Stratus, the World Health Organization's risk evaluation for XFG, and a Nature article on the evolving understanding of COVID-19 symptoms. It demonstrated that the viral video's symptom list substantially overlapped with the CDC's standard symptom list, undermining the claim that these were "very different signs." The piece was a competent debunking of a specific piece of alarmist content.

Yet, as with the J&J vaccine article, the Snopes piece operated within a frame that may have been too dismissive of legitimate public concern. The "COVID-25" label was indeed not an official designation, and the specific TikTok video did not accurately describe novel symptoms. But the underlying reality — that new SARS-CoV-2 variants continued to emerge with distinct clinical presentations — was true. The gap between the viral claim and the factual reality was smaller than the Snopes headline suggested.

## 2. What's Actually True

To understand the claim and its rebuttal, one must understand the virological landscape of 2025. By November 2025, the SARS-CoV-2 virus had been circulating in the human population for approximately six years. It had evolved through numerous major lineages — from the original Wuhan strain through Alpha, Delta, and the Omicron family that had dominated since late 2021. Within the Omicron lineage, countless subvariants had emerged, each carrying mutations that affected transmissibility, immune evasion, and, in some cases, clinical presentation.

In fall 2025, two subvariants were of particular concern. The first, NB.1.8.1, had acquired the informal nickname "Nimbus." According to Stony Brook Medicine, Nimbus was characterized by notably severe sore throat — often described by patients as feeling like swallowing razor blades — accompanied by persistent fatigue, mild cough, nasal congestion, fever, muscle aches, sneezing, and sometimes digestive symptoms. The "razor blade throat" was a genuinely distinctive symptom that distinguished Nimbus from earlier Omicron subvariants in the experience of many patients.

The second subvariant, XFG, known colloquially as "Stratus," was, according to the World Health Organization's June 2025 risk evaluation, outcompeting Nimbus in many regions due to its enhanced ability to evade existing immune defenses. Its signature symptom was a persistent, dry cough that could last for weeks, along with tiredness, fever, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and in some cases loss of taste and smell and brain fog. Again, the persistent dry cough — more prominent and longer-lasting than in prior variants — was a genuine clinical feature.

So the truth was more complicated than "this is just the same old symptoms repackaged." New variants did produce somewhat different clinical pictures. Nimbus's severe sore throat and Stratus's protracted dry cough were real phenomena that clinicians were observing and documenting. The CDC's standard symptom list, which Snopes cited to argue that "Dr. Tony's" symptoms were not new, is a comprehensive list of possible COVID-19 symptoms — but it does not capture which symptoms predominate in which variants, nor does it track the evolution of symptom profiles over time. Saying "these symptoms are on the CDC list" is different from saying "these symptoms are not noteworthy or distinctive."

The official designation is also worth clarifying. There has never been a "COVID-25" variant recognized by the World Health Organization's variant tracking system. The WHO uses Greek letters for Variants of Concern (Alpha, Beta, Gamma, Delta, Omicron) and a scientific lineage system for subvariants. "COVID-25" is a label invented on social media, presumably by analogy to "H1N1" or "COVID-19" itself — suggesting it is the 25th iteration of the virus. It has no official or scientific standing. The Snopes article was correct on this point.

However, the viral claim's substance — that new strains had new symptoms warranting public awareness — was considerably more accurate than the Snopes verdict acknowledged. The problem was not with the fact-checking of "Dr. Tony's" specific symptom list, which was indeed recycled from general COVID-19 symptom lists and presented as novel. The problem was with the framing that implied the entire concern was baseless.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

The Snopes article excels in several dimensions. It correctly identifies that the "COVID-25" label is not an official designation. It accurately documents and traces the specific viral TikTok post that spread the rumor. It consults authoritative sources — the CDC, Stony Brook Medicine, the WHO, Nature — and presents their findings clearly. It demonstrates that the symptom list in the viral video substantially overlaps with the standard CDC symptom list for COVID-19 generally. It provides useful information about the actual circulating variants, Nimbus and Stratus, and their distinguishing features. As a piece of journalism explaining a specific rumor and its inaccuracies, the article is well-executed.

But the article's framing exhibits a pattern common in COVID-19 fact-checking during the later stages of the pandemic: a tendency to characterize any deviation from official messaging as dangerous misinformation, even when the deviation contains significant kernels of truth. The headline — "No, doctors weren't warning about new 'COVID-25' strain with 'very different signs'" — is technically true in its narrowest reading but misleading in its broader implication. Doctors were, in fact, warning about new strains with somewhat different symptoms, even if they were not using the name "COVID-25" and even if the specific symptom list in the viral video was not novel.

The article also underplays the significance of genuine symptom evolution in SARS-CoV-2 variants. By focusing on the overlap between "Dr. Tony's" list and the CDC's master symptom list, Snopes creates the impression that nothing was new or notable about the clinical presentation of 2025 variants. In fact, the shift toward more pronounced upper respiratory symptoms, more severe sore throat, and more persistent cough was a genuine clinical development that public health authorities were tracking. A reader who finished the Snopes article might reasonably conclude that the Nimbus and Stratus variants were essentially the same as earlier strains — which would be a misunderstanding.

The article also does not adequately address the broader phenomenon it was reporting on: pandemic fatigue and the erosion of trust in official communications. By fall 2025, many people had received multiple vaccine doses, had been infected once or multiple times, and had grown weary of the constant cycle of variant news. The viral "COVID-25" video thrived not because people were gullible but because official communication channels had, over years, established a pattern of downplaying variant concerns. The WHO's decision to stop using Greek letters for subvariants after Omicron and to switch to a more technical naming system, while scientifically sound, created a communication vacuum that informal nicknames filled. Nimbus, Stratus, and "COVID-25" all emerged to fill that vacuum, but only the latter was treated as misinformation.

Furthermore, the article does not explore the question of why "Dr. Tony's" specific symptom list resonated. A list of symptoms including extreme fatigue, sore throat, dry cough, intermittent fever, migraine-like headache, and gastrointestinal distress does not describe the common cold or seasonal influenza well; it describes a post-viral syndrome that matches many people's actual experience with COVID-19 in 2025. The symptom overlap with the CDC list is less damning than Snopes implies — the CDC list describes the full range of possible COVID-19 symptoms; any given individual may experience a different subset, and variant-specific symptom clustering is a real phenomenon.

## 4. Why This Matters

The COVID-19 pandemic created an unprecedented demand for real-time health information and an equally unprecedented apparatus for correcting misinformation. Snopes, along with other fact-checking organizations, played a vital role in this ecosystem, flagging dangerous falsehoods about treatments, transmission, and vaccines. But as the pandemic entered its later years, the nature of misinformation changed. The early pandemic was marked by flagrant falsehoods — that bleach could cure COVID, that 5G towers spread the virus, that the vaccine contained microchips. By 2025, the misinformation landscape was characterized by subtler distortions: claims that were not exactly false but that were framed in alarming or unofficial terms.

The "COVID-25" story is a paradigmatic example of this later-stage problem. The core observation — that new strains were circulating with somewhat different symptoms — was correct. The mistake was in the name and in the implication that official channels were suppressing this information. The Snopes response, by emphasizing what was false about the claim rather than what was true, may have inadvertently reinforced the perception that "they" (the media, the authorities, the experts) were covering up variant evolution.

This matters because the relationship between public health authorities and the public depends on trust, and trust depends on the perception of honesty. When fact-checkers dismiss claims that contain substantial true elements, they risk being perceived as propagandists rather than truth-seekers. Over time, this erodes the credibility of the fact-checking enterprise itself, making it harder to flag genuinely dangerous misinformation when it appears.

The article also touches on a deeper question about how we talk about viral evolution. SARS-CoV-2 will continue to evolve for the foreseeable future. New variants will continue to emerge, some with clinically meaningful differences in symptom presentation. The public needs a communication framework that can convey this reality without inducing panic and without being dismissed as alarmism. The current framework — in which any unofficial variant naming is treated as misinformation — is not sustainable. People will notice that new variants cause different symptoms, and if official channels do not acknowledge this, unofficial channels will fill the gap.

There is also a question about the role of platforms like TikTok in health communication. "Dr. Tony" may or may not have been a medical doctor; Snopes noted that it could not confirm his credentials. But the fact that a single TikTok video could reach 3.1 million views speaks to both the hunger for variant-specific health information and the failure of official channels to provide it in an accessible format. The response from fact-checkers should include not just debunking but also a self-critical examination of why the official information ecosystem is losing the attention battle.

## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article draws primarily on medical and public health sources. The CDC symptom page is a standard reference for COVID-19 clinical presentation. Stony Brook Medicine's overviews of Nimbus and Stratus are institutionally credible and provide useful detail on variant-specific symptoms. The WHO risk evaluation for XFG is an authoritative source on variant epidemiology. The Nature article by Katie Kavanagh provides scientific context on evolving COVID-19 symptoms.

These sources are appropriate for the factual claims the article makes. However, there are notable gaps in the sourcing. The article does not cite any primary epidemiological data on symptom prevalence by variant, such as the Zoe Health Study or the UK Health Security Agency's variant surveillance reports. These sources would have allowed Snopes to substantiate or refute more precisely the claim that the symptom profile was genuinely different for 2025 variants.

The article also does not include any perspective from clinicians working in emergency medicine or primary care during fall 2025. A practicing physician or nurse practitioner could have provided firsthand testimony about whether the symptom presentation they were seeing differed from earlier waves. The absence of frontline clinical voices is a significant gap.

The article fails to engage with the phenomenon of variant naming and public communication. Communications scholars, health literacy experts, and public health messaging researchers have written extensively about the challenges of communicating variant evolution to the public. The WHO's own advisory group has published guidance on variant naming conventions. Engagement with this literature would have deepened the article's analysis.

The article also does not adequately contextualize the CDC symptom list. The CDC list is cumulative, encompassing all symptoms reported in any COVID-19 case since the beginning of the pandemic. It is not a list of symptoms specifically associated with 2025 variants. Citing the CDC list to argue that symptoms are "not new" is therefore somewhat circular — of course any symptom ever reported will appear on a list of all symptoms ever reported.

Notably, the article does include an archival link to the TikTok video, allowing readers to verify the claim for themselves. This is a good practice in fact-checking and should be commended. The article also provides a useful overview of the actual variants circulating in fall 2025 and their distinguishing features, which serves as a constructive counter-narrative to the viral alarmism.

## 6. Verdict

The Snopes article "No, doctors weren't warning about new 'COVID-25' strain with 'very different signs'" is a factually sound debunking of a specific viral claim. There is no officially designated "COVID-25" variant. The specific symptom list in the viral TikTok video was not novel — it substantially overlapped with the general symptom profile of COVID-19. The real variants circulating in fall 2025 were Omicron subvariants NB.1.8.1 (Nimbus) and XFG (Stratus), and while these had some distinctive clinical features — notably Nimbus's severe sore throat and Stratus's persistent dry cough — these did not match "Dr. Tony's" list.

However, the article's framing is narrower than the phenomenon it addresses. By focusing on the specific inaccuracies of one TikTok video, Snopes understates the legitimate truth at the core of the claim: new viral variants do produce somewhat different clinical presentations, and public awareness of this fact is both warranted and useful. The article would have been strengthened by a more balanced assessment of which parts of the concern were valid, why people were turning to TikTok for variant information, and how official communication channels could better meet this legitimate need.

The gap category for this entry is "distorted but grounded": the specific claim about "COVID-25" with a set of entirely new symptoms is a distortion, but the underlying observation that new strains have distinct symptom profiles is factually grounded. The distortion is in the packaging — the invented name, the implication of coverup — not in the core observation.

The verdict reveals a recurring tension in pandemic-era fact-checking. A fact-check that says "this is false" about a claim that contains substantial truth risks alienating the audience it seeks to inform. The reader who knows that they personally experienced different symptoms in 2025 than in 2023 may reject the entire Snopes article as another example of media dishonesty, undermining the very trust that fact-checking is supposed to build. The lesson for the Truth Vault is that fact-checking in the post-pandemic era requires a higher degree of nuance: the ability to say "this specific claim is inaccurate, but the concern driving it is real."
