## 1. The Claim

On August 30, 2022, Snopes published an article titled "COVID Researchers: Racial Disparities In Vaccine Booster Rates Persist," reporting on findings from the National Foundation for Infectious Diseases (NFID) that significant racial and ethnic disparities persisted in COVID-19 booster shot uptake across the United States. The report, based on CDC data from mid-2022, showed that while 41 percent of White American adults had received a booster dose, only 29 percent of Hispanic American adults and 28 percent of Black American adults had done so. The article framed these disparities as a matter of health equity concern, quoting NFID experts who attributed the gap to a combination of factors including access to care, trust in the healthcare system, and exposure to vaccine information.

Snopes rated the claim "Correct" — a category meaning that the core factual assertion made by the NFID and covered by Snopes was accurate according to the available data. And they were right about the data: the CDC numbers were real, the disparities were real, and the NFID's concern was justified.

But the "Correct" verdict, while factually sound, obscures a far more complex reality about racial disparities in vaccine uptake. The question is not whether the disparities exist — they do — but what causes them and what they mean. The Snopes article, like much mainstream coverage of vaccine equity, implicitly attributed the disparities to a combination of "vaccine hesitancy" and access barriers, without adequately examining the structural factors that produced both the hesitancy and the access barriers. The result is a verdict that is "Correct" in a narrow, data-verification sense but that may contribute to a shallow understanding of a deep and systemic problem.

This entry in the Truth Vault examines what the NFID data actually showed, what the Snopes article got right and wrong, and what the persistence of racial disparities in vaccine uptake tells us about the limitations of fact-checking as a tool for understanding structural inequality.

## 2. What's Actually True

The CDC data underlying the NFID report was collected through the National Immunization Survey Adult COVID Module, a telephone survey conducted by the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics. The data reflected booster uptake as of June 2022, approximately eight months after the CDC first recommended booster doses for all adults aged 18 and older. The data showed the following pattern:

- White American adults: 41% received at least one booster dose
- Hispanic American adults: 29% received at least one booster dose
- Black American adults: 28% received at least one booster dose
- Asian American adults: 57% received at least one booster dose
- American Indian / Alaska Native adults: 31% received at least one booster dose

The disparities were statistically significant and persisted even after controlling for age, geographic region, and other demographic variables. The pattern was consistent across the course of the booster rollout: from the initial authorization in September 2021 through the expanded eligibility in the spring of 2022, Black and Hispanic Americans consistently lagged behind White Americans in booster uptake.

The Snopes article accurately reported these numbers. It quoted NFID Medical Director Dr. William Schaffner, who attributed the disparities to "a combination of factors, including access to care, trust in the healthcare system, and exposure to vaccine information." The article also noted that the disparities were part of a longer-term pattern in COVID-19 vaccination, dating back to the original primary series rollout in early 2021.

There is no factual dispute about the data. The CDC numbers are public, verifiable, and consistent with other analyses of vaccine uptake by race and ethnicity. The Snopes article's central claim — that racial disparities in booster rates persisted through mid-2022 — is correct in every particular.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's August 2022 article was a straightforward instance of data verification: a research organization (NFID) released a report summarizing CDC data, Snopes checked the numbers against the source data, found them accurate, and rated the report "Correct." For the narrow question of whether the NFID's factual assertions matched the CDC data, the "Correct" verdict is appropriate.

But the article's framing and its omissions reveal the limitations of fact-checking when applied to structural inequality. Several issues deserve scrutiny.

First, the Snopes article implicitly attributed the disparity to a combination of "access" and "trust" without examining what those terms mean in practice. "Access" is not a single variable that can be fixed by opening more clinics or extending hours. It encompasses a complex web of factors: transportation availability, paid time off for medical appointments, childcare, language barriers, digital divide issues in scheduling platforms, and the geographic distribution of vaccination sites. Black and Hispanic communities disproportionately faced each of these access barriers. The Snopes article did not explore this complexity, leaving readers with the impression that the access problem was simple and soluble.

Second, the article's treatment of "trust in the healthcare system" was similarly shallow. Medical mistrust in Black and Hispanic communities is not an abstract cultural attitude; it is a rational response to documented histories of medical abuse and discrimination. The Tuskegee Syphilis Study, in which Black men with syphilis were left untreated for decades so that researchers could study the progression of the disease, is the most famous example, but it is far from the only one. Forced sterilizations of Black, Indigenous, and Latina women, the use of Black bodies for anatomical dissection without consent, and contemporary documented disparities in pain treatment between Black and White patients are all part of the lived experience that shapes medical trust. The Snopes article, by framing trust as a simple "barrier to overcome," implied that the problem was on the patient side rather than on the system side.

Third, the article did not address the role of structural racism in producing the disparities it reported. The CDC data showed racial disparities in booster uptake, but those disparities are not a natural phenomenon; they are the downstream consequence of centuries of unequal access to healthcare, differential treatment within the healthcare system, and the concentration of environmental and occupational risks in communities of color. The NFID's framing, quoted approvingly by Snopes, attributed the disparities to individual-level factors (trust, information exposure) rather than structural ones. Snopes did not question this framing.

Fourth, and most critically, the article did not explore the interaction between the booster disparity and the primary series disparity. By mid-2022, Black and Hispanic Americans had already been less likely to receive the primary vaccine series than White Americans. The booster disparity was, in large part, a continuation of the primary series disparity — a cumulative gap that widened with each successive recommendation. The Snopes article presented the booster gap as a standalone finding without acknowledging that it was the predictable continuation of a pattern that had been observed and reported for over a year.

Fifth, the Snopes article's focus on the NFID report's factual accuracy rather than its interpretive framework meant that the article missed an opportunity to challenge the NFID's implicit narrative. The NFID's framing — that disparities are caused by "hesitancy" and "access" — is the standard public health framing, and it has been criticized by health equity scholars for placing the onus on marginalized communities rather than on the systems that produce marginalization. Snopes verified the numbers but did not question the story the numbers were being used to tell.

## 4. Why This Matters

The persistence of racial disparities in COVID-19 booster uptake is not merely a public health data point; it is a measure of the depth and durability of health inequality in the United States. The Snopes "Correct" verdict, by focusing narrowly on whether the NFID accurately reported the numbers, sidesteps the most important question: what do these numbers mean, and what should be done about them?

First, the booster disparity matters because it had concrete consequences for the health of Black and Hispanic communities. As the pandemic evolved and immunity from the primary series waned, booster doses were the primary tool for maintaining protection against severe illness, hospitalization, and death. Communities with lower booster rates experienced higher rates of breakthrough infections, hospitalizations, and mortality. The disparities in booster rates translated directly into disparities in health outcomes.

Second, the booster disparity matters because it reflects the failure of public health institutions to address structural inequality. Throughout the pandemic, federal, state, and local health agencies declared their commitment to health equity. The CDC launched initiatives to address vaccine disparities, the White House made equity a stated priority of the vaccination campaign, and billions of dollars were allocated to community-based vaccination efforts. Despite these efforts, the disparities persisted. The fact that the same pattern of racial inequality that characterized the primary series rollout was reproduced in the booster campaign suggests that the interventions were insufficient — not that the public health community failed to try, but that the underlying structural barriers were too deep to be addressed by the strategies deployed.

Third, the booster disparity raises questions about the concept of "vaccine hesitancy" and its role in public health discourse. The term "vaccine hesitancy" has been criticized by health equity scholars for individualizing a systemic problem. When Black and Hispanic communities have lower vaccine uptake, the question is not simply "are they hesitant?" but "what is their relationship with the medical system, and what has that system done to earn or lose their trust?" The hesitancy framing places the burden of change on the marginalized group rather than on the institutions that have produced the conditions for mistrust.

Fourth, the booster disparity matters because it has been used to support narratives about "irrational" behavior in communities of color. Conservative commentators have pointed to lower vaccine uptake in Black and Hispanic communities as evidence of "liberal hypocrisy" — arguing that if vaccines really worked, minority communities would embrace them. This narrative is both factually wrong (Black and Hispanic communities have not been uniquely resistant to vaccines historically; in fact, Black seniors have higher flu vaccination rates than White seniors) and morally problematic (it blames marginalized communities for the consequences of structural racism). The Snopes "Correct" verdict, by not challenging the interpretive framing of the data, does nothing to counter these harmful narratives.

## 5. Research Notes

The CDC's National Immunization Survey Adult COVID Module (NIS-ACM) is the primary data source for vaccine uptake by race and ethnicity. The survey is conducted by telephone and online, with a sample size large enough to produce reliable estimates for major racial and ethnic groups. The survey data is publicly available through the CDC's COVID-19 Vaccination Trends dashboard.

The NFID report cited by Snopes, "National Survey on COVID-19 Attitudes, Behaviors, and Barriers," was published on August 23, 2022. It surveyed 1,000 adults and reported findings on vaccine and booster attitudes, including racial breakdowns. The report's methodology and full results are available on the NFID website.

The broader literature on racial disparities in vaccine uptake includes important studies. A 2021 study in JAMA Network Open by Viswanath et al. found that Black and Hispanic adults reported higher levels of willingness to receive a COVID-19 vaccine than White adults, even as actual uptake rates were lower — suggesting that the disparity was driven by access barriers rather than by hesitancy. A 2022 analysis by the Kaiser Family Foundation (KFF) found that the primary reasons for non-vaccination among unvaccinated Black adults were concerns about side effects, lack of trust in the government, and concerns about the speed of vaccine development — concerns that are rooted in legitimate skepticism about the pharmaceutical industry and government health institutions.

The structural analysis of vaccine disparities draws on the work of scholars like Dr. Camara Phyllis Jones, whose concept of "race-associated differences in health outcomes" distinguishes between mere statistical differences and differences caused by racism. Jones argues that when we observe racial disparities in health, the default question should be "What structures and systems produce this disparity?" rather than "What is wrong with this population?"

The history of medical abuse in Black and Indigenous communities has been extensively documented. Harriet Washington's "Medical Apartheid" (2006) provides a comprehensive history of medical experimentation on Black Americans from slavery through the present. The Tuskegee Syphilis Study is the best-known example, but Washington documents dozens of other cases, from the gynecological experiments of J. Marion Sims to the radiation experiments of the Cold War era.

## 6. Verdict

**Factual Accuracy (Data):** The central claim that racial disparities in COVID-19 booster uptake persisted through mid-2022 is factually accurate. The CDC data supports the NFID's finding that White Americans were significantly more likely to have received booster doses than Black or Hispanic Americans. The Snopes "Correct" verdict is appropriate for this narrow question.

**Interpretive Accuracy (Framing):** The Snopes article accurately reported the data but did not critically examine the interpretive framework used to explain it. The standard public health framing — that disparities are caused by "access" and "hesitancy" — was presented without scrutiny, omitting the structural factors that produce both access barriers and medical mistrust. The "Correct" verdict implies that the article's interpretive stance is also correct, which is a different and more contestable claim.

**Distortion Type:** Distorted but grounded. The claim about the existence of racial disparities is grounded in verifiable CDC data. The distortion operates through the article's interpretive framing, which presents the data within a narrow, individual-focused explanation that omits structural and historical context. The distortion is not in what the article says but in what it does not say — the frame through which the facts are presented.

**Snopes Verdict Assessment:** The "Correct" verdict is technically correct for the data verification component of the claim but does not adequately serve readers who need to understand what the data means. A more comprehensive analysis would have examined both the existence of the disparities and the structural factors that produce them, and would have acknowledged that the NFID's framing is one interpretation of the data, not a neutral description.

**Gap Analysis:** The gap between the raw data (boosters are lower in Black and Hispanic communities) and a genuine understanding of the phenomenon is filled by the absence of structural analysis. The data alone cannot explain why the disparities exist; that explanation requires historical knowledge, attention to systemic factors, and a willingness to examine the role of racism in healthcare. Closing this gap requires moving beyond fact-checking as data verification into what Snopes's format does not easily support: narrative and contextual analysis.

**Truth Vault Rating:** EARC Mode R (Retrospective). A retrospective analysis is warranted because the "Correct" verdict, while accurate for the data it verified, presents a misleadingly simple picture of a structurally complex phenomenon. The retrospective lens allows for the addition of the contextual and structural analysis that the original article omitted.

**Bottom Line:** Yes, the racial disparities in COVID-19 booster rates that the NFID and Snopes reported were real and accurately described in the CDC data. But the "Correct" verdict obscures the deeper truth: these disparities were the predictable product of structural racism in healthcare, not simply of individual "hesitancy" or access barriers. The facts are correct. The interpretation requires more than a verdict.
