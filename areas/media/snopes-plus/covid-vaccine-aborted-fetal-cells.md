## 1. The Claim

The claim that the Johnson & Johnson COVID-19 vaccine contains aborted fetal cells emerged almost immediately after the U.S. Food and Drug Administration granted Emergency Use Authorization to the single-dose vaccine in late February 2021. Within days, the U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops (USCCB) issued a statement cautioning the faithful that the J&J vaccine was "developed, tested and is produced with abortion-derived cell lines," raising what the bishops described as "moral concerns" for believers. The Archdiocese of New Orleans went further, labeling the immunization "morally compromised." These pronouncements from high-ranking Catholic authorities provided powerful institutional cover for a narrative that quickly spread across conservative media, religious publications, and social media platforms: that the J&J shot somehow contained the physical remains of an aborted fetus.

The claim took multiple forms. Some iterations maintained that fetal tissue itself was a direct ingredient in the vaccine, a claim that implied that receiving the shot would make one complicit in abortion. Others suggested that Johnson & Johnson relied on an ongoing, active supply of tissue from elective abortions to manufacture its vaccine. Still others asserted that any use of cells derived from historical fetal tissue — no matter how remote — rendered the vaccine morally contaminated. These formulations ranged from the scientifically naive to the theologically sophisticated, but they all shared a common thread: the belief that the J&J vaccine was ethically tainted by its relationship to abortion.

The Snopes article by Jessica Lee, published March 11, 2021, addressed this claim with a "False" rating. The piece correctly established that the vaccine formula contains no fetal tissue, explained the role of cell lines in vaccine production, and cited expert testimony to support these conclusions. Snopes drew on interviews with Dr. Amesh Adalja of the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security, a statement from Johnson & Johnson itself, and published scientific literature to make its case. The article was thorough, professionally sourced, and technically accurate on the narrow question of whether fetal cells appear in the final vaccine product.

Yet the Snopes article, for all its fact-checking competence, operated within a framework that systematically minimized the legitimate ethical dimensions of the question. By reducing the inquiry to a binary ingredient test — does the vaccine contain fetal cells, yes or no? — Snopes sidestepped the deeper moral calculus that religious believers and bioethicists were actually engaged in. The claim was not merely a factual error to be corrected; it was a moral judgment embedded in a factual dispute, and Snopes addressed only the factual layer.

## 2. What's Actually True

The factual picture is more complex than either the alarmist version (fetal tissue is in the shot) or the dismissive version (there is nothing to see here) acknowledges. Let us begin with what is uncontroversially established.

Johnson & Johnson's COVID-19 vaccine is a viral vector vaccine. It uses a modified adenovirus — a harmless cold virus — engineered to carry the genetic code for the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein. When administered, the viral vector enters cells and instructs them to produce spike proteins, which in turn trigger an immune response. This is the same technological platform used by the AstraZeneca COVID-19 vaccine and by numerous experimental vaccines developed over the preceding two decades.

To produce the viral vector at commercial scale, manufacturers need a medium in which the virus can replicate. For the J&J vaccine, that medium is the PER.C6 cell line. PER.C6 is a line of human embryonic retinal cells that were originally derived from the tissue of a single electively aborted 18-week fetus in the Netherlands in 1985. The cells were isolated by scientists at Introgene B.V., a Dutch biotechnology company, who recognized that embryonic cells — because of their rapid division and genetic plasticity — could serve as effective factories for producing recombinant proteins and viral vectors.

It is critical to understand what PER.C6 cells are and are not. They are not fresh fetal tissue harvested for vaccine production. They are not cells taken from a recent abortion. They are the immortalized descendants of cells that were isolated from a single termination event forty years ago. These cells have been grown in laboratories, frozen, thawed, and propagated through thousands of generations. The original fetal tissue was used to establish the cell line; it was not used to manufacture any individual dose of vaccine.

In the production process, PER.C6 cells are infected with the modified adenovirus. The virus replicates inside the cells, causing them to burst. Scientists then harvest the virus and subject it to a rigorous purification process that removes cellular debris, including any residual DNA or proteins from the PER.C6 cells. What remains is purified viral vector, formulated with stabilizers and preservatives into the final vaccine product. As Dr. Adalja told Snopes, "They grow the virus through those cells. They then harvest the virus, filter all of that material. What you get in your vaccine is actually the virus that's been engineered."

Johnson & Johnson itself stated unequivocally: "Our COVID-19 vaccine is made using a harmless cold-like virus into which we insert a piece of the coronavirus spike protein. ... Our vaccine contains no fetal tissue whatsoever."

This is, in the narrow sense, true. The final vaccine product does not contain PER.C6 cells, fetal tissue, or any cellular material from the 1985 abortion. The cells serve as a production substrate — analogous to the chicken eggs used to produce influenza vaccines — and are removed before the product is finished.

Beyond the J&J vaccine, it is worth noting that the use of cell lines derived from historical fetal tissue is widespread in biomedical research and pharmaceutical manufacturing. The HEK 293 cell line, derived from fetal kidney tissue in the 1970s, is one of the most commonly used cell lines in laboratory science worldwide. The MRC-5 and WI-38 cell lines, derived from fetal lung tissue from abortions performed in the 1960s, are used to produce vaccines for rubella, hepatitis A, rabies, and shingles. If the ethical concern is about cell lines with fetal origins, then a vast swath of modern medicine — not just the J&J vaccine — would fall under the same moral shadow.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes deserves credit for several aspects of its reporting. The article correctly identified that the claim in its most literal form — that the vaccine contains aborted fetal cells — was false. It provided a clear explanation of the distinction between using cell lines as production substrates and including fetal tissue as an ingredient. It consulted qualified experts, cited reputable sources, and published Johnson & Johnson's own denial. The piece was a competent work of factual verification on a narrowly defined question.

But these strengths also reveal the article's limitations. The Snopes approach to this claim exemplifies a pattern that recurs throughout the platform's fact-checking work: the reduction of complex, multi-layered controversies to binary yes-or-no propositions. The question "Does the J&J vaccine contain aborted fetal cells?" is indeed answerable with a "no" — but that answer, while technically correct, fails to capture why the claim resonated in the first place.

The most significant shortcoming is Snopes's reluctance to engage with the theological and bioethical frameworks that gave the claim its weight. For Catholic moral theology, the question of cooperation with evil is not resolved by a chemical assay of the finished product. The Church distinguishes among different levels of cooperation — formal versus material, immediate versus remote, proximate versus mediate. When the USCCB stated that the J&J vaccine was "developed, tested and is produced with abortion-derived cell lines," it was making a moral claim about the nature of cooperation, not a scientific claim about the vaccine's composition. Snopes treated the bishops' statement as a factual assertion to be disproven rather than a moral argument to be engaged.

The article also understated the extent to which the vaccine development process involved the PER.C6 cell line at multiple stages. It is not merely that the cells were used in production; they were also used in the research and development phase to test the viral vector's efficacy and safety. This multi-stage reliance on a cell line with fetal origins is precisely what the bishops and other religious leaders found morally problematic. Snopes's framing — "the cells are factories, not ingredients" — was accurate but incomplete.

Furthermore, the article's dismissive tone toward religious concerns — treating them as founded on a misunderstanding of biology rather than on a serious engagement with moral theology — risked alienating the very audience most in need of careful, respectful explanation. When Snopes wrote that the claim "was a misrepresentation of facts" and quoted a source saying "there are no fetal cells used in any vaccine production process," it implicitly portrayed religious objectors as either ignorant or dishonest, rather than as people reasoning from different premises about what constitutes moral complicity.

Finally, Snopes did not address the broader context: that alternatives existed. The Pfizer-BioNTech and Moderna vaccines, both using mRNA technology, were produced without any involvement of fetal cell lines. A reader seeking to make an ethically informed choice might reasonably have wanted to know that two of the three authorized vaccines in early 2021 had no connection to fetal cell lines whatsoever. Snopes's failure to provide this comparative context was a significant omission.

## 4. Why This Matters

The J&J vaccine controversy was never really about whether fetal cells were physically present in the syringe. It was about something deeper: the relationship between scientific progress and ethical boundaries, between institutional trust and religious freedom, and between the way we fact-check and the way we persuade.

At stake was the practical question of vaccine uptake among religious communities. The COVID-19 pandemic demanded extraordinarily high vaccination rates to achieve herd immunity, and every population segment that hesitated or refused represented a vulnerability in the public health response. If Catholic leaders discouraged the J&J vaccine — and by implication cast doubt on all COVID-19 vaccines associated with cell lines — the effects on vaccination rates could be measurable and consequential. The moral stakes were not abstract; they included preventable hospitalizations and deaths.

At a deeper level, the controversy exposed a fundamental tension in the fact-checking enterprise. Fact-checking is, by design, a reductive enterprise. It takes a claim and evaluates it against evidence, producing a verdict that is categorical: true, false, misleading. This approach works well for straightforward empirical claims — "the moon landing was faked," "the 2020 election was stolen" — where the evidence overwhelmingly points in one direction. But it works far less well for claims that embed normative judgments within empirical assertions. When someone says "the vaccine uses aborted fetal cells," they may be making both a factual claim (about what is in the vaccine) and a moral claim (about what it means to use cells derived from an abortion). A fact-check that addresses only the factual dimension and declares victory has, in an important sense, missed the point.

This matters because the erosion of institutional trust in the United States has been driven in part by the perception that elite institutions — including media organizations — do not take ordinary people's moral and religious concerns seriously. When a Catholic who objects on moral grounds to the J&J vaccine reads a Snopes article that says "False: The vaccine does not contain aborted fetal cells," she may feel that her actual concern has been dismissed rather than addressed. The fact-check, intended to build trust in accurate information, may instead deepen the gulf between the fact-checker and the audience.

The case also illustrates a structural challenge: the difficulty of communicating scientific nuance in a media environment that rewards categorical verdicts. The truth about the J&J vaccine is that it does not contain fetal cells, but it was produced using a cell line derived from an aborted fetus. That is a nuanced position that does not map cleanly onto a "true" or "false" rating. Snopes's "Mixture" rating — which it applies to claims that contain elements of both truth and falsehood — might have been more appropriate than "False," since the claim that the vaccine has some relationship to abortion-derived cells is factually accurate, even if the claim that the cells are ingredients is not.

## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article relies primarily on three categories of sources: scientific experts, corporate representation, and published scientific literature. Dr. Amesh Adalja, the primary expert source, is a well-credentialed infectious disease physician and senior scholar at the Johns Hopkins Center for Health Security. His testimony is relevant and reliable on the technical question of vaccine production. However, the article does not include a single source representing the religious or bioethical perspective that motivated the claim. There is no theologian, no medical ethicist, no representative of the USCCB whose viewpoint is presented and engaged with on its own terms. The bishops' statement is cited, but only as the source of the claim being debunked, not as a position warranting substantive engagement.

The Johnson & Johnson statement comes from a corporate email to Snopes and should be evaluated with appropriate caution. While there is no reason to doubt the company's factual assertions about its manufacturing process, a corporation with billions of dollars in vaccine revenue at stake has an obvious interest in minimizing any suggestion that its product is ethically compromised. Snopes appears to have accepted the corporate statement without independent verification of the manufacturing details, though the description is consistent with what is known about viral vector vaccine production from other sources.

The scientific literature cited includes a Science Magazine article on the role of fetal cell lines in vaccine development and a PMC article on purification processes. Both are appropriate references for the technical claims they support. However, the article does not cite any primary literature on the PER.C6 cell line specifically, nor does it address the long history of ethical debate surrounding the use of cell lines with fetal origins.

Notably absent from the article is any discussion of the position taken by the Pontifical Academy for Life, which issued guidance on COVID-19 vaccines that distinguished among different vaccines based on their relationship to cell lines. The Vatican's position was more nuanced than Snopes's framing suggests: it acknowledged moral differences among the vaccines while ultimately concluding that it was acceptable to receive any of them when no alternative existed. This kind of nuance — recognizing moral concern while still endorsing vaccination — would have enriched the Snopes analysis.

The article also fails to engage with the work of bioethicists who have written on the question of cooperation with evil in the context of vaccine production. Scholars such as Dr. T. Patrick Hill and Dr. John D. Lantos have explored the ethical frameworks that apply when medical goods are produced using morally contested means. Their work offers a way of understanding the claim that is neither dismissive nor alarmist — precisely the middle ground that Snopes's fact-checking approach cannot reach.

## 6. Verdict

The Snopes article "Does J&J COVID-19 Vaccine Contain Aborted Fetal Cells?" is a technically accurate fact-check that answers a narrow empirical question correctly while missing the broader moral and theological dimensions that made the question consequential. The vaccine does not contain aborted fetal cells as ingredients. The viral vector production process uses the PER.C6 cell line, derived from a 1985 abortion, as a growth medium from which the final product is purified. This is factually different from the claim as stated.

However, the Snopes analysis operates within a framework that systematically excludes the ethical reasoning that animated the original concern. By reducing a complex question of moral complicity to a simple ingredient test, Snopes provides an answer that is true but insufficient. The article would have been stronger if it had acknowledged the validity of the moral concern while explaining the factual distinctions, if it had included perspectives from bioethics and theology, and if it had provided comparative context about alternative vaccines.

The gap category for this entry is "distorted but grounded": the claim that the J&J vaccine is connected to abortion-derived cell lines is factually grounded in the vaccine's production history, but the specific assertion that the vaccine contains fetal cells is a distortion of that reality. The conversation that needs to happen is not about ingredients but about cooperation — and Snopes, for all its fact-checking competence, did not facilitate that conversation.

The deeper lesson is one that applies across the Truth Vault's mission: rigorous factual accuracy is necessary but not sufficient for building understanding. When fact-checking encounters claims that carry moral weight, it must engage with that weight seriously, not treat it as a distraction from the empirical bottom line. The vaccine is safe, effective, and free of fetal tissue. It was also developed using technology that depends on a cell line with an ethically contested origin. Both statements are true, and a mature information ecosystem must be able to hold both in view.
