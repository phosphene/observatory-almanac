## §1 — Claim & Verdict Overview

The claim under review — that Girl Scout Thin Mint cookies contain crack cocaine, explaining their apparently addictive quality — was published by Snopes on 4 March 2015 and carries the rating **FALSE**. The piece was authored by **David Mikkelson** and last updated 24 March 2015. The rating is unambiguous and requires no hedging: crack cocaine does not appear on the ingredient list of Thin Mint cookies, no regulatory authority has found evidence of crack in Girl Scout cookies, and the claim originated from a Photoshopped image and social media humor.

That the claim is false is obvious enough to make its entry in a serious fact-checking archive seem almost self-defeating. And yet the queasy-mac case aside, this may be the most instructive of Snopes's 2015 food-related entries precisely because the obvious falseness requires explanation: why would anyone believe this, and what does the fact that some people did believe it reveal about how humor and hyperbole function in digital information environments? This entry focuses on those questions rather than on the (trivial) factual refutation.

Thin Mints are the best-selling variety of Girl Scout cookies, produced by two licensed bakeries: ABC Bakers and Little Brownie Bakers (a subsidiary of Nestlé). The cookies are a peppermint-flavored chocolate wafer, sold seasonally through Girl Scout cookie programs across the United States. Their popular image as intensely addictive — generating a kind of compulsive consumption disproportionate to their straightforward ingredients — is an established cultural meme preceding the crack claim by at least two decades. The crack claim is a specific elaboration of this pre-existing addictive meme.

## §2 — The Photoshop, The Joke, and the Journey to Literal Belief

The specific artifact from which the social media claim derives is a Photoshopped image of a Thin Mint cookie box in which the ingredient list has been modified to include "crack" as one of the listed ingredients. According to Snopes, this image has been circulating on the internet since at least 2009, when it was posted to the website Runnersworld.com. Its shared context at that time was clearly humor: a running community associating the compulsive consumption of a sugar-fat-chocolate-peppermint combination with drug dependency. The joke was neither subtle nor ambiguous in that original context.

Mikkelson's article states plainly: "This photograph does not show the actual ingredients in Thin Mints. This is merely a humorous image commenting on the deliciousness of the popular line of Girl Scout cookies." The analytical move Snopes performs here is to document the transition from joke to sincerely deployed claim — a transition that, by 2015, had proceeded far enough that the fact-check was warranted.

The evidence that some fraction of the population was treating the image as genuine documentation rather than parody comes from the specific phrasing of collected Twitter examples cited in the article — language suggesting that users believed they were sharing a discovery rather than a joke. The tweet cited as the exemplary claim ("Thin mints have to have some sort of secret ingredient in them that make them so addictive and I'm pretty sure it's crack") is itself somewhat ambiguous — it reads as hyperbolic expression of affection rather than sincere belief — but the Photoshopped image, when shared without the original satirical framing, functions more unambiguously as a spurious ingredient document.

The mechanism by which obviously false humorous claims begin to circulate as sincere beliefs is an active area of research in communication studies and cognitive psychology. Tandoc and colleagues (2017, *Digital Journalism*) developed a typology of "fake news" that explicitly includes satire and parody as categories in which the intent-to-deceive differs from the effect-of-deception: satirical content produced without deceptive intent can be shared in contexts that strip the satirical framing, generating deception at the audience level even when no deception was intended by the producer.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Humor-to-Belief Transition in Food Claims

The specific cognitive and social mechanisms by which a food-related joke becomes a food-related false belief, transmitted as sincere information, constitute an understudied phenomenon at the intersection of humor theory, memory research, and digital communication studies. The Thin Mint case is a clean natural experiment within the broader ecology of food humor/fear content because the original satirical provenance of the Photoshopped image is well-documented (2009 origin, humor website context), and the subsequent transition to sincere claim circulation is documented by Snopes's receipt of sufficient inquiry to warrant a fact-check by 2015.

Research on the "illusory truth effect" is relevant but does not directly address the humor dimension. Hasher, Goldstein, and Toppino (1977, *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*) first documented that repeated exposure to a statement increases its rated truth, regardless of actual truth value. Subsequent replications and extensions have confirmed this effect robustly across diverse populations and content types (Dechêne et al., 2010, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*). This effect is consistent with the observation that a Photoshopped image circulating for six years — even in nominally humorous contexts — could have accumulated enough exposure-based familiarity to be rated as more plausible than a stranger's cold assessment would suggest.

However, the humor dimension adds a complication: there is evidence that humorous content is remembered and shared more readily than neutral content (the "humor heuristic"), but also evidence that content experienced as humorous initially is processed with less critical scrutiny (Klein and Kuiper, 2006, *Humor: International Journal of Humor Research*). The combination produces a pathway not yet fully modeled: a humorous image shared with humorous intent is processed with reduced scrutiny, remembered with humor-enhanced fidelity, but — when shared into a context lacking the original humor frame — activates the illusory truth effect without the protective irony that marked the original encounter.

The role of the Photoshopped image format specifically deserves notice. A text claim that "Thin Mints contain crack" is categorically different from an image that appears to show an actual ingredient list containing the word "crack." Images of ingredient labels carry an implicit authority derived from the regulatory requirement to provide accurate ingredient lists: consumers have been taught, correctly, that ingredient lists are legally required to be accurate. A Photoshopped ingredient list therefore exploits a legitimacy transfer that a text claim does not, because the image adopts the visual grammar of a trusted information category. Roozenbeek and van der Linden (2019, *Royal Society Open Science*) have documented this "inoculation" dynamic from the other direction: informing audiences about manipulation techniques reduces their susceptibility to manipulated content. The Thin Mint case is an early natural example of the visual-authority-exploiting manipulation that inoculation research attempts to pre-empt.

The time dimension is also meaningful. The 2009 origin of the image to the 2015 Snopes inquiry spans six years, suggesting that this particular humor-to-belief transition was slow and possibly bounded to specific consumption communities. The running-community origin of the image implies that original viewers were fitness-conscious individuals who framed the joke within a specific community understanding of "forbidden indulgence." As the image migrated to broader and more heterogeneous audiences, the community context that anchored the satire dissipated, leaving only the image-as-artifact. This community-context-stripping dynamic is characteristic of how content migrates across platform communities on the internet generally.

There is no published study, to this author's knowledge, that has directly measured the rate at which explicitly humorous food-related claims become sincere false beliefs in the population, nor identified the social or cognitive variables that moderate this transition rate. Such a study would be valuable for public health communication precisely because food humor (jokes about addictive properties, strange ingredients, secret recipes) is ubiquitous and some fraction of it clearly does generate sincere false beliefs that affect purchasing behavior, brand perception, and in the most extreme cases, consumer anxiety.

## §4 — What Thin Mints Actually Contain

The official ingredient list for Thin Mint cookies, sourced from the ABC Smart Cookies portal cited in Mikkelson's article, includes: enriched flour (wheat flour, niacin, reduced iron, thiamine mononitrate, riboflavin, folic acid), sugar, vegetable shortening (palm and/or partially hydrogenated palm kernel oils), cocoa (processed with alkali), caramel color, and less than 2% of: high fructose corn syrup, salt, baking soda, soy lecithin, natural and artificial flavor, peppermint oil.

Notably absent from this list: crack cocaine, cocaine in any form, controlled substances of any kind, novel psychoactive substances, or any ingredient that would trigger a DEA Schedule I classification. The ingredient list is entirely consistent with what a food scientist would expect in a mass-market chocolate mint cookie: refined carbohydrates, sugar, fat, cocoa powder, and flavor compounds.

The psychoactivity of Thin Mint cookies, such as it is, is attributable primarily to: (1) the opioid receptor-relevant palatability response to the sugar-fat combination, a mechanism documented by Rogers and Smit (2000, *Pharmacology Biochemistry and Behavior*) who reviewed the neurobiological basis of food "craving" and found it is mediated by conditioned dopaminergic responses to food reward rather than by pharmacological agonism of addiction-related receptors; (2) the menthol-flavored peppermint oil component, which activates TRPM8 cold-receptor channels in oral mucosa, creating a mild physiological "cooling" sensation that is genuinely distinctive and reinforces palatability; and (3) the well-documented motivational salience generated by seasonal availability — scarcity signals increase perceived value and consumption urgency, a behavioral economic phenomenon documented by Worchel, Lee, and Adewole (1975, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) in the original "cookie jar" scarcity study.

None of these mechanisms constitutes addiction in the pharmacological sense, which requires tolerance development, withdrawal phenomena, and compulsive use despite adverse consequences. The "addictive" language applied colloquially to Thin Mints is metaphorical affection rather than clinical description, and it is precisely this colloquial metaphor that the "it must have crack in it" joke deploys.

## §5 — The Cultural Context: Thin Mints, Seasonal Scarcity, and the Addictive-Food Trope

The Thin Mint crack claim is part of a broader cultural discourse about addictive foods in which the vocabulary of drug addiction is applied metaphorically to particularly palatable foods. This discourse pre-dates social media substantially — the "Twinkies defense" of the 1970s, the "chocolate addiction" literature of the 1980s and 1990s, and persistent popular claims about MSG, cheese, and sugar all reflect the same underlying cultural anxiety about loss of dietary control. The specific drug being attributed varies with what the target demographic finds most dramatically alarming — in 2015, crack cocaine; in other eras, opium, heroin, or methamphetamine analogies have appeared in popular food writing.

Gordon (1999, *Social History of Medicine*) traced the historical development of the "addictive food" discourse in the United States, arguing that it emerges at the intersection of anxieties about industrial food processing, pharmaceutical knowledge of addiction mechanisms, and the moral weight that American culture places on dietary self-control. The crack claim is, on this reading, a culturally condensed expression of multiple anxieties: about the industrial production of food designed for palatability, about the moral valence of enjoying something intensely, and about the perceived incompatibility of sophisticated self-presentation with consuming Girl Scout cookies at high volume.

The Girl Scout angle is specifically relevant. The Girl Scout cookie enterprise — an annual ritual involving children as salespeople, neighborhood community networks, and cookies with established brand identities — carries strong cultural associations of wholesomeness, community service, and childhood innocence. Attributing crack to Girl Scout cookies generates a kind of comic incongruity from the collision of these associations with the most stigmatized category of illicit drug. The humor depends on the perceived maximum distance between "Girl Scout" and "crack dealer" — which is substantial in the American cultural imagination. This incongruity structure is textbook "benign violation" humor theory (McGraw and Warren, 2010, *Psychological Science*), in which humor arises when something is both norm-violating (crack in cookies) and psychologically safe (obviously a joke, obviously no actual crack).

## §6 — Summary and Research Gap Assessment

Thin Mint cookies do not contain crack cocaine. They contain the standard ingredients of a mass-market chocolate mint cookie, publicly disclosed on the product's official ingredient list. The claim evolved from a Photoshopped satirical image circulating since at least 2009 and transitioned, over six years, from obvious humor to a claim sincere enough for Snopes to fact-check. The claim is archetypal of the humor-to-belief transition problem: content produced as parody, stripped of satirical framing through platform migration, exploiting the visual authority of an ingredient list format, and activating illusory truth effects through repeated exposure.

**EARC Classification: A (Analytical)** — The factual claim is trivially false, but the mechanism by which it entered sincere circulation is epistemically interesting and generalizable. The analytical value of this entry is in documenting that mechanism rather than in refuting the content.

**Research Gap:** No published study has systematically examined the pathway by which food-related humor claims transition to sincere false consumer beliefs in measurable populations, controlled for the type of humor (satire, hyperbole, Photoshop), the platform migration pathway, and the community-context-stripping effect that distinguishes an image shared within its original satirical community from the same image shared in contexts lacking that framing. Such a study would contribute to both humor theory (boundary of the benign violation context) and health communication (when does food-humor require correction?).
