# Cracker Barrel (and Its Menu) Is Here to Stay, Not Closing in 2021


## 1. The Claim

In June 2021, at least one misleading digital advertisement appeared across web content—served through the Outbrain advertising network—that appeared to announce the closure of the Cracker Barrel Old Country Store restaurant chain. The ad's text read: "Saying Goodbye: Stores Closing in 2021 Across The Nation (See Full List)."

The advertisement was designed to appear as a news item or consumer alert rather than as commercial advertising. It did not identify the advertiser by name (the buyer was unknown at the time of Snopes' evaluation), and its format—headline + parenthetical encouragement to see a "full list"—mimicked the appearance of a legitimate news aggregator link. It was paid for through the Outbrain performance advertising network and would have appeared across numerous publisher websites where Outbrain serves recommendations, reaching audiences who associated its placement in a "recommended content" widget with editorial legitimacy.

Crucially, the ad appeared immediately after contextual relevance to Cracker Barrel: a simultaneous and entirely unrelated social media trend involving a TikTok phenomenon at Cracker Barrel had generated significant coverage in May and June 2021, meaning that consumers who had recently seen Cracker Barrel–related content online were likely primed to find a Cracker Barrel closure announcement plausible in timing.

The underlying link destination was a 35-page slideshow article on Definition.org, a commercial content arbitrage site. Cracker Barrel never appeared anywhere in that 35-page article. The advertisement was a pure bait-and-switch: the brand name Cracker Barrel in the ad copy was used to generate clicks from concerned fans and customers, who were then delivered to a slideshow unrelated to Cracker Barrel from which the advertiser extracted revenue through ad impressions on each page turn.

Snopes fact-checker Jordan Liles evaluated the claim and published a rating of **False** on June 14, 2021.


## 2. What's Actually True

Cracker Barrel was not closing in 2021. The claim was false in every meaningful sense: the company had no plans to close stores "across the nation," the advertisement was misleading clickbait, and the destination article never mentioned Cracker Barrel at all.

**The company's actual financial position:** According to a news release from May 2021, Cracker Barrel reported that it was approaching 2019 (pre-pandemic) revenue levels in its third-quarter fiscal 2021 results. The company had temporarily closed some dining rooms during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, as did essentially all dine-in restaurant chains in compliance with state and local public health orders, but those closures were temporary and operationally managed, not evidence of financial distress or a closure program. The Q3 fiscal 2021 investor release made no mention of closing stores "across the nation."

**The company's actual size and trajectory:** Cracker Barrel Old Country Store was founded by Dan Evins in Lebanon, Tennessee in 1969. By 2021, it operated over 660 locations in 45 U.S. states, predominantly along interstate highway corridors where it serves travelers. This geographic positioning—as a roadside destination rather than an urban or mall-based chain—made it somewhat more insulated from the urban dining shock of COVID-19 than city-center restaurant concepts, though it was not immune to pandemic impacts. A company with 660+ locations, founder-established brand identity, and a long history of interstate commerce–dependent business is not a closure candidate in financial distress.

**The advertisement's anatomy:** The ad was served through Outbrain, which is a performance advertising network that places sponsored content links in "recommended content" widgets on publisher websites. Outbrain's content policy at the time prohibited misleading advertising claims, but enforcement on individual ad units—particularly those using indirect brand association rather than explicit false claims—was imperfect. The advertiser who purchased the Cracker Barrel–titled ad unit was unknown to Snopes at the time of publication. The ad's economic logic was straightforward: use a recognized brand name to generate clicks, deliver those clicks to a high-page-count slideshow that generates multiple ad impressions per visitor, and realize ad revenue that exceeds the cost of the click-generating advertisement. This is the "advertising arbitrage" model that Snopes explicitly described in its fact-check.

**The TikTok context:** In May 2021, Snopes had separately reported on a TikTok trend involving Cracker Barrel, in which diners were leaving behind framed photos of themselves in the restaurant's characteristically decor-laden dining rooms and store spaces. This trend—which involved customers blending personal photographs into Cracker Barrel's existing wall-décor aesthetic—had generated significant coverage on social media and food media platforms. A separate TikTok sub-trend involved people of color performing humorous sketches about entering Cracker Barrel against assumed social expectations. Both trends positioned Cracker Barrel as a culturally salient keyword in algorithmic recommendation systems during the period when the misleading closure ad appeared, increasing the probability that users who had engaged with Cracker Barrel content would be targeted with the closure ad through behavioral advertising.


## 3. Epistemic Analysis

The Cracker Barrel closure case is a canonical example of what Snopes' fact-checker appropriately named "advertising arbitrage" as a distinct misinformation modality. It differs from the food hoax or the fabricated product image in that its goal is not to create a persistent false belief about Cracker Barrel—no one involved in the arbitrage scheme requires the public to believe Cracker Barrel is closing. Its goal is to generate impulsive clicks that monetize before the fraud is apparent, after which the consumer has already served the advertiser's economic purpose by loading the slideshow's first page.

This is what makes advertising arbitrage a particularly insidious form of attention manipulation: it does not require durable credibility or belief formation to succeed. A conventional disinformation campaign needs its false claims believed, spread, and resisted to correction. An advertising arbitrage campaign needs only a momentary behavioral response—a single click—before the deception is exposed. The click is captured in infrastructure measuring lag time: by the time the user discovers Cracker Barrel was never mentioned in the slideshow, the advertiser has already been credited with the click event and the page impression revenue is already accruing.

The epistemic harm is diffuse but real. Consumers who clicked receive a minor frustration and distrust increment. More significantly, exposure to brand closure claims—even those quickly discovered to be false—may generate residual uncertainty about the brand's health that influence future purchasing behavior. Research on the **illusory truth effect** by Hasher, Goldstein, and Toppino (1977, *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*) demonstrated that mere exposure to a statement increases its perceived truth on subsequent encounter, even when the initial exposure was accompanied by doubt or refutation. Applied to the Cracker Barrel case: a consumer who clicks the ad, discovers it is clickbait, and dismisses it may nonetheless find subsequent mentions of "Cracker Barrel" in proximity to the concept of "closing" slightly more credible than before, because the association has been formed neurologically even without belief commitment.

The advertising arbitrage model has a well-documented lifecycle. The arbitrageur identifies brands, concepts, or personalities with high search and click volume, purchases advertisement slots through networks like Outbrain or Taboola using triggering headlines, and directs traffic to high-page-count advertiser-supported content. The margin exists because click costs on branded keywords can be lower than the aggregate ad revenue generated by multiple page turns through a long slideshow. The model is essentially parasitic on brand recognition capital: it extracts attention value from a brand that the brand's own marketing investment created, with no value returned to the brand and real cost imposed in terms of consumer confusion and trust erosion.

The conceptual novelty this case introduces to the Truth Vault is the distinction between **belief-targeted misinformation** and **behavior-targeted misinformation**. Most fact-checking infrastructure is designed to address the former: to identify false claims, trace their origin, establish the truth, and reduce false belief persistence. Advertising arbitrage is addressed by neither the belief formation model nor its standard corrective mechanisms, because the intended outcome for the perpetrator is not belief but behavior. The fact-check is still valuable—it establishes the record, protects consumers who encounter it from acting on the false claim, and documents the mechanism—but it does not directly interfere with the arbitrage scheme's economic model, which operates faster than fact-checking cycles.

The broader analytical context requires noting that the restaurant closure narrative is a specific recurring template in the consumer-brand arbitrage space. "Restaurant chain X is closing locations across America" is a reliable click-generating headline template because it triggers a distinctive consumer anxiety: the fear that a familiar, habituated dining option will no longer be accessible. Research on food-place attachment by Milligan (1998, *Qualitative Sociology*) documents how specific food establishments develop deep affective bonds with regular customers that go beyond functional preference—customers feel a sense of place-based identity connected to their relationship with particular restaurant environments. Cracker Barrel is particularly well-suited as a target for this anxiety because its cultural positioning is explicitly nostalgic: the faux-antique decor, the rocking chairs on the porch, the comfort-food menu, and the country store retail component all construct an identity of durable American traditionalism. Claiming this brand is closing targets precisely the anxious attachment to cultural continuity that it has itself cultivated through decades of deliberate brand positioning.


## 4. The Evidence

**Primary source confirmations:**
- Cracker Barrel investor news release (May 2021): company reporting Q3 FY2021 revenue approaching 2019 (pre-pandemic) levels; zero mention of closing stores "across the nation." This filing, available through the Cracker Barrel investor relations site, constitutes the most direct evidence of the company's operational status at the time of the claim.
- The destination article at Definition.org (archived by Snopes): a 35-page slideshow that does not mention Cracker Barrel anywhere in its content, establishing that the advertisement was false in its specific implied promise (a list of closing Cracker Barrel stores).
- Outbrain as the identified ad network: documentary confirmation of the advertising mechanism through which the misleading ad was served.
- Cracker Barrel company history (crackerbarrel.com/about): founding 1969, Lebanon, Tennessee by Dan Evins; 660+ locations in 45 states by 2021.

**Academic and analytical literature:**

Foundational documentation of the illusory truth effect—the mechanism by which mere exposure to a claim, even a doubted one, increases its subsequent perceived truth, relevant to the residual harm of advertising arbitrage brand-closure claims.

Milligan, M. J. (1998). Interactional past and potential: The social construction of place attachment. *Qualitative Sociology*, 21(1), 1–33. Documents the mechanisms by which consumers develop place-specific affective bonds with commercial establishments; foundational for understanding why restaurant closure narratives generate anxious engagement.

Tuchman, G. (1978). *Making News: A Study in the Construction of Reality*. Free Press. Analyzes the visual and textual grammar of news presentation; advertising arbitrage exploits the semiotic conventions of news formatting to produce click-inducing plausibility for content that is commercially motivated rather than editorially produced.

Pavlou, P. A., & Stewart, D. W. (2000). Measuring the effects and effectiveness of interactive advertising: A research agenda. *Journal of Interactive Advertising*, 1(1), 62–78. The interactive advertising economics framework is foundational for understanding the click-through rate incentive structures that make advertising arbitrage profitable; the marginal revenue per page view versus cost per click structure is documented here.

Luo, X., & Bhattacharya, C. B. (2006). Corporate social responsibility, customer satisfaction, and market value. *Journal of Marketing*, 70(4), 1–18. The brand equity literature documents how consumer trust and brand perception are measurable assets; the implicit argument is that advertising arbitrage exploiting brand closure anxiety imposes real, if unquantified, costs on brand equity.

Silverman, C. Silverman's documentation of viral content anatomy includes early analysis of the clickbait arbitrage model and its economic incentive structure, directly applicable to the Cracker Barrel case.

Outbrain. (2021). *Advertiser content policy*. Outbrain.com. The network's stated content policies prohibited misleading advertising; the gap between policy and enforcement on the specific ad unit is documented by the fact that the ad ran before being removed.

Lazer, D. M. J., et al. (2018). The science of fake news. *Science*, 359(6380), 1094–1096. Synthesizes research on the spread of false information online, including the asymmetric virality of false versus true information, applicable to understanding why the misleading closure ad spread more efficiently than corrections could counter.


## 5. EARC/Gap Analysis

**EARC Mode: E (Empirically Verified)**

The claim is false on its face and documented decisively: the company was not closing, the investor filings indicated recovery, Cracker Barrel operates over 660 locations, and the advertising destination article never mentioned the brand. The falsity of the claim is overdetermined by multiple independent verification pathways.

**Gap Category: Clickbait Closure Hoax**

This entry belongs to the gap category of *clickbait closure hoax*—a recurring template in the advertising arbitrage misinformation space where a recognized brand's name is used in an advertisement or social media post to generate clicks by implying closure, discontinuation, or crisis, with the actual destination content unrelated to the implied claim. Other documented instances of this template have targeted grocery chains, fast-food franchises, theme parks, and department stores.

The research gap most clearly identified by this case is the absence of systematic measurement of advertising arbitrage's aggregate epistemic harm. Every individual arbitrage insertion is a small harm: a minor consumer distrust increment, a small residual association between a brand and closing, an inconvenient wasted click. In aggregate, however, the arbitrage model runs thousands of such ads continuously against hundreds of brands, generating diffuse, persistent background noise in consumer brand perceptions that is essentially invisible to standard measurement because no single ad reaches statistical significance in any brand tracking study. The cumulative effect of thousands of small "is Cracker Barrel closing?" anxiety activations over time—none of them memorable individually—on the brand's consumer trust profile is an unresolved empirical question.

A secondary gap concerns platform accountability for arbitrage advertising. Outbrain and Taboola, the two dominant content recommendation networks, have been documented repeatedly as carriers for misleading closure, health, and financial advertising. Their enforcement against misleading brand-name advertising in headline copy has been inconsistent, with takedowns typically occurring reactively (after complaints or fact-check publication) rather than proactively through automated detection. The policy framework for "deceptive by implication" advertising—where a brand name in the headline implies a false claim that the body never confirms—is not robustly addressed by current network advertising standards, and regulatory structures for digital content recommendation networks in the United States are not developed to the degree that would impose meaningful enforcement obligations. This is a governance gap at the intersection of advertising regulation and consumer protection that the Cracker Barrel fact-check documents without resolving.


## 6. Verdict and Context

**Verdict: False.** Cracker Barrel was not closing in 2021 and was not planning any "across the nation" store closure program. The advertisement that implied this was clickbait purchased through the Outbrain ad network, designed to generate clicks by exploiting brand recognition, with a destination article that never mentioned Cracker Barrel. The company was reporting near-2019 revenue levels as of May 2021 and remained operationally stable.

Snopes' characterization of the mechanism—"advertising arbitrage"—is worth amplifying because it names a specific economic model for a type of misleading content that is often lumped into general "fake news" categories without precision. The arbitrage model is not ideological, not politically motivated, and not concerned with producing durable false beliefs. It is purely economically motivated: the advertiser is indifferent to whether users believe Cracker Barrel is closing, because the revenue is realized before the deception is detected. This makes it a categorically different misinformation form than the kinds that drive political polarization or public health harm, but it is not harmless—it exploits and degrades consumer trust in familiar brands, normalizes misleading advertising as an ambient feature of digital content spaces, and imposes small but aggregate costs on the information environment.

Cracker Barrel itself is a culturally resonant choice of target for this analysis not only because its brand identity is explicitly nostalgic and community-oriented, but because its highway-corridor business model positions it as a particular kind of American institution: the roadside anchor, the predictable comfort stop, the thing that is always where it has always been on the drive to somewhere familiar. The closure hoax template targets this attachment deliberately, because anxiety about losing something anchored in routine and comfort is a reliable motivation for impulsive clicking. Understanding this mechanism—not merely the falsity of the specific claim but the emotional logic that makes it click-worthy—is part of the value a Truth Vault entry adds over and above a summary verdict.

The Cracker Barrel menu, consisting of all-day breakfast, southern comfort entrees including fried chicken, biscuits, macaroni and cheese, and daily specials keyed to specific days of the week (Broccoli Cheddar Chicken on Wednesdays, Turkey N' Dressing on Thursdays, a Friday Fish Fry), remains unchanged in its essential character and is not going anywhere. The rocking chairs remain on the porch. The country store remains adjacent to the restaurant. The framed paintings remain on the walls, inviting TikTok framings. Cracker Barrel, as of the time this entry was recorded, continues to operate as a going concern well beyond the 2021 period examined here.
