# Cracking your knuckles causes arthritis


## 1. The Claim

The belief that cracking your knuckles causes arthritis is among the most durably transmitted health warnings in informal parenting culture. It is routinely delivered by parents to children, by grandparents to grandchildren, by teachers to students, and by social peers to one another with a force of conviction entirely disproportionate to the evidence. The claim typically takes one of several forms: that habitual knuckle cracking damages the cartilage in the joints of the fingers, leading eventually to osteoarthritis; that the pop or crack sound itself is evidence of bone damage or cartilage grinding; or in more dramatic versions, that the knuckles of habitual crackers will eventually become so damaged and enlarged that manual dexterity suffers significantly.

The claim has roots in aesthetic displeasure as much as medical reasoning. Knuckle cracking produces a sound that many people find viscerally irritating, and the social context of the warning — typically issued from older to younger, often as a reprimand — means it arrives encoded with authority, disgust, and implicit consequence. Children told not to crack their knuckles because "it will give you arthritis" tend to receive the warning not as a testable empirical proposition but as a moral instruction whose medical framing lends it the additional authority of science. The claim is thus doubly insulated from challenge: as a parental directive, it demands compliance regardless of evidence; as a medical claim, it invites listeners to imagine the respectable authority of clinical research behind it.

In adult social settings, the claim migrates from parental authority to social norm enforcement. People who crack their knuckles in public are sometimes told by acquaintances or colleagues that they are damaging their joints — an interaction that positions the speaker as a concerned informant sharing useful medical knowledge. The claimant typically does not know the source of their belief, having received it in childhood, and repeats it as established common knowledge. Persistence of this kind — a belief that has been transmitted orally across generations without any significant verification chain — is itself a phenomenon worth examining, and the knuckle-cracking arthritis myth is a particularly clean case study.

Medical consensus, as expressed in orthopaedic literature and in major clinical reviews, does not support the claim. Multiple studies have examined the relationship between habitual knuckle cracking and hand osteoarthritis and have found no causal association. The mechanism by which cracking is supposed to cause arthritis is either unstated or, when stated, is biologically implausible given what is known about the physiology of synovial joints. This article examines the evidence, the mechanism, the notable experimental history of the question, and what the persistence of the myth tells us about how health folklore propagates.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The anatomy and physiology of the knuckle crack.** The joints of the fingers — specifically the metacarpophalangeal joints at the base of each finger — are synovial joints: enclosed cavities filled with synovial fluid that lubricates the articular surfaces. When a finger is pulled, bent backward, or otherwise subjected to rapid distraction force, the expansion of the joint space reduces the pressure in the synovial fluid below the threshold required to keep dissolved gases in solution. Carbon dioxide, oxygen, and nitrogen rapidly come out of solution and form a gas cavity. The pop or crack that is heard is the sound of this cavity forming, or — as video analysis and more recent research suggests — the sound of the cavity's partial collapse in the brief period after formation (Kawchuk et al., 2015, "Real-Time Visualization of Joint Cavitation," *PLOS ONE*, 10(4), e0119470). The Kawchuk et al. study used MRI to capture knuckle cracking in real time, providing the first direct visualisation of the phenomenon and establishing that the sound corresponds to the formation of the gas cavity rather than, as previously theorised, its collapse.

After the cavity forms, the gas is slowly reabsorbed into the synovial fluid over a period of approximately fifteen to thirty minutes — the time range within which the knuckle cannot be cracked again, known colloquially as the refractory period. There is no structural damage in the process. The articular cartilage, bone, tendon, and ligament structures of the joint undergo temporary mechanical stress when the joint is distracted, but no greater stress than occurs in ordinary grip strength and hand use. The popping sound, however alarming it may seem to bystanders, is not evidence of bone meeting bone, cartilage tearing, or any pathological process.

**The study evidence on arthritis.** Osteoarthritis — the form of arthritis most plausibly linked to mechanical joint use — is a degenerative condition of cartilage and subchondral bone that develops over time as a result of cumulative mechanical load, biomechanical abnormality, genetic susceptibility, metabolic factors, and age. Its pathogenesis does not require the acute gas cavity formation that characterises knuckle cracking. Several studies have directly examined whether habitual knuckle cracking is associated with increased rates of osteoarthritis in the hands.

Castellanos and Axelrod (1990, "Effect of Habitual Knuckle-Cracking on Hand Function," *Annals of the Rheumatic Diseases*, 49(5), 308–309) evaluated 300 patients over an extended period and compared rates of arthritis between habitual knuckle crackers and non-crackers. They found no difference in the prevalence of osteoarthritis between the two groups. However, they did observe that habitual crackers had slightly higher rates of hand swelling and reduced grip strength — findings that received some attention as suggesting possible soft-tissue consequences, though the study's limitations in controlling for other variables were noted by subsequent researchers.

A later population study by de Weber et al. (2011, "Knuckle Cracking and Hand Osteoarthritis," *Journal of the American Board of Family Medicine*, 24(2), 169–174) examined 215 patients presenting to a primary care clinic and found no statistically significant association between habitual knuckle cracking and hand osteoarthritis after controlling for age, sex, and manual labour history. The association between knuckle cracking and the soft-tissue changes observed in Castellanos and Axelrod was not replicated. De Weber and colleagues concluded that habitual knuckle cracking should not be advised against on the grounds of arthritis risk.

**The sixty-year self-experiment.** The most celebrated piece of evidence in the knuckle-cracking literature is a controlled n=1 study conducted by Donald Unger, a physician who cracked the knuckles of his left hand at least twice daily for sixty years while refraining from cracking the knuckles of his right hand, and then compared the prevalence of arthritis in the two hands. Unger published his findings in *Arthritis & Rheumatism* in 1998 (Unger, 1998, "Does Knuckle Cracking Lead to Arthritis of the Fingers?", *Arthritis & Rheumatism*, 41(5), 949–950) and found no arthritis in either hand. He was subsequently awarded the Ig Nobel Prize in Medicine in 2009 — a recognition given for research that "makes people laugh, then think." Unger's study has obvious methodological limitations: n=1 self-reporting with no blinding is not the gold standard of clinical evidence. But its longevity (sixty years exceeds the duration of most controlled clinical studies) and the unusual dedication of its author give it a cultural presence in discussions of the claim far exceeding its formal statistical weight.

Taken together, the evidence does not support the claim that habitual knuckle cracking causes arthritis. Neither the proposed mechanism nor the epidemiological pattern is consistent with the claim. The claim is false.


## 3. The Epistemic Novelty

**The gap: why does this myth succeed, and what does its success structure tell us?**

The knuckle-cracking arthritis myth is frequently treated in debunking literature as a simple case of popular misinformation — a false medical claim that persists because people haven't been exposed to the correct information. This framing is insufficient. The myth has been reliably debunked in popular science writing for decades: Snopes rated it false before 2010; medical Q&A columns have cleared it for at least thirty years; and the Unger self-experiment has received enough popular press attention that a substantial fraction of adults who have encountered the debunking have likely continued transmitting the original claim without modification.

The more interesting epistemic question is not whether the claim is true (it isn't) but why the claim is so reliably transmitted and why debunking has so little effect on its propagation velocity. Several mechanisms are at work.

First, the claim is structurally supported by *disgust-medicalisation*. Many folk health warnings originate in aesthetic repulsion and are subsequently given a medical veneer that provides a socially legitimate rationale for expressing that repulsion. Knuckle cracking is viscerally annoying to many people — the sound is startling, the motion is visually strange, and it is often performed in settings (classrooms, offices, quiet shared spaces) where any repetitive noise is socially marginal. An adult who tells a child to stop cracking their knuckles because "it'll give you arthritis" has converted an aesthetic preference into a medical directive, which is both more authoritative and less obviously about the speaker's own comfort. The medical framing protects the speaker from being seen as merely annoyed.

Second, the claim operates within an *authority gradient that predates the evidence*. Children receive the warning from parents at ages when parental authority vastly outweighs any competing source, and the warning is delivered in a context where the child has no means of verifying or contesting it. By the time the child encounters the debunking — if they encounter it at all — the belief has been encoded as background knowledge of the kind that is rarely subjected to reflective scrutiny. Beliefs formed in this context are highly resistant to updating because they were never consciously adopted in the first place: they were absorbed as part of the social world, not reasoned into.

Third, the myth exploits a genuine inferential heuristic: that loud mechanical sounds in joints are associated with damage. This heuristic is not entirely wrong in other contexts — crepitus (the grinding or popping of arthritic joints) is genuinely a symptom of joint pathology in certain conditions, and knees or hips that make grinding sounds during movement may indeed be manifesting cartilage degradation. The error is in inferring that *all* joint sounds represent the same underlying process. The sound of a gas cavity forming in a healthy synovial joint is not the sound of cartilage grinding. But both are described informally as "cracking" or "popping," and the auditory similarity is enough to make the inferential link plausible without specialist knowledge.

What these three mechanisms together suggest is that the knuckle-cracking myth is not primarily a case of ignorance that correct information would remedy. It is a case of a belief system that performs social functions (aesthetic preference enforcement, parental authority, health-adjacent care-giving) that would persist even if every adult in the relevant transmission chain encountered and retained the correct medical information. This is a category of belief that behaves more like social norm than like empirical claim, and it requires different debunking strategies than simple information provision.

The failure of repeated debunking to substantially reduce the claim's transmission rate is also a data point worth registering. In information diffusion research, beliefs that are resistant to debunking even after repeated, high-quality corrections are sometimes described as exhibiting *backfire*-adjacent dynamics — not the classic backfire effect (in which correction strengthens the original belief), which has been significantly questioned in replication research (Wood and Porter, 2019, "The Elusive Backfire Effect," *Political Behavior*, 41, 135–163), but a related dynamic in which the belief is simply not updated because it functions in a domain — social norm transmission — where factual accuracy is not the primary metric.


## 4. Snopes & Fact-Checker Comparison

Snopes rates the claim that "cracking your knuckles causes arthritis" **FALSE**, citing the absence of controlled clinical evidence and noting the Unger self-experiment and the Castellanos/Axelrod study. The Snopes assessment is accurate as far as it goes and presents the summary medical consensus correctly.

Other major fact-checking outlets and medical authority sources are aligned with this verdict. Harvard Health Publishing has addressed the question multiple times, consistently concluding that no credible link has been established between habitual knuckle cracking and osteoarthritis. The Mayo Clinic's patient education materials make the same point. The National Institutes of Health-derived consumer health resources are consistent.

Where this article extends beyond the Snopes verdict is in examining why the myth has resisted correction so successfully despite repeated clear debunking. Snopes, in its standard format, provides the corrective claim without significant analysis of the social and cognitive mechanisms by which the original claim is maintained. This entry treats those mechanisms as the intellectually productive part of the case — the "plus" in "Snopes-plus." The fact that the claim is false is the starting point, not the endpoint.

There is also a minor complication in the Castellanos and Axelrod (1990) finding of associations between habitual cracking and hand swelling and reduced grip strength, which some popular treatments have used to partially rehabilitate the claim. This finding has not been replicated at scale, the study had significant methodological limitations, and the observation — even if real — does not constitute arthritis and does not support the specific claim that cracking causes osteoarthritis. Snopes's rating of "false" remains correct even accounting for this outlier study, but the full picture requires noting the replication failure.


## 5. Cultural & Historical Dimensions

The medicalisation of knuckle-cracking disgust appears to be primarily a twentieth-century development in Anglophone cultures. Historical documentation of the specific "causes arthritis" formulation is limited before the mid-twentieth century, suggesting that the medicalism of the claim — as distinguished from a simpler instruction not to crack knuckles because it is rude or annoying — is relatively recent. This is consistent with the broader observation that biomedical vocabulary became available for the medicalisation of everyday behaviour as public education about medicine expanded over the twentieth century.

The claim's geography is worth noting. In many non-Anglophone cultural contexts, folk beliefs about joint cracking exist, but they are not uniformly structured around arthritis risk as the specific consequence. In some East Asian traditional medical contexts, joint cracking is associated with Qi flow and may be interpreted positively or as an indicator of excess energy rather than joint damage. In Brazilian folk medicine, different explanations circulate. The "causes arthritis" formulation appears to be culturally specific rather than universal, which is itself evidence that the claim is not grounded in observed empirical regularities that would transcend cultural context — if cracking actually caused arthritis, one would expect the association to be observed and transmitted across any culture in which both practices (cracking and ageing) occur.

In popular culture, knuckle cracking appears as a character-signalling device in film and television — typically associated with menace, nonchalance, or preparation for physical action. The fact of the pop functions as a kind of audio-visual shorthand for toughness or deliberateness, which is in interesting tension with the folk-medical claim that it is damaging. The character who cracks their knuckles before a fight is not typically coded as someone who is damaging their joints; they are coded as someone who is dangerous. This cultural ambivalence — knuckle cracking as simultaneously self-harming and threatening — probably reflects the underlying truth that the sound is more emotionally significant than medically relevant.

The claim also has a parenting culture dimension that stretches across generations. It is one of a class of childhood-directed health warnings — don't sit too close to the television or you'll go blind; don't swallow watermelon seeds or they'll grow in your stomach; don't make that face or it'll stick that way — that function more as behavioural controls than as medical advice. These warnings share a structure: a common childhood behaviour, an implausible but superficially plausible consequence, and an authoritative tone that forecloses inquiry. They are transmitted more reliably than most correct medical information because they are bound up with relationships of care and authority rather than with abstract informational content.


## 6. Verdict

**FALSE.** Cracking your knuckles does not cause arthritis. The available clinical and epidemiological evidence, including multiple controlled studies and a sixty-year controlled n=1 self-experiment, consistently shows no association between habitual knuckle cracking and osteoarthritis or any other form of arthritis in the fingers. The mechanism by which cracking is supposed to cause arthritis is not biologically plausible given the understood physiology of synovial joints. The pop produced by knuckle cracking is the sound of rapid gas cavity formation in synovial fluid — a benign mechanical phenomenon with no documented pathological consequences.

The claim's persistence despite clear and repeated debunking reflects the social functions it performs — aesthetic preference enforcement, parental authority, care-giving behaviour — rather than any evidential basis. It is a case where the belief is doing work other than accurately representing reality, and where understanding that work is necessary to understand why the belief continues to circulate despite being straightforwardly false.

This is not a case of contested science, partial evidence, or reasonable expert disagreement. The expert consensus in rheumatology and orthopaedics is clear and consistent. The claim should not be transmitted to children or adults as medical fact.
