## The Claim

Creatine supplements damage your kidneys. This is one of the most persistent warnings in popular fitness culture — offered by well-meaning personal trainers, cautious physicians, skeptical relatives, and internet comment sections in roughly equal measure. The concern runs something like this: creatine is a nitrogen-containing compound processed in the liver and kidneys; taking it in supplemental doses forces the kidneys to work harder; over time, this additional burden degrades kidney function; and because the kidneys are silent organs that show symptoms only late in disease progression, the damage accumulates unnoticed until it becomes serious.

In more detailed versions of the claim, the concern is tied to the observation that creatine supplementation reliably raises serum creatinine levels — a standard blood marker used to assess kidney function — creating what appears to be a measurable biochemical signal of kidney stress. Some versions of the claim specifically target high-dose creatine loading protocols, while others treat any supplemental creatine as categorically risky.

## What's Actually True

The evidentiary situation is more nuanced than either "totally safe" or "definitely dangerous," and the distinction between healthy individuals and those with pre-existing conditions is critical to understanding what the evidence actually shows.

**Long-term studies in healthy adults have not found kidney damage.** The most significant longitudinal study is by Kreider and colleagues, published in *Molecular and Cellular Biochemistry* in 2003. The study followed athletes taking creatine supplements for up to five years and found no adverse effects on kidney or liver function markers. This remains one of the longest-duration controlled studies of creatine supplementation in athletes, and its results have been broadly consistent with subsequent shorter-duration studies. When researchers have measured standard kidney function markers — glomerular filtration rate (GFR), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine, urinary protein — in healthy individuals supplementing with creatine at typical doses (3–5 grams per day maintenance, or 20 grams per day during loading phases), they have generally not found evidence of impaired kidney function.

A 2021 systematic review examining kidney function across 15 randomized controlled trials of creatine supplementation found no significant differences in kidney function markers between creatine users and controls in healthy participants. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN), in its 2017 position statement on creatine, reviewed the accumulated safety literature and concluded that creatine monohydrate is safe for healthy individuals when used at recommended doses, and that the available evidence does not support concerns about kidney damage in this population.

**The creatinine problem is real and clinically important.** The single most important technical fact in this debate is that creatine supplementation predictably and substantially raises serum creatinine levels. This happens not because the kidneys are being damaged but because of basic biochemistry: creatine is metabolized in muscle into creatinine, which is then filtered by the kidneys and excreted in urine. More creatine intake means more creatinine production, which means higher serum creatinine — even when kidney filtration function is completely normal.

Serum creatinine is one of the two primary markers used in standard kidney function panels (along with blood urea nitrogen). When a physician orders routine bloodwork and sees elevated creatinine, the reflexive interpretation is reduced kidney clearance — which typically indicates impaired kidney function. In a patient who is supplementing with creatine, this interpretation is wrong: the elevated creatinine reflects increased substrate production, not impaired clearance. But because most laboratory reports do not flag creatine use as a variable, and because many patients do not disclose supplement use during routine blood draws, the confusion is common. There are published case reports of patients receiving unnecessary follow-up testing, being referred to nephrology, and in some cases having creatine supplementation incorrectly attributed as a cause of apparent kidney decline when the creatinine elevation was entirely artifactual.

This false-positive problem is not a minor technicality. It is one of the primary reasons clinical wariness about creatine has persisted despite the safety data. When general practitioners, who see aggregate population data rather than controlled clinical trial populations, observe elevated creatinine in patients who report taking creatine, the association becomes embedded in clinical pattern recognition — even if the mechanism is pharmacokinetic rather than nephrotoxic.

**Case reports and pre-existing conditions introduce genuine uncertainty.** The safety data is strong for healthy adults, but the literature also contains a non-trivial number of case reports documenting kidney problems in individuals taking creatine who had undisclosed or undiagnosed kidney conditions. The central issue is that creatine may impose a genuine additional burden on kidneys that are already operating with reduced reserve capacity. In a kidney functioning at 60–70% of normal capacity, the metabolic demands associated with processing additional creatinine load may be clinically meaningful in ways that they are not in a fully healthy organ.

The 2017 ISSN position statement explicitly noted that individuals with kidney disease should not supplement with creatine and should consult a physician. This is not a hedge against liability — it reflects a genuine mechanistic concern that the safety data from healthy populations does not straightforwardly generalize to impaired populations. The distinction matters because a significant fraction of the population has undiagnosed chronic kidney disease (CKD); prevalence estimates in the United States suggest approximately 15% of adults have CKD, most of them unaware of it. For that population, the safety calculus may be different.

**Long-term data beyond 5 years is sparse.** One honest limitation in the literature is that the strongest studies extend to five years, not ten or twenty. Some nephrologists who remain cautious about creatine do so not because they believe the current evidence shows harm, but because they believe the absence of evidence of harm is not the same as evidence of absence of harm, and that chronic low-level effects on renal tissue might require longer observation periods to become detectable. This is a methodologically legitimate position, even if most sports scientists consider it overly conservative given the mechanistic plausibility and the existing data.

## Why People Believe This

The persistence of the creatine-kidney warning in popular culture reflects a confluence of factors: biochemical plausibility, legitimate clinical caution, the false-positive problem in blood panels, and a general cultural suspicion of performance-enhancing supplements.

**The biochemical story sounds plausible.** Creatine is processed in the kidneys. Giving the kidneys more to do must stress them. This is a coherent causal narrative that requires no specialized knowledge to follow, and it is the kind of story that sticks precisely because it is not obviously wrong. In medicine, many things that sound like they should cause harm do cause harm. The liver can be damaged by supplements that overload its detoxification capacity. Kidneys can be damaged by excessive protein consumption in some contexts. The inference that creatine — a nitrogen-containing compound whose metabolites must be excreted — follows the same logic is intuitive, even if the experimental evidence does not confirm it.

**Clinical observation is genuinely confusing.** As discussed above, the reliable elevation of serum creatinine in creatine users creates a systematic pattern in clinical practice where creatine use appears correlated with elevated kidney markers. Physicians who see this pattern repeatedly across their patient panels are not being irrational when they develop caution about the supplement. The problem is that the mechanism behind the correlation is different from what it appears to be — but clinical settings are not always structured to distinguish between the two.

**The supplement industry has a credibility problem.** Dietary supplements operate in a regulatory environment with substantially less rigorous safety oversight than pharmaceutical drugs. The FDA does not require pre-market safety testing for supplements; manufacturers are responsible for ensuring safety; and the market has produced numerous supplements that turned out to be harmful, adulterated, or simply ineffective. This context gives many health professionals and consumers a reasonable prior toward skepticism about supplement safety claims. Creatine, which is one of the most extensively studied and apparently safe supplements, benefits less from this skepticism than it deserves to because it is categorized alongside a much larger and less well-studied universe of products.

**Early media coverage generated lasting impressions.** In the late 1990s and early 2000s, as creatine use became widespread in amateur and professional sports, a number of high-profile health scare stories appeared in mainstream media associating creatine use with kidney problems, muscle cramps, and other adverse effects. The causal evidence in these stories was typically weak — often single case reports or anecdotal associations — but they reached large audiences at a formative moment for the supplement's public reputation. The cultural memory of these scare stories has outlasted the subsequent accumulation of safety data.

**Sports culture generates moral suspicion.** Creatine has been associated, in popular imagination, with the general culture of performance enhancement in sports — which includes substances that are genuinely dangerous, banned, and ethically contested. Even though creatine is legal, widely sold, and mechanistically distinct from anabolic steroids, the associative context shapes how people receive information about it. When something is perceived as belonging to a morally suspect category, claims about its safety tend to receive more skepticism than they would in isolation.

## Verdict

**Contested, with the weight of evidence favoring safety in healthy adults.** The claim that creatine causes kidney damage in healthy individuals is not well-supported by the available controlled evidence. Long-term studies in healthy adults supplementing at typical doses have not found evidence of kidney impairment by standard functional markers. The ISSN and other sports medicine bodies consider creatine monohydrate safe for healthy adults at recommended doses.

However, the claim is not simply a myth. The false-positive problem on creatinine panels is real and has genuine clinical consequences. The case report literature documents kidney problems in creatine users with pre-existing renal conditions. The long-term data has a real ceiling at approximately five years. And the mechanistic concern — that additional creatinine load may be harmful in kidneys with reduced reserve capacity — is not unreasonable.

The honest verdict: creatine appears safe for healthy individuals based on the best available evidence, but healthy individuals means people without kidney disease (including undiagnosed CKD), and the absence of harm evidence over five years is not the same as the absence of harm over two or three decades. People with kidney disease, a family history of kidney problems, or who are taking nephrotoxic medications should consult a physician before supplementing with creatine.

## The Wider Picture

The creatine case is a rich example of a broader category of health claims where the mechanistic concern is legitimate but the epidemiological evidence does not confirm the anticipated harm at typical doses — and where clinical practice has responded to real (if misinterpreted) signals in ways that outlasted the updating of the evidence.

**The creatinine false-positive problem points to a broader measurement issue.** Serum creatinine is one of the most commonly ordered laboratory tests in medicine. Its reliability as a kidney function marker depends on assumptions about the relationship between creatinine production and clearance that are violated in creatine users. As creatine supplementation has become more common — the global creatine market is measured in hundreds of millions of dollars annually, with tens of millions of regular users — the clinical significance of this measurement artifact has grown. Electronic health record systems, standardized lab panels, and automated flagging algorithms are not routinely updated to account for supplement use. The result is a systematic source of clinical noise whose magnitude is difficult to estimate but is clearly non-zero.

**Creatine is an unusual case of a well-studied supplement.** The depth of the creatine safety literature is atypical for the supplement industry. Most supplements have never been the subject of a five-year longitudinal controlled study. Creatine has been studied more rigorously than almost any other non-pharmaceutical performance supplement, partly because it was widely used by competitive athletes who were already enrolled in sports science research programs. This makes the creatine case somewhat unrepresentative of the broader supplement landscape — it is the kind of data depth that would be required to be confident about a supplement's safety, and most supplements lack it.

**The distinction between healthy and unhealthy populations matters more than headlines suggest.** Much health journalism and much popular health culture operates with a kind of demographic universalism: a finding in one population is treated as applicable to all. The creatine safety literature is specifically robust for healthy adults with normal kidney function. It says less about older populations, populations with higher rates of undiagnosed CKD, or populations with other comorbidities that affect kidney metabolism. Public health messaging that translates "safe for healthy adults in controlled trials" into "safe for everyone" is making a generalization that the evidence does not support.

**The nephrologist perspective deserves respect even when the data is unfavorable to their caution.** Nephrologists — kidney specialists — tend to be more cautious about creatine than sports medicine physicians or exercise scientists. This is partly because they see a selected population: patients with kidney problems. Their clinical experience is not representative of the population of healthy creatine users who never develop problems and thus never appear in their offices. But it is also partly because their professional framework weights irreversible harm heavily — kidneys that fail do not regenerate — and the absence of definitive long-term data (beyond five years) is, from that perspective, a genuine reason for caution rather than a simple failure to update on the existing evidence. The sports medicine consensus and the nephrology consensus can both be rational responses to the evidence from within their respective professional contexts.

**Supplement regulation is a relevant background condition.** The fact that creatine is sold without pre-market safety review, that products labeled as creatine can be adulterated with other substances, that dosing varies widely, and that consumers often combine creatine with other supplements whose interactions have not been studied — all of this creates uncertainty that controlled trials of pure creatine monohydrate in controlled conditions do not fully resolve. The safety data is for creatine monohydrate at specific doses taken by otherwise-healthy people. The real-world supplementation context is more heterogeneous, and some of the case reports of harm may involve products that were not purely what the label claimed.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The creatine claim presents a pattern common in sports and nutrition misinformation: a claim that is not simply false but that has enough plausible components and enough legitimate hedges to resist clean categorical verdicts.

**Snopes has not issued a formal verdict on this claim.** As with many sports nutrition claims, the specific assertion about creatine and kidney damage does not appear in Snopes' database as a discrete checked claim. This is partly because the claim does not originate in a specific viral piece of content (a tweet, a news story, a political statement) but rather circulates as ambient cultural knowledge — the kind of thing people believe without necessarily having heard it from a specific source.

**Health journalism coverage has been improving.** The best current science journalism on creatine distinguishes clearly between healthy and unhealthy populations, flags the creatinine false-positive issue, cites the ISSN position statement and the Kreider longitudinal data, and avoids both blanket reassurance and alarmism. Publications like *Examine.com* — which specializes in evidence-based supplement analysis — have produced nuanced treatments of the creatine-kidney claim that model what responsible coverage looks like: graded confidence levels, explicit acknowledgment of evidence gaps, and clear distinction between the healthy-adult data and the general-population inference.

**The challenge for general fact-checkers is calibration, not verification.** The relevant facts in the creatine case are not disputed — the Kreider study results, the ISSN position, the creatinine false-positive mechanism — and can be verified by consulting primary sources. The challenge is calibrating the appropriate level of confidence given those facts. A verdict of "False" (creatine does not cause kidney damage) misrepresents the legitimate uncertainty about long-term effects, the real risk for people with pre-existing conditions, and the unresolved regulatory questions about supplement purity. A verdict of "True" (creatine does cause kidney damage) ignores the weight of the controlled evidence. "Mostly False" or "Unproven" are closer, but the specific application of those labels depends on assumptions about what population the claim is meant to describe and what time horizon is relevant.

**The disclosures that matter most.** The most valuable thing a fact-checker or health journalist can do with the creatine claim is to be specific about what population the evidence covers, acknowledge the creatinine false-positive issue, note the five-year data ceiling, and clearly separate the healthy-adult consensus from the uncertainty about pre-existing kidney disease. The single most harmful failure mode in coverage of this claim is to translate evidence for healthy adults into blanket reassurance — because the people most at risk (those with undiagnosed CKD) are precisely the people for whom the safety data is least applicable, and they are the people who may be most reassured by confident public messaging that creatine is safe.
