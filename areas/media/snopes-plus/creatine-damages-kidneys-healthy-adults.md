## The Claim

Creatine supplements damage kidney function. This is a widely repeated caution passed between gym-goers, parents of young athletes, and even some medical professionals: taking creatine puts the kidneys under dangerous stress, risks kidney disease, and should be avoided or treated with great caution. The claim often comes paired with a physical intuition — you are flooding your body with an unusual compound, and the kidneys have to filter it. Something must suffer.

This claim circulates on health forums, in well-meaning parental advice, and occasionally in clinical settings where a physician unfamiliar with sports supplementation sees an elevated creatinine reading in a patient who takes creatine and connects the dots in the wrong direction. The concern is understandable. Creatine is one of the most popular dietary supplements on earth, used by competitive athletes, recreational gym members, and increasingly by older adults interested in muscle retention and cognitive performance. If it were genuinely damaging kidneys at scale, that would be a substantial public health problem. So it is worth examining both what the evidence actually says and why the claim persists with such tenacity.

---

## What's Actually True

The current body of evidence is extensive, spans multiple decades, and reaches a consistent conclusion: creatine supplementation at standard doses (typically 3–5 grams per day for maintenance, with a loading phase of up to 20 grams per day for approximately one week) does not damage kidney function in healthy adults.

### The major studies

The landmark long-term study on creatine and kidney safety is Kreider et al. (2003), which followed trained athletes supplementing with creatine for up to 21 months. Participants underwent repeated clinical evaluations of kidney function, including measurements of blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine, and other markers. The study found no adverse effects on kidney function over this extended period — one of the longest prospective studies of its kind at the time. Athletes were consuming between 5 and 20 grams daily, encompassing the range of real-world supplementation patterns.

This was not an isolated finding. A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials found no significant negative effects of creatine on kidney function in healthy populations. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) reviewed the accumulated evidence in a comprehensive position paper (Kreider et al., 2017) and concluded that creatine monohydrate is safe for healthy individuals across the typical supplementation ranges and durations studied. The position statement has been widely cited and endorsed by sports medicine organizations.

Longitudinal surveillance of powerlifters, bodybuilders, and endurance athletes — populations with the highest rates of creatine use and the longest supplementation histories — has not revealed elevated rates of chronic kidney disease, kidney failure, or progressive renal dysfunction attributable to creatine. These are the populations that would constitute the canary in the coal mine if the concern were valid.

### The creatinine confusion

The central epistemological problem here is a genuine and important one: creatine supplementation reliably elevates serum creatinine levels. This is not a sign of kidney damage. It is an expected metabolic consequence.

Here is why. Creatine is stored primarily in skeletal muscle as phosphocreatine and, in its free form, as creatine itself. During normal energy metabolism and through spontaneous chemical conversion, creatine breaks down into creatinine, which is then filtered by the kidneys and excreted in urine. When you supplement with creatine, you increase the total pool of creatine in your muscles, which in turn increases the rate of creatinine production. More creatine in → more creatinine out. This is a mass-balance effect, not a pathological one.

The problem is that serum creatinine is a standard clinical marker used to estimate kidney filtration rate (via calculations like the Cockcroft-Gault equation or the MDRD formula). Elevated serum creatinine is routinely interpreted as reduced kidney clearance, which suggests kidney dysfunction. When a physician sees an elevated creatinine in a patient who supplements with creatine, the standard clinical interpretation points in the wrong direction. The kidneys are actually filtering the creatinine just fine — there is simply more of it to filter because the patient is supplementing.

This is a well-documented interpretive hazard. Several studies and case reports have documented situations where athletes on creatine were flagged for elevated creatinine readings, underwent unnecessary kidney workup, and were advised to stop supplementation — only for the marker to return to baseline levels after cessation. The kidneys were never the problem; the reference range interpretation was.

More sensitive markers of kidney function — glomerular filtration rate measured by inulin clearance or cystatin C, tubular enzymes, albuminuria — do not show the same elevations in creatine users and have not demonstrated kidney damage in controlled trials.

### Pre-existing kidney conditions

There is a genuine and important caveat. While the evidence supports safety in healthy adults, individuals with pre-existing kidney conditions or reduced kidney function are a different population. Case reports in the nephrology literature describe individuals with pre-existing kidney disease or with conditions predisposing to kidney problems (such as polycystic kidney disease, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, or severe dehydration combined with high-dose creatine) who experienced worsening kidney function in temporal association with creatine use.

These case reports are real and clinically meaningful. They do not, however, support the claim that creatine damages kidneys in healthy individuals. They support the specific, narrower claim that individuals with existing kidney vulnerabilities should consult their physician before using creatine and may be advised to avoid it. That is appropriate clinical guidance. The error occurs when this specific contraindication gets generalized into a blanket condemnation of creatine for everyone.

The ISSN position explicitly distinguishes these populations and recommends that people with kidney disease or risk factors for it seek medical guidance rather than simply generalizing the healthy-population evidence to themselves.

## Why People Believe This

### The creatinine lab artifact

The elevated serum creatinine produced by creatine supplementation is probably the single largest driver of the belief. Millions of creatine users have routine bloodwork, see an elevated creatinine, are told by their doctors or lab reports that elevated creatinine is associated with kidney problems, and conclude — entirely reasonably given the information they have — that the supplement is causing harm. The biochemical distinction between "more creatinine substrate means more creatinine production" and "kidneys are failing to clear creatinine" is not intuitive and is not well explained on standard laboratory reports.

This is not a failure of gullibility. It is a failure of information design. The lab report says "creatinine: elevated." It does not say "elevated due to dietary creatine, not indicative of kidney dysfunction." The physician who has not seen the recent sports nutrition literature may not know to make that distinction either.

### Dose and purity concerns

Early creatine supplements, particularly in the 1990s and early 2000s, had quality control problems. Third-party analyses found some products contaminated with creatinine (the waste byproduct), dicyandiamide, or dihydrotriazine. Some observed adverse effects in early users may have been attributable to impurities rather than creatine itself. Modern pharmaceutical-grade creatine monohydrate is far purer, and the evidence base largely comes from studies using verified creatine monohydrate rather than unregulated early formulations.

This historical dimension means that some older medical training materials and some physicians who trained in the 1990s absorbed information that reflected the impurity problem rather than the pharmacology of pure creatine. That guidance then got transmitted forward.

### Case reports and availability bias

The nephrology literature contains documented case reports of kidney problems in creatine users. These reports exist, have been published in peer-reviewed journals, and are readily findable by anyone doing a PubMed search for "creatine kidney case report." For a reader who is not trained in distinguishing case reports from controlled studies, or who is not practiced at reading the detailed case histories (which typically reveal either pre-existing kidney vulnerability, extremely high doses, or simultaneous use of other supplements and medications), these reports constitute powerful persuasive evidence.

A person who reads a case report describing a 22-year-old bodybuilder who developed kidney failure while using creatine, and who does not know that this person also had undiagnosed focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, will reasonably conclude that creatine is dangerous. The causal story is absent from the abstract. The availability of these dramatic individual narratives, combined with the genuine elevation of creatinine on standard lab tests, produces a coherent and memorable belief structure that is very difficult to dislodge with population-level statistics.

### Cultural transmission among healthcare workers

Nursing, pharmacy, and medical training sometimes incorporates conservative safety stances on supplements by default, both because clinical training focuses on treating illness rather than evaluating wellness supplements, and because the liability calculus favors caution. A nurse trained a decade ago may have been taught that creatine is nephrotoxic without having been given the full evidentiary picture. That nurse then advises patients accordingly. The belief propagates through trusted, authoritative sources even as the research evidence has grown considerably more reassuring.

### Conflation with protein intake myths

Creatine belief sometimes merges with a separate, better-founded concern about high protein intake and kidney stress. High protein diets do increase the filtration load on kidneys, and for individuals with reduced kidney function, high protein is a genuine concern. Creatine is associated with muscle building and gym culture, as is high protein consumption. The two sometimes get bundled together in health advice, so the more valid caution about protein in compromised kidneys attaches, wrongly, to creatine as well.

## Verdict

**Contested — safety-complex.** Creatine supplementation is well-evidenced as safe for kidney function in healthy adults at standard doses and durations studied. The claim that it damages kidneys in this population is not supported by the current weight of evidence, including long-term prospective studies and systematic reviews. The elevated creatinine readings seen in creatine users are a metabolic artifact, not evidence of kidney damage.

The legitimate concern applies to a specific sub-population: individuals with pre-existing kidney conditions or known risk factors for renal disease. For these people, creatine is a meaningful clinical consideration and should be evaluated with appropriate medical oversight. The ISSN recommendation to consult a physician if kidney risk factors exist is sound.

The claim as commonly stated — that creatine damages kidneys — is too broad and not well-supported for healthy populations. The claim that people with kidney problems should be cautious is well-supported. These are meaningfully different claims, and the failure to distinguish them accounts for most of the ongoing controversy.

## The Wider Picture

### Creatine's broader evidence profile

Creatine monohydrate is one of the most studied supplements in sports science. Its efficacy for increasing muscle phosphocreatine stores, enhancing short-burst power output (anaerobic performance), and supporting recovery from high-intensity training is robustly documented. It is not a miracle compound — it helps most at the margin for trained individuals doing the types of exercise where ATP resynthesis rates matter — but its efficacy within that scope is as well-established as any supplement in the science.

Increasingly, creatine is being studied beyond athletic performance. Research interest has grown in its potential role in cognitive function (particularly under conditions of sleep deprivation or mental fatigue), in sarcopenia prevention in older adults, in neurological conditions including Parkinson's disease and Huntington's disease, and in mood regulation. These application areas are at earlier stages of evidentiary development, but they have expanded the relevance of the kidney safety question beyond the athletic population.

### Supplement regulation and trust problems

The creatine kidney debate exists within a broader context of supplement market dysfunction. Dietary supplements in the United States are regulated less stringently than pharmaceutical drugs. The FDA does not require pre-market safety testing for supplements, and third-party quality verification is voluntary. This creates a genuine trust problem: even if creatine monohydrate from reputable sources is well-evidenced as safe, the consumer buying a product labeled "creatine" from an unverified source may not be getting pure creatine monohydrate. The advice to verify third-party testing (NSF, Informed Sport, USP) before using any supplement is sound and directly relevant to risk assessment.

### The interpretive challenge for lab values

The creatine/creatinine problem is a microcosm of a broader challenge in clinical interpretation: standard reference ranges are calibrated to a general population and do not account for all the ways that normal biological variation or benign lifestyle factors can shift a single marker. Athletes routinely have lab values outside "normal" ranges — higher red blood cell mass, altered hormone levels, higher muscle enzyme levels — that are not signs of disease but artifacts of training adaptations. Clinicians working with active populations need literacy in these pattern differences. The creatine-creatinine confusion is one of the best-documented and most practically important examples of this general problem.

### Practical guidance for supplementers

For healthy adults with no known kidney disease or risk factors, the evidence does not support concern about standard creatine supplementation. Staying hydrated, using pharmaceutical-grade creatine monohydrate from verified sources, and not dramatically exceeding recommended doses are reasonable practices. If routine bloodwork shows elevated creatinine and the person is using creatine, that is worth mentioning to their physician so the clinical interpretation is appropriately calibrated — not as a sign of harm, but as a communication exercise to prevent unnecessary downstream testing.

For individuals with diabetes, hypertension, a family history of kidney disease, or any known renal condition, the picture is genuinely more uncertain, and individual medical consultation is appropriate before supplementing.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

### The structural difficulty

Creatine and kidney health presents a fact-checking challenge because the claim is not uniformly false — it is false for healthy adults and potentially true for a specific sub-population. A simple "false" verdict risks being misread as "creatine is always safe for everyone," which is not quite right. A "true" verdict would be badly wrong. The claim sits in territory that requires distinguishing between populations and between different kinds of evidence, which is more work than most quick fact-check formats accommodate easily.

### Current coverage

Snopes does not appear to have a dedicated entry on this claim as of the time of writing, though it has addressed adjacent supplement safety questions. Healthline, Medical News Today, and similar science-adjacent health publications have published reasonably accurate explainers on creatine kidney safety, typically following the ISSN position. These are not full fact-checks with methodology documentation, but they provide accessible guidance that is broadly consistent with the research.

The difficulty is that even accurate health articles tend to hedge heavily, saying things like "creatine appears to be safe for most healthy adults," which while epistemically defensible can still leave readers uncertain about whether they should be concerned. The hedge is warranted, but it can inadvertently sustain doubt in readers who would benefit from more confident reassurance about the healthy-population case.

### What rigorous coverage looks like

A rigorous fact-check of this claim would need to: (1) specify which population is being assessed; (2) distinguish case reports from controlled trials and explain why the methodological hierarchy matters; (3) explain the creatinine artifact explicitly; (4) make clear what the ISSN position is and what evidence informs it; (5) separately address the legitimate concerns for at-risk populations without allowing those concerns to contaminate the healthy-population conclusion. Most health journalism stops short of this level of structured analysis.

The epistemological lesson this claim illustrates — that a single lab marker can be simultaneously elevated and unindicative of harm, and that our clinical interpretation infrastructure can be structurally misleading about certain lifestyle factors — is one that warrants more explicit treatment in science communication than it typically receives.
