# "Creatine Supplementation Damages Kidneys in Healthy Individuals"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: Taking creatine supplements — most commonly creatine monohydrate, the most widely used form — causes kidney damage in otherwise healthy people. According to this claim, creatine supplementation produces nephrotoxic effects that over time degrade renal function, potentially leading to chronic kidney disease, renal insufficiency, or at minimum measurably impaired kidney filtration capacity.

**Who makes this claim**: The creatine-kidney concern has circulated in medical advisory settings (some physicians recommend against creatine for this reason), popular fitness media, and parental discourse about adolescent supplementation. Chain gyms, sports medical practitioners, and nutrition counselors have at various times repeated the warning. The concern is frequently framed as precautionary ("the long-term effects aren't known") rather than as a claim of established harm — but both framings reflect the same underlying assumption that creatine poses renal risk.

**The population framing**: The claim typically targets healthy individuals — those without pre-existing kidney disease, diabetes, hypertension, or other conditions associated with renal vulnerability. A distinct and empirically more defensible concern attaches to creatine supplementation in individuals with pre-existing renal impairment, where the elevated creatinine artifact has genuine clinical implications. The healthy-population claim is the contested one.

**The laboratory value context**: Many versions of the concern trace back to a specific clinical observation: creatine supplementation elevates serum creatinine levels. Serum creatinine is the most widely used clinical biomarker for kidney function — elevated creatinine typically suggests reduced glomerular filtration rate (GFR) and deteriorating renal function. Physicians who see creatinine elevation in a creatine-supplementing patient may correctly flag the finding as clinically notable, and this concern has propagated into broader advisory statements about creatine safety.

**The sociological fitness context**: Creatine monohydrate is among the most extensively studied ergogenic aids in sports science. It has been in widespread athletic use since the early 1990s and is probably the most documented performance-enhancing supplement in sport. It is not a controlled substance, is available over the counter at low cost, and is used by millions of athletes globally. Its safety profile has been directly tested in dozens of controlled trials spanning multiple decades.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Creatine biochemistry and the creatinine artifact**: Creatine (methylguanidino-acetic acid) is a nitrogen-containing compound found primarily in skeletal muscle tissue, synthesized endogenously in the liver, pancreas, and kidneys from the amino acids arginine and glycine, and obtained exogenously from dietary meat and fish. In muscle cells, creatine is phosphorylated to phosphocreatine, which serves as a rapid energy buffer for ATP regeneration during high-intensity exercise. When phosphocreatine is used, the dephosphorylated creatine undergoes spontaneous, non-enzymatic cyclization to creatinine, which exits cells and is filtered from blood by the kidneys at a nearly constant rate. Serum creatinine concentration is thus a function of both kidney filtration and the rate of creatinine generation from creatine.

**Why creatine supplementation elevates serum creatinine**: When exogenous creatine is consumed, total creatine pool in muscle increases, which directly increases the rate of spontaneous cyclization to creatinine. This elevates serum creatinine — but through increased creatinine production, not through reduced kidney clearance. The distinction is critical: creatinine-GFR relationships assume a constant rate of creatinine generation; creatine supplementation violates this assumption. Standard kidney function equations (Cockcroft-Gault, CKD-EPI, MDRD) that calculate estimated GFR from serum creatinine were not validated in creatine-supplementing populations and will systematically underestimate true GFR in these individuals (Poortmans, J.R. & Francaux, M., 1999, *Kidney International*, 55(5): 1316–1317).

**The Poortmans and Francaux landmark study**: A landmark investigation by Poortmans and Francaux (1999, *Kidney International*, ibid.) directly tested renal function in long-term creatine users (median supplementation duration of 4.6 years) versus non-users. Although creatine users showed elevated serum creatinine on standard clinical tests, direct measures of GFR using clearance of exogenous filtration markers (specifically creatinine clearance) showed no difference between groups. The authors concluded that elevated serum creatinine in creatine users represents increased production, not impaired clearance, and that renal function was intact. This study is foundational in the creatine-kidney literature.

**Randomized controlled trial evidence**: Multiple RCTs have examined kidney function before and after creatine supplementation protocols. A systematic review by Gualano et al. (2008, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 5(1): 5162–2008) analyzed available RCTs and case studies and found no evidence of nephrotoxicity in healthy subjects at doses of 20 grams/day during loading phases or 2–5 grams/day during maintenance phases. The authors noted that the creatinine elevation was consistent and should not be misinterpreted as a kidney function signal in supplementing populations.

**The Terjung et al. consensus review**: A comprehensive review sponsored by the American College of Sports Medicine (Terjung, R.L. et al., 2000, *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 32(3): 706–717) evaluated the mechanisms, efficacy, and safety of creatine supplementation across available evidence at the time. On kidney function, the review concluded that no evidence of adverse effects on renal function existed in healthy individuals using creatine at recommended doses and that available studies were of insufficient duration to conclude on very long-term effects (decades), but that shorter-term studies consistently showed no impairment.

**The Gualano et al. long-term RCT**: One of the most methodologically rigorous direct safety tests was conducted by Bruno Gualano and colleagues in Brazil (Gualano, B. et al., 2011, *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*, 43(6): 1108–1111), who randomized 46 sedentary older adults to creatine supplementation (10 g/day in divided doses) or placebo for 12 weeks and measured renal function using multiple approaches: serum creatinine (expected to be elevated by artifact), cystatin C-based GFR estimation (not affected by creatine supplementation), urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (a sensitive early marker of renal damage), and calculated creatinine clearance. No differences between creatine and placebo groups were found in any marker except serum creatinine, which was elevated in the creatine group as expected by the artifact. The conclusion was that creatine supplementation did not impair kidney function by any marker not confounded by the creatine-creatinine pathway.

**The cystatin C solution**: Cystatin C is an alternative GFR biomarker that is produced at a constant rate and cleared by glomerular filtration with no significant non-GFR determinants — unlike creatinine, its production is not affected by muscle mass or creatine supplementation. Studies measuring cystatin C-derived GFR in creatine-supplementing populations consistently find no impairment, providing the critical validation that the creatinine elevation is artifactual rather than functional (Spillane, M. et al., 2009, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 6(14): documenting cystatin C stability with creatine supplementation).

**The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand**: The most comprehensive and regularly updated consensus document on creatine safety is the International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) position stand on creatine supplementation (Kreider, R.B. et al., 2017, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 14: 18). Among its stated positions regarding kidney health: "Creatine supplementation in healthy individuals does not cause renal impairment or negatively affect kidney function" — a position described as supported by the vast majority of available clinical studies. The document notes explicitly that clinicians should be aware that serum creatinine elevation in creatine-supplementing patients represents a metabolic artifact rather than a clinical warning sign.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the biomarker misinterpretation model and its structural invisibility**:

The creatine-kidney myth is epistemically unusual because it persists through the corruption of a legitimate clinical test rather than through the absence of information. The mechanism by which creatine creates the false kidney-damage signal is itself not widely understood even among clinicians, creating a situation where more clinical vigilance — more testing, more biomarker checking — can paradoxically reinforce the false belief.

Standard clinical measurement of kidney function relies heavily on serum creatinine because it is cheap, universally available, and closely tracks GFR across the range of normal and pathological conditions in unmedicated, non-supplementing patients. The fundamental assumption behind this measurement is that creatinine production rate is constant and depends only on muscle mass (hence the correction for age, sex, and race in GFR estimation equations). This assumption is correct for the vast majority of patients in clinical practice, who are not supplementing with creatine. For this majority, a rising serum creatinine genuinely signals declining GFR. The test was designed, validated, and deployed for this population, and it works well.

Creatine supplementation is a rare condition that systematically and specifically violates the constant-production assumption of the serum creatinine test. It does so through a biochemical mechanism — increased creatine pool → increased non-enzymatic cyclization → increased creatinine generation — that is causally disconnected from GFR. Yet because this mechanism is unusual and because clinicians rarely encounter supplementing patients in the populations where the test was validated, many clinicians do not know the artifact exists. They see elevated creatinine in a patient taking creatine, they apply the standard test interpretation framework, and they conclude impaired kidney function — because that is what elevated creatinine means in the absence of known sources of increased creatinine production.

The result is a structurally invisible false positive: the test reports a finding, the clinician interprets it correctly using standard reasoning, the patient is warned about kidney damage, and the warning is disseminated. This error propagates not from ignorance but from coherent application of a valid clinical methodology to a population for which it was not validated. It is an instance of what might be called the *domain-validity problem*: a tool is accurate in its validated domain, misleading in a closely related domain, and there is no built-in signal indicating when the domain has shifted.

The invisibility is compounded because alternative GFR markers like cystatin C, which would correctly detect the artifact, are more expensive and less routinely ordered. Most clinical checkups do not include cystatin C. The cheaper and more common test gives the wrong answer, and the right answer requires a less common test. In healthcare settings where cost and routine drive test selection, the false positive dominates clinical inference.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE**: Creatine supplementation at standard doses (loading: up to 20 g/day for 5–7 days; maintenance: 2–5 g/day) does not damage kidneys in healthy individuals. The robust evidence base — comprising multiple randomized controlled trials, long-term observational studies of habitual users, systematic reviews, and consensus statements from major sports nutrition organizations — consistently finds no impairment of glomerular filtration rate, no increase in urinary albumin excretion (an early sensitive marker of renal damage), and no progression toward renal dysfunction.

**The creatinine artifact is real but not harmful**: The laboratory finding of elevated serum creatinine in creatine-supplementing individuals is real, reproducible, and predictable, but it represents a production artifact rather than a clearance failure. Clinicians evaluating kidney function in patients known to be taking creatine should use cystatin C-based GFR estimation or creatinine clearance methods rather than relying on standard serum creatinine equations. Attributing the creatinine elevation to kidney damage in these patients is a clinical error.

**The caveat for pre-existing renal disease**: The healthy-population safety finding does not extend automatically to individuals with chronic kidney disease, diabetic nephropathy, or other conditions that have already compromised renal reserve. In these populations, elevated creatinine has genuine diagnostic significance, creatine supplementation's effects on phosphate metabolism and water retention create additional considerations, and clinical guidance advising against supplementation is reasonable. The false-belief problem is specific to healthy individuals, not to this vulnerable group.

**Long-term data extending across years**: The Poortmans & Francaux (1999) cohort studied individuals who had supplemented for up to 5.6 years, and their direct GFR measures showed no impairment. No clinical case study has established creatine-induced nephrotoxicity in a healthy individual with documented normal baseline kidney function (Persky, A.M. & Brazeau, G.A., 2001, *Pharmacological Reviews*, 53(2): 161–176). The absence of such cases in a supplement used by millions for three decades provides additional epidemiological evidence against nephrotoxicity in healthy users.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The ergogenic evidence base for creatine**: Creatine's performance benefits are among the best-documented in sports nutrition science. Supplementation consistently increases intramuscular phosphocreatine stores, enhancing ATP resynthesis capacity during high-intensity, short-duration exercise (Greenhaff, P.L. et al., 1994, *Acta Physiologica Scandinavica*, 150(2): 232–234). Meta-analyses of creatine's effect on strength gains in resistance-trained individuals have found significant positive effects on maximal strength and lean mass accrual (Lanhers, C. et al., 2017, *European Journal of Sport Science*, 17(8): 1070–1077). This unusually strong evidence base makes creatine interesting as a case study in how a well-evidenced ergogenic aid can simultaneously be the target of persistent unfounded safety scares.

**The adolescent supplementation debate**: Considerable attention in the creatine literature has been directed at adolescent use. Sports medicine organizations including the American Academy of Pediatrics have historically recommended against creatine in adolescents primarily due to insufficient safety data in developing populations rather than established harm (AAP Committee on Sports Medicine, 2005, *Pediatrics*). More recent evidence has not found harm in adolescent studies, but the gap in confirmed long-term pediatric data remains a genuine reason for caution — distinct from the false kidney-damage claim.

**Creatine and therapeutic uses**: In addition to its ergogenic applications, creatine is under active study for therapeutic use in neurodegenerative conditions including Parkinson's disease (Bender, A. et al., 2006, *Parkinsonism & Related Disorders*, 12(8): 537–541), Huntington's disease, and creatine transporter deficiency syndromes. These therapeutic applications make the kidney safety question directly relevant to clinical practice beyond sports medicine, as patients taking creatine therapeutically may be simultaneously under treatment for other conditions and subject to routine kidney function monitoring.

**The supplement industry context**: Creatine is manufactured and sold by hundreds of supplement companies with variable quality control. While the primary form (creatine monohydrate) has a favorable safety and purity profile, some creatine products have been found to contain contaminants, adulterants, or undeclared substances — concerns that the kidney-safety literature mostly controls for by specifying creatine monohydrate at defined doses. The existence of a large, heterogeneous supplement market means that safety claims about "creatine" as a category need to be read as applying to pure creatine monohydrate rather than to every product marketed under that label.

**The downstream cost of the false belief**: If the kidney-damage myth deters healthy individuals from using creatine, the cost is primarily foregone ergogenic benefit — which may be consequential for competitive athletes but is modest for general health. More significant downstream costs may arise in clinical settings where the biomarker artifact causes physician concern, triggers unnecessary follow-up testing, leads to discontinuation of potentially beneficial supplementation, or generates patient anxiety about organ function that is not warranted by actual kidney status. The clinical communication burden imposed by the myth may be its most concrete harmful consequence.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes verdict**: Snopes rates the claim that creatine damages kidneys in healthy people as false, noting that the scientific literature has extensively studied this question and consistently found no evidence of nephrotoxicity at normal supplementation doses. Snopes contextualizes the serum creatinine elevation as a well-understood artifact and distinguishes the healthy-population finding from appropriate caution in populations with pre-existing renal disease.

**Scientific consensus institutions**: The International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand (Kreider et al., 2017) represents the most authoritative consensus statement and is explicitly negative on renal harm in healthy individuals. The American College of Sports Medicine's earlier review (Terjung et al., 2000) reached the same conclusion within the evidence available at that time. Both documents note the creatinine-artifact problem and advise against over-interpreting serum creatinine in supplementing populations.

**The clinical education gap**: A notable feature of the creatine-kidney literature is the repeated observation that the risk belief persists primarily in clinical medicine settings rather than in sports science or nutrition science communities, which have largely converged on the false verdict. Physicians, particularly those who graduated before the mid-2000s systematic reviews became widely available, may hold the kidney concern as received clinical wisdom that predates the comprehensive evidence synthesis. This represents a science-communication lag in which the relevant scientific community has reached a clear consensus that has not fully diffused into the clinical communities that see the affected patients.

**The precautionary-framing escape hatch**: A significant proportion of the persistence of the creatine-kidney concern operates through a precautionary framing: "we don't know the long-term effects, so caution is advisable." This framing is technically unchallengeable — the absolute longest human studies are around 5–6 years, and evidence beyond that window relies on the absence of cases rather than active monitoring. But the precautionary framing functions as an untestable claim when the available evidence consistently shows no harm, because it implicitly requires indefinitely longer studies to satisfy it. In practice, this precautionary framing has allowed the kidney concern to persist in clinical advice even as the direct evidence against harm has accumulated, because the evidentiary burden it implies can never fully be met.
