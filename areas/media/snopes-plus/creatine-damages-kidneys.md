## 1. The Claim

Creatine supplements damage your kidneys. Athletes and gym-goers who take creatine to enhance performance are putting themselves at risk for kidney disease, kidney failure, or long-term renal impairment. The supplement industry downplays these risks, and most people who take creatine are doing so without understanding the toll it takes on one of the body's most vital organs.

This claim circulates persistently across fitness forums, health blogs, parental warnings to teenagers lifting weights, and cautious advice from physicians who may not have closely examined the primary literature. It has the surface plausibility of a metabolic story: creatine is processed in the body, kidneys process waste, therefore creatine strains kidneys. That chain of intuition is the foundation of the concern — and also the source of its error.

## 2. What's Actually True

Creatine monohydrate is among the most extensively studied dietary supplements in existence. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN), in its 2017 position stand on creatine supplementation, concluded with high confidence that creatine is safe for use by healthy individuals at recommended doses, and that there is no evidence it damages kidney function in people without pre-existing renal disease.

The ISSN position statement — authored by a panel of researchers including Richard Kreider, Douglas Kalman, Jose Antonio, and others — reviewed decades of controlled trials, long-term observational studies, and clinical data. Their conclusion: "The vast majority of the research indicates that creatine supplementation does not adversely affect kidney function in healthy individuals when taken at recommended doses." The statement further noted that creatine supplementation has been studied extensively in populations ranging from young athletes to older adults and people with various health conditions, and the renal safety profile is consistently unremarkable in healthy cohorts.

The critical mechanism behind the misunderstanding involves creatinine, not creatine. When the body metabolizes creatine — whether from diet, endogenous production, or supplementation — it produces creatinine as a byproduct. Creatinine is cleared by the kidneys and excreted in urine. Importantly, elevated serum creatinine is one of the primary biomarkers physicians use to detect kidney dysfunction. When the kidneys are impaired, creatinine accumulates in the blood.

This is where the confusion enters: creatine supplementation increases the body's creatine pool, which increases creatine turnover, which increases creatinine production and clearance. Blood creatinine levels rise. A clinician who sees an elevated creatinine reading without knowing the patient is on creatine may interpret it as a warning sign of kidney disease.

But elevated creatinine from creatine supplementation does not indicate kidney damage. The kidneys are handling the load appropriately — they are simply clearing more creatinine than usual. Multiple studies have confirmed that other kidney function markers — glomerular filtration rate (GFR), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), albumin excretion, kidney morphology on imaging — remain normal in creatine users. The creatinine elevation is a metabolic artifact of supplement use, not a signal of nephrotoxicity.

Typical supplementation protocols involve a loading phase (20 grams/day for 5–7 days, split into multiple doses) followed by a maintenance dose of 3–5 grams per day. Studies of these regimens, including trials lasting years rather than months, have found no evidence of kidney impairment in healthy participants. Longitudinal studies of athletes who have used creatine continuously for multiple years find kidney function within normal reference ranges.

The one genuine caveat: people with pre-existing kidney disease, or those with a single kidney or reduced renal reserve, should consult a physician before supplementing with creatine. In compromised kidneys, the added metabolic load of elevated creatinine clearance may be meaningful. This is not a concern for healthy individuals — it is a population-specific consideration that applies to many substances, from protein intake to certain over-the-counter medications.

Creatine is also not a manufactured pharmaceutical. It is a compound naturally produced in the body — primarily in the liver — and obtained from dietary protein sources, especially meat and fish. Vegetarians and vegans often have lower baseline creatine levels and can see performance benefits from supplementation that omnivores may not experience as dramatically.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The creatine-kidney myth is architecturally elegant as a misconception. It follows a simple, internally coherent causal chain — you put something foreign in your body, your kidneys filter it, therefore your kidneys are stressed — and it is partially grounded in real biochemistry. Creatine metabolism really does produce creatinine. Creatinine really is a kidney marker. Elevated creatinine really does concern physicians.

The error is not in the observations but in the inference: moving from "creatinine is elevated" to "kidneys are damaged" requires distinguishing between an increased functional load and actual pathology. That distinction is clinically significant but conceptually non-obvious to non-specialists.

Several factors amplify the myth's durability:

**Medical authority as telephone game.** Physicians who were trained before creatine supplementation became widespread may not have tracked the subsequent research. A doctor who remembers vaguely that "creatine raises creatinine" and draws the wrong inference can lend professional authority to the misconception. Patients trust their doctors; if a physician advises against creatine on renal grounds, that advice carries weight even if it reflects outdated concern rather than current evidence.

**Case reports without context.** There are scattered case reports in the medical literature of individuals with kidney problems who were using creatine. Case reports are not controlled studies — they cannot establish causation. Many of these cases involve confounding factors: polypharmacy, pre-existing conditions, other supplement stacks, or extreme dehydration from other causes. But case reports circulate and are cited as if they demonstrate systematic harm.

**The supplement industry suspicion.** Many people are appropriately skeptical of sports nutrition marketing, which is often riddled with exaggeration and cherry-picked evidence. Creatine's safety profile has been established by independent academic researchers, not supplement companies — but the generalized distrust of the supplement industry can bleed into skepticism about creatine's safety evidence, even when that evidence comes from disinterested sources.

**Gym culture's aversion to nuance.** Health and fitness culture has strong current of "natural is better" thinking, and introducing synthetic supplements is often framed in terms of risk. The precautionary framing ("why risk your kidneys for a few extra pounds on your squat?") is emotionally compelling even when the actual risk is negligible.

**Hydration conflation.** Creatine does increase water retention in muscle tissue, and there is an older view — not strongly supported by current evidence — that this could stress kidneys if hydration is not maintained. Dehydration during intense exercise is a genuine risk factor for kidney stress (exertional rhabdomyolysis, for instance, can cause acute kidney injury). The pairing of "creatine user who got dehydrated" with "kidney problem" can suggest causation where coincidence or independent mechanism was operative.

## 4. Verdict

**Exaggerated (EARC: E) — Distorted but Grounded.** The underlying concern is real in principle but wrong in application. Creatine does affect a kidney biomarker (creatinine), and that effect is clinically legible in a way that can mislead. However, the inference that elevated creatinine from creatine supplementation indicates kidney damage is incorrect. The safety literature is extensive, consistent, and reassuring for healthy individuals.

The claim as typically stated — "creatine damages kidneys" — is false as a general statement. The more accurate framing: "creatine supplementation raises creatinine levels, which can appear concerning on routine bloodwork but does not indicate kidney damage in healthy people. Those with pre-existing kidney conditions should consult a physician."

This is a case where the surface form of the myth (creatine → creatinine → kidney marker → kidney damage) is close enough to the underlying chemistry that debunking it requires explaining a mechanism, not merely asserting a contradiction. That makes it harder to correct and more likely to persist.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The creatine story is part of a broader category of supplement myths where popular understanding lags behind the research literature by a decade or more. Supplement science is not glamorous, and rigorous meta-analyses in sports nutrition journals rarely generate mainstream coverage. The vacuum is filled by anecdote, precautionary logic, and recycled medical advice that is no longer current.

Creatine is actually notable for having one of the better-established safety profiles of any performance supplement. Most ergogenic substances sold in gyms have much thinner evidence bases. The irony is that creatine — one of the supplements with the most rigorous independent validation — has nonetheless attracted sustained suspicion, while many less-studied supplements are consumed without concern.

There is also a broader lesson here about the relationship between biomarkers and pathology. Medical diagnostics work by measuring proxies for underlying conditions — we measure creatinine because we cannot directly assess glomerular function in routine clinical settings. When something intervenes in the production of a proxy biomarker without affecting the underlying condition it proxies, the result is a false positive. Understanding the difference between a biomarker being elevated and the condition it signals actually being present is a fundamental task in medical interpretation.

For individuals considering creatine supplementation, the current evidence supports its safety at standard doses (3–5 grams per day maintenance). Staying adequately hydrated is good practice during any intense exercise regimen, creatine or not. Athletes with any history of kidney problems should get clearance from a sports medicine physician. For everyone else, the kidney concern is a legacy of misinterpretation rather than genuine risk.

The ISSN 2017 position statement is available in the Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition and serves as a comprehensive current reference on creatine's safety and efficacy evidence base. It is peer-reviewed, openly available, authored by a multi-institutional team, and represents the current scientific agreement on creatine's safety profile after more than three decades of controlled research. For any reader encountering the kidney concern in a medical or fitness context, the ISSN statement is the appropriate primary reference — more reliable than secondhand clinical advice, more comprehensive than product-specific marketing, and more current than much of the popular health literature in circulation.

**What creatine actually does in the body.** Creatine's primary mechanism is increasing phosphocreatine stores in muscle tissue. Phosphocreatine is a rapid energy reserve: when ATP (adenosine triphosphate, the universal cellular energy currency) is depleted during high-intensity effort, phosphocreatine donates a phosphate group to ADP to regenerate ATP. This process happens on a millisecond timescale and sustains maximal effort that would otherwise be limited by ATP availability. For athletes performing repeated short bursts of high-intensity activity — sprinting, weightlifting, team sports — the creatine-phosphocreatine system is a direct performance bottleneck that supplementation can expand.

The 3–5 gram maintenance dose represents approximately what the body produces endogenously and obtains from a typical omnivorous diet. Vegetarians and vegans, whose dietary creatine intake is near zero (plant foods contain no creatine), often have lower muscle creatine saturation and can see more dramatic performance responses to supplementation than omnivores who are already partially saturated. This dietary dimension of creatine's effects is underappreciated in popular coverage, which tends to frame creatine as a purely additive supplement rather than as a correction for a dietary gap that many people have.

Research in the past decade has expanded creatine's studied applications beyond athletics. Trials have examined creatine supplementation in older adults for sarcopenia prevention, in patients with neuromuscular diseases, and in cognitive contexts — creatine plays a role in brain energy metabolism, and supplementation has been studied for potential effects on cognitive performance under sleep deprivation and other conditions. None of this research has identified kidney concerns as a significant risk in healthy populations.

**The broader context of supplement misinformation.** The kidney myth is one node in a broader landscape of exercise and supplement misinformation where physiological plausibility substitutes for evidence. The pattern is consistent: a real physiological pathway is identified (creatine → creatinine → kidney filtration), an inference is drawn from mechanism rather than data (therefore kidney strain), and the inference circulates as if the data had been collected to support it. Real mechanism, wrong inference, no controlled trial to anchor the claim — this is the architecture of many persistent supplement myths.

For practitioners of strength training and high-intensity sports, the practical significance is that creatine is one of the most evidence-backed performance supplements available, with a safety record commensurate with that evidence base. The kidney concern, insofar as it deters athletes from a useful and well-studied supplement, does real harm by displacing a safe, effective compound with either nothing or with less-studied alternatives that carry their own unknowns.

**The population-specificity of caution.** One of the recurrent issues in supplement communication is that appropriate caution for one population gets applied universally. Creatine caution for people with pre-existing kidney disease is medically sound and should be maintained. Creatine caution for healthy athletes and recreationally active adults extrapolates from a specific population concern to a general prohibition without the evidence to support the generalization. This conflation is common in medical advice about supplements: legitimate warnings for vulnerable populations (immunocompromised patients, people with organ disease, pregnant individuals) are often communicated without adequate population specificity, becoming generalized cautions that affect everyone regardless of their actual risk profile.

For physicians, the practical implication is worth internalizing: when a patient taking creatine presents with elevated serum creatinine, the appropriate response is not alarm but investigation. Is GFR normal? Are other kidney function markers within range? Is the patient symptomatic? If the answer to those questions points to normal renal function, the elevated creatinine is most likely a creatine artifact, not a kidney problem. Stopping the creatine and re-testing will confirm this. Unnecessary referrals to nephrology and unnecessary supplement cessation have real costs — financial, relational, and in terms of athlete performance — that the evidence does not justify.

The broader principle: a single elevated biomarker should be interpreted in clinical context, not in isolation. Creatine supplementation provides exactly the kind of context that changes the interpretation of a creatinine reading. Clinicians who ask their patients about supplement use and who know creatine's metabolic effects will handle this correctly. Those who do not may generate unnecessary medical workup based on a misread biomarker.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Mainstream fact-checking outlets have not extensively addressed creatine's kidney effects specifically — this topic sits at the intersection of sports nutrition and medical misinformation, a space where the major platforms (Snopes, PolitiFact, FullFact) have relatively thin coverage. Most of the corrective literature lives in academic and medical journals rather than popular fact-checking venues.

Health journalism outlets including Examine.com (which maintains continuously updated synthesis of supplement research) and the work of sports nutrition researchers like Alan Aragon and Brad Schoenfeld have addressed the myth directly, citing the ISSN position statement and the underlying trial data. Consumer health sites like WebMD and Mayo Clinic have begun to reflect the more nuanced position — acknowledging the creatinine-creatine confusion explicitly — though their caution language sometimes still overstates risk relative to the evidence.

The clinical context is also shifting: sports medicine physicians increasingly recognize that an elevated creatinine reading in an athlete using creatine does not require further investigation if other markers are normal. This clinical norm is now reflected in sports medicine training, though it takes time to diffuse fully into general practice.

For readers encountering this claim, the most direct verification path is the ISSN 2017 position statement (Kreider et al., *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 14:18) — a peer-reviewed consensus document that represents the current scientific agreement on creatine's safety profile.
