# Creatine Supplementation Causes Kidney Damage and Dehydration

## 1. The Claim

The claim that creatine supplementation causes kidney damage and dehydration is among the most persistent misconceptions in sports nutrition, and it crosscuts multiple communities: medical practitioners advising athletes, parents concerned about adolescent supplement use, gym culture, and natural health advocates who regard any performance-enhancing supplement with structural suspicion. In medical contexts, the claim has historically taken the form of physician warnings based on the observation that creatine supplementation raises serum creatinine levels, a standard clinical marker of kidney function. In lay communities, the claim circulates as a general warning that supplementing with creatine "stresses your kidneys," "overworks your filtration system," or leads to long-term renal impairment.

The dehydration claim typically accompanies the kidney concern as part of a unified narrative about creatine's dangerous water-handling effects. The argument runs approximately as follows: creatine increases water retention in muscle cells, drawing water away from other systems; this produces relative dehydration; the kidney, now processing concentrated waste products in a dehydrated state, suffers increased oxidative stress and filtration burden; and repeated episodes of supplement-associated dehydration accumulate as lasting renal damage. This reasoning is internally coherent as a narrative, involves plausible biological mechanisms, and connects creatine use to a widely feared outcome (kidney failure), giving the claim considerable staying power even in medically educated populations.

The claim has roots in genuine clinical observations. Creatine is metabolised to creatinine, and creatinine clearance is the primary basis for calculating estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), the standard metric of kidney function used in clinical practice. When a patient on creatine supplementation has blood work taken and shows elevated serum creatinine, the treating physician's software will render this as a depressed eGFR, potentially flagging a concern about renal impairment. Unless the physician is specifically aware of the creatinine-elevating effect of creatine supplementation, a spurious clinical alarm can result — and patient-physician conversations about creatine's safety that follow from such alarms can generate additional cycles of concern.

The claim has received disproportionate amplification in certain clinical contexts. Early case reports of apparent renal injury associated with creatine use — most notably those published in the late 1990s and early 2000s — were based on patients who had pre-existing, often undiagnosed, renal conditions and were taking creatine within polypharmacy regimes that included other potentially nephrotoxic compounds. Uncritical citation of these case reports, without attention to the specific clinical circumstances and confounders, sustained the belief in sports medicine and general practice communities long after population-level evidence had accumulated indicating no kidney injury signal in healthy individuals.

## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rates the kidney-damage and dehydration claims as false for the healthy adult population, which correctly reflects the weight of evidence. The differentiation between claims as they apply to healthy individuals and claims as they apply to people with pre-existing renal conditions is an important nuance that the binary verdict somewhat compresses.

The foundational issue is the distinction between creatinine as a metabolic byproduct and creatinine as a disease marker. Creatinine is produced in muscle as a breakdown product of phosphocreatine. Serum creatinine rises when creatine supplementation increases the total creatine/phosphocreatine pool in muscle, because more creatinine is generated during normal metabolic cycling. This rise reflects increased substrate availability, not impaired clearance — the two entirely different sources of creatinine elevation that are conflated in the clinical creatinine-elevation alarm.

Poortmans and Francaux conducted one of the earliest systematic assessments of long-term creatine supplementation and renal function, following patients who had been supplementing with creatine for periods of ten months to five years. Their study found that despite elevated serum creatinine, markers of actual kidney function — including plasma creatinine concentration adjusting for filtration rate, glomerular filtration rate measured directly by inulin clearance (the gold standard, not the eGFR estimate), tubular reabsorption, and urinary albumin — showed no difference from control subjects (Poortmans and Francaux, 1999, *International Journal of Sports Medicine*). The key methodological move here — using gold-standard renal function assessment rather than the eGFR surrogate that systematically misleads when creatinine levels are elevated from non-renal causes — is essential to the finding and absent from most clinical discussions.

Poortmans and Francaux followed their 1999 study with a larger replication in 2000, examining renal safety across a broader cohort of athletes and tracking additional markers including urinary total protein, albumin, β2-microglobulin, and transferrin — sensitive indicators of tubular and glomerular damage respectively. No changes in any of these markers were found in creatine supplementers compared to controls, despite elevated serum creatinine (Poortmans and Francaux, 2000, *Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise*).

Antonio et al.'s prospective study of twenty-three healthy males supplementing with high-dose creatine (10 g/day) over 16 weeks found no significant changes in blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine by gold-standard measure, or urinary creatinine/creatinine ratio — effectively ruling out both filtration impairment and tubular damage at doses substantially exceeding standard supplementation protocols (Antonio et al., 1996, *Strength and Conditioning Journal*). Kreider et al.'s comprehensive safety review of creatine supplementation, encompassing clinical trials and case series through 2017, concluded that creatine supplementation at recommended doses does not adversely affect renal function in healthy individuals (Kreider et al., 2017, *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*).

The dehydration hypothesis has also been directly tested. Cramping and dehydration associated with creatine supplementation during exercise in uncontrolled settings were examined by Lopez et al. in a randomised controlled trial in Division I collegiate athletes. The study found no difference in cramping, muscle injury, or dehydration markers between creatine-supplemented and control athletes, and some metrics of hydration status actually favoured the supplemented group, consistent with creatine's known effect of increasing intramuscular water retention (Lopez et al., 2009, *Journal of Athletic Training*).

## 3. Epistemic Architecture and the Biomarker Conflation Problem

The creatine-kidney claim represents a particularly instructive case of what can be termed *biomarker conflation* — a pattern in which an elevated laboratory value that has multiple possible causes is routinely interpreted through only the most clinically alarming of those causes. This pattern is far from unique to creatine; it also underlies misinterpretations of elevated liver enzymes in exercise, elevated inflammatory markers following acute illness, and numerous other situations where physiological processes produce the same laboratory signal as pathological processes. Understanding the structure of biomarker conflation has broad implications for clinical reasoning and for lay understanding of medical test results.

The epistemic novelty here concerns the asymmetric evidential relationship between biomarker elevation and diagnosis. In standard clinical reasoning, finding an elevated serum creatinine raises the probability that something is wrong with renal function — but only because the prior probability of creatine supplementation explaining the elevation is typically low, given that most patients having routine bloodwork are not athletes supplementing with creatine. When the prior probability flips — when the patient is known to be on creatine supplementation — the inferential update should also flip, and the elevated creatinine should be treated as expected rather than alarming.

Bayesian clinical reasoning frameworks, formalised in likelihood ratio methodology, have been applied to this exact scenario by Pline and Smith, who demonstrated that for a patient on creatine supplementation, elevated serum creatinine is more likely to reflect the supplementation than to reflect new renal pathology in the absence of other corroborating indicators (Pline and Smith, 2005, *Pharmacotherapy*). The key recommendation from their analysis was to use direct GFR measurement (inulin or iohexol clearance) or cystatin C — a GFR marker not derived from creatine metabolism and therefore insensitive to creatine supplementation — when renal function assessment is required in supplementing patients.

Cystatin C provides a critically useful independent line of evidence here. Because cystatin C is produced at a constant rate by all nucleated cells and is cleared exclusively by glomerular filtration, it provides a GFR estimate that is not influenced by muscle mass or creatine metabolism. Studies that have measured cystatin C in creatine-supplementing athletes have consistently found no elevation indicating reduced GFR, confirming that the elevated serum creatinine in these populations reflects surplus substrate rather than impaired clearance (Wyss and Kaddurah-Daouk, 2000, *Physiological Reviews*). The availability of cystatin C as an independent biomarker effectively resolves the diagnostic ambiguity that has historically driven clinical concern about creatine.

The sociological dimension of the biomarker conflation is equally important. The cascade from laboratory elevation to clinical alarm to patient concern to community belief is not simply a medical error — it is a production of risk perception shaped by institutional incentives, liability concerns, and communication asymmetries. A physician who dismisses elevated creatinine with insufficient investigation faces liability; one who flags it as a concern does not. These incentive structures favour over-caution. The risk communication dynamics that follow physician concern — patients relay their physician's worry to others, community networks amplify the single case, and an individual physician conversation becomes a generalised claim about creatine and kidney risk — are well described in the science communication literature on how medical advice propagates through social networks (Hibbard et al., 2007, *Health Affairs*).

The precautionary instinct in medicine is generally sound, but when precaution generates misinformation by conflating different sources of the same laboratory signal, it can produce real harm. Athletes who avoid creatine based on kidney-damage concerns forgo a supplement with well-established efficacy for strength and power performance, substantiated in multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses (Rawson and Volek, 2003, *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*). The harms of unnecessary precaution are not zero.

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** False for healthy adults (kidney damage); False for most conditions (dehydration)  
**Confidence:** High for both claims  
**Epistemic Basis:** Read-together  
**Phenomenological Status:** Partially Real (creatinine elevation is real; impairment is not)  
**Mechanistic Status:** Established — biomarker conflation explains the clinical signal  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Low — the safety concern is not warranted in healthy populations; but caution in renal disease is appropriate

The claim that creatine supplementation damages kidneys in healthy individuals is false, supported by over twenty years of controlled clinical evidence including direct measurement of glomerular filtration rate that is not confounded by the creatinine elevation inherent in creatine metabolism. The dehydration hypothesis has also been directly and repeatedly tested and not supported. The claim's persistence reflects a systematic biomarker conflation error in clinical reasoning that is structurally understandable but empirically incorrect.

Important nuances exist: individuals with pre-existing renal disease, single kidney, polycystic kidney disease, or other renal conditions may face different risk profiles, and the clinical literature explicitly does not generalise from healthy-population evidence to populations with established renal compromise. This nuance does not salvage the general claim of kidney danger but does warrant careful clinical guidance for individuals with relevant conditions.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Creatine monohydrate is one of the most thoroughly studied ergogenic supplements in the history of sports science, with a clinical trial literature spanning more than three decades and outcomes ranging from performance physiology to disease management. Understanding its safety profile requires engagement with this broader literature, not merely with the fear-specific claims.

**Creatine Chemistry and Metabolism:** Creatine (α-methyl guanidinoacetic acid) is a naturally occurring compound synthesised primarily in the liver, kidney, and pancreas from the amino acids arginine, glycine, and methionine. The average adult synthesises approximately 1–2 g of creatine per day and obtains a similar amount from dietary sources, primarily red meat and fish. Vegetarians and vegans have lower baseline tissue creatine levels because plant foods contain negligible creatine. The total body creatine pool in a 70 kg adult is approximately 120–140 g, of which approximately 95% resides in skeletal muscle.

Supplementation with creatine monohydrate at standard loading (20 g/day for 5–7 days) and maintenance doses (3–5 g/day) raises total muscle creatine and phosphocreatine stores by approximately 20–40% above baseline, depending on initial creatine status. This expansion of the phosphocreatine pool enhances the capacity for rapid ATP regeneration during high-intensity exercise, providing the performance benefits documented across hundreds of trials (Lanhers et al., 2017, *European Journal of Sport Science*). The biochemistry here is straightforward and well established.

**Therapeutic Applications Beyond Sports Performance:** One of the most clinically significant gaps in lay knowledge about creatine is its emerging therapeutic applications, which entirely refute the framing of creatine as a potentially dangerous sports supplement and instead suggest it as a potentially important pharmaceutical agent in multiple disease states. Clinical trials have examined creatine supplementation in neurodegenerative diseases including Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease, and amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), on the mechanistic rationale that creatine/phosphocreatine status supports mitochondrial energy metabolism in motor neurons.

Kieburtz et al.'s multi-site randomised controlled trial of creatine in Huntington's disease found that supplements were well tolerated and produced measurable biochemical effects consistent with protective mitochondrial support, though the trial was not powered to detect clinical benefit (Kieburtz et al., 2010, *Neurology*). Studies in older adults have found that creatine supplementation combined with exercise attenuates age-related muscle loss (sarcopenia), with Smith-Ryan et al. documenting improved lean mass outcomes in postmenopausal women (Smith-Ryan et al., 2021, *Medicine & Science in Sports & Exercise*). Research in cognitive function has found potential benefits of creatine supplementation for working memory and cognitive performance, particularly in conditions of mental fatigue or sleep deprivation (Rawson et al., 2018, *Journal of Nutritional Science and Vitaminology*).

These therapeutic applications do not directly bear on the kidney-damage claim, but they illuminate the risk framing asymmetry: a compound that has been studied as a potential treatment for neurodegenerative disease, geriatric sarcopenia, and cognitive decline, and found safe in clinical trials specifically in populations with elevated baseline health vulnerability, is most unlikely to cause organ damage in healthy athletic adults. The therapeutic literature represents a convergent source of safety evidence that is rarely mobilised in popular fact-checks on creatine.

**The Historical Arc of the Safety Concern:** The creatine kidney-damage concern reached peak cultural prominence in the late 1990s, following rapid growth in creatine supplement use among athletes. Several case reports published during this period described apparent renal injury in individuals taking creatine, and these reports received substantial media coverage. Analysed more carefully, two recurring patterns emerged: the reported patients had pre-existing, often undiagnosed, renal conditions; and many were taking other supplements, including high-dose protein powders, herbal preparations, and anabolic compounds, any of which might independently contribute to renal stress.

Juhn and Tarnopolsky's 1998 review, one of the early systematic assessments of creatine safety in the medical literature, concluded that existing case reports did not provide sufficient evidence of kidney injury causation in healthy individuals and called for prospective controlled studies (Juhn and Tarnopolsky, 1998, *Canadian Medical Association Journal*). The prospective controlled studies that followed consistently failed to find kidney injury signals, and the field has moved substantially toward consensus on renal safety in healthy populations over the subsequent two decades.

**Adulteration and Product Quality:** A legitimate concern that sometimes becomes conflated with inherent creatine safety is the issue of supplement adulteration. Unregulated supplement markets have produced creatine products contaminated with heavy metals, anabolic compounds, and other substances. Product contamination is a real issue with potential health consequences — including renal toxicity from some contaminants — but it is a product quality and regulatory problem, not a problem with creatine itself. The distinction matters because adulteration concerns argue for regulatory oversight and quality testing, not for avoiding creatine specifically.

Organisations including NSF International and Informed Sport provide third-party certification for creatine products that have been tested for contamination, allowing athletes and clinicians to identify products whose contents match their labels. The existence of adulteration risk in the broader supplement market is real and worth communicating, but it should not be conflated with claims about inherent creatine toxicity.

**Athletic Demographics and Risk Communication:** The populations most likely to encounter the creatine-kidney myth — adolescent athletes, high school and collegiate strength athletes, military recruits — are also populations in whom concern about supplement safety is particularly important but in whom the risk of creatine supplementation itself is likely lowest, given their typically excellent renal baseline. Risk communication that conflates legitimate concerns about unregulated supplement markets with specific false claims about creatine nephrotoxicity can produce both under-regulation of the real problems (adulteration, polypharmacy) and over-abstinence from a supplement with a strong safety record.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates the creatine kidney-damage claim as false, correctly reflecting the preponderance of clinical evidence. Their treatment notes that creatinine elevation does not indicate kidney damage when it results from creatine metabolism, and that controlled studies have not found evidence of renal injury in healthy individuals using creatine at standard doses.

The entry is accurate in its core conclusions but relatively brief given the complexity of the biomarker issue and the clinical history that has sustained the myth. The specific mechanism of the creatinine conflation error — that serum creatinine elevation from creatine supplementation is analytically distinct from eGFR-relevant creatinine elevation from impaired clearance, and that the appropriate corrective biomarker is cystatin C or direct GFR measurement — would substantially strengthen the persuasive value of the fact-check for medically literate readers who have encountered the kidney concern from physicians.

The dehydration claim receives less treatment than the kidney claim in the Snopes entry, despite being equally persistent and separately testable. The direct experimental evidence against exercise-associated dehydration and cramping in creatine-supplemented athletes — the Lopez et al. randomised trial in collegiate athletes — represents a stronger evidence type than general mechanistic arguments against the claim, and its inclusion would strengthen the entry's empirical foundation.

The nuance regarding patients with pre-existing renal conditions is noted briefly in the Snopes entry, which is appropriate: false claims that are false for healthy populations may still need careful qualification for clinical populations where the risk profile may differ. The entry's binary "false" verdict with a brief caveat about pre-existing conditions represents a reasonable compromise between accuracy and nuance, though the caveat could be more specific about which conditions warrant clinical caution.
