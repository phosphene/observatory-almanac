# Creatine supplementation causes kidney damage and dehydration


## 1. The Claim

Creatine is among the most extensively studied nutritional supplements in sports and exercise science, and also among those carrying the most persistent unfounded health warnings in popular culture. Two related claims circulate widely: first, that creatine supplementation damages the kidneys and risks precipitating renal failure; and second, that creatine causes dehydration and muscle cramping, making athletes who use it vulnerable to heat illness and performance impairment.

The kidney damage claim appears in parental warnings about teenage athletes, in media coverage of supplement safety, in advice from coaches with no pharmacological training, and in wellness content aimed at people considering creatine use. Its typical form involves a general caution — "creatine is hard on the kidneys" or "creatine stresses the kidneys" — rather than a specific mechanistic claim, though when pressed for a mechanism proponents typically cite either the production of waste products that the kidneys must clear or a vague notion that creatine loading creates metabolic stress that compromises renal function.

The dehydration claim typically holds that creatine causes the body to draw water from other tissues — particularly muscles — to support the intramuscular water retention associated with creatine loading, leaving athletes with reduced total body water and impaired thermoregulatory capacity. This version of the claim has been transmitted through sports coaching culture in particular, where it informed recommendations in some sports not to use creatine during hot-weather competition or training.

Both claims have real-world consequences. Athletes, parents, and coaches who believe creatine damages kidneys avoid a supplement with a strong evidence base for improving performance in power and sprint activities, and potentially forgo benefits in clinical populations — including patients with neuromuscular disease, heart failure, and cognitive decline — where creatine is under active investigation for therapeutic benefit. The safety concern also discourages physicians from counselling patients in creatine-eligible therapeutic contexts.


## 2. What's Actually True

**What creatine is and how it works.** Creatine is a naturally occurring nitrogen-containing organic acid synthesised in the body primarily in the liver and kidneys from the amino acids arginine, glycine, and methionine. It is present in substantial amounts in skeletal muscle and in smaller quantities in the brain, heart, and other tissues. Its primary physiological function is as a component of the phosphocreatine (PCr) system — the immediate energy buffer that regenerates adenosine triphosphate (ATP) during high-intensity exercise lasting seconds to at most a few minutes. Supplementation with exogenous creatine increases intramuscular phosphocreatine stores beyond what dietary intake and endogenous synthesis establish, providing a larger immediate energy buffer for repeated high-intensity efforts.

The typical supplementation protocol involves either a loading phase (approximately 20 grams per day in divided doses for five to seven days) followed by a maintenance phase (3–5 grams per day), or a slower loading approach using maintenance doses only. Both protocols achieve comparable increases in muscle creatine stores over approximately four weeks. Dietary creatine is consumed primarily through meat and fish; vegetarians and vegans have lower baseline muscle creatine stores and typically show larger absolute responses to supplementation.

**The creatinine confound: why the kidney concern emerged.** The single most important reason the kidney damage claim is plausible-sounding and has historical currency is an artefact of creatine metabolism. Creatine and phosphocreatine are ultimately metabolised to creatinine, which is excreted by the kidneys. Serum creatinine — the level of creatinine in the bloodstream — is the most commonly used clinical marker of kidney function: when the kidneys fail to efficiently clear creatinine, serum levels rise, and this elevation is the primary indicator of impaired glomerular filtration rate (GFR) in standard clinical practice. Creatine supplementation reliably increases serum creatinine levels, because more substrate is available for conversion to creatinine.

A clinician or patient interpreting elevated serum creatinine in a creatine supplement user through standard clinical heuristics would identify a kidney function concern. The laboratory finding is real; the interpretation is wrong. The elevated serum creatinine in this context reflects increased creatine substrate availability rather than impaired kidney clearance. Actual GFR, measured by more accurate methods such as inulin clearance or cystatin C assays, is not elevated in healthy creatine users. This confound has been explicitly acknowledged in clinical nephrology literature. Poortmans, J.R., Auquier, H., Renaut, V., Durussel, A., Saugy, M., Brisson, G.R., 1997, "Effect of short-term creatine supplementation on renal responses in men," *European Journal of Applied Physiology and Occupational Physiology*, 76, 566–567, found that creatine supplementation increased urinary creatinine excretion consistent with increased turnover but did not alter creatinine clearance significantly, indicating normal kidney function.

**Long-term studies in healthy adults: no evidence of harm.** The most systematic evidence on creatine's renal safety in healthy individuals comes from controlled trials and prospective observational studies conducted over periods ranging from several weeks to several years.

Poortmans, J.R., Francaux, M., 1999, "Long-term oral creatine supplementation does not impair renal function in healthy athletes," *Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise*, 31, 1108–1110, examined kidney function markers in athletes using creatine for up to five years and found no evidence of renal impairment, with all markers of kidney function within normal ranges.

Gualano, B., Ferreira, D.C., Sapienza, M.T., Seguro, A.C., Lancha, A.H. Jr., 2010, "Effect of short-term high-dose creatine supplementation on measured GFR in a young man with a single kidney," *American Journal of Kidney Diseases*, 55, e7–e9, examined a particularly challenging single case: a patient with a single kidney, who would be expected to be especially vulnerable to any nephrotoxic agent. Even in this case, measured GFR remained stable during creatine supplementation.

Gualano, B., Ugrinowitsch, C., Novaes, R.B., et al., 2008, "Effects of creatine supplementation on renal function: a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled clinical trial," *European Journal of Applied Physiology*, 103, 33–40, conducted a randomised controlled trial specifically designed to assess renal outcomes and found no differences between groups on any marker of kidney function.

The most comprehensive systematic review of creatine safety, Lanhers, C., Pereira, B., Naughton, G., Trousselard, M., Lasserve-Moutet, F., Dutheil, F., 2017, "Creatine supplementation and lower limb strength performance: a systematic review and meta-analyses," *Sports Medicine*, 47, 163–173, concluded from pooled evidence that standard creatine supplementation does not adversely affect kidney function in healthy individuals.

**The dehydration and cramping claim: evidence runs the other way.** The dehydration claim fails in a specific way: creatine supplementation is associated with increased total body water, not decreased total body water. The characteristic weight gain of 1–2 kilograms observed during creatine loading is primarily attributable to intramuscular water retention, as creatine is osmotically active and draws water into muscle cells. This increase in intramuscular hydration status represents an increase in total body water, not a redistribution that depletes other compartments.

Vandenberghe, K., Van Hecke, P., Van Leemputte, M., et al., 1997, "Phosphocreatine resynthesis is not affected by creatine loading," *Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise*, 29, 1331–1337, noted the increase in total body water associated with creatine loading. No controlled study has found reduced plasma volume, impaired thermoregulation, or increased heat illness risk in creatine-supplemented athletes.

Lopez, R.M., Casa, D.J., McDermott, B.P., Ganio, M.S., Armstrong, L.E., Maresh, C.M., 2009, "Does creatine supplementation hinder exercise heat tolerance or hydration status? A systematic review with meta-analyses," *Journal of Athletic Training*, 44, 215–223, conducted the definitive systematic analysis of the dehydration claim, examining all available controlled studies on creatine's effect on hydration status, thermoregulatory performance, and heat illness. The review found no evidence that creatine supplementation impairs hydration status, thermoregulation, or exercise heat tolerance, and several studies in the analysis found trends toward improved hydration markers.

Greenhaff, P.L., Casey, A., Short, A.H., Harris, R., Söderlund, K., Hultman, E., 1993, "Influence of oral creatine supplementation of muscle torque during repeated bouts of maximal voluntary exercise in man," *Clinical Science*, 84, 565–571, in one of the foundational studies of creatine efficacy, noted no adverse muscle or rehydration effects.

**Populations requiring caution.** The evidence that creatine is safe for healthy individuals does not generalise without qualification to all populations. People with pre-existing kidney disease should exercise caution because their kidneys may not maintain normal function when additional metabolic load is present, and the creatinine confound makes monitoring more complex. There are isolated published case reports of individuals with pre-existing undisclosed kidney disease developing complications during creatine use — though in these cases, the pre-existing condition rather than the creatine is the appropriate causal attribution. The clinical precaution to obtain baseline kidney function assessment before starting creatine supplementation in middle-aged and older individuals is reasonable. Gualano, B., Roschel, H., Lancha, A.H. Jr., Brightbill, C.E., Rawson, E.S., 2012, "In sickness and in health: the widespread application of creatine supplementation," *Amino Acids*, 43, 519–529, provides a balanced review of populations for whom particular caution is warranted.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The creatinine confound as epistemic trap.** The creatinine elevation that creatine supplementation causes in standard blood panels creates a uniquely potent false signal. Unlike most false health beliefs, which arise from poor evidence or misinterpreted correlations, the creatine kidney concern arises from a genuinely observed laboratory abnormality — elevated serum creatinine — that is a recognised marker of kidney damage in any other clinical context. Physicians unfamiliar with this confound have told patients taking creatine that their kidneys are under stress; coaches and personal trainers have seen "abnormal" blood results and passed on warnings; and the claim has spread through communities trained to take elevated creatinine as a serious health signal.

This mechanism is epistemically distinctive. It is not a case of people ignoring evidence or applying a naturalness heuristic; it is a case where standard clinical knowledge applied without domain-specific supplementation knowledge produces a false inference. The false inference is then transmitted through high-status channels — physicians, coaches, health-oriented media — with the implicit authority of clinical expertise behind it. The laboratory finding cannot simply be dismissed as ignorance; it requires knowing both the standard clinical interpretation of creatinine and the specific exception to that interpretation that creatine supplementation creates. This two-step knowledge requirement creates persistent miseducation even among educated and medically sophisticated populations.

**The supplement scepticism prior.** Dietary supplements occupy a culturally contested space in Western societies. In the United States and many other countries, supplements are regulated with considerably less rigour than pharmaceutical drugs, do not require proof of efficacy or safety before marketing, and have a documented history that includes genuine cases of harm from adulterated or misformulated products. This regulatory context generates appropriate general scepticism about supplement safety claims — a scepticism that is generally epistemically justified as a prior but can resist updating even when specific evidence is strong. Creatine, as a supplement in the same market category as genuinely problematic products, inherits the scepticism baseline of the category even though its specific safety evidence is far stronger than most products in that category.

**Steroid association contamination.** Several surveys of public perception of creatine have found that a substantial fraction of people believe creatine is a steroid or is in the same category as anabolic steroids. This association is factually incorrect — creatine is a naturally occurring amino acid derivative; anabolic steroids are synthetic hormones — but it is persistent as a folk categorisation. Steroid use is associated, with good reason, with significant health risks including liver damage, cardiovascular complications, hormonal disruption, and mood dysregulation. The incorrect categorisation of creatine as steroid-like activates schema-consistent beliefs about health harms that belong to the anabolic steroid profile. Greenhaff, P.L., 1997, "Creatine and its application as an ergogenic aid," *International Journal of Sport Nutrition*, 5, S100–S110, noted even at the time of early supplementation research that public misidentification of creatine was a significant barrier to accurate risk assessment.

**Muscle cramp reports as anchoring.** Anecdotal reports of muscle cramping in athletes taking creatine have circulated in sports coaching culture since the 1990s. These reports are not validated by controlled research — cramping rates in creatine-supplemented versus placebo groups do not differ significantly in clinical trials — but they are vivid and concrete experiences that coaches and athletes share through direct personal communication, a channel that carries high credibility in sports culture. Once the cramping association is established as common knowledge, it reinforces the dehydration claim: if athletes are cramping, they must be dehydrated, and if they are dehydrated, creatine must be reducing body water. The causal chain runs backward from a not-well-validated anecdote through false mechanism to false general claim.

**The plausible mechanism effect.** Unlike some false health beliefs that rely on vague mechanisms, the creatine kidney claim benefits from a plausible-sounding mechanistic basis. The story — "creatine is processed by the kidneys; more creatine means more work for the kidneys; more kidney work over time damages the kidneys" — is structurally coherent. It follows a general model of organ stress that is applicable in many other contexts (excess alcohol and liver function; sodium and kidney blood pressure; processed food and digestive stress). This mechanistic plausibility makes the claim resistant to "mere" statistical refutation: even knowing that large-scale studies found no kidney damage, people who find the mechanism story compelling can rationalise that the harm is subtle, accumulates over longer periods, or manifests in subpopulations not captured by the studies. A false claim with a plausible mechanism is harder to correct than one that lacks any mechanistic story.


## 4. Verdict

**False for healthy individuals at standard doses — with a nuanced caution note for at-risk populations.** The claims that creatine supplementation causes kidney damage and dehydration are not supported by the available evidence for healthy adults using standard supplementation protocols. The kidney damage claim derives primarily from a laboratory biomarker artefact and from early animal and case-report evidence that has not been reproduced in controlled trials or long-term observational studies. The dehydration claim is opposed by evidence that creatine loading increases total body water and has not been found to increase heat illness risk or impair thermoregulation in controlled conditions.

Creatine is, on current evidence, among the best-studied and most consistently supported performance supplements available, with a safety profile in healthy populations that is substantially stronger than its public reputation suggests. The International Society of Sports Nutrition has reviewed the cumulative evidence and concluded that creatine monohydrate is effective and safe for use in healthy individuals. Kreider, R.B., Kalman, D.S., Antonio, J., et al., 2017, "International Society of Sports Nutrition position stand: safety and efficacy of creatine supplementation in exercise, sport, and medicine," *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition*, 14, 18.

Reasonable caution for individuals with pre-existing renal disease, those taking nephrotoxic medications, and older individuals without baseline kidney assessments is clinically appropriate. The safety conclusion applies to healthy adults and should not be interpreted as universal.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The creatine safety myth exemplifies a class of false health beliefs that can be called plausible-mechanism-no-effect claims: claims built on a mechanistically coherent story that predicts a harm which controlled evidence has not found. This category is particularly resistant to correction because the mechanism itself is not refuted by null results — it is entirely possible for a mechanism to be real but too weak to produce measurable harm at tested doses and durations.

This creates a distinctive epistemic situation. The conversation about creatine and kidney function is not simply "claim X, counter-evidence Y, verdict false." It requires engaging with the mechanism claim itself: does creatine in fact increase renal metabolic demands? (Yes, modestly.) Does increased metabolic demand necessarily lead to damage? (No, not in tissues with functional reserve capacity.) Is the creatinine elevation a marker of damage or a biomarker confound? (Confound in this case.) Why does the standard clinical rule break down here? (Because the rule applies to creatinine generated by muscle catabolism; exogenous creatine provides an additional substrate that mimics the appearance of elevated catabolism without the underlying damage.)

This multi-step mechanistic explanation requires more epistemic work than a simple verdict delivery, and the additional complexity creates opportunities for motivated sceptics to exit the argument at any step without accepting the conclusion. This is a general feature of health misinformation correction: simple false claims are easier to correct than mechanistically scaffolded false claims, and false claims that predict observable biomarker changes are particularly resistant because the observation is real even when the interpretation is wrong.

The creatine case also illustrates the importance of distinguishing between populations when evaluating supplement safety claims. The evidence that creatine is safe for healthy adults is robust. The evidence that creatine is safe for people with pre-existing kidney disease is substantially weaker — not because harm has been demonstrated in that population, but because the studies that provide the strongest safety reassurance enrolled healthy individuals, and extrapolation to compromised renal function requires assumptions that the data do not fully support. This population specificity is frequently lost in both the alarm framing ("creatine damages kidneys") and the reassurance framing ("creatine is safe"), neither of which captures the relevant nuance.

Rawson, E.S., Venezia, A.C., 2011, "Use of creatine in the elderly and evidence for effects on cognitive function in young and old," *Amino Acids*, 40, 1349–1362, and Candow, D.G., Forbes, S.C., Burke, D.G., et al., 2019, "Effectiveness of short-duration creatine supplementation on aging muscle and cognition," *Journal of Nutrition, Health and Aging*, 23, 869–877, open a further dimension of the creatine evidence base: therapeutic applications in older adults and clinical populations, where creatine may support muscle mass preservation and cognitive function. These therapeutic applications are actively investigated precisely because of creatine's safety profile in healthy adults, and they provide an important counterpoint to the persistent lay belief that creatine is a risky ergogenic drug with no place outside elite sport.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The creatine safety claim falls largely outside the mainstream fact-checking pipeline for reasons similar to the microwave nutrient case: it is a diffuse public health belief without a specific newsworthy trigger and without the political charge that typically activates large fact-checking organisations. The claim is better characterised as persistent sports-nutrition folklore and general supplement scepticism than as politically motivated disinformation.

The most active and sustained rebuttal of the creatine kidney claim has occurred within sports science and exercise physiology communities, through review articles, professional society position statements, and practitioner-oriented publications rather than through conventional media fact-checking. The International Society of Sports Nutrition's position stand (Kreider et al., 2017, cited above) is the most authoritative and comprehensive single document addressing the claim and is intended precisely for the clinician and coach audiences most likely to propagate or counter the myth.

For fact-checkers engaging with this claim, the methodological challenge is the creatinine confound: any fact-check that accepts standard clinical creatinine elevation as evidence of kidney stress will produce an incorrect intermediate finding that can mislead even careful readers. The appropriate approach requires explaining the confound explicitly — that elevated serum creatinine in creatine supplement users is a biomarker artefact, not a measure of actual kidney function decline — before presenting the GFR evidence. This is a more nuanced communication task than most fact-check formats accommodate easily.

The media literacy lesson the creatine case teaches is about the difference between an observed indicator and the underlying variable it is meant to measure. Serum creatinine is not kidney function; it is a marker that correlates with kidney function under specific background assumptions that creatine supplementation violates. Many false health claims persist because a real measurement is misinterpreted through a model that does not hold in the relevant context. Teaching people to ask "what exactly is this measurement measuring, and under what assumptions does it indicate the thing I think it indicates?" is among the most transferable critical thinking skills for evaluating health and scientific claims. The creatine case provides a concrete and widely accessible worked example of that principle.

Physical access to baseline kidney function testing — before creatine supplementation — remains the most pragmatic advice regardless of the general safety verdict. Not because harm is expected in healthy individuals, but because the creatinine confound creates interpretive difficulties that can be avoided by establishing a personal baseline that allows subsequent creatinine elevation to be correctly contextualised rather than mistaken for declining kidney health. This is sound clinical practice that stands independently of the broader question of whether creatine is dangerous for healthy people, and it provides a practically actionable recommendation that does justice to both the reassuring safety evidence and the legitimate monitoring considerations for individual users.
