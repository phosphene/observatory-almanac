# Creatine Supplementation Causes Kidney Damage in Healthy People

## 1. The Claim

Creatine supplementation, widely used by athletes and fitness enthusiasts to enhance performance and muscle mass, causes kidney damage. According to this claim, taking creatine stresses or harms the kidneys over time, potentially leading to impaired kidney function, chronic kidney disease, or more acute forms of kidney injury. Proponents of this belief often advise against creatine use for anyone concerned about long-term health, suggesting the performance benefits come at an unacceptable physiological cost. The claim has circulated in gyms, health forums, medical waiting rooms, and popular media for decades, becoming one of the most persistent and anxiety-inducing misconceptions in the world of sports nutrition.

The claim takes several forms. Some versions are moderate: creatine "puts stress on the kidneys" and should be cycled, or used only short-term. Others are more alarming: creatine "destroys" kidney function, leads to renal failure, or creates a chronic toxic burden that accumulates invisibly until damage is done. A third variant is more targeted: creatine may be safe for most people, but it is particularly dangerous for those who already have any kidney vulnerability whatsoever. Each version of the claim carries different implications for who should avoid creatine, for how long, and under what conditions.

The persistence of this claim matters because creatine monohydrate is among the most extensively studied nutritional supplements ever evaluated in human clinical trials. It is also one of the most widely consumed. Understanding what the research actually says — and why the concern arose in the first place — is essential for anyone navigating the enormous, often confusing landscape of sports nutrition advice.

## 2. What's Actually True

The scientific consensus, supported by decades of research and systematic reviews, is that creatine supplementation at recommended doses does **not** cause kidney damage in healthy individuals. This consensus is robust, though not without nuance.

### The foundational research

Two of the most cited researchers on creatine safety are Poortmans and Francaux, Belgian exercise scientists who published extensively on creatine's renal effects beginning in the late 1990s. Their 1999 study in *Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise* examined long-term creatine supplementation in trained athletes and found no adverse effects on kidney function as measured by standard biomarkers — glomerular filtration rate (GFR), creatinine clearance, and urinary protein. This was significant because it directly addressed the emerging concern in the sports medicine community that creatine might overwork renal filtration systems.

They followed this with additional investigations, including one of the most comprehensive early assessments of creatine's effects on renal markers over varied supplementation periods. Their conclusion, echoed in reviews they published into the 2000s, was consistent: in healthy individuals, there is no evidence that therapeutic doses of creatine impair kidney function.

### The JISSN position stand

One of the most authoritative recent summaries of creatine safety comes from the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* (JISSN). The 2017 position stand by Kreider et al. — co-authored by more than a dozen leading exercise scientists and nutrition researchers — evaluated hundreds of studies and concluded that creatine monohydrate is the most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement currently available for athletes seeking to increase high-intensity exercise capacity and lean body mass. Crucially, on the question of safety, the position stand states:

> "Short- and long-term supplementation (up to 30 g/day for 5 years) is safe and well-tolerated in healthy individuals."

The review specifically addressed renal concerns, noting that creatinine clearance and other markers of kidney health do not deteriorate in healthy subjects supplementing with creatine at standard doses. This comprehensive synthesis of the evidence base represents the current scientific consensus.

### The American College of Sports Medicine

The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM), one of the most respected bodies in exercise medicine, has reviewed creatine safety and concluded that it is safe for healthy adults when used as directed. Institutional endorsement from the ACSM carries significant weight in evaluating claims about exercise-related supplements, given the organization's rigorous standards for evidence evaluation.

### Why the claim has a kernel of truth: the creatinine problem

Here is where the genuine complexity enters. Creatine is metabolized in the body and converted — among other products — to **creatinine**, a waste product that is excreted by the kidneys. Creatinine levels in the blood are one of the primary biomarkers clinicians use to assess kidney function: high creatinine generally signals impaired renal clearance.

When someone takes creatine supplements, their blood creatinine levels rise — not because their kidneys are failing, but because there is simply more creatine substrate being metabolized and converted to creatinine. The kidneys are doing their job; there is just more of the product to clear. This creates an artifact in standard kidney function panels that can appear alarming to an uninformed reader or a clinician unfamiliar with creatine supplementation.

Multiple studies have confirmed this phenomenon. Poortmans and Francaux (1999) documented it clearly: creatine supplementation raises serum creatinine, but when kidney function is assessed by more sensitive and direct measures — specifically, creatinine clearance tests that account for elevated input, GFR measurement, and urinary biomarkers — no functional impairment is found.

This is a critical distinction that is frequently lost in popular communication. The number that looks bad on the lab report is not measuring what people assume it is measuring in the context of creatine use. It is analogous to misinterpreting elevated glucose readings after eating a meal as evidence of diabetes, without accounting for the ordinary metabolic response to food intake.

### What the case reports actually show

There are case reports in the medical literature of kidney dysfunction associated with creatine use. These reports have contributed to the persistence of the concern and deserve honest examination.

The most frequently cited is a 1998 case report by Pritchard and Kalra in *The Lancet*, describing a 25-year-old man with a single kidney who experienced progressive kidney impairment after beginning creatine supplementation. This is a single case with a highly specific risk factor — having only one kidney significantly reduces renal reserve and makes the patient far more vulnerable to any substance that increases kidney workload.

Other case reports involve patients with pre-existing chronic kidney disease, polycystic kidney disease, or other conditions that compromise renal function. In nearly every case, there is an underlying vulnerability that would have warranted caution regardless of creatine use.

The existence of these case reports does not demonstrate that creatine is harmful to healthy kidneys. It demonstrates — as nearly all evidence in medicine demonstrates — that substances must be understood in context, and that people with compromised kidney function should consult a physician before supplementing.

### The pre-existing condition divide

This is the genuine source of legitimate debate in the literature. The consensus that creatine is safe for healthy people is strong. The question of how creatine affects people with **pre-existing kidney vulnerabilities** is more contested.

Some nephrologists advise caution for patients with any degree of chronic kidney disease, arguing that the increased creatinine load from supplementation creates additional filtration burden at a time when reserve capacity is already reduced. Others argue that the evidence for harm even in this population is insufficient to justify a blanket prohibition.

This debate among specialists is real and unresolved. But it is a debate about a specific clinical subpopulation — patients with compromised renal function. It is not a debate about healthy people, and conflating the two is a common source of confusion in popular discussions of creatine safety.

## 3. Why People Believe This

Several interlocking factors explain the remarkable persistence of this claim despite the weight of evidence against it.

### The creatinine-creatine name confusion

The names are similar enough to create a persistent folk-epidemiology. Creatine is a supplement. Creatinine is a metabolic waste product measured to assess kidney function. Many people, upon learning that creatine raises creatinine, assume a direct and obvious causal story: the supplement overloads the kidneys, which are now producing more waste, which means the kidneys are being damaged. This reasoning is intuitive, phonologically supported by the near-identical names, and wrong.

The actual mechanism — that creatine supplementation increases creatinine as a metabolic byproduct without impairing kidney filtration — requires a level of biochemical granularity that most consumers and many non-specialist health communicators have not encountered. In the absence of that knowledge, the simpler story fills the gap.

### Physician caution and precautionary advising

Many patients report being warned away from creatine by their general practitioners or family medicine physicians. This is not necessarily because GPs have evaluated the evidence and concluded creatine is harmful; it more often reflects a general precautionary instinct combined with the observation that the patient's blood creatinine level has risen since starting supplementation.

A physician who sees elevated creatinine on a lab panel, without knowing the patient has started creatine supplementation, might appropriately flag it as a concern. A physician who does know about the supplementation but is unfamiliar with the creatine-creatinine dynamic may still flag it out of caution. Neither response is irrational given incomplete information, but both contribute to a cultural impression — transmitted from patient to patient — that creatine is something doctors are worried about.

### The "natural skepticism" of unfamiliar supplements

Creatine began widespread use in gyms in the early 1990s, partly driven by the 1992 Barcelona Olympics, after which several high-profile athletes publicly credited creatine for performance gains. For a public and a medical community that had not yet encountered significant clinical data, treating the supplement with suspicion was a rational default position. That precautionary stance calcified into folk belief even as the evidence base accumulated over the following decades.

### Survivorship bias in case report communication

Medical case reports are by nature unusual events — they document atypical outcomes because that is what makes them worth reporting. A study showing that healthy people took creatine for two years with no adverse kidney effects is not a publishable case report; it is a data point in a clinical trial. A patient with pre-existing kidney disease who develops worsening function after starting creatine is a case report.

This creates a publication structure in which the documented events associated with creatine and kidney problems are cases involving unusual circumstances, but those cases are more memorable and emotionally vivid than the large-scale trial data. People remember the alarming exception.

### Social diffusion in gym culture

Gym culture is a powerful vector for health misinformation. Advice circulates peer-to-peer with significant authority — based on visible results, experienced presence, and the plausibility of claims that fit existing frameworks. In that culture, "creatine is hard on your kidneys" has been a standard warning for thirty years. It is transmitted not through journals but through word-of-mouth from experienced lifters who themselves received it from their predecessors, who may have been at the frontier of creatine use when evidence was thinner.

### Media coverage of isolated incidents

When a young athlete experiences kidney problems and has been taking creatine, the creatine is news. The background rate of kidney problems in athletes not taking creatine, the presence of other risk factors, the absence of established causality — these are not news. The resulting media environment makes creatine appear more closely associated with kidney damage than the evidence supports.

## 4. Verdict

**Mostly false for healthy people. Genuinely uncertain for those with pre-existing kidney conditions.**

The claim that creatine supplementation causes kidney damage in healthy people is not supported by the current body of evidence. The consensus across multiple systematic reviews, position stands from major sports nutrition bodies, and decades of clinical research is that creatine monohydrate at recommended doses (typically 3–5 grams per day, or higher loading doses of 20 g/day for 5–7 days) does not impair kidney function in people with healthy kidneys.

The mechanism behind the widespread concern — elevated blood creatinine levels in creatine users — does not represent kidney damage. It represents an increase in a metabolic byproduct that happens to share a name and a standard measurement panel with a genuine kidney marker. More sensitive assessments of kidney function consistently fail to find evidence of harm.

Where legitimate caution applies is in people with pre-existing kidney disease or significantly compromised renal function. For those individuals, the evidence is not as reassuring, expert opinion is divided, and consultation with a nephrologist before supplementing is genuinely warranted. The claim, as typically stated, confuses the experience of this subpopulation with a general danger for everyone.

**EARC classification: C (Contested consensus).** The general safety consensus is real and well-supported, but contested at the margins — specifically regarding long-term use, high doses, and vulnerable populations — by a subset of specialists and case report literature that raises questions the controlled trial evidence has not fully resolved for every individual circumstance.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### The anatomy of a persistent health myth

The creatine-kidney story is instructive as a case study in how health misinformation persists despite contrary evidence. It involves several features that are common to long-lived health myths:

**A mechanistic story with surface plausibility.** People take creatine → creatinine levels rise → creatinine is used to measure kidney function → therefore the kidneys are being damaged. Each link in the chain sounds reasonable on its own; the chain as a whole leads to a wrong conclusion.

**Institutional momentum.** Once a precautionary stance is adopted by practicing physicians, it tends to persist even when evidence accumulates against it. Changing established clinical advice requires more than new data — it requires the new data to penetrate professional education, continuing medical education, and the informal communication networks through which clinical wisdom is transmitted.

**A real phenomenon being overextended.** Creatine does appear to pose risks for people with pre-existing kidney disease. That real risk, in a small subpopulation, has been generalized to a claim about healthy people. This generalization is wrong but understandable — most precautionary advice in medicine does arise from genuine risks in specific contexts.

### The broader challenge of supplement science

Creatine represents one end of the evidence quality spectrum for nutritional supplements. It has been studied more extensively than most supplements, in better-designed trials, with longer follow-up periods. The verdict on creatine — at least for kidney effects in healthy people — is about as clear as supplement science gets.

Most supplements are not this well studied. The creatine controversy illustrates how even a well-studied supplement can carry persistent false associations. For less studied supplements, the epistemic problem is much worse: we often cannot distinguish between "this is safe" and "we haven't studied it enough to know what's dangerous."

### What the debate reveals about risk communication

One of the lessons of the creatine story is the difficulty of communicating nuanced risk accurately across populations with different levels of background knowledge. The statement "creatine is safe for healthy people" requires a simultaneous understanding of what "healthy" means in this context (primarily: normal kidney function), what "safe" means in terms of evidence standards, and why elevated creatinine on a lab panel doesn't mean what it usually means for someone supplementing with creatine.

That constellation of knowledge is not unreasonable to expect from a sports medicine specialist. It is less reasonable to expect from the general public or even from non-specialist physicians. The gap between what the evidence says and what gets communicated to patients is partly a problem of health literacy and partly a structural problem of how risk information travels through the medical system.

### Long-term use and evolving science

One area where the confidence in creatine's safety is somewhat lower is in truly long-term use over decades. The longest controlled studies have run for a few years at most. Most of the reassuring data comes from populations supplementing over months to a few years. The claim that creatine is safe "for life" is harder to make from the existing evidence than the claim that it is safe for the durations studied.

This is not a reason to believe creatine is harmful over the long term; it is a reason to maintain epistemic humility about claims at the edges of what the evidence actually covers. Sports nutrition, like most areas of health research, would benefit from longer-term natural history studies.

### Performance without paranoia

Creatine remains, as the JISSN 2017 position stand concludes, the most well-evidenced performance-enhancing supplement available to recreational and competitive athletes. Its mechanism is well understood (increasing phosphocreatine availability in muscle tissue, thereby enhancing ATP regeneration during short-duration, high-intensity efforts), its ergogenic effects are reproducible, and its safety profile in healthy people is among the cleanest in nutrition science.

The unnecessary avoidance of creatine by healthy athletes who have absorbed the kidney-damage myth represents a real cost — people forgoing a genuinely effective and well-characterized performance aid because of a misunderstanding that has been correctable by the evidence for over two decades.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The creatine-kidney claim occupies an interesting position in the fact-checking ecosystem. Snopes has not formally addressed it as a distinct claim (as of this writing), which is notable given its prevalence. The claim tends not to attach itself to specific viral content in a way that triggers the attention of fact-checking organizations oriented toward debunking specific articles, images, or social media posts. Instead, it circulates as background folk wisdom — present everywhere, attributable nowhere, and therefore harder to catch in the specific formats that fact-checkers work best with.

Where the claim has been addressed, it has been addressed by science and medicine journalists operating in the broader fact-check-adjacent space. Publications like *Examine.com*, a research-focused supplement information resource, provide detailed assessments that align with the scientific consensus: creatine does not damage healthy kidneys, elevated creatinine is a metabolic artifact, and caution is warranted only for those with pre-existing kidney conditions.

Mainstream health journalists have addressed it with varying degrees of accuracy. Some have accurately conveyed the consensus position; others have amplified the concern by covering case reports without adequate contextualization of the broader evidence base. The latter pattern is common in health journalism: the case report is a concrete, human-interest story, while the multi-year clinical trial is abstract and statistical.

### The challenge of diffuse misinformation

Fact-checking organizations face a structural challenge with claims like this. Standard fact-check methodology works best when a specific claim is made in a specific venue by a specific actor. "Creatine destroys your kidneys" exists as ambient cultural belief rather than as a discrete, attributable claim. It cannot be assigned a source, linked to an article, or traced to an origin point in the same way that, say, a viral post claiming a celebrity died can be traced and debunked.

This diffuseness is itself a kind of protection for the belief. There is no single node to correct. The correction has to be distributed through the same peer-to-peer channels that transmitted the original claim — through gym conversations, through physicians updating their knowledge, through personal trainers taking continuing education, through health journalists learning the distinction between creatine and creatinine.

### What responsible coverage looks like

The most accurate coverage of the creatine-kidney claim reliably includes several features:

1. **Explicit distinction between creatinine (the metabolite, a lab marker) and creatine (the supplement).** This distinction is the core of the issue and should be explained, not assumed.

2. **Distinction between healthy populations and those with pre-existing kidney disease.** The consensus finding applies to the former; the more cautious recommendations apply to the latter.

3. **Reference to the systematic review literature,** particularly the Kreider et al. 2017 JISSN position stand and the Poortmans and Francaux body of work, rather than relying on case reports or anecdote.

4. **Acknowledgment of what we don't know.** Long-term studies spanning decades do not exist. Scientific humility on this point is appropriate and does not undermine the broader safety finding for the durations that have been studied.

Coverage that relies primarily on case reports, physician anecdote, or the observable lab artifact of elevated creatinine without contextualizing these against the controlled trial evidence is likely to leave readers with a more alarmed impression than the evidence warrants.

### The standard of contested consensus

This entry is classified under "contested consensus" because the scientific mainstream has arrived at a reasonably confident position — creatine is safe for healthy people — while a legitimate subset of specialists continues to urge caution, particularly for edge cases and long-term use. That is a different epistemic situation from a claim that is simply false (debunked consensus) or one where the evidence genuinely points nowhere (genuine scientific controversy). The creatine story is one where the evidence is fairly clear for the core case (healthy adults, standard doses, normal timeframes) and genuinely murkier at the edges (people with kidney vulnerabilities, very high doses, very long-term use).

Honest fact-checking in this space requires communicating that gradient clearly: the mainstream finding is strong, the edge cases are genuinely uncertain, and the alarm about kidney damage in healthy people is disproportionate to the evidence — while acknowledging that this assessment could be revised if better long-term data emerges.
