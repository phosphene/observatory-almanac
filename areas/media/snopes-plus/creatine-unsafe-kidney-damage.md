# Creatine supplements cause kidney damage and are unsafe

## 1. The Claim

Creatine supplements are dangerous for your kidneys. Taking creatine, especially over long periods or at high doses, causes kidney damage, raises creatinine levels as evidence of renal stress, and poses a serious health risk that outweighs any performance benefit. This message circulates persistently in gyms, on social media, and even in some clinical settings where physicians advise patients against taking the supplement entirely. The alarm is most commonly raised by pointing to the biochemical marker creatinine—when creatine is metabolized, creatinine is produced, and elevated creatinine in blood tests is a classic indicator of kidney dysfunction. The logic seems airtight: creatine supplement → more creatinine → kidneys under stress → kidney damage.

This claim has been reinforced by case reports of athletes experiencing kidney problems while taking creatine, by the supplement's general classification as something doctors ought to be cautious about, and by general cultural anxiety around the category of performance-enhancing substances. It has also been perpetuated by a conflation between correlation and causation: people who are physically active and supplement aggressively sometimes do develop health problems, and creatine often gets blamed.

## 2. What's Actually True

The scientific consensus, built over more than three decades of controlled research, is clear: creatine supplementation at recommended doses does not cause kidney damage in healthy individuals. This conclusion holds across short-term loading protocols, long-term maintenance use, and a range of populations including trained athletes, sedentary adults, the elderly, and children with certain metabolic disorders where creatine is used therapeutically.

The most authoritative summary of this evidence is the position stand published in 2017 by Kreider and colleagues in the *Journal of the International Society of Sports Nutrition* (JISSN). This paper reviewed decades of peer-reviewed research and concluded that creatine monohydrate is one of the most thoroughly studied and safe dietary supplements available, with no evidence of adverse effects on kidney function in healthy populations when used at recommended doses. The authors found that short-term loading (approximately 20 grams per day for 5–7 days) and long-term supplementation (3–5 grams per day for months or years) had no detrimental effect on markers of renal health such as glomerular filtration rate (GFR), serum creatinine adjusted for creatine intake, or urinary protein excretion.

Earlier foundational research arrived at the same conclusion. A study by Poortmans and Francaux published in *Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise* (2000) followed athletes who had been using creatine for an average of nearly five years. When kidney function was measured through multiple indicators—including GFR, plasma creatinine, plasma urea, urinary total protein, and albumin—no evidence of renal dysfunction emerged compared to controls. This was a critical study because it addressed the long-term use question directly, at a time when concerns were being raised about whether short-term studies were missing cumulative damage.

A 2003 study by the same authors involving 23 athletes who had taken creatine for up to five years reinforced these findings. The physiological markers all remained within normal reference ranges. The kidneys, in short, appeared entirely unaffected.

The creatinine confusion is central to understanding why this myth persists—and why it isn't entirely without basis. Creatinine is produced when creatine and creatine phosphate are metabolized by muscle tissue. A person taking creatine supplements will, almost inevitably, have elevated serum creatinine levels compared to a non-supplementing baseline. In routine blood panels, elevated creatinine is a standard red flag for kidney dysfunction—it reflects the kidney's failure to clear the waste product efficiently. The problem is that this interpretation assumes a static creatine load. When creatine supplementation is increasing the total pool of creatine in the body, more creatinine is being produced regardless of how well the kidneys are functioning. A physician who sees elevated creatinine in a patient who hasn't disclosed creatine supplementation may reasonably—but incorrectly—suspect kidney disease. This is a diagnostic artifact, not evidence of harm.

The correct interpretation requires either knowing about supplementation and adjusting reference ranges accordingly, or using more specific markers of kidney function that aren't confounded by creatine metabolism—such as cystatin C, a protein produced at a constant rate by all nucleated cells and filtered by the glomerulus, which is unaffected by muscle mass or creatine loading.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The creatine-kidney-damage belief survives because several distinct threads converge to make it seem plausible, even to medically sophisticated observers.

**The creatinine marker trap.** As explained above, creatine supplementation mechanically elevates serum creatinine. When someone has a blood panel done and their doctor sees elevated creatinine without knowing about supplementation, the natural response is concern. The doctor may advise stopping the supplement. The patient, understandably, concludes the supplement was harming their kidneys. This cycle reinforces the myth even in clinical settings without any actual kidney damage occurring.

**Genuine case reports.** A small number of case reports in the medical literature do document kidney dysfunction in individuals taking creatine. These cases are real, but they consistently involve complicating factors: pre-existing kidney disease, use of other nephrotoxic substances (including NSAIDs, which many athletes take for recovery), extreme doses well above recommended levels, or underlying conditions that predispose individuals to renal problems. Case reports are valuable for generating hypotheses, but they cannot establish causation in heterogeneous populations. The existence of a handful of cases in a global supplement market serving tens of millions of users does not constitute evidence of general harm.

**The "performance-enhancing" stigma.** Creatine is primarily marketed and perceived as a performance-enhancing supplement used by bodybuilders and strength athletes. This category carries cultural associations with excess, risk-taking, and the potential for long-term health consequences—associations informed partly by the genuine risks of anabolic steroids and other more dangerous compounds. Creatine gets bundled, in many minds, with a general category of "gym supplements you probably shouldn't take," even though its safety profile is radically different from most of that category.

**Medical conservatism and the precautionary principle.** Physicians often advise patients to discontinue supplements when there is any ambiguity about safety, particularly patients with existing health conditions. This is rational caution given that many supplements are poorly regulated and understudied. But creatine is exceptionally well-studied, and the precautionary advice—while understandable given general supplement skepticism—often isn't proportionate to the actual evidence base.

**Confounded observational data.** Athletes who develop health problems are often taking multiple supplements, training at extreme volumes, consuming high-protein diets, using NSAIDs, and living stressful lives. When kidney issues emerge in this population, isolating creatine as the cause is methodologically difficult, and the media and folklore tend to latch onto the most named, most visible supplement rather than the full cocktail of confounds.

**Internet health culture.** Health forums and social media perpetuate fears about supplements with remarkable persistence. A study finding no harm gets less engagement than an alarming case report or a cautionary post from a fitness influencer. The information ecosystem rewards the dramatic, and kidney damage is dramatic. The actual state of the science—dozens of well-designed studies finding no harm—is less shareable than a horror story.

## 4. Verdict

**Contested consensus.** The overwhelming weight of scientific evidence supports the safety of creatine supplementation in healthy individuals. The consensus among exercise scientists and sports nutritionists is robust and has been remarkably stable for decades. However, this consensus comes with two genuine qualifications that prevent a simple "false" ruling.

First, individuals with pre-existing kidney disease or reduced kidney function represent a population for whom the evidence is thinner, where caution is genuinely warranted, and where physician oversight before supplementation is appropriate. Most of the large-scale studies establishing creatine's safety have excluded participants with renal conditions, so the data on this subgroup is less definitive.

Second, the elevated creatinine phenomenon is real and clinically meaningful in the sense that it can lead to misdiagnosis and inappropriate medical responses if creatine use is not disclosed. This is a genuine risk even if it isn't the risk the claim implies.

For healthy adults seeking to use creatine for performance or other purposes: the scientific evidence strongly supports safety at standard doses. For individuals with compromised renal function: consult a physician, disclose supplementation, and use more specific markers of kidney health if monitoring is needed. The claim as broadly stated—that creatine "causes kidney damage"—is not supported by the evidence.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Creatine's place in the history of sports nutrition is instructive. When it became commercially popular in the early 1990s—following high-profile use by British athletes at the 1992 Barcelona Olympics—it entered a discourse environment almost completely lacking in rigorous supplementation research. The early 1990s were also a period of growing concern about performance-enhancing drugs in sports, and creatine entered that charged atmosphere even though it is a naturally occurring compound found in meat and synthesized endogenously in the liver, pancreas, and kidneys.

The initial safety concerns were not irrational given the absence of long-term data. But the research has since accumulated in extraordinary volume and with extraordinary consistency. Creatine monohydrate is now often cited by exercise scientists as the single most evidence-supported sports supplement in existence—not merely for performance enhancement (where its effects on high-intensity, short-duration performance are well-documented), but as a research subject that has been examined more carefully for potential harms than almost any other nutritional supplement.

The 2017 Kreider position stand surveyed over 500 studies. It found consistent evidence of efficacy for improving high-intensity exercise performance, body composition, and recovery. It found no evidence of harm to kidneys, liver, cardiovascular function, or other organ systems in healthy populations at recommended doses. It noted therapeutic applications in conditions involving creatine synthesis deficiencies, neurological disorders, and age-related muscle loss. The overall picture is of a compound that has been extensively studied, found to be effective, and found to be safe—a combination that is genuinely unusual in the supplement landscape.

Creatine's rehabilitation in mainstream medicine has been gradual but real. It is now used therapeutically in some pediatric populations with creatine synthesis disorders, where it can be life-changing. Research into its neuroprotective properties is ongoing. It is included in the nutritional protocols of elite athletes across dozens of sports. The kidney damage narrative persists as a kind of cultural lag—a belief that was understandable when the evidence was thin but has not caught up with what the science actually shows.

Beyond creatine's specific profile, its history raises important questions about how scientific evidence is communicated to the public in domains where regulatory oversight is weak. Unlike pharmaceutical drugs, dietary supplements in the United States do not require pre-market approval by the FDA. Manufacturers do not need to demonstrate safety or efficacy before selling products. The FDA can act against supplements found to be dangerous after the fact, but the burden of evidence falls on regulators rather than producers. In this environment, consumer reliance on peer-reviewed research is unusually important—but access to and literacy about that research is unequal.

Creatine also offers an instructive case about the interaction between a valid safety question and an invalid generalization from it. The valid question: is creatine safe for people with compromised kidney function? The evidence is genuinely thinner here, and physicians are right to be more cautious about this population. The invalid generalization: therefore creatine is unsafe for everyone. This kind of overgeneralization from a genuine subgroup concern to a population-wide prohibition is common in health communication, and creatine illustrates both its mechanism and its cost.

There is also a growing body of research on creatine's benefits beyond athletic performance—applications that mainstream awareness of the compound has been slow to absorb. Studies on creatine supplementation in the elderly show benefits for muscle maintenance and functional strength, with implications for fall prevention and independence. Research in neurological applications is exploring its potential in Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, and depression—applications that remain experimental but have a plausible mechanistic basis in creatine's role in brain energy metabolism. If public perception of creatine were updated to reflect what the research actually shows, the conversation about it would look very different from its gym-supplement-danger framing.

One broader lesson this case teaches is about the persistence of safety concerns in supplement culture once they become established. It is very easy to establish a fear ("this might harm your kidneys") and very difficult to un-establish it, even when the evidentiary base shifts substantially. This asymmetry between the propagation of alarm and the propagation of reassurance is a structural feature of health information ecosystems that applies far beyond creatine.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim presents challenges for standard fact-checking formats because it sits in the "contested consensus" zone—where a strong scientific consensus exists but genuine nuances around subpopulations and diagnostic confounding prevent a clean binary verdict.

Standard fact-checking approaches tend to work best on claims that are straightforwardly true or false. Creatine and kidney damage falls into a more complex category where the right answer is: "False for healthy adults, genuinely uncertain for those with pre-existing kidney disease, and complicated by a real diagnostic artifact that can mislead clinicians." This nuance is awkward for a headline verdict but important for practical decision-making.

Fact-checkers covering this topic typically cite the JISSN position stand and the Poortmans and Francaux studies as the primary evidence base. They note the creatinine confusion explicitly, because it is the mechanical engine driving much of the misunderstanding. They distinguish between case reports (which exist but are confounded) and controlled studies (which consistently show no harm). And they typically recommend physician consultation for individuals with existing kidney conditions—not because the evidence suggests harm, but because individual medical oversight is appropriate for any supplementation in populations with compromised organ function.

What good fact-checking on this topic avoids: declaring creatine "perfectly safe for everyone" without acknowledging the pre-existing kidney disease caveat, dismissing case reports entirely rather than contextualizing them, and ignoring the legitimate clinical issue of creatinine elevation confounding routine bloodwork. The story isn't that creatine is harmless—it's that the claim of kidney damage is not supported by the preponderance of evidence for the population in which it's most widely consumed, while genuine nuances exist for specific subgroups.

The absence of Snopes coverage is notable given the claim's persistence and widespread reach. It is the kind of health claim—involving a popular supplement, a plausible mechanism, and vivid consequences—that circulates at high volume and would benefit from authoritative fact-checker attention. The diagnostic confound around serum creatinine is particularly important to communicate clearly, because it means that even a responsible, medically engaged person could receive a false alarm from routine bloodwork and conclude they've been harmed. Without guidance on interpreting that result in the context of supplementation, the creatinine confusion will continue to reinforce the kidney-damage narrative in clinical encounters, regardless of how clearly the research literature has settled the population-level question.
