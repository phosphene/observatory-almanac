# Was Crisco Invented for Use as a Submarine Lubricant?

## 1. The Claim

The claim that Crisco was originally invented as a submarine lubricant circulates across blog posts, social media platforms, and YouTube videos. Its most detailed and attributable iteration was provided by Joel Wallach, co-founder of the lifestyle company Youngevity International, Inc. (YGI), in a video uploaded to YouTube on December 10, 2019. In the video, Wallach stated:

> "It all started back in 1901 when a German chemist was actually commissioned, I guess would be the word, by the German navy, to come up with a lubricant — a synthetic lubricant — for diesel engines used in submarines, and he was successful."

Wallach further claimed that this German chemist — whom he identified as Wilhelm Normann — subsequently sold the patent to Procter & Gamble, which repurposed the hydrogenated vegetable oil for the food market, resulting in Crisco.

Similar versions of the claim appear on various lifestyle and alternative health blogs, and the story has been repeated frequently on TikTok and Facebook, often as a standalone "fact" without attributed source — the origin story of America's most commercially prominent vegetable shortening being a German naval lubricant. The claim's general purpose is to imply that Crisco was never intended for human consumption and therefore should be viewed with skepticism as a food ingredient — a framing that typically supports arguments for avoiding processed oils in favor of animal fats or other alternatives.

Snopes journalist Madison Dapcevich investigated the claim in December 2023, consulting with the American Oil Chemists' Society (AOCS), one of the primary professional scientific organizations for lipid science and oil chemistry in the United States.

## 2. What's Actually True

**Wilhelm Normann and the invention of hydrogenation:** Wilhelm Normann (1870–1939) was a German industrial chemist who in 1901 developed the process of catalytic hydrogenation as applied to fatty acids — a chemical process that converts liquid unsaturated vegetable oils into solid or semi-solid saturated fats by adding hydrogen across carbon-carbon double bonds in the presence of a metal catalyst (typically nickel). This was a genuine, major industrial chemistry innovation. Normann patented the technology in Germany on August 14, 1902 (German patent number 141,029), and obtained a British patent on January 21, 1903 (British patent number 1515), described as a "Process for Converting Unsaturated Fatty Acids or their Glycerides into Saturated Compounds." These patents and their dates are documented in historical chemistry records and are not in dispute.

**Hydrogenation's industrial applications:** The hydrogenation process invented by Normann was not a uniquely food-oriented discovery. The chemistry of hydrogenation has industrial applications in a broad range of contexts, including margarine production, soap manufacturing, lubricant production, and fuel processing. It is a general-purpose chemical technique. The observation that oils and fats could be solidified at room temperature through hydrogenation had obvious commercial relevance across multiple industries simultaneously. However, documentation of whether Normann was specifically commissioned by any organization — let alone the German Navy — to develop the process for any specific application (submarine lubrication or otherwise) has not been produced by any researcher or institution. The AOCS Lipid Library, which maintains an extensive historical record of lipid science and technology, documented Normann's contributions without any reference to a German naval commission.

**Crisco's actual origin and timeline:** Crisco the product was launched by Procter & Gamble in 1911 — a full decade after Normann's hydrogenation patent. According to Crisco's own corporate heritage documentation, Crisco was "the first shortening product made entirely of vegetable oils." The original formulation used hydrogenated cottonseed oil as its primary ingredient. Work to bring hydrogenation technology to the United States began in 1908, resulting in two patents issued by the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office in 1910 — Food Products patents 1,135,351 and 1,135,951 — both granted on April 13, 1915, and assigned to Procter & Gamble Co. of Cincinnati, Ohio. The first patent was based on partial hydrogenation of cottonseed oil; the second on blending completely hydrogenated cottonseed oil with liquid cottonseed oil.

Published research in the *Journal of Industrial Engineering Chemistry* in 2007 traces this development arc specifically (Lampi et al., cited in USDA publication): approximately five years elapsed between patent application and issuance, reflecting the standard commercial development timeline for a new food ingredient technology.

The 10-year gap between Normann's 1901 hydrogenation patent and Crisco's 1911 launch, and the further chain of American patent activity from 1908 to 1915, are inconsistent with the implied narrative of the claim: that Normann invented Crisco-the-product, immediately sold the patent to P&G, and P&G immediately converted it to food use. The actual historical record shows a decade-long commercial development chain, multiple intermediate patent filings, and a sequence in which hydrogenation technology was developed in Europe, transferred to the United States through licensing or independent parallel development, and then applied specifically to cottonseed oil as a Crisco precursor by P&G's chemists.

**The German Navy connection — what the AOCS found:** Rebecca Guenard, AOCS Director of Publications, communicated to Snopes via email that the AOCS had nothing in its archives that would support the claim that the German navy commissioned Normann's work or that hydrogenated oils were used as submarine lubricants. She described the naval origin story as "just conjecture." The AOCS would have access to the professional-historical literature on lipid science including corporate and institutional archives that document the development of hydrogenation — the absence of any navy-related documentation from this source is a meaningful negative finding, not simply an absence.

**The specific mechanics of the submarine lubricant claim:** Diesel engines — cited specifically in the Wallach version — were indeed used in early German U-boats during the period in question: Germany launched its first operational U-boats in the first decade of the twentieth century, and diesel engines had become the standard propulsion system for submarine surface travel by the early 1910s. Lubricants for diesel engines exist and are important to engine maintenance. However, vegetable-oil-derived hydrogenated fats are poorly suited for use as diesel engine lubricants relative to mineral oils: they have inferior oxidative stability at high temperatures, poor viscosity maintenance, and poor lubrication film strength compared to petroleum-derived lubricants that were available during the same period. The plausibility argument for hydrogenated vegetable oil as a diesel lubricant is therefore chemically weak. This does not disprove the claim — implausible things are sometimes true — but it undermines the coherence of the Wallach narrative.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The Crisco-as-submarine-lubricant claim participates in a specific genre of food origin mythology that has well-understood psychological and epistemic properties.

**The industrial-origin narrative as a specific misinformation genre:** A recurring pattern in food misinformation is the claim that a widely consumed food product was originally developed for industrial, non-food purposes — and that this origin history reveals something hidden or alarming about the product's suitability for human consumption. Margarine (sometimes said to have been developed as candle wax), artificial sweeteners (said to have been accidentally discovered as pesticide adjuvants), and various processed food ingredients are subjected to this narrative template. The genre functions epistemically by leveraging the true fact that industrial chemistry and food chemistry share many underlying methods — the same chemical processes that create lubricants, paints, or explosives can in some cases produce food ingredients or food precursors. The inference from "shared method" to "originally intended as industrial product" is, however, a non sequitur. That something can be used industrially does not mean it was invented for industrial use; that a substance was discovered in one application context does not make it inappropriate for another.

**Joel Wallach and the conflict of interest structure:** The Crisco-as-submarine-lubricant claim's most traceable origin is Wallach's December 2019 YouTube video. Wallach is co-founder of Youngevity International, a multi-level marketing company that sells nutritional and wellness products including dietary supplements and alternative health products. His promotion of the submarine lubricant narrative occurs within a broader argument that dietary cholesterol and saturated fat have been falsely demonized, that vegetable oils are inappropriate for human consumption, and that his products offer a correct nutritional alternative. The argument that Crisco originated as an industrial lubricant — if it were true — would serve as a powerful rhetorical anchor for Wallach's health product claims. The evidentiary problem is that Wallach provides no source for the naval commission claim: no archival document, no historical memoir, no patent record, no naval history, no industrial chemistry history references the German Navy in connection with Normann's 1901 hydrogenation work. Lead Stories, *inter alia*, documented the broader pattern of Wallach's unfounded nutritional claims when reporting on his public statements in 2023 (Lead Stories, 2023).

**Epistemic novelty: the underdetermination of the "unfounded" verdict:** A distinctive feature of this claim compared with the "false" claims examined elsewhere in this series is that the Snopes verdict is not "false" but rather "unfounded." This distinction carries epistemic significance. A "false" rating would require affirmative evidence that Normann did not work with the German Navy — a historical negative that is difficult to certify. The "unfounded" rating, by contrast, reflects the correct epistemic standard: there is no evidence for the claim, and the authority most likely to have archival records bearing on the claim (AOCS) has no such records and characterizes the claim as conjecture. This is the appropriate standard for a historical claim about events in early twentieth-century Germany — the obligation to provide evidence rests with those who assert the connection, not with those who examine it. The absence of supporting archival evidence from the AOCS, the German naval records that have been publicly analyzed by historians, and the Crisco corporate record constitutes a meaningful negative finding, even in the absence of an explicit documentary refutation.

This is also an instance where the available record is asymmetric: the parts of the claim that are true (Normann existed, invented hydrogenation, patented the process in 1901–1903; hydrogenation was eventually used to make Crisco) are precisely verified, while the parts that are distinctive and alarming (the German Navy commissioned it, for submarine lubrication purposes) are the parts for which no evidence has been produced by anyone. This pattern — verified frame, undocumented center — is typical of mythologized historical narratives and is worth flagging as an epistemological structure distinct from the fully invented claims examined in other Truth Vault entries.

## 4. The Evidence in Full

The following evidentiary record supports the Unfounded verdict:

1. **Normann's hydrogenation patent timeline:** German patent 141,029, filed 1902; British patent 1515, filed 1903. Both describe a "Process for Converting Unsaturated Fatty Acids or their Glycerides into Saturated Compounds." Neither references the German Navy or submarine lubrication. Documented by Chem Europe and the AOCS Lipid Library.

2. **No AOCS archival record:** AOCS Director of Publications Rebecca Guenard communicated to Snopes that the organization had nothing in its archives supporting a German Navy connection to Normann's work or to the use of hydrogenated oils as submarine lubricants, and described the claim as "just conjecture."

3. **Crisco launched 1911:** Crisco's corporate heritage documentation dates the product's launch to 1911, a decade after the 1901 hydrogenation patent. The product was described at inception as "the first shortening product made entirely of vegetable oils."

4. **Commercial development timeline:** U.S. patent activity for hydrogenated cottonseed oil as a food ingredient began in 1908; Food Products patents 1,135,351 and 1,135,951 were issued in 1915, assigned to Procter & Gamble Co., Cincinnati (per USDA-cited research in *Journal of Industrial Engineering Chemistry*, 2007).

5. **Source of claim identified:** The most detailed version of the submarine lubricant claim was made by Joel Wallach in a YouTube video (December 10, 2019). Wallach is co-founder of Youngevity International, a nutritional supplement MLM company, and made the claim without archival citation or documentary evidence as part of an argument opposing processed vegetable oils.

6. **Wallach's track record of unfounded claims:** Lead Stories documented in May 2023 that Wallach has made multiple unfounded claims regarding nutrition and health, including claims about professional athletes and lifespan.

7. **Hydrogenated oil as diesel lubricant — chemical plausibility:** Chemically, hydrogenated vegetable oils are poorly suited for diesel engine lubrication relative to contemporarily available mineral oils, due to inferior oxidative stability, viscosity maintenance, and film strength. This reduces the prior plausibility of the naval commission claim without constituting direct disproof.

8. **Historical record of German U-boat development:** Germany did develop and deploy U-boats with diesel propulsion in the first decade of the 1900s, which is consistent with the era in the claim. However, no German naval history documentation links submarine diesel lubrication research with Normann's hydrogenation work.

## 5. What Remains Uncertain

**Whether hydrogenated vegetable oils were ever used as submarine lubricants:** The Snopes investigation and the AOCS consultation addressed the commission claim (Normann commissioned by German Navy) and the origin claim (Crisco invented as submarine lubricant). Neither specifically investigated and documented whether hydrogenated vegetable oils were ever used, in any context, as a lubricant for submarine or diesel machinery — as a secondary application of a pre-existing food or industrial product. The AOCS characterized this as undetermined. It is conceivable that in wartime supply-constrained environments, hydrogenated plant oils were used in improvised lubrication applications; the assertion of this would be a different and weaker claim than the one Wallach made.

**German naval archives and Normann's institutional sponsors:** Germany maintained detailed naval records from the Imperial era; certain archives have been partially studied by historians, while others have not been systematically examined for connections to early industrial chemistry commissions. The full German naval archive has not been audited for Normann-related documentation by any source cited in the available literature. The evidentiary situation is therefore "best available record shows no connection; full record has not been exhaustively searched" — which supports the "unfounded" rather than "false" characterization, but does not eliminate the claim as a logical possibility.

**EARC classification:** Classified A (Assessed/Ambiguous). Best-available evidence from the most relevant professional authority (AOCS) finds no support for the naval commission origin story and characterizes it as conjecture. The claim is rated Unfounded because it lacks evidentiary support, not because it has been affirmatively disproved by documentary evidence.

## 6. Verdict and Further Reading

**Verdict: UNFOUNDED.** The claim that Crisco was invented by German chemist Wilhelm Normann in 1901 at the commission of the German Navy, for use as a synthetic lubricant for submarine diesel engines, is unfounded. Normann did invent the hydrogenation process in 1901 and patented it in 1902–1903, and that chemistry does form the foundation for Crisco's eventual manufacture. However, no documentary evidence connects Normann's work to a German naval commission, German naval archives, or submarine lubrication applications. Crisco was launched as a food product in 1911 — a decade after the hydrogenation patent — following several years of American commercial development by Procter & Gamble. The American Oil Chemists' Society, the relevant professional authority, has no archival record supporting the naval connection and describes it as conjecture. The claim's most prominent propagator, Joel Wallach, provided no documentary source and has a documented history of making unfounded nutritional claims in connection with supplement product promotion.
