# "Photographs Show a Crow Riding Atop a Bald Eagle in Flight"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: Photographs circulating on the internet purport to show an American crow (Corvus brachyrhynchos) perched on the back of a bald eagle (Haliaeetus leucocephalus) while the eagle is in flight. In the most striking images, the crow is shown clearly riding atop the eagle's back, appearing to face forward while the eagle soars beneath it — an image so unusual that many viewers initially assumed it was digitally manipulated or misidentified.

**The photographer and context**: The photographs were taken by bird photographer Phoo Chan in May 2014 near Seabeck in the Kitsap Peninsula of Washington State. Chan had been specifically photographing bald eagles in the area and captured the crow-eagle interaction as part of a longer sequence of images documenting the event as it unfolded. He subsequently published the photographs online and provided an account of the circumstances under which they were taken.

**What the images show**: In the sequence of images, a crow can be seen approaching a soaring bald eagle, landing on the eagle's back, remaining perched briefly as both birds continue in flight, and then departing. The images were clear and high-resolution, showing the crow's feet on the eagle's back, the eagle's characteristic white head and yellow beak, and both birds' wings in flight positions consistent with gliding. The visual was arresting because it appeared to show a much smaller bird using a much larger predatory bird as an airborne ride.

**Why it was questioned**: The image appeared to many viewers to be physically implausible. A bald eagle is a large, powerful raptor capable of catching fish, mammals, and birds in flight. An American crow is a corvid of roughly 0.3–0.6 kg body mass — substantially smaller than a bald eagle — and though corvids are renowned for their intelligence, boldness, and tendency to harass larger birds, the specific behavior of landing on and briefly perching on the back of a soaring eagle seemed too unusual to be real. Additionally, the era of digital photomanipulation and AI image generation has sensitized many internet users to the possibility that extraordinary wildlife photographs may be fabricated.

**Viral circulation**: The photographs were widely shared via social media platforms and bird photography websites following their initial publication in 2014, and recirculated several times thereafter whenever wildlife photography stories gained popular attention. The images attracted commentary from professional ornithologists, birders, and wildlife photographers, as well as skeptical responses from viewers who doubted their authenticity.

**What was at stake in verifying them**: Verifying this type of wildlife photograph claim requires both technical photographic authentication (was the image manipulated?) and biological plausibility assessment (does the behavior depicted actually occur?). Both lines of inquiry were pursued in the subsequent evaluation of Chan's photographs.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The photographs are authentic**: Snopes and multiple ornithologists confirmed that the Phoo Chan photographs were not digitally manipulated. Chan provided the original RAW files and contextual photographs from the broader shooting session, making verification of the images' authenticity straightforward. Reverse image searching confirmed the photographs originated with Chan in 2014 and had not been previously associated with other events or compositional sources.

**Crow mobbing behavior — the biological context**: The behavior that preceded the dramatic riding moment is biologically well-documented and commonplace. American crows, along with American jays, red-winged blackbirds, and many other passerine and corvid species, engage in "mobbing" — a behavioral strategy in which individual birds (often recruited into loosely coordinated groups) approach, harass, dive at, and generally annoy larger raptors like eagles, hawks, and owls (Caro, T., 2005, *Antipredator Defenses in Birds and Mammals*, University of Chicago Press). Mobbing serves multiple functions: it may directly discourage a predator from remaining in the area; it functions as alarm signaling to conspecifics and other birds; and it may allow mobbers to gain information about the predator's capabilities through close observation at lower risk than individual confrontation.

**The aerodynamics of crow landing on eagle**: Whether it is physically possible for a crow to land on the back of a soaring eagle was questioned by some viewers. Kevin McGowan, an ornithologist specializing in crow behavior at the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, addressed this question directly after the images circulated. McGowan confirmed that the behavior was physically achievable and represented a plausible escalation of typical mobbing behavior (McGowan, K., *Cornell Lab of Ornithology*, peer media commentary, 2014). Crows during mobbing interactions make contact passes close to raptors' backs; landing briefly on the back is a variation of this behavior, perhaps facilitated by the eagle's attitude of ignoring the crow rather than attempting to evade it.

**The eagle's non-reaction**: One detail that supports the authenticity of the encounter and contributes to its biological interpretation is that the eagle in Chan's images appears unbothered by the crow's presence. The eagle maintains a relaxed soaring posture, wings extended, without the banking, rolling, or diving maneuvers a raptor typically uses to dislodge a mobbing bird. This behavioral congruence — a crow on an eagle's back, and the eagle apparently indifferent — is consistent with McGowan's interpretation that the eagle was simply ignoring the crow as it had been ignoring the earlier mobbing. The crow, in this reading, took advantage of the eagle's indifference to briefly land, perhaps because landing and "hitchhiking" required less energy than continued dive-bombing.

**Prior documentation of similar behaviors**: While a crow riding on a soaring eagle's back is rare enough to be photographically exceptional, contact between mobbing birds and the raptors they mob has been documented in other contexts and species. Photographers have captured red-winged blackbirds landing briefly on the backs of red-tailed hawks during mobbing interactions; similar behaviors have been described for various corvid-raptor pairs in the ornithological literature. These observations provide a behavioral precedent class into which the Chan photographs fit, even if the specific combination of species and the clarity of documentation is unusual.

**The duration of the interaction**: Phoo Chan noted that the crow appeared to ride the eagle for approximately two seconds in the full sequence, suggesting this was a transient event rather than sustained behavior. This brief duration is consistent with both the physical interpretation (the crow landed, found itself unexpectedly atop a soaring eagle, and departed) and the behavioral interpretation (the crow's motivation was harassment, not sustained transport, and it disengaged once the initial landing was accomplished or the tactical opportunity had passed).

**Biological plausibility assessment**: The encounter is plausible given what is known about corvid intelligence, boldness, and behavioral flexibility. Crows are recognized as among the cognitively most sophisticated birds; they use tools, recognize individual human faces, engage in play, and demonstrate behavioral novelty in response to environmental challenges (Marzluff, J. M., & Angell, T., 2005, *In the Company of Crows and Ravens*, Yale University Press). The behavior captured by Chan — briefly riding a mobbed raptor — falls within the range of behavioral flexibility that corvid cognition research would predict is achievable for a crow.


## 3. Why People Believe This Might Be Fake

**Epistemic novelty — the photography-verification paradox in the age of AI imagery**:

The crow-on-eagle photographs occupy a particularly interesting epistemic position: they are unusual enough to attract immediate skepticism, but completely authentic. This combination places them at the center of a general epistemic challenge that has intensified dramatically since 2014: how to evaluate the authenticity of visually extraordinary photographs in an environment where fabricated images are increasingly common and increasingly convincing.

When Chan's photographs first circulated in 2014, the primary tool for image skepticism was reverse image searching (to detect prior circulation) and artifact analysis (to detect pixel-level evidence of compositing or manipulation). These tools were adequate to confirm the images' authenticity at that time. By 2024, when AI image generation tools had matured substantially, the same images would face a more complicated verification environment: a viewer encountering them for the first time might reasonably ask not only whether the specific images were manipulated but whether they were generated entirely from scratch using a model trained on wildlife photography.

The crow-on-eagle case illustrates a more general epistemic problem that might be called the "authentic extraordinary" problem: genuinely unusual real-world events produce photographs that are superficially indistinguishable from sophisticated fabrications of those events. A crow briefly riding an eagle's back in real life produces an image that looks exactly like what an AI image generator would produce if asked to generate "crow riding bald eagle in flight." The visual properties of the image provide no basis for distinguishing authentic documentation from plausible fabrication, once fabrication technology is sufficiently advanced.

This means that the epistemic burden falls on provenance rather than appearance. Chan's photographs were verified not because they looked authentic — they look indistinguishable from what a capable fabrication would look like — but because their origin could be traced through raw files, shooting session context, photographer credentialing, and biological expert review. The verification protocol is therefore metalevel rather than object-level: we trust the image not because we can see it is real, but because we can trace where it came from through channels that are harder to fabricate than the image itself.

This provenance-based verification model is the correct epistemic response to the photograph-authentication problem in the current media environment, and the crow-eagle case is a useful illustration of why visual inspection alone is insufficient. It also illustrates why biological expert review is valuable as a verification component: McGowan's confirmation that the behavior was biologically plausible added a constraint on the fabrication hypothesis that pure image analysis could not. A fabricated image that was also biologically implausible would fail at two levels; one that is both visually convincing and biologically plausible requires more careful provenance investigation to either confirm or deny.


## 4. Verdict

**TRUE**: The photographs of a crow riding atop a bald eagle in flight are authentic. They were taken by Phoo Chan in Seabeck, Washington in May 2014, depict a real behavioral event, and have been confirmed as genuine by the photographer, by reverse image analysis, and by ornithological review by specialists including Kevin McGowan of the Cornell Lab.

**What the photographs actually document**: A brief, transient episode — approximately two seconds — in which an American crow, engaged in the mobbing of a soaring bald eagle, landed on the eagle's back and remained for a short interval before departing. The eagle appeared indifferent to the crow's presence throughout. The event represents an escalation of typically documented mobbing behavior into unusual direct-contact territory, made possible by the eagle's non-evasive response to the crow's earlier aerial harassment.

**Biological interpretation**: The crow was not "riding" the eagle in any purposive navigational sense — this was not a tool-use event or an example of intentional interspecies transport. It was most likely a brief, opportunistic landing during a mobbing interaction, facilitated by the large flat surface the eagle's back represented and the eagle's indifference to the crow's presence. Whether the crow intended to land or momentarily touched down is unknowable; what is observable is that it remained briefly and then departed.

**Photographic significance**: The event was unlikely to be photographically documented even if it occurs occasionally, because of the brief duration and the requirement that a photographer be present with a telephoto lens during mobbing interactions. Chan's capture of it was genuinely exceptional from a wildlife photography standpoint — a case of skill and timing producing documentation of an event that, though real, may rarely be witnessed long enough to be recorded.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**Corvid cognition and boldness research**: American crows have been the subject of intensive cognitive research over the past three decades, driven by their obvious behavioral sophistication. Studies at the University of Washington and Cornell have documented crows' ability to recognize individual human faces, remember and hold grudges against specific people who have harassed them, use and manufacture tools, and engage in complex social behaviors including teaching and cultural transmission (Marzluff, J. M., et al., 2010, *Animal Behaviour*). This research context explains why ornithologists were unsurprised by the Chan photographs: a species capable of tool use and individual facial recognition is certainly capable of novel behavioral improvisations like landing on a soaring eagle. Crows' behavioral flexibility is among the widest documented for any bird species.

**Mobbing behavior — functional ecology**: While mobbing is intuitively risky for the smaller bird that approaches a larger predator, research has established that the costs are typically modest and the benefits — both immediate (predator displacement) and indirect (social information sharing, conspecific recruitment, potential mate-quality display) — are substantial (Sordahl, T. A., 1990, *The Wilson Bulletin*, on shorebird mobbing; general reviews in Caro, 2005). Eagles typically do not retaliate against mobbing crows, likely because the energetic cost of pursuit is not offset by the modest potential meal value, and because crows' agility in the air renders them difficult targets. This behavioral ecology of indifference is precisely what created the opportunity for the crow to land on the eagle in Chan's photographs: an eagle that is actively ignoring a mobbing crow provides a stable platform.

**Interspecies interaction photography as a genre**: Wildlife photography focusing on unusual interspecies interactions is a recognized genre with both amateur and professional practitioners. Photographs and videos documenting unexpected or counterintuitive inter-species behaviors — predator and prey species resting near each other, unexpected affiliative behaviors between taxonomically distant species — regularly attract outsized public attention. This genre's popularity means that truly exceptional images, like Chan's, circulate widely and are subject to authenticating scrutiny that typical wildlife photographs are not. The scrutiny simultaneously validates exceptional images and filters out fabrications, though the filtering is imperfect.

**The role of Cornell Lab of Ornithology in public bird-behavior verification**: The Cornell Lab of Ornithology plays a notable role in public evaluation of bird photography and behavioral claims in the United States. Through its eBird citizen-science program, Merlin bird identification app, and the institutional standing of its researchers (including Kevin McGowan), the Lab serves as a reference institution for questions about bird behavior and identification that receive public attention. McGowan's specific commentary on the crow-eagle photographs provided credentialing that was important in establishing the images' biological credibility. This institutional role — researchers providing rapid expert commentary on viral claims within their specialty — is an increasingly important component of fact-checking practice for biological claims.

**The symbolic register of eagles and crows in American culture**: Beyond the biological interest, the photographs carry cultural resonance that contributed to their viral appeal. The bald eagle is the national symbol of the United States, associated with power, freedom, and sovereign authority. The American crow is associated in folklore and popular culture with intelligence, cunning, and irreverence. An image in which a crow rides unmolested on an eagle's back thus resonates with a familiar set of cultural themes about the relationship between power and cleverness — themes that are particularly legible in American political symbolism. Whether consciously or not, viewers found in the images a satisfying narrative of the clever small defeating or at least riding the powerful large. This cultural legibility amplified the images' shareability and emotional resonance beyond what their zoological interest alone would generate.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes' approach**: Snopes addressed the crow-eagle photographs by verifying the photographer's identity and source, checking the images for evidence of digital manipulation, consulting ornithological expertise to assess biological plausibility, and reviewing the contextual information provided by Chan about the circumstances of the shoot. All lines of verification converged on an authentic event, and Snopes rated the claim as true.

**The role of original-source documentation**: A critical element in the verification of wildlife photography claims is access to original camera files, particularly RAW (unprocessed) files generated by the camera sensor before any image processing is applied. RAW files contain metadata including camera model, timestamp, GPS coordinates (on cameras with GPS capability), and exposure settings, and they preserve the original unaltered sensor data against which processed JPEG copies can be checked for signs of compositing. Chan's provision of original files was important in establishing the authenticity baseline.

**Biological plausibility as a constraint**: The involvement of McGowan and his confirmation that the behavior was within the range of documented corvid-raptor interaction was important not only for its direct credentialing function but as a check on an alternative explanation: that the photographs documented a trained-bird interaction at a wildlife display or captive-raptor demonstration rather than a wild encounter. Wild eagles do not generally tolerate human-associated bird handling, and the body language of both birds in the images is consistent with wild animals rather than trained ones. Biological experts are able to identify these behavioral cues in ways that general image analysis cannot.

**Historical image verification case**: The 2014 verification of Chan's photographs preceded major developments in AI image generation (which accelerated from roughly 2021 onward with models like DALL-E and Stable Diffusion). From a current perspective, the case is historically interesting as an example of pre-AI-era photograph verification that relied on image-analysis tools and biological expert review without needing to consider whether the images might have been generated from scratch by a neural network. The verification methodology would be essentially the same today but would require the additional step of checking for AI generation artifacts and using AI-detection tools — a dimension of verification that did not exist in 2014.

**The problem of recirculation**: Wildlife photographs verified at initial publication may be recirculated years later, stripped of attribution and original context, in ways that make them appear as new and unverified content. Chan's crow-eagle images have recirculated multiple times since 2014. Each recirculation represents a new opportunity for skepticism to be appropriate (the circulating version may lack attribution and context) but also a case where the verification has already been done. Fact-checkers dealing with recirculated claims must navigate the question of whether to re-verify or simply retrieve and link to prior verification, a challenge that applies broadly across the viral image category.
