# Cut Onions Contamination Warning

## §1 — Claim and Circulation Pattern

The claim examined here holds that bacteria formed on cut onions and potatoes are responsible for more food poisoning cases than spoiled mayonnaise, and carries with it a practical warning: people should not cook with an onion that has been cut and left in the refrigerator, as it is "highly poisonous," and should not leave a cut onion exposed in the kitchen overnight.

Barbara Mikkelson at Snopes fact-checked this claim, published February 16, 2009, and rated it False. The warning had been circulating in email form since at least March 2008, when the earliest instance Snopes documented appeared. The piece is attributed in various versions to a writer named Sarah McCann, though the provenance of her credential claims is not established.

The warning draws on two distinct psychological levers common to food safety misinformation. First, it invokes familiar food-poisoning anxiety — mayonnaise contamination, pathogenic bacteria — and redirects that anxiety toward a less scrutinized and more common foodstuff. Second, it attaches specific-sounding epidemiological authority (a comparative claim about case rates) that implies statistical grounding without providing any verifiable source. The combination of familiar-anxiety redirection and numerical pseudo-authority is characteristic of what has been called "authoritative fear forwarding" in health communication literature.

Unlike some food safety hoaxes that are straightforwardly confabulated, the cut onion claim occupies an interesting middle space: there are genuine food safety considerations around improper storage of any cut produce, including alliums, making the claim partially anchored in real substrate while the specific assertions about relative hazard and mechanism depart significantly from the evidence base.

## §2 — Food Microbiology of Alliums and the Evidence Base

To evaluate the specific contamination claim, it is necessary to understand what food microbiology actually says about Allium cepa (common onion) and its behavior after cutting.

Onions contain organosulfur compounds — principally propanethial S-oxide (the lachrymatory factor responsible for eye irritation) and various thiosulfinates including allicin precursors — that are released upon cell damage and have measurable antimicrobial properties. Kyung (2012, *Journal of Medicinal Food*) reviewed the antimicrobial activity of allium-derived sulfur compounds and documented their inhibitory effects on a range of bacterial species including Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and Salmonella enterica. The irony of the cut-onion contamination myth is that the food it targets is, from a bacteriostatic standpoint, among the more resistant fresh vegetables — not because alliums are sterile after cutting but because their chemical profile actively suppresses bacterial proliferation relative to more neutral substrates.

This does not mean cut onions are contamination-free. Like any damaged plant tissue, a cut onion is metabolically active and presents new surfaces for environmental microbial exposure. Moisture content, storage temperature, and cross-contamination from cutting surfaces or utensils are all relevant variables. The USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) guidelines on produce safety recommend refrigeration of cut produce and consumption within one to three days — guidance applicable to all cut produce, not specifically to onions.

The specific comparative claim — that cut onions and potatoes cause *more* food poisoning than mayonnaise — lacks any foundation in CDC foodborne illness surveillance data. The CDC's Foodborne Diseases Active Surveillance Network (FoodNet) and annual foodborne illness outbreak attribution reports (typically published in Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report) systematically track outbreak attribution by food category. The categories responsible for the largest outbreak burdens include poultry, beef, produce (as a general category), and dairy — with the specific produce items most commonly associated with outbreaks being leafy greens, tomatoes, and sprouts, not onions. While Salmonella outbreaks associated with onions have occurred — notably a 2020 multi-state outbreak linked to red onions from Thomson International Inc. that sickened over 1,000 people across the United States (CDC Outbreak Investigation, 2020) — these are specific documented outbreak events, not evidence that onions as a category exceed mayonnaise in general food poisoning incidence.

Mayonnaise, historically, had a genuine food safety reputation issue: home-made or improperly handled mayonnaise made with raw eggs can harbor Salmonella, and salads bound with mayonnaise held at unsafe temperatures can grow Staphylococcus aureus to hazardous levels. However, commercial mayonnaise — which constitutes the overwhelming majority of mayonnaise consumed in the United States — is an acidic emulsion (pH typically 3.6–4.0) that has been shown to be bacteriostatic rather than bacteriogenic. Radford and Board (1993, *International Journal of Food Science and Technology*) demonstrated that commercial mayonnaise's low pH inhibited Salmonella growth in contaminated mixtures and in fact had a bacteria-reducing effect on inoculated samples. The mayonnaise-food-poisoning association is largely a historical artifact of an era before commercial food production standardized egg pasteurization.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: How False Food Safety Claims Persist Despite Institutional Consensus

The most epistemically interesting feature of the cut-onion contamination warning is not its falsity but its persistence in the face of easily accessible and authoritative contradictory information. The USDA, CDC, and FDA all publish food safety guidance freely online; none of these agencies singles out cut onions as a particular contamination risk deserving special precautionary attention. Yet the email chain persisted for years after Snopes debunked it and continues to circulate in evolved forms.

This persistence exemplifies what researchers in the misinformation correction literature call the "continued influence effect" — the empirical finding that corrections of misinformation, even when believed, frequently fail to eliminate the original information's influence on reasoning and behavior. Lewandowsky and colleagues (2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) synthesized research on this phenomenon and found that corrections are least effective when the original misinformation is: (a) consistent with prior beliefs or anxieties about the domain, (b) more concrete and specific than the correction, and (c) embedded in an established emotional narrative. All three conditions hold for the cut-onion warning.

The claim is consistent with general food anxiety (food can poison you; bacterial contamination is invisible; common foods are secret dangers). It is more concrete than the correction: "cut onions poisoned more people than mayonnaise" is a specific quantitative-sounding claim, whereas the correction "no, that specific comparison is not supported by food safety data" is epistemically accurate but narratively weak. And the warning is embedded in a broader self-protective narrative: the person forwarding the email is cast as a protective community member sharing dangerous information others don't know.

Additionally, the "bacteria are attracted to onions" mechanism in some versions of the warning — suggesting that the allium actively draws pathogenic microorganisms from the environment — engages an intuitive "magnet" or "toxin attractor" folk model of contamination that has been studied in the anthropology of purity and disgust. Mary Douglas's foundational work (*Purity and Danger*, 1966, Routledge) and Paul Rozin's subsequent empirical studies on disgust contagion (Rozin, P., Millman, L., & Nemeroff, C., 1986, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) demonstrate that people apply a law of contagion that functions like a physical attractor: contact with one contaminated entity contaminates previously clean entities, and cut surfaces (which represent disrupted integrity) trigger heightened contamination sensitivity even when the actual microbial risk is low or absent.

## §4 — The Potato Variable

The warning's inclusion of potatoes alongside onions is peculiar from a food safety standpoint and suggestive of the claim's non-expert origin. While the combination "onions and potatoes" sounds like a coherent food category (both are root-adjacent vegetables common in European-American home cooking), they present quite different food safety profiles.

Potatoes have a well-documented food safety concern that is entirely distinct from bacterial contamination: the production of solanine and chaconine — naturally occurring glycoalkaloids — when potatoes are exposed to light or physical damage. Green potatoes, which have been exposed to sunlight and begun chlorophyll production (which co-occurs with glycoalkaloid production), can accumulate these compounds to levels that cause neurological symptoms in humans. This is a genuine hazard, though one minimized by peeling away green tissue. Gosselin and colleagues (*Clinical Toxicology of Commercial Products*, 5th ed., 1984) documented human toxicity cases; the FDA has published guidance on the hazard.

However, the glycoalkaloid hazard has nothing to do with cutting potatoes and leaving them overnight; it is a function of light exposure, not post-cutting bacterial contamination. Someone unfamiliar with potato food safety might conflate the legitimate solanine concern with a false bacterial contamination concern, bundling two distinct risk categories into a single warning that is incoherent in its mechanism while gesturing vaguely at real underlying hazards. This bundling is a common feature of secondhand food safety advice propagated by non-experts through social networks.

## §5 — The Mayonnaise Inversion and Its Social Function

The comparative structure of the claim — cut onions and potatoes are MORE dangerous than spoiled mayonnaise — serves a specific rhetorical function. Mayonnaise is culturally coded as suspect food in popular American health culture: it is calorie-dense, fat-forward, egg-based, and frequently blamed in food poisoning episodes (including, historically, at large gatherings where potato salad has sat out in summer heat). By invoking mayonnaise as the comparison benchmark and exceeding it, the claim performs an inversion: the really dangerous thing is the ordinary thing you trust, not the thing you already distrust.

This rhetorical inversion is a recurring structure in health misinformation. Its social function is to generate recalibration anxiety in recipients who believe they already practice adequate food safety. People who are careful about mayonnaise — who refrigerate it promptly, who are wary of room-temperature dishes at picnics — are reconfigured as dangerously misinformed about an even greater threat. This positions the claim as knowledge upgrade information rather than simply alarm, making it more likely to be forwarded by people who see themselves as knowledgeable health advocates.

## §6 — Practical Guidance and Epistemic Classification

The correct food safety guidance on cut onions is modest: refrigerate cut onions promptly, store in a sealed container (to prevent flavor transfer to other refrigerator contents), and use within a few days — guidance broadly applicable to all cut produce. There is no basis for discarding an onion simply because it was cut the previous day; doing so would be wasteful without corresponding safety benefit.

The claim that cut onions are more dangerous than spoiled mayonnaise is not supported by any accessible body of food safety surveillance data, food microbiology research, or regulatory guidance from any national food safety authority. The mechanism proposed — that alliums are peculiarly bacteria-friendly — is contrary to laboratory evidence of their bacteriostatic chemical profile.

For EARC purposes, this item is classified in mode **R** (Refutation): the specific comparative claim is false, the mechanism is biochemically inverted, and the social persistence of the warning is explainable by well-understood misinformation resilience mechanisms. The one genuine gap warranting further investigation is the specific origin and author credential of the "Sarah McCann" attribution: the original March 2008 email claims a specific author but without traceable institutional affiliation, and determining whether McCann is a real person who authored the claim in good faith from misremembered advice versus a pseudonym attached to fabricated content would help characterize the intent and genesis of this particular myth.
