## 1. The Claim

In mid-2011, photographs began circulating online showing an unusual shark specimen: a small, preserved fetus with a single large eye positioned centrally on the front of its head. The animal appeared to be a recognisable shark in miniature, anatomically normal in most respects but with one striking anomaly — instead of two laterally positioned eyes, it had a single, forward-facing eye in the middle of its snout. The images were accompanied by various captions, some claiming the specimen was a fabrication, a taxidermy hoax, a photoshop composite, or an elaborate prank.

The circulation of these images prompted a fact-checking question with an unusual answer: the photographs were real, the specimen was real, and the condition it exhibited — cyclopia — is a scientifically documented developmental anomaly found across vertebrate species.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

The cyclopic shark fetus in the photographs was genuine. The specimen was discovered in mid-2011 by a Mexican fisherman named Enrique Lucero León, working waters near Cerralvo Island in the Gulf of California, off the coast of Baja California Sur. León caught a pregnant dusky shark (*Carcharhinus obscurus*), a commercially fished species found widely across tropical and warm-temperate ocean zones. Upon opening the animal, he found a litter of ten embryonic shark pups. Nine of the embryos were anatomically typical. One was not.

The anomalous pup was approximately 22 inches long and displayed a single, visually functional eye located in the centre of its snout — the defining feature of a condition called cyclopia. León photographed the specimens and later came into contact with researchers at the Interdisciplinary Center of Marine Sciences (CICIMAR) in La Paz, Mexico. Biologist Felipe Galván-Magaña and colleague Marcela Bejarano-Álvarez examined the fetus, conducted X-ray imaging to confirm internal anatomy, reviewed the existing scientific literature on cyclopia in elasmobranch (shark and ray) species, and confirmed the identification.

Their findings were reported through National Geographic and subsequently covered by mainstream science outlets. The scientific confirmation was unambiguous: this was a cyclopic dusky shark embryo, a rare but real example of a well-understood developmental condition.

**What is cyclopia?**

Cyclopia is a congenital defect in vertebrate animals resulting from incomplete or failed division of the embryonic forebrain (prosencephalon) during early development. Normally, the prosencephalon divides into two separate cerebral hemispheres — a process called prosencephalon division — and this separation drives the lateral movement and individuation of the two eye fields. When this division fails partially or completely, the two eye fields fail to separate and fuse into a single central eye socket (cyclopia) or produce partial fusion variants. The condition is called holoprosencephaly in its broadest sense; cyclopia is its most severe expression.

Cyclopia is known across a remarkably wide range of vertebrate taxa. In humans, it occurs at an estimated rate of approximately 1 in 16,000 pregnancies when all holoprosencephaly variants are counted, with cyclopia proper being rarer. It is uniformly lethal; human infants with true cyclopia are typically stillborn or survive only hours. Cyclopia has been documented in horses, cattle, sheep, cats, dogs, pigs, rabbits, fish, and multiple shark species. In domestic animals, particularly sheep, cyclopia epidemics have historically been traced to specific plant toxins (notably the steroidal alkaloid cyclopamine, isolated from the false hellebore *Veratrum californicum*) that interfere with the hedgehog signalling pathway critical for prosencephalon division.

In sharks, cyclopia has been documented previously, but exclusively in embryonic specimens — no cyclopic shark has ever been caught outside the womb or observed surviving in open water. This is consistent with the condition's lethality in other species; the developmental disruption that produces cyclopia affects, by definition, critical neural structures that would make post-birth survival impossible.

**Was the photograph genuine?**

Yes. Three layers of verification confirm this. First, the physical specimen was examined in person by professional researchers who conducted X-ray imaging and documented their examination. Second, the biological reality of the condition is well-established across multiple species, making the existence of such a specimen scientifically expected (rare, but not impossible). Third, the chain of custody from discovery to scientific documentation was traced: León caught the shark, photographed it, the images entered online circulation, researchers sought León out, the specimen was examined. No fabrication, manipulation, or staging was identified at any point in this chain.

## 3. Why People Believe This Is Fake

The cyclops shark case is the inverse of the standard Truth Vault scenario. Most entries deal with false claims that people incorrectly believe to be true. This entry deals with a true claim that people incorrectly believed to be fake. Understanding why a genuine anomaly gets disbelieved is as epistemically interesting as understanding why a false claim gets believed.

**It violates expected morphology.** Human visual expectations of sharks are firmly set. We know what a shark looks like. A shark with one central eye looks wrong in the specific way that a Photoshop composite looks wrong — it violates the visual grammar of the animal. The dissonance between "this is a real shark" and "this shark has one eye in the middle of its face" is strong enough that many viewers default to "forgery" before they default to "unusual biological event."

**The photographs circulated without institutional context.** The images spread through social media and photo-sharing platforms before the scientific confirmation was published. In the early phases of circulation, viewers had no scientific authority figure attached to the images, no institution vouching for the specimen, and no explanatory framework. In the information-sparse environment where a single arresting image circulates without provenance, accusations of fakery are a predictable response.

**Hoaxes in the adjacent space are common.** The natural history and marine biology space has a well-established tradition of fabricated curiosities — Victorian taxidermy "mermaids" (typically constructed from fish and monkey remains), doctored photographs of sea monsters, and claimed discoveries of anomalous animals. Prior exposure to this tradition of hoaxes trains a reasonable sceptical response that occasionally fires incorrectly on genuine anomalies.

**The condition is genuinely rare and sounds implausible.** Cyclopia, as a word, sounds almost mythological — and indeed the mythological Cyclops of Greek tradition likely drew on observations of anencephalic or cyclopic skulls found in the ancient Mediterranean. When a term sounds like it belongs in mythology, the leap to "someone made this up" is shorter than it should be.

**Online verification was limited.** In 2011, accessible, high-quality scientific communication about teratology in sharks was not easily findable for lay audiences. If a viewer looked for "cyclops shark" online before the scientific coverage was published, they would find other sceptics rather than authoritative scientific sources. The verification asymmetry reinforced the false consensus that the image was a hoax.

## 4. Verdict

**True.** The photographs show a genuine cyclopic dusky shark embryo discovered in the Gulf of California in 2011. The specimen was examined and confirmed by professional marine biologists, X-rayed to verify internal anatomy, and reported through scientific and mainstream media channels. The condition — cyclopia — is a well-documented developmental anomaly across vertebrate species, including multiple elasmobranch taxa. The photographs were not fabricated, manipulated, or staged. This is a case where something that looks too strange to be real is, in fact, real.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The cyclops shark case sits at the intersection of two interesting epistemological phenomena: the teratology of belief (how people respond to biological anomalies) and the viral dynamics of uncontextualised imagery.

**The biology of developmental anomaly.** Cyclopia, and its parent condition holoprosencephaly, is a window into some of the most fundamental processes in vertebrate development. The hedgehog signalling pathway — specifically the Sonic hedgehog (SHH) pathway, named with characteristic molecular biology whimsy — is one of the most ancient and conserved regulatory systems in animal development. It co-ordinates midline patterning: the establishment of the animal's central axis and the symmetrical separation of bilateral structures including the eyes, nasal passages, and cerebral hemispheres. When this pathway is disrupted during early embryogenesis, the results range from subtle midline defects to the complete failure of prosencephalon division that produces cyclopia.

The conservation of this pathway across vertebrate taxa is precisely why cyclopia occurs in sharks, mammals, amphibians, and fish. It is not a taxonomically restricted aberration; it is a failure mode of development that exists wherever the hedgehog pathway operates — which is essentially everywhere in the vertebrate clade.

What makes the cyclops shark's discovery scientifically interesting is less the existence of the condition (known) than the window it opens on developmental biology in wild elasmobranch populations. Sharks are not typically closely monitored during pregnancy; their embryos are rarely examined. The incidental discovery of a cyclopic shark embryo in a commercially caught animal is one of very few documented instances in wild sharks, and it confirms that the developmental mechanisms producing cyclopia operate in cartilaginous fish in essentially the same way they operate in mammals — a data point, however small, supporting the deep evolutionary conservation of midline patterning pathways.

**Cyclopia and mythology.** The cultural resonance of the single-eyed being is worth noting. From the Cyclops of Homer's *Odyssey* to the Arimaspi of Herodotus and the various monocular monsters of European heraldry and medieval bestiaries, the single central eye has ancient cultural presence. Palaeontologists and anatomists have suggested that cyclopic skulls discovered in antiquity — from animals, occasionally from human remains — may have contributed to the cyclopean tradition in human mythology. Elephant skulls, for instance, have a large central nasal cavity that superficially resembles a single large eye socket when the bones are disarticulated; they have been proposed as a source for the Cyclops myth among ancient Mediterranean peoples who may have excavated dwarf elephant remains without having a framework for interpreting them. The cyclops shark, then, inadvertently reconnects a genuine biological phenomenon with one of the oldest strands of human mythological imagination.

**The epistemology of biological anomaly.** Teratology — the scientific study of developmental abnormalities — occupies a peculiar cultural space. Its subjects are simultaneously objects of scientific documentation and objects of fascination, discomfort, and disbelief. The history of teratology is entangled with sideshows, exhibitions, and commercial spectacle. Conjoined twins, polydactylism, microcephaly — conditions that are medically real and scientifically documented — have historically also been staged as entertainment. This legacy creates an ambient scepticism about any "freak animal" claim that is, in one sense, epistemically reasonable (many such claims are hoaxes) and in another sense misleading (legitimate cases exist and are documented in peer-reviewed literature).

The cyclops shark's journey from fisherman's catch to internet hoax accusation to scientific confirmation maps this territory precisely. The appropriate epistemic response to an anomalous biological image is not immediate credulity (many such images are fabricated) but it is also not immediate dismissal (biological anomaly is real and documented). It is suspended judgement pending verification — exactly the response that scientific institutions provide when they investigate a claim before confirming it.

Jim Gelsleichter, a shark biologist at the University of North Florida, noted at the time that cyclopic sharks had been documented by scientists before, but always as embryos. The fact that none has been found outside the womb is consistent with what we know about cyclopia in other taxa — it is developmentally devastating, and no animal with true cyclopia has been documented as a viable free-living organism.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated this claim **True** in an entry published 20 October 2011, shortly after the scientific confirmation was reported. The Snopes investigation traced the specimen's origin to fisherman Enrique Lucero León's catch near Cerralvo Island, confirmed the scientific examination by Felipe Galván-Magaña and Marcela Bejarano-Álvarez at CICIMAR, cited the National Geographic reporting on the case, and noted Jim Gelsleichter's confirmation that cyclopic shark embryos had been documented previously.

National Geographic published the primary English-language scientific report on the specimen, including photographs of the external appearance and X-ray imaging of the internal anatomy. The article was authored by Christine Dell'Amore and published on 13 October 2011.

This is an interesting case for fact-checking methodology because the standard workflow — identify the claim, check whether it is true or false — yields an answer that runs counter to the initial popular verdict. The internet crowd responded to the images with scepticism and accusations of fabrication; the scientific investigation found confirmation. This represents one of the corrective functions of formal fact-checking that is less commonly celebrated: not just debunking false claims, but validating true ones that have been unjustly dismissed.

The EARC mode for this entry is E (Evidence-based confirmation) rather than R (Refutation) precisely because the task here is not to correct a false positive but to establish a true positive — to confirm that something most people assumed was fake is actually real. The epistemological challenge is different: instead of explaining why a plausible-sounding claim is false, we must explain why an implausible-seeming claim is true, and why the implausibility is a function of our morphological expectations rather than of the underlying reality.

The lesson is a general one: biological reality does not constrain itself to human intuitions about what is possible. Developmental biology produces extraordinary morphological outcomes, documented in scientific literature, that would look staged if encountered without context. Robust fact-checking must be capable of moving in both directions — against false claims and against false denials of genuine phenomena.
