# Photographs Show a Real One-Eyed (Cyclops) Shark Fetus


## 1. The Claim

In mid-2011, photographs began circulating on social media and image-sharing sites showing what appeared to be a small shark with a single, large, centrally positioned eye. The animal was pale — nearly white — and its lone eye occupied the center of its face where two eyes would normally sit. The images were widely dismissed as Photoshop fabrications, digital art, or promotional material for a horror film. The claim that fact-checkers would eventually evaluate was simple and direct: "These photographs show a real one-eyed shark."

The images arrived without context, as viral content typically does. They were shared on entertainment sites, reposted through email chains, and aggregated by content farms that specialized in "weird but true" or "believe it or not" galleries. The shark's appearance was so dramatically unusual — so symmetrically wrong — that the default response for most viewers was disbelief. A one-eyed shark seemed to violate basic biological plausibility, placing the images in the same category as jackalopes, mermaids, and other photographic fabrications.

The underlying question was not simply whether the image was manipulated, but whether biology could actually produce such an organism. The claim forced an encounter between popular intuition about what living things can look like and the actual range of developmental biology, where the answer proved to be far stranger — and far more scientifically illuminating — than either the credulous or the skeptical response anticipated.


## 2. What's Actually True

The photographs are genuine. The animal is real. And the science that explains its existence opens a window onto some of the most fundamental processes in embryonic development.

**The Specimen.** In June 2011, fisherman Enrique Lucero León was trolling the waters near Cerralvo Island in the Gulf of California, off the coast of Baja California Sur, Mexico. He hooked a pregnant dusky shark (*Carcharhinus obscurus*). Upon cutting open the mother to clean his catch, León found ten shark fetuses inside. Nine were normal. One was not.

The anomalous fetus measured approximately 22 inches (56 centimeters) in length. It was albino — lacking normal pigmentation throughout its body. And it had a single, large, functional eye positioned centrally on its face, where the bridge of the nose would be in a normally developed shark. The fetus had no distinct nasal structure and displayed the characteristic facial geometry of cyclopia: a single midline orbit housing a single ocular globe.

**Scientific Documentation.** León's discovery came to the attention of Felipe Galván-Magaña, a marine biologist at the Centro Interdisciplinario de Ciencias Marinas (CICIMAR) in La Paz, Mexico, and his colleague Marcela Bejarano-Álvarez. With León's permission, the researchers borrowed the specimen for formal scientific examination. They X-rayed the fetus, conducted detailed morphological measurements, and compared their findings to the existing literature on cyclopia in vertebrates. Their analysis confirmed that the specimen was a genuine case of cyclopia in a dusky shark, and their findings were subsequently published in the scientific journal *Marine Biodiversity Records* through Cambridge University Press.

**Independent Verification.** Jim Gelsleichter, a shark biologist at the University of North Florida in Jacksonville, confirmed to National Geographic that cyclops sharks had been documented scientifically several times before, always as embryos. The fact that none had ever been caught outside the womb strongly suggested that cyclopic sharks do not survive long after birth — if they survive birth at all. The Cerralvo Island specimen, found inside its mother, was entirely consistent with this pattern.

**The Condition: Cyclopia.** Cyclopia — also known as cyclocephaly — is a form of alobar holoprosencephaly, the most severe type of a group of congenital brain malformations collectively called holoprosencephaly (HPE). In normal embryonic development, the prosencephalon (forebrain) divides into two hemispheres, which in turn generate two separate optic vesicles that develop into two eyes. In cyclopia, this division fails. The forebrain remains as a single, undivided structure, producing a single optic vesicle, a single orbital cavity, and a single eye.

Cyclopia is not limited to sharks. It has been documented across vertebrates, including cattle, sheep, horses, cats, pigs, and humans. In humans, cyclopia occurs in approximately 1 in 16,000 live-born infants and at a significantly higher rate — approximately 1 in 200 — in miscarried fetuses, suggesting that most cyclopic embryos are spontaneously aborted. In all species, cyclopia is invariably lethal. The associated brain malformations — including the absence of separate cerebral hemispheres, severely malformed or absent olfactory structures, and compromised regulatory functions — are incompatible with postnatal survival.

**The Molecular Biology: Sonic Hedgehog.** The primary molecular pathway implicated in cyclopia is the Sonic hedgehog (SHH) signaling pathway. The SHH gene — named, in a moment of scientific whimsy, after the Sega video game character — encodes a protein that functions as a morphogen: a secreted signaling molecule that directs cell fate decisions based on its concentration gradient during embryonic development.

SHH is critical for establishing the midline of the developing forebrain. During normal development, SHH protein secreted from the prechordal plate (a structure underlying the developing brain) travels upward and signals the overlying neural tissue to separate into left and right hemispheres. It is this same signal that separates the single embryonic eye field into two bilateral eye primordia. When SHH signaling is absent, insufficient, or disrupted at the critical developmental window, midline separation fails, and the single eye field develops as a single cyclopean structure.

Mutations in the *SHH* gene are the most common identified genetic cause of non-syndromic holoprosencephaly in humans. But genetic mutation is not the only route to cyclopia. Environmental factors can also disrupt SHH signaling, and one of the most dramatic examples comes from agricultural teratology.

**Cyclopamine: The Plant That Creates Cyclopes.** In the 1950s and 1960s, sheep ranchers in the American West reported an alarming increase in cyclopic lambs. Investigation by USDA researchers, particularly the work of Lynn James and colleagues at the Poisonous Plant Research Laboratory in Logan, Utah, traced the cause to pregnant ewes grazing on corn lily (*Veratrum californicum*), a plant native to high-altitude meadows in the western United States. The teratogenic compound was isolated and, with pointed accuracy, named cyclopamine.

Cyclopamine works by directly binding to Smoothened (Smo), a transmembrane protein in the SHH signaling pathway, and inhibiting its function. When a pregnant animal ingests cyclopamine during the critical window of forebrain development (approximately days 13–15 of gestation in sheep), SHH signaling is blocked, and cyclopia results. The molecular specificity of this mechanism was so precise that cyclopamine ultimately became an important research tool for studying hedgehog signaling — and eventually inspired the development of hedgehog pathway inhibitors used in cancer treatment.

For the Cerralvo Island shark, the cause of cyclopia was not determined. The waters near Cerralvo Island were considered clean, making environmental teratogen exposure unlikely. Genetic mutation in the SHH pathway (or in genes encoding other components of the hedgehog signaling cascade, such as *PTCH1*, *GLI2*, or *ZIC2*) is the most probable explanation, though definitive genotyping was not performed.

**The Albinism Component.** The shark's albinism — its complete lack of pigmentation — adds another developmental abnormality to the picture. Albinism in sharks, while rare, has been documented in multiple species. It results from mutations affecting melanin synthesis or melanocyte development. The co-occurrence of cyclopia and albinism in the same individual may represent independent developmental accidents in the same embryo, or it may reflect a broader disruption of neural crest cell development, since both melanocytes (pigment cells) and certain craniofacial structures derive from neural crest cell populations.

Neural crest cells are a migratory cell population that originates at the edges of the developing neural plate and subsequently disperses throughout the embryo to form a remarkable diversity of cell types, including peripheral neurons, craniofacial cartilage and bone, smooth muscle, and melanocytes. Disruption of neural crest development could, in principle, simultaneously affect both pigmentation (producing albinism) and craniofacial patterning (contributing to the severity of the cyclopic phenotype).


## 3. Why People Believe This

The more precise question here is why people *disbelieved* the photographs — why the default response to genuine biological evidence was rejection.

**The Uncanny Valley of Biology.** The cyclops shark occupies territory that the human perceptual system processes as "wrong" rather than "unusual." Two-eyed faces are so deeply embedded in our pattern recognition that a single-eyed face triggers not mere surprise but active rejection — a sense that the image has been manufactured. This is a biological analogue of the uncanny valley phenomenon described in robotics: when something is close enough to normal to be recognizable but different enough to be disturbing, the default cognitive response is to classify it as artificial.

**Photoshop Has Eroded Trust in Images.** The prevalence of digitally manipulated images, particularly in the "weird science" and "bizarre nature" genres that dominate viral sharing, has created a standing default of skepticism toward unusual biological photographs. This skepticism is generally healthy — most "amazing nature" images that circulate virally are manipulated, composited, or decontextualized. But it produces a specific failure mode: genuine anomalies are rejected along with the fabrications. The cyclops shark is a case where healthy media skepticism produced the wrong conclusion.

**Biological Literacy Is Low.** The average person's mental model of biological development does not include the concept of morphogen gradients, midline signaling, or holoprosencephaly. Without these frameworks, a one-eyed animal seems not just unusual but impossible — a violation of some perceived biological law. The reality is that embryonic development is a complex, error-prone process in which dramatic structural abnormalities occur with statistical regularity. Cyclopia, polydactyly, conjoined twinning, situs inversus, and other major developmental anomalies are well-documented across species. But this knowledge is largely confined to developmental biologists, teratologists, and veterinary scientists.

**Viral Context Contaminates Credibility.** The photographs first circulated through entertainment sites and email chains — channels that routinely traffic in fabrications. The context of distribution created a frame that prejudiced interpretation. Had the same images been first published in a peer-reviewed journal or a major newspaper's science section, the credibility threshold would have been met immediately. Instead, the images arrived through channels associated with hoaxes, which activated the hoax-detection heuristic regardless of the images' actual provenance.

**Mythological Resonance.** The cyclops is one of the oldest figures in Western mythology, from Polyphemus in Homer's *Odyssey* to the Cyclopes of Hesiod's *Theogony*. This mythological association cuts both ways: it makes the image instantly recognizable but also frames it as fictional. A one-eyed creature belongs to mythology, not biology — or so the intuition goes. The irony is that some scholars, including Adrienne Mayor, have argued that cyclopia in real animals (particularly elephant skulls, whose large central nasal cavity resembles a single eye socket) may have contributed to the origin of cyclops mythology in the first place.

**The Internet Optimization Problem.** Images that circulate virally are optimized for emotional response, not informational accuracy. The cyclops shark images were shared because they were shocking, not because they were accompanied by explanatory context. By the time most viewers encountered the photographs, they had been stripped of attribution, date, location, and scientific explanation — leaving nothing but the raw visual stimulus and the viewer's existing priors. This stripping of context is not incidental to viral distribution; it is the mechanism of viral distribution.


## 4. Verdict

**True. The photographs are genuine and show a real cyclopic shark fetus.**

The one-eyed dusky shark fetus found near Cerralvo Island in the Gulf of California in 2011 is a documented scientific specimen. It was examined by credentialed marine biologists, X-rayed, compared to the existing literature on cyclopia, and published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal. The condition that produced it — cyclopia, a severe form of holoprosencephaly — is a well-characterized developmental anomaly with a known molecular basis involving the Sonic hedgehog signaling pathway.

The specimen is not Photoshopped, digitally manipulated, a promotional prop, or a hoax. It is a biological fact — one of the most striking examples of a documented developmental anomaly in an elasmobranch, and a vivid illustration of what happens when the fundamental signaling events of embryonic development go wrong.

As Jim Gelsleichter, the shark biologist at the University of North Florida, put it: "It's a humbling experience to realize you ain't seen it all yet."


## 5. The Wider Picture

The cyclops shark is epistemologically loaded not because the basic fact-check is difficult — Snopes resolved it correctly and succinctly as "True" — but because the case illuminates several broader questions about how we evaluate visual evidence, what we consider biologically possible, and how scientific knowledge reaches (or fails to reach) the public.

**Development Is Not Design.** The cyclops shark is a dramatic refutation of folk biology's implicit assumption that organisms are "designed" — that there is a blueprint guaranteeing that every animal will have two eyes, four limbs, and a symmetrical body plan. Embryonic development is not the execution of a blueprint. It is a cascade of biochemical signals, each contingent on the ones before it, each subject to disruption by genetic mutation, environmental insult, or stochastic error. The two-eyed, bilaterally symmetrical body plan is not guaranteed — it is the statistical outcome of a process that usually works but sometimes fails in spectacular ways.

This understanding has profound implications beyond developmental biology. It connects to fundamental questions in the philosophy of biology: is there a "normal" body plan, or only a distribution of developmental outcomes? Is cyclopia a "defect" or simply the low-frequency tail of a developmental process? These questions are not merely semantic — they affect how we conceptualize disability, define normalcy, and understand the relationship between genotype and phenotype.

**From Teratology to Oncology.** The cyclopamine story illustrates one of science's most productive patterns: the path from observing a pathological phenomenon to understanding its mechanism to developing therapeutic applications. Cyclopic lambs in the 1950s led to the isolation of cyclopamine. Cyclopamine led to the characterization of the hedgehog signaling pathway. Understanding the pathway revealed that aberrant hedgehog signaling drives certain cancers, including basal cell carcinoma — the most common form of human cancer. This led to the development of vismodegib (Erivedge), the first FDA-approved hedgehog pathway inhibitor, and subsequently sonidegib (Odomzo), both used to treat advanced basal cell carcinoma.

The intellectual path from cyclopic lambs in Idaho to cancer therapy runs through exactly the same molecular understanding that explains the cyclops shark. The SHH pathway does not belong to any single medical discipline — it is a fundamental axis of developmental biology that manifests in teratology when it fails during embryogenesis and in oncology when it reactivates inappropriately in adult tissues.

**The Epistemic Cost of Default Skepticism.** The cyclops shark case reveals an underappreciated cost of the "everything online is fake" heuristic. Most unusual images on the internet are indeed manipulated or decontextualized, and a standing posture of skepticism protects against credulity. But when skepticism becomes the default regardless of evidence, it becomes its own form of epistemic failure. The reflexive "it's Photoshopped" response to the cyclops shark was wrong — and it caused thousands of people to dismiss genuine scientific evidence because it arrived through channels they had learned to distrust.

This dynamic has parallels in more consequential domains. Climate data, vaccine efficacy studies, and pandemic modeling are all communicated through channels (news media, social media, government websites) that many people have learned to distrust. A healthy skepticism that demands evidence and verification is productive. An indiscriminate skepticism that rejects evidence based solely on its channel of delivery is not.

**Viral Distribution and Scientific Literacy.** The cyclops shark reached millions of people through viral sharing. The scientific explanation of why it exists reached a tiny fraction of that audience. This asymmetry — between the reach of the image and the reach of the explanation — is a structural feature of information ecosystems. Images travel fast because they require no background knowledge to process. Explanations travel slowly because they require exactly that.

Bridging this gap is one of the core challenges of science communication. The cyclops shark is an ideal case study for this challenge because the explanation is genuinely fascinating — the Sonic hedgehog pathway, the cyclopamine discovery, the connection to cancer therapy — and because it meets audiences exactly where they already are: looking at a bizarre photograph and wondering if it's real.

**What Rarity Tells Us About Normalcy.** Every cyclopic fetus is, implicitly, evidence about the millions of embryos in which the SHH pathway functioned correctly. Developmental anomalies are informative precisely because they are rare. They mark the failure modes of a system that usually succeeds, and in doing so they reveal the system's architecture. Before cyclopia was understood at the molecular level, the process by which a single embryonic eye field separates into two eyes was largely invisible — a developmental event so reliable that no one noticed it happening. Cyclopia made it visible by showing what happens when it doesn't.

This principle — that understanding failure illuminates normal function — is foundational to developmental biology, to medicine, and to engineering. It is the reason that genetic diseases teach us about normal gene function, that structural failures teach engineers about normal load bearing, and that cognitive biases teach psychologists about normal reasoning. The cyclops shark is not merely a curiosity. It is a data point in one of biology's most important inferential frameworks.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes' Approach.** Snopes addresses the claim concisely and correctly, rating it **True**. The article identifies the specimen's origin (Enrique Lucero León's catch near Cerralvo Island), documents the scientific examination by Felipe Galván-Magaña and Marcela Bejarano-Álvarez, cites Jim Gelsleichter's confirmation that cyclops sharks had been documented before, and quotes National Geographic's reporting on the find. The Snopes entry explains the basic biology of cyclopia — the fusion of orbital cavities into a single structure — and notes that the absence of cyclopic sharks caught outside the womb suggests the condition is not survivable.

**What Snopes Does Well.** The entry correctly identifies the key facts: the specimen is real, it was scientifically examined, and cyclopia is a documented condition. The inclusion of Gelsleichter's quote — "It's a humbling experience to realize you ain't seen it all yet" — captures the appropriate scientific response, which is neither sensationalism nor dismissal but genuine interest in an anomalous finding. The Snopes entry accomplishes its primary function: answering the question "is this real?" with documentary evidence.

**The Gap This Entry Fills.** Snopes' entry is brief — approximately 400 words of substantive content. It identifies cyclopia and confirms the specimen's authenticity but does not explore the molecular biology that produces the condition, the broader significance of holoprosencephaly in developmental biology, the cyclopamine connection, or the epistemological dimensions of why the photographs were so widely dismissed as fake. The entry provides the verdict without the education.

This Truth Vault entry enhances the Snopes fact-check by providing the scientific architecture behind the verdict. It explains the SHH signaling pathway at a level accessible to non-specialist readers. It traces the agricultural teratology pathway from cyclopic lambs through cyclopamine to hedgehog pathway inhibitors in cancer therapy. It examines the epistemological question of why genuine biological evidence was rejected by default. And it uses the case to explore broader themes in developmental biology, science communication, and media literacy.

**The Verification Challenge.** The cyclops shark presents a specific verification challenge that fact-checkers will increasingly face: evaluating biological specimens documented primarily through photographs in an era of sophisticated digital manipulation. In this case, the chain of evidence was robust — the fisherman, the scientists, the X-rays, the publication. But future cases may involve specimens photographed in remote locations with less institutional infrastructure for documentation. Developing verification protocols that can authenticate biological specimens through photographic evidence alone, without necessarily having physical access to the specimen, is an emerging challenge for fact-checking organizations.

**Classification Considerations.** The Snopes entry rates the claim "True" without further qualification. This is correct for the specific claim ("photographs show a real one-eyed shark"), but it may not fully capture the nuance of the broader context. The photographs are real. But they circulated in an informational ecosystem that provided no context for understanding them, which led many viewers to incorrectly classify them as fake and others to interpret them as evidence of supernatural phenomena, government experiments, or Fukushima radiation effects. A more complete approach to this type of claim might include not just the verdict but also a brief note on the specific misconceptions that the images generated — debunking the false explanations alongside confirming the authentic image.
