# Will Dangerous Cosmic Rays Pass Close to Earth 'Tonight'?

## §1 — Claim & Verdict Surface

The claim, circulating in chain-message form at least since 2014 and documented continuously since, reads in part: "Tonight at 00:30 to 03:30am make sure to turn off your phone, cellular, tablet etc & put far away from your body! Singapore TV announced on the news! Tonight 12 to 3:30 am for our Planet will be very high radiation! Cosmic rays will pass close to Earth…" The Snopes verdict is **False** (published May 24, 2017; updated July 12, 2023 by Alex Kasprak).

The message has several self-undermining features that the Snopes piece identified. The total absence of specific dates makes it perennially applicable and therefore perennially shareable — a design feature, not an oversight, in viral chain-message architecture. The invocation of "Singapore TV" as a source lends geographically diffuse but authoritative-feeling provenance. The directive to turn off mobile devices is viscerally actionable advice, making the message feel useful rather than merely alarming. These rhetorical affordances are characteristic of a specific genre of health-scare chain message that has flourished since the SMS era (Kata, Anna. 2012. "Anti-Vaccine Activists, Web 2.0, and the Postmodern Paradigm: An Overview of Tactics and Tropes Used Online by the Anti-Vaccination Movement." *Vaccine* 30(25):3778–3789; the broader pattern of unverifiable-provenance health chain messages predates Kata's analysis).

EARC classification: **E** (Empirically checkable; the physics and monitoring infrastructure are well-documented in peer-reviewed literature and agency science). The epistemic gap is **low**: cosmic ray physics is mature, Earth's shielding mechanisms are thoroughly characterised, and the claim's core premise is falsifiable against multiple publicly available monitoring datasets.

## §2 — Origin, History, and Spread of the Claim

The oldest confirmed version of this claim is tracked to 2014 by the debunking website Hoax-Slayer. It travelled through SMS, WhatsApp, and Facebook chains before entering English-language media reporting cycles. The message has been particularly persistent in Southeast Asia (the "Singapore TV" attribution suggests regional seeding) but also circulates in South Asia, West Africa, and Latin America, with local adaptations substituting regional media organisations for "Singapore TV."

The message's structure — urgent timing window, specific but unverifiable authority citation, actionable protective advice — follows a template documented in public health communication research on "disease rumours" (Bordia, Prashant, and Nicholas DiFonzo. 2004. "Problem Solving in Social Interactions on the Internet: Rumor as Social Cognition." *Social Psychology Quarterly* 67(1):33–49). The three-hour window is particularly notable: it is long enough to feel serious but short enough to imply a discrete, avoidable event, which is categorically unlike how any real astrophysical phenomenon operates. Galactic cosmic rays are a continuous background phenomenon; solar energetic particle events (SEPs) develop over hours to days following a solar flare or coronal mass ejection, not in a three-hour predictable window.

The claim's persistence across nine or more years (2014–2023) despite consistent debunking is a case study in what psychologists call "belief perseverance" — the tendency for false beliefs, once held, to resist correction even after the source evidence is discredited (Ross, Lee, Mark R. Lepper, and Michael Hubbard. 1975. "Perseverance in Self-Perception and Social Perception: Biased Attributional Processes in the Debriefing Paradigm." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 32(5):880–892). The lack of any falsifiability marker — no specific date, no specific star or galaxy of origin — means the message can never be "disproven by events" in the way, say, a specific date prediction can.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty and Scientific Mechanisms

The claim's most remarkable epistemic failure is its treatment of cosmic rays as a discrete, directional event analogous to a meteor shower or planetary conjunction — something that "passes by" at a specific time rather than an omnipresent background feature of the near-Earth environment. This fundamentally misrepresents what cosmic rays are at every level of the term's usage.

**Galactic cosmic rays (GCRs):** High-energy charged particles, predominantly protons and alpha particles, originating from sources distributed throughout and beyond the Milky Way. Their defining characteristic is isotropy — they arrive at Earth from all directions simultaneously, continuously, with only slow secular variation tied to the solar cycle. The correlation between solar activity and GCR flux at Earth's surface was quantified by Forbush (Forbush, Scott E. 1954. "World-Wide Cosmic-Ray Variations, 1937–1952." *Journal of Geophysical Research* 59(4):525–542) and has been studied continuously ever since. A Forbush decrease — a *reduction* in GCR flux following a solar CME — is the most dramatic near-term GCR variation, not a dangerous spike (Dumbović, Mateja, et al. 2018. "The Drag-Based Model for Interplanetary CME Propagation Part II: CME-CME Interaction." *Astronomy & Astrophysics* 615:A180). There is no known astrophysical mechanism that would produce a three-hour cosmic ray spike at Earth from an external galactic source.

**Solar Energetic Particles (SEPs):** These are accelerated during major solar flares or CMEs. They are genuinely monitored at concern level by NASA and NOAA because of risks to spacecraft electronics, satellite operations, and polar aviation. They do not, however, pose a health risk to humans on Earth's surface. Earth's magnetosphere deflects low-to-moderate energy SEPs; the atmosphere attenuates higher-energy particles to levels well below those posing direct biological harm to ground-based populations. The NASA Space Weather website documents this clearly: surface humans are protected; the concern is for astronauts in high-inclination orbits above the magnetosphere (Cucinotta, Francis A., et al. 2010. "Space Radiation Risks to the Central Nervous System." *Life Sciences in Space Research* — updated in Cucinotta, Francis A. 2014. "Space Radiation Risks for Astronauts on Multiple International Space Station Missions." *PLOS ONE* 9(4):e96099).

**Epistemic novelty §3:** The specific instruction to turn off mobile devices reveals a secondary confusion: SEPs and GCRs do not interact with mobile devices to increase local radiation dose to their users. There is a real phenomenon in which solar events can disrupt radio communications, GPS accuracy, and power grids through geomagnetically induced currents (Pirjola, Risto. 2000. "Geomagnetically Induced Currents during Magnetic Storms." *IEEE Transactions on Plasma Science* 28(6):1867–1873), but this is an infrastructure-level effect, not a personal radiation exposure pathways issue. The suggestion to distance your phone from your body during a cosmic ray event conflates three entirely separate physical mechanisms: (1) GCR background flux, (2) SEP geomagnetic effects on infrastructure, and (3) the mobile phone radiofrequency non-ionising radiation debate (which has its own independent literature). The message synthesises these unrelated concerns into a single fictional danger.

Additionally, the claim's reference to "gamma rays" in its title (as rendered in the Snopes URL slug) is worth examining. Gamma rays from cosmic sources — gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) specifically — are real and among the most energetic events in the universe. However, the Earth's atmosphere provides approximately 1,000 g/cm² of shielding against primary gamma rays, converting them into extensive air showers of secondary particles that carry a tiny fraction of the original energy. A known gamma-ray burst occurring in the Milky Way at relatively short range (within a few kiloparsecs) and directed at Earth could potentially produce biologically significant ionisation at ground level, but this is a multi-second event well outside any "3 am window" framework, and the probability of a Galactically-directed GRB within relevant range in any human lifetime is exceptionally low (Thomas, Brian C., et al. 2005. "Gamma-Ray Bursts and the Earth: Exploration of Atmospheric, Biological, Climatic, and Biogeochemical Effects." *Astrophysical Journal* 634(1):509–533).

The claim fails on multiple independent scientific grounds: (1) GCRs don't "pass by" in time-bounded windows; (2) neither GCRs nor SEPs pose health risks to Earth surface residents; (3) cell phone proximity is irrelevant to any form of cosmic radiation exposure; (4) no real monitoring agency has ever issued advice of this kind. Each failure is independently sufficient to render the claim false.

## §5 — Sociological and Cultural Context

The cosmic ray scare is part of a rich tradition of "science-adjacent" health alarms that exploit the gap between specialist knowledge and public science literacy. Space weather is a legitimate field with real societal consequences (power grid vulnerability, satellite operations, GPS accuracy), but those consequences are categorically different from personal health effects in ways that are not immediately obvious to non-specialists. The existence of real agencies (NASA, NOAA, ESA) conducting real monitoring operations implies, to some readers, that a genuine personal danger must be being monitored.

The message's construction of authority is worth close study. "Singapore TV," "Check Google & NASA BBC News" — these invocations gesture toward verifiable institutions without providing any verifiable specific claim. The injunction to "check Google" is particularly revealing: in an epistemic culture where search engines feel authoritative, the instruction to verify appears to discharge the message from responsibility for accuracy. It functions as a credibility signal that actually prevents verification by making the verification act feel pre-authorized (Metzger, Miriam J. 2007. "Making Sense of Credibility on the Web: Models for Evaluating Online Information and Recommendations for Future Research." *Journal of the American Society for Information Science and Technology* 58(13):2078–2091).

The geographic spread and cultural durability of the message also reflects patterns in health communication in regions where formal public health infrastructure is underdeveloped or distrusted. In such contexts, peer-to-peer forwarding of health warnings can serve legitimate social functions — genuine disease outbreaks are sometimes first reported through informal networks — which makes community members less likely to apply skepticism to false alarms sharing the same format (Friesen, Justin P., et al. 2015. "The Dual Function of Social Rumor: Differentiation between Hostile Versus Benign Forms." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 109(6):981–999).

## §6 — Synthesis and Gap Analysis

**Verdict: False.** The Snopes rating is correct and robustly supported. The chain message misrepresents the nature of cosmic rays, exaggerates their Earth-surface hazard by multiple orders of magnitude, and provides advice (turning off mobile phones) that is physically unrelated to the imagined threat.

**What Snopes got right:** The Kasprak piece distinguished clearly between galactic cosmic rays and solar energetic particles, and productively quoted the Caltech astronomer's definition to clarify terminology. The observation about the perennial datelessness of the warning — its structural immortality — is analytically astute.

**Gap:** The piece did not address the atmospheric shielding argument at any depth, which is arguably the most important single reason surface humans are not at risk. Mentioning the approximately 1,000 g/cm² of atmospheric column above sea level would have provided intuitive physical scale. The piece also did not cite the GRB literature, which handles the only scenario (nearby directed GRB) where cosmic radiation could conceivably matter to surface biology. That scenario is orders of magnitude more energetic than anything in the chain message, would not be preceded by a WhatsApp warning, and would be unmitigable by turning off a phone.

**Open questions:**
1. The "silent" three-hour window structure — does it map to any real-time solar monitoring cycle (e.g., NOAA space weather alert cycles or GOES satellite update cadences) that could have seeded the specific timeframe in an earlier confused media report? This genealogical question remains unanswered.
2. What is the comparative reach of this particular chain message versus other space-weather misinformation? Tracking social media amplification data would allow epidemiological modeling of the claim's spread.
3. Has the message ever been associated with real world behavior change (mass cell phone shutdowns, ER visits for "radiation exposure")? If so, quantifying the public health cost of health-scare misinformation chains would strengthen the case for proactive science communication.

**Confidence in verdict:** Very high.
