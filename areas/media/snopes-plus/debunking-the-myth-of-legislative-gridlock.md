# Congress Is Paralyzed by Partisan Gridlock and Cannot Pass Meaningful Legislation

## The Claim

The claim is ambient rather than specific — it is not a single viral post or a shareable copypasta but a persistent background assumption that saturates American political discourse. It takes many forms: "Congress is broken." "Nothing gets done in Washington." "Partisan gridlock has made it impossible to pass legislation." "The system is paralyzed." Polls consistently show that Americans hold Congress in deep contempt — Gallup's congressional approval ratings have hovered between 10% and 25% for most of the past two decades — and a central pillar of that contempt is the belief that Congress is functionally incapable of doing its job.

This belief is not confined to casual political observers. Media coverage relentlessly frames congressional dynamics in terms of obstruction, stalemate, and dysfunction. Political commentators across the ideological spectrum treat gridlock as an established fact rather than a hypothesis to be examined. Campaign rhetoric from both parties reinforces the narrative: each side blames the other for preventing progress, implicitly validating the premise that progress has indeed been prevented.

The claim, as assessed by political scientist Jeb Barnes of USC in a 2021 analysis republished by Snopes, rests on "half-truths about the legislative process and a basic misunderstanding of how contemporary policymaking works." The reality is considerably more complex — and in some ways more troubling — than the simple narrative of paralysis.

## What's Actually True

### Congress Passes More Substantive Legislation Than People Realize

The most direct challenge to the gridlock narrative is quantitative: Congress actually passes a lot of laws. The 115th Congress (2017-2019), convened after the election of President Trump — a period widely characterized as one of intense partisan warfare — enacted 442 laws, the most in a decade. Of these, the Pew Research Center classified 306, or 69%, as substantive rather than merely symbolic. These included a $1.5 trillion tax overhaul, bipartisan criminal justice reform (the First Step Act), comprehensive farm policy legislation, a major opioid crisis response package, and sanctions on Russia.

This output is not anomalous. Congress has averaged approximately 311 substantive laws per session since 1989, according to Pew. The 116th Congress (2019-2021) had the busiest post-election lame-duck session in recent history. The idea that Congress produces nothing of consequence is flatly contradicted by the legislative record.

This does not mean Congress is functioning optimally. Its failure rate on key agenda items — the percentage of issues identified as priorities that go unaddressed — has more than doubled since World War II, rising from roughly 30% to over 60%. Congress does fewer of the things people most visibly expect it to do. But "doing fewer of the most prominent things" is categorically different from "doing nothing," and the gridlock narrative conflates the two.

### The Filibuster Blocks Some Things, Not Everything

The Senate filibuster — the procedural mechanism that effectively requires 60 votes rather than a simple majority to advance most legislation — is a genuine obstacle to legislative action. Its routine use has increased dramatically in recent decades, and it has prevented the passage of legislation on issues ranging from gun control to immigration reform to campaign finance.

But the filibuster is not a universal barrier. Budget reconciliation — the process that allows fiscal legislation to pass the Senate with a simple majority — has been used to enact some of the most consequential laws of the past several decades, including the 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act, the 2010 Affordable Care Act's final passage, and the 2021 American Rescue Plan. The reconciliation process has significant limitations (it applies primarily to taxing and spending measures), but its existence means that the most economically impactful legislation often has a pathway around the filibuster.

Additionally, Congress regularly passes bipartisan legislation on matters that do not split neatly along party lines. Infrastructure, defense authorization, disaster relief, trade agreements, and other issues frequently attract cross-party support that exceeds the 60-vote threshold. The gridlock narrative, by focusing almost exclusively on the issues that divide the parties most sharply — abortion, gun control, immigration — creates a distorted picture in which the exceptional becomes the representative.

### Policymaking Extends Far Beyond Congressional Votes

Perhaps the most important corrective to the gridlock myth is the recognition that policy — the set of principles and goals that guide governmental decision-making — is made through many channels, not just congressional legislation. The Snopes-republished analysis by Barnes identifies at least four major channels of non-legislative policymaking that operate continuously regardless of congressional output:

**Executive orders and presidential directives.** Presidents Bush, Obama, and Trump averaged 36, 35, and 55 executive orders per year, respectively. President Biden issued 31 executive orders in his first weeks in office alone. Executive orders can reshape policy on immigration, environmental regulation, labor standards, civil rights enforcement, and dozens of other domains without any congressional vote at all. The Trump administration reportedly issued over 1,000 changes to immigration policy using internal guidance memos, circulars, bulletins, and other administrative directives — cutting legal immigration to the United States by roughly half through mechanisms that fell entirely outside the legislative process.

**Regulatory action by executive agencies.** Federal agencies issue thousands of regulations annually through notice-and-comment rulemaking, shaping policy in areas from workplace safety to environmental protection to financial market oversight. These regulations have the force of law but are produced by bureaucratic processes, not legislative votes.

**Judicial interpretation.** Federal courts — and especially the Supreme Court — make policy through the interpretation of existing statutes and constitutional provisions. Decisions on issues ranging from campaign finance (Citizens United) to LGBTQ+ rights (Obergefell v. Hodges) to gun rights (District of Columbia v. Heller) have reshaped American policy as profoundly as any legislation.

**Policy conversion.** This is the least visible but potentially most consequential mechanism: the repurposing of existing laws to new ends. When the application of a law shifts to serve different interests or address different problems than those originally intended, the policy changes even though the statute remains identical. Barnes cites the example of antitrust laws, initially directed at business trusts, being reinterpreted to apply to labor unions — same law, fundamentally different policy. Similar conversion processes have been documented in consumer protection law, disability policy, and social welfare programs.

### Inaction Is Itself a Form of Policy

The gridlock narrative assumes that when Congress fails to act, the policy landscape remains static. This is false. Inaction in a changing world produces policy change through what scholars call "policy drift" — the gradual erosion of a policy's real-world effect when it is not updated to keep pace with changing conditions.

The federal minimum wage illustrates this mechanism precisely. Set at $7.25 per hour in 2009, it remained unchanged through 2021 (when the Snopes article was published) and continues to remain unchanged in 2026. Inflation over that period has reduced the purchasing power of $7.25 to substantially less than $6.00 in 2009 dollars. Congress did not vote to cut the minimum wage. Congress did *nothing* — and the minimum wage was effectively cut by more than 15% through the silent operation of inflation. Every year of continued inaction produces a further reduction.

Policy drift operates across the entire social safety net. When eligibility thresholds, benefit levels, and program parameters are not adjusted for inflation, demographic change, or evolving social conditions, programs silently shrink in real terms. Scholars have argued that policy drift has been the primary mechanism through which the American social safety net has contracted since the 1980s — not through dramatic legislative repeal but through the quiet refusal to maintain.

This means that congressional "inaction" is, in many cases, an active policy choice with distributable consequences. When Congress fails to update the minimum wage, it is effectively transferring wealth from low-wage workers to employers. When Congress fails to adjust environmental regulations for new pollutants, it is effectively permitting those pollutants. Calling this "gridlock" obscures who benefits and who is harmed by the status quo.

## Why People Believe This

### The Media Covers Conflict, Not Legislation

The structural economics of American media produce a systematic bias toward conflict narratives. A congressional standoff — a government shutdown, a filibuster, a party-line vote on a controversial issue — generates attention, engagement, and advertising revenue. The quiet passage of a bipartisan farm bill does not. Newsrooms with shrinking budgets allocate their reduced reporting capacity toward the most attention-generating stories, which are almost always stories of failure, dysfunction, and confrontation.

Over time, this coverage pattern creates a self-reinforcing perception: if the only congressional action that receives sustained media attention is the action that fails (or the dramatic confrontation that precedes success), then citizens will naturally conclude that Congress only fails. The 306 substantive laws passed by the 115th Congress received a tiny fraction of the coverage devoted to the handful of high-profile failures — the repeal of the Affordable Care Act that didn't happen, the border wall funding that wasn't provided, the infrastructure bill that was deferred.

This is not a conspiracy; it is an emergent property of an attention economy. Journalists are covering what generates audience interest. Audience interest is drawn to conflict. The result is a public record that systematically overrepresents dysfunction and underrepresents function.

### The Visible vs. the Invisible

The issues on which Congress most visibly fails to act are, by definition, the ones people care about most. Gun control, immigration reform, climate legislation, police reform — these are the issues that dominate public discourse, and they are the issues on which the filibuster and partisan polarization are most effective at preventing action. When Americans say "Congress doesn't do anything," they often mean "Congress hasn't done the thing I most want it to do."

Meanwhile, the legislation Congress does pass often operates at a level of specificity and technicality that makes it invisible to casual observers. Reauthorizations of existing programs, adjustments to regulatory frameworks, incremental reforms to criminal justice or agricultural policy — these are consequential actions that affect millions of lives but lack the narrative simplicity and emotional charge of the issues that dominate headlines.

### Both Parties Benefit from the Gridlock Narrative

The gridlock narrative serves both parties' electoral interests. For the party in the minority, it validates obstruction: "We're blocking the other side's dangerous agenda." For the party in the majority, it excuses failure: "We would have delivered on our promises, but the other side prevented us." Neither party has an incentive to challenge the premise that Congress is dysfunctional, because both parties use that dysfunction as a campaign argument.

This bipartisan investment in the gridlock narrative makes it uniquely resistant to correction. Unlike a factual claim that one party has an interest in debunking, the gridlock myth serves everyone's messaging needs. Challenging it requires acknowledging that both parties are getting more done than they publicly admit — an acknowledgment that would undermine the electoral strategies of both.

### The Psychology of Decline Narratives

The gridlock myth is part of a broader class of beliefs that might be called "decline narratives" — the conviction that things were once better, that institutions that once functioned are now broken, that the present is a degraded version of the past. These narratives are psychologically compelling regardless of their accuracy because they align with the human cognitive bias toward nostalgia and loss aversion.

Research consistently shows that people recall the past as better than it was and perceive the present as worse than it is. Applied to Congress, this means that Americans compare a selectively remembered past in which Congress "got things done" (ignoring the intense partisan battles of every era) with a selectively perceived present in which Congress "does nothing" (ignoring the hundreds of laws it passes each session). The gridlock myth is, in part, a specific instance of a universal cognitive distortion.

## Verdict

**The claim that Congress is paralyzed by gridlock is a distortion, not a description.** Congress passes hundreds of substantive laws per session. Policy is made through executive action, regulatory processes, judicial interpretation, and the silent operation of drift. The American political system produces continuous policy change, even — especially — when Congress appears to be doing nothing.

**The real concern is not paralysis but accountability.** When legislative stalemate shifts policymaking to executive orders, agency regulations, and judicial decisions, the result is not inaction but action by less publicly accountable actors through less transparent mechanisms. The danger is not that nothing changes; it is that things change in ways that are harder to see, harder to attribute, and harder to reverse.

**The gridlock narrative is not merely inaccurate — it is harmful.** By fostering the belief that the political system is incapable of producing change, it discourages civic engagement, validates cynicism, and obscures the real mechanisms through which policy affects people's lives. Citizens who believe "nothing gets done" are less likely to hold their representatives accountable for what actually does get done — or for the consequences of what is deliberately left undone.

## The Wider Picture

### The Kludgeocracy Problem

Political scientist Steven Teles coined the term "kludgeocracy" to describe the American system's tendency to address problems not through elegant, comprehensive legislation but through layers of patched-together policy mechanisms: tax credits layered on regulatory mandates layered on block grants layered on court orders. Each layer adds complexity; none provides a clean solution.

Kludgeocracy is the real consequence of the conditions that the gridlock narrative misinterprets. When Congress cannot pass comprehensive reform on a major issue, policymaking doesn't stop — it migrates to other channels, producing a patchwork of executive orders, agency rules, state-level legislation, and judicial decisions that collectively address the issue but in a fragmented, inconsistent, and often contradictory manner. The result is a policy landscape that is active but incoherent — the opposite of the paralysis the gridlock narrative describes.

Healthcare is the paradigmatic example. The Affordable Care Act was itself a massive kludge — a Rube Goldberg machine of exchanges, mandates, subsidies, and Medicaid expansion designed to achieve near-universal coverage without the political infeasibility of single-payer healthcare. Subsequent executive actions, agency regulations, court decisions, and state-level variations have added additional layers of complexity. The system is not gridlocked; it is hyperactive but illegible.

### Policy Drift as Stealth Redistribution

The concept of policy drift has profound implications for how we understand distributional politics in America. If Congress can effectively reduce benefits, lower wage floors, and shrink the social safety net simply by doing nothing — by allowing inflation and changing conditions to erode existing policy — then the inaction itself becomes a form of political choice with identifiable winners and losers.

Political scientists Jacob Hacker and Paul Pierson have argued that policy drift has been the primary mechanism of economic redistribution in America since the 1980s. Tax brackets that are not adjusted for inflation push middle-class earners into higher brackets. Regulatory standards that are not updated for new technologies permit environmental or financial harms that existing law was intended to prevent. Social program eligibility thresholds that remain fixed while costs rise quietly exclude people who would have qualified under the original policy intent.

In each case, the effect is to shift costs and risks from employers, polluters, and government to workers, communities, and individuals — not through dramatic legislative action but through the quiet politics of maintenance failure. The gridlock narrative, by framing inaction as mere inability, conceals this redistributive function.

### The Accountability Displacement Problem

When policymaking migrates from Congress to executive agencies, courts, and presidential directives, the accountability structure of democratic governance is quietly rewritten. Elected legislators — the officials most directly answerable to voters — are displaced by appointed bureaucrats, life-tenured judges, and presidential staff operating through arcane administrative procedures.

This has several consequences:

**Reduced transparency.** Congressional legislation is debated publicly, subjected to committee hearings, and voted on in recorded roll calls. Executive orders, agency guidance documents, and judicial opinions are produced through processes that are far less visible to the general public.

**Weakened democratic control.** Voters can replace legislators who pass unpopular laws. They cannot replace federal judges, and they have only indirect influence over executive agency actions.

**Increased technical complexity.** As policymaking migrates to administrative and judicial channels, it becomes increasingly dependent on technical expertise — legal interpretation, regulatory science, economic modeling — that excludes most citizens from meaningful participation.

**Reduced reversibility.** Some non-legislative policy changes are harder to reverse than legislation. Court decisions establishing constitutional rights can only be overturned by subsequent court decisions or constitutional amendments. Agency regulations that have generated reliance interests are subject to legal challenges when revoked. Executive orders, while theoretically easy to reverse, often create institutional facts on the ground that subsequent presidents find difficult to undo.

### The International Comparison

The gridlock narrative is peculiarly American. Parliamentary systems — which concentrate legislative power in the governing majority and lack the American system's multiple veto points (bicameral legislature, presidential veto, filibuster, judicial review, federalism) — do not generate the same narrative of legislative paralysis. This is partly because parliamentary systems genuinely produce more legislation, but it is also partly because the gridlock narrative is a cultural artifact specific to American expectations about democratic governance.

Americans expect their legislature to be simultaneously responsive to popular will (passing the policies that majorities support), deliberative (carefully considering consequences), representative (reflecting the diversity of a continental nation), and efficient (producing results quickly). These expectations are in fundamental tension. The American constitutional system was designed to prioritize deliberation and representation over responsiveness and efficiency — to make it *hard* to pass laws, as a check on majoritarian excess. The gridlock narrative, in a sense, is the complaint that the system is working as designed — a design that was never intended to maximize legislative throughput.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes piece on legislative gridlock is unusual in the Truth Vault corpus because it is not a fact-check in the traditional sense. It is an opinion essay by political scientist Jeb Barnes, originally published in The Conversation and republished by Snopes with an explicit disclaimer that the content "does not represent the work of Snopes fact-checkers or editors." Snopes did not rate the gridlock claim as True or False; it offered the analysis as context for readers considering the widespread assumption of congressional dysfunction.

This editorial choice is itself epistemologically interesting. Traditional fact-checking works best with discrete, falsifiable claims: "A rock star had semen pumped from their stomach" (False). "Taps was found in a Confederate soldier's pocket" (False). The gridlock narrative is a different kind of claim — diffuse, qualitative, rooted in interpretation rather than fact. Is "Congress is gridlocked" true or false? The answer depends on what you mean by "gridlocked," what baseline you're comparing against, and which dimensions of policymaking you include in your assessment.

Snopes' decision to publish an analytical essay rather than a binary verdict reflects the limits of the True/False framework when applied to systemic claims about institutional performance. The gridlock myth is not factually false in the way that the stomach pump legend is factually false. It is a framing — a way of organizing real observations (the filibuster does block legislation, partisan polarization has increased, high-profile legislative initiatives do fail) into a narrative (the system is broken, nothing gets done) that misrepresents the totality of the evidence.

Addressing this kind of claim requires not debunking but reframing — providing a more complete picture that includes the evidence the gridlock narrative excludes. Barnes' essay does this by widening the lens: from Congress alone to the full policymaking apparatus, from failed bills to passed laws, from legislation to executive action to policy drift.

The challenge for fact-checkers dealing with systemic myths is that no single correction can displace a narrative that is reinforced daily by media coverage, political rhetoric, and lived experience. A person who believes Congress is gridlocked because they watched a month of CNN coverage and saw only stories about obstruction cannot be corrected by a single article pointing to legislative output statistics. The correction must compete not with a single false claim but with an entire information environment structured to produce and reproduce the belief.

This is the frontier of fact-checking as a discipline: the territory beyond discrete claims, where the objects of analysis are not individual falsehoods but systemic distortions — the frames, assumptions, and narratives that shape how people interpret facts they encounter. The gridlock myth is false not because its component observations are false (the filibuster does exist, partisan polarization is real) but because the interpretive frame it applies to those observations is misleading. Fact-checking this kind of claim requires something more than verification; it requires epistemological reconstruction — showing not just what is true but how the same set of truths can support very different understandings of reality.
