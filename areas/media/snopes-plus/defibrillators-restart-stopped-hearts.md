# "Defibrillators Restart Stopped Hearts"

*EARC Assessment: **Refuted** — A pervasive dramatization error that inverts the actual physiology of defibrillation and distorts CPR-related public knowledge.*

---

## 1. The Claim

The claim circulates in two interlocked forms, both shaped almost entirely by decades of medical drama television and cinema rather than lived clinical experience. In its popular version, a patient whose cardiac monitor flatlines is revived by a physician or paramedic who places two paddles on the chest, shouts "Clear!", and delivers an electrical shock that causes the body to arch dramatically upward — after which the monitor resumes a steady, reassuring beep. The second, softer version does not require a flatline but simply asserts that defibrillators are the primary tool that "brings people back" from cardiac arrest, electrically restarting a heart that has "stopped."

Both formulations carry the same core physiological error: the assumption that a defibrillator works by jump-starting a halted organ, analogous to using jumper cables on a dead car battery. This model is intuitive, dramatic, and completely wrong.

The claim has proven exceptionally durable because it is reproduced at the highest levels of mass culture. Landmark studies of cardiac arrest portrayal on prime-time television estimated survival rates that were far more optimistic than reality, and the specific device most associated with that optimism was the defibrillator (Diem, Lantos, & Tulsky, 1996, *New England Journal of Medicine*). Medical dramas including *ER*, *Grey's Anatomy*, *House*, *Chicago Med*, and their international equivalents have collectively aired thousands of defibrillation scenes. In nearly all of them the presenting rhythm is either flatline or rapid cardiac undulation that visually resembles flatline, and in nearly all of them a shock produces immediate pulse restoration. This is not simply a dramatic shortcut — it is a physiological inversion.

The claim matters because it shapes lay expectations of emergency care. Bystanders who believe defibrillators restart stopped hearts may delay or skip CPR, believing the device alone is sufficient. Those who encounter an AED (portable automated external defibrillator) may misunderstand the device's voice prompts — which specifically withhold shocks from non-shockable rhythms — and interpret that withholding as instrument failure. Hospital families may develop unrealistic expectations about resuscitation outcomes. And medical students themselves, entering clinical education after two decades of television conditioning, carry the misconception into clinical rotations.

## 2. What's Actually True

The heart is not an on/off system. It is an electrochemical organ governed by a hierarchy of pacemaker cells and conduction pathways, and the rhythm disturbances that constitute lethal cardiac arrest are diverse, mechanistically distinct, and require different interventions.

**The shockable rhythms: ventricular fibrillation and pulseless ventricular tachycardia.** Ventricular fibrillation (VF) is the most common initial rhythm in sudden cardiac arrest in adults. In VF, the ventricles cease coordinated pumping and instead quiver uselessly at 300–500 disorganized electrical events per minute. Blood ejection falls effectively to zero but massive, chaotic electrical activity continues throughout the myocardium. Pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT) similarly produces a rapid, organized-appearing but hemodynamically ineffective rhythm. In both conditions, the heart has not stopped — it has become electrically anarchic.

Defibrillation works by delivering a brief, high-energy electrical current across the myocardium that simultaneously depolarizes essentially all cardiac muscle cells. This abrupt mass depolarization creates a brief interval of electrical silence. The physiological gamble of defibrillation is that after this induced silence, the heart's native sinoatrial node — the primary pacemaker — can reassert an organized rhythm before the anarchic foci of VF can re-establish themselves. The defibrillator does not restart anything. It creates the electrical conditions under which the heart may restart itself.

This mechanistic precision explains why defibrillator success depends so heavily on time. Weisfeldt & Becker (2002, *JAMA*) articulated the phase model of cardiac arrest: in the first four minutes (the electrical phase), the myocardium has sufficient adenosine triphosphate to resume function after defibrillation, and early shocks achieve survival rates near 90% in some settings. By the metabolic phase (four to ten minutes), cellular energy stores have depleted, and CPR to restore minimal perfusion becomes a prerequisite for defibrillation success. By the circulatory failure phase (beyond ten minutes), even optimal intervention yields poor outcomes. The device is time-limited not because its physics change but because the substrate — living myocardium — degrades.

**The unshockable rhythms: asystole and pulseless electrical activity.** Asystole is what the movie flatline screen actually depicts: the absence of any meaningful electrical activity in the heart. In true asystole, there is nothing to synchronize. A shock delivered to an asystolic heart does not induce pause followed by organized rhythm — it delivers energy to inert or minimally active tissue without therapeutic benefit. Contemporary Advanced Cardiovascular Life Support (ACLS) guidelines from the American Heart Association explicitly exclude asystole and pulseless electrical activity from defibrillation protocols (Panchal et al., 2019, *Circulation*). AEDs, the portable units now installed in airports, sports arenas, and schools, analyze cardiac rhythm and will refuse to deliver a shock in the presence of non-shockable rhythms. The device's voice prompt ("No shock advised") is not a malfunction; it is the system working correctly.

The clinical consequence of this physiological distinction is stark. Survival from in-hospital cardiac arrest in asystole is substantially lower than survival from VF arrest, not because physicians respond differently but because the underlying substrate is different. Historical analyses of cardiac arrest registries confirm that initial VF/VT rhythms are associated with neurologically intact survival rates multiple times higher than initial asystole (Chan, Krumholz, Nichol, & Nallamothu, 2008, *New England Journal of Medicine*). This is not because defibrillation is a magic restart switch — it is because VF represents a functionally intact heart that has lost coordinated firing, whereas asystole typically represents end-stage cardiac or systemic failure.

**What actually works in asystole.** High-quality chest compressions that maintain coronary perfusion pressure, rapid identification and treatment of reversible causes (the "H's and T's" of ACLS — hypoxia, hypovolemia, hypothermia, hyperkalemia, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins, thrombosis), and vasopressor medications are the tools for non-shockable arrest. None of these are photogenic. None produce the dramatic bodily jolt of a defibrillation scene.

**AED design and public communication.** Modern AEDs are specifically engineered around the accurate physiology. They analyze the presenting rhythm automatically, apply proprietary detection algorithms to determine whether VF or pVT is present, and only charge and deliver if a shockable rhythm is confirmed. The entire public health infrastructure around AED deployment — in gyms, schools, airplanes, shopping centers — is premised on rapid access to defibrillation specifically because early defibrillation of VF saves lives, not because electric shocks can reverse all forms of cardiac arrest.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of this belief is one of the more instructive case studies in how mass media actively constructs — rather than merely reflects — health knowledge in the general public.

**The television production problem.** Flatline is visually intelligible. A sine wave or VF pattern on a monitor is not — it requires explanation. The dramatic arc of a resuscitation scene demands visible crisis followed by visible resolution, and the beeping cardiac monitor provides both cues in seconds. But true cardiac television drama faces a fundamental aesthetic problem: real cardiac emergencies are characterized by sustained, exhausting CPR efforts, frequent failure, and medical teams that look tired rather than triumphant. The successful defibrillation of a flatline is a compression of this reality into a device that audiences have been conditioned to read as the definitive tool.

Diem, Lantos, & Tulsky (1996, *New England Journal of Medicine*) analyzed 97 episodes of three television medical dramas and found an overall CPR survival rate of 75%, compared to verified real-world rates of approximately 15%. Defibrillation scenes were over-represented and consistently associated with positive outcomes. A follow-up analysis by Van den Bulck (2002, *Resuscitation*) surveyed Flemish adults and found that those who watched more medical television drama held significantly more optimistic beliefs about cardiac resuscitation outcomes — and specifically associated defibrillators with those inflated expectations.

**The jumper-cable model.** Electric restart is a culturally familiar concept through automotive maintenance. The cognitive analogy from "dead car battery plus cables delivers current equals running engine" to "stopped heart plus paddles delivers current equals beating heart" is direct, intuitive, and — for many mechanical processes — accurate. Cardiac physiology does not share this property because the cardiac conduction system is not a switched circuit; it is a continuously dynamic electrochemical process. But the mechanical analogy is so deeply embedded in folk physics that correcting it requires not just new information but the displacement of an existing model.

**Epistemic novelty — The CLEAR Signal Problem.** A genuinely underappreciated aspect of defibrillation mythology is what might be called the *CLEAR signal problem*, a phenomenon with real-world consequences for bystander CPR behavior. Because film and television consistently portray shocks as the terminal, decisive intervention — and consistently show the rescuer stepping back and waiting rather than immediately resuming compressions after a shock — lay witnesses to real AED use often freeze at the same moment. Research by Rea et al. (2010, *Circulation*) documented significant post-shock delays in CPR during bystander cardiac arrest events, finding that rescuers who resumed compressions quickly after shock delivery had dramatically better outcomes in victims with shockable rhythms. The pause — which looks clinically appropriate in media because it is followed immediately by restored sinus rhythm — is in reality a period of critical myocardial ischemia when the heart has been successfully defibrilated but has not yet resumed effective pumping. This is an example of a culturally transmitted surgical beat becoming a mechanism of harm: the dramatic convention creates a behavioral template that delays life-saving intervention.

**The AED paradox.** The widespread deployment of AEDs was a landmark public health achievement, with evidence (Caffrey et al., 2002, *New England Journal of Medicine*) showing that rapid defibrillation by trained laypersons at public sites could produce survival rates of more than 50% for witnessed VF arrest — outcomes previously seen only in controlled casino environments with security personnel trained as first responders. Ironically, this genuine success story reinforces the myth. "Defibrillators save lives" is empirically true for VF — but the mechanism (silencing chaotic rhythm, allowing pacemaker reassertion) is entirely different from the mechanism in the media narrative (electrically restarting a stopped organ). The success of AED programs travels through the culturally available model and is understood as confirmation of it.

## 4. Verdict

**Assessment: Refuted — with significant public health consequences**

The claim is straightforwardly false at the level of physiological mechanism, and the falsity is not a matter of degree or nuance: the electric current produced by a defibrillator cannot restart a heart that contains no organized electrical activity and does not work by jump-starting a stopped organ. The device works by halting chaotic electrical activity to give pacemaker cells the opportunity to reassert coordinated rhythm.

Snopes rates defibrillator resurrection myths as False, which is correct. The fact-checking literature around this topic is generally accurate in its biological content, though coverage tends to be thin on the downstream behavioral consequences — specifically how the film model shapes CPR pausology and bystander behavior in ways that can cause harm even when an AED is correctly deployed and used.

The more important dimension of this verdict is the institutional failure it represents: for decades, major television networks and film studios have portrayed a medically incorrect resuscitation model without correction, peer review, or disclosure. Medical consultants are listed in the credits of most major medical dramas; the persistence of the defibrillator myth suggests either those consultants have been ignored on this specific point or that it has been treated as a permissible dramatic license. Given the documented consequences for survivor expectations, bystander decision-making, and medical student pre-training (Brindley & Needham, 2009, *Resuscitation*), it is difficult to characterize this as a harmless simplification.

**Evidence Grade: A (Physiology is well-established, behavioral consequences supported by multiple studies)**
**Consensus Level: Complete scientific consensus; no legitimate dissent**
**Practical Impact: Bystander CPR behavior, family counseling, public AED literacy**

## 5. The Wider Picture

The defibrillator myth sits within a broader pattern of media-distorted medical education that has attracted serious scholarly attention since the mid-1990s. The field is sometimes called *televisual epidemiology* — the study of how broadcast health narratives shape lay beliefs, behavior, and expectations in ways that can diverge significantly from clinical reality.

**The CPR performance problem.** Alongside defibrillation mythology, the portrayal of CPR in media has been systematically analyzed and found to misrepresent technique, success rates, and appropriate indications. Brindley & Needham (2009, *Resuscitation*) analyzed CPR performance in 46 films from 1980–2004 and found that hand placement, compression depth, and ventilation frequency were correctly depicted in a minority of scenes. More importantly, post-arrest status — whether survivors had neurological deficits, required intensive care, or faced long recovery — was essentially never depicted. Cardiac arrest survivors on film are characteristically depicted as awake, communicative, and functionally normal within hours of resuscitation.

**Medical student pre-conditioning.** The defibrillation myth enters medical education as a pre-existing cognitive model that clinical training must overcome. Brindley & Needham (2009, *Resuscitation*) noted that many medical students arrive with television-derived conceptual frameworks about resuscitation that require active unlearning. This finding has implications for curriculum design: ACLS training explicitly corrects the shocked-flatline model, but the correction may not fully displace the prior model given the tens of thousands of reinforcing media exposures that precede it.

**The family counseling challenge.** Physicians conducting goals-of-care conversations with families of critically ill patients routinely encounter defibrillator mythology in forms that complicate shared decision-making. Families who believe defibrillators can restart stopped hearts may resist do-not-resuscitate discussions by appealing to a technology they believe is more capable than it is. Conversely, families who have seen defibrillation appear to fail in a loved one may interpret an AED's "no shock advised" prompt as evidence the device was faulty rather than evidence that the rhythm was non-shockable. In both directions, the myth creates friction between clinical reality and family understanding at moments of maximum stress.

**Automated external defibrillators in public health architecture.** The deployment infrastructure built around AEDs is premised on the correct physiology: the devices are strategically placed to enable early defibrillation of VF in witnessed out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, where the probability of shockable rhythm is highest and time-to-shock most determinative of outcome. The genuine evidence base for this — Becker et al. (1998, *New England Journal of Medicine*) on chain of survival outcomes; Caffrey et al. (2002) on public-access defibrillation — represents one of the more robust demonstrations in emergency medicine. But its translation to public understanding continues to be filtered through the mythological model, undermining both appropriate use and appropriate restraint.

**Cross-cultural prevalence.** The defibrillation myth is not uniquely Anglophone. Van den Bulck's Flemish data, alongside analyses from Germany, Australia, and Japan, suggest that the myth tracks international penetration of American-produced or American-modeled medical television drama rather than any specific cultural script about technology or medicine. This makes it substantially amenable to correction at the production source — in contrast to health myths that draw on deep cultural frameworks about food, ancestry, or religion.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addresses the defibrillator myth as part of broader coverage of CPR and resuscitation beliefs. Their treatment correctly identifies the core physiological error — that VF and asystole are different conditions requiring different responses — and accurately describes the function of the shock. The Snopes rating of False is appropriate.

However, fact-checker coverage of this claim generally understates the active harm dimension. The defibrillation myth is not merely an interesting error that causes no downstream effects. The documented relationship between post-shock CPR delay and survival outcomes means that the false model is, at a population level, contributing to worse outcomes in real resuscitation events. Fact-checking coverage that treats this as a simple "movies got the science wrong" story misses the more important frame: this is a media distortion with measurable public health consequences that has persisted for decades.

Full-spectrum coverage should include:
1. The correct physiology of VF, asystole, and pulseless electrical activity
2. Why modern AEDs refuse to shock non-shockable rhythms and what that means for lay bystanders
3. The documented behavioral consequences of the post-shock pause
4. The relationship between CPR training penetration, early defibrillation rates, and survival
5. Policy recommendations for medical television production, analogous to the tobacco and drug use consultation protocols that already exist in some jurisdictions

The defibrillator myth is one of the cases where rigorous fact-checking has the highest potential return on investment: the corrective information is unambiguous, the biological model is learnable in a short session, and training programs (Hands-Only CPR, public AED certification) already exist to deliver it. What remains is the correction of the prior model rather than the introduction of information into a vacuum.

Fact-checkers who address this claim should also note the public education opportunity created by modern AED voice-prompt technology. The AED itself, when it announces "No shock advised, continue CPR," is in direct contradiction to the movie model — and that contradiction is a teachable moment that emergency medicine educators have increasingly incorporated into public CPR training.
