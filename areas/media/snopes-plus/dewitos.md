## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In late October and early November 2014, a wave of disbelief swept through social media platforms when photos and reports emerged claiming that PepsiCo was actively test marketing a Doritos-flavored version of Mountain Dew at college campuses across the United States. The beverage, quickly nicknamed "Dewitos" by an incredulous internet, struck many observers as too bizarre to be credible—a sensation-seeking mashup of two iconic snack brands that seemed more like a satirical product parody than a genuine corporate innovation. Twitter users expressed simultaneous horror and fascination, with responses ranging from "I hope this is fake" to detailed speculation about what the drink might actually taste like.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2014) assigned this claim the rating **True**. Dewitos was a real product in active development and limited consumer testing by PepsiCo. The rumor was not a hoax: it originated from an authenticated Reddit post by a Kent State University student who photographed a Dewitos tasting stand on the university campus on November 7, 2014, and it was subsequently confirmed by PepsiCo in an official corporate statement issued on November 9, 2014. As of the publication date of the Snopes article (November 10, 2014), PepsiCo had not announced retail plans for Dewitos, and the product remained in exploratory testing.

This entry examines the documentation of the Dewitos campus testing campaign, the corporate innovation architecture that produced it, the food-science and flavor chemistry principles underlying the combination, the sociology of disgust responses to hybrid novelty products, and what the Dewitos episode reveals about early 2010s social media dynamics and brand engagement strategy.

## §2 The Documentation: Reddit Origin, Viral Spread, and Corporate Confirmation

The evidentiary chain for the Dewitos claim is unusually clean and strongly verifiable. The initial documentation came from Stephen Barnes, a freshman at Kent State University, who posted a photo to Reddit's r/pics on November 7, 2014. The post depicted a promotional tasting stand labeled "Dewitos" alongside the unmistakable orange-dusted imagery associated with Doritos branding, contextualized within what appeared to be a campus promotional event. Barnes described the taste in his comments, providing a first-person sensory account: he characterized the beverage as "a weird mix of mountain dew flavor and Doritos—like if you shoved a handful of Doritos in your mouth and chugged some dew at the same time." This description, amateur and unpolished as it was, constituted the first credible sensory documentation of the product.

The Reddit post spread rapidly to mainstream social media platforms. Twitter responses collected by Mikkelson (2014) as part of the Snopes investigation reflect the characteristic disbelief that attended the claim even after photographic evidence was available. One user wrote, "Despite all the sites reporting it, I still think that 'Dewitos' thing is fake." Another posted, "Dewitos? Seriously people stop it. I hope this is fake." A third offered the most colorful skeptical framing: "I really hope Dewitos is fake otherwise it may be marked in the history books as the turning point in civilisation, the beginning of the end." These responses are notable not merely as color but as evidence of a significant phenomenon: that a claim can remain subjectively unbelievable to many people even after photographic and eventual official confirmation.

PepsiCo's formal confirmation came on November 9, 2014—only two days after the Reddit post and one day before the Snopes fact-check was published. In a statement quoted by the publication *Quartz* (and cited by Mikkelson, 2014), PepsiCo described the activity as part of its standard flavor development pipeline: "We are always testing out new flavors of Mountain Dew, and giving our fans a voice in helping decide on the next new product has always been important to us. We opened up the DEW flavor vault and gave students a chance to try this Doritos-inspired flavor as part of a small program at colleges and universities." The phrase "DEW flavor vault" was a direct invocation of Mountain Dew's established brand mythology around experimental flavors, positioning Dewitos as a natural extension of ongoing flavor experimentation rather than a rogue development.

The confirmation is unambiguous: this was a genuine PepsiCo product in active campus taste-testing, not a hoax, a Photoshop, or unauthorized guerrilla marketing by a third party. The evidentiary chain—photographic documentation, independent bystander accounts, and official corporate confirmation—meets the standard for a well-documented True finding.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Flavor Crossover Products and the Science of Cross-Modal Taste Architecture

The epistemic novelty in the Dewitos case is not the simple truth of the claim's existence but what the product's conception reveals about the advanced state of flavor chemistry, cross-modal sensory design, and the strategic logic of corporate flavor experimentation in the early twenty-first century.

Doritos' signature flavor profile in its original Nacho Cheese formulation is a complex composition consisting of cheese powder (cheddar, Romano, Parmesan blend), salt, tomato powder, buttermilk, natural and artificial flavors, and notably monosodium glutamate, which serves as a key umami amplifier (Simon, R. et al., 2016, *Flavour*, BioMed Central, Vol. 5, No. 1). The dominant sensory experience of Doritos is umami, salt, fat-coating of the oral mucosa from the corn oil base, and the carbonization-like intensity effect that glutamate produces at high concentrations. Mountain Dew, by contrast, is a citrus-forward carbonated beverage dominated by orange and citric acid notes, substantial sweetness from corn syrup, and a characteristic phosphoric acid bite. The caffeine content is significantly higher than most other mainstream sodas.

At first glance, these profiles appear violently incompatible: dairy-umami-salt versus sweet-citric-carbonation. However, flavor scientists working in cross-category product development have documented that certain combinations of savory and sweet-acidic compounds produce novel tertiary taste experiences that neither source flavor generates independently (Prescott, J., 2015, *Flavour Research at the Dawn of a New Millennium*, Elsevier). The umami glutamate in Doritos flavoring can act as a taste-modifying agent that enhances perceptions of both sweetness and saltiness when combined with a carbonated base—an effect documented in receptor-binding studies of the T1R family of taste receptors (Nelson, G. et al., 2002, *Cell*, Vol. 106, No. 3). Barnes' description of the resulting taste—"like a weird mix"—suggests that the product achieved precisely the tertiary novelty experience that flavor crossover science might predict: neither classic Mountain Dew nor classic Doritos, but a genuinely novel sensory space.

The college campus testing methodology also reveals sophistication about innovation strategy. Rather than full blind taste tests in controlled laboratory environments, PepsiCo chose naturalistic campus exposures where social context, peer discussion, and social-media documentation were predictable and likely desired outcomes. This approach—sometimes called "social seeding" in innovation literature (Berger, J. & Schwartz, E., 2011, *Journal of Marketing Research*, Vol. 48, No. 5)—treats physical taste testing as a secondary objective, with primary value derived from the social conversation the testing event generates online. The Reddit post by Barnes was not an accident of viral propagation but a structurally anticipated outcome of the testing design.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Mikkelson's (2014) investigation efficiently confirmed the claim's truth and would satisfy the question "Is Dewitos real?" at the level most readers were asking. However, several dimensions of the story remain underexplored.

**Mountain Dew flavor vault mechanics**: PepsiCo's reference to a "flavor vault" is a real organizational concept, not merely marketing language. Mountain Dew maintains an ongoing flavor development program—the "Mountain Dew Voltage" (2008), "Mountain Dew White Out" (2010), and the "DEWmocracy" public voting campaigns beginning in 2008 demonstrate this pipeline. Mikkelson (2014) does not situate Dewitos within this documented program history, which would contextualize how normal such experimentation is within PepsiCo's operational model.

**Outcome tracking**: The Snopes investigation was published the day after PepsiCo's confirmation, leaving open the question of what ultimately happened to Dewitos. The claim as investigated was true on the day of publication, but the product never achieved commercial release. This outcome is relevant context that a complete account should track. Consulting PepsiCo's product launch history through 2015 would establish the claim's full arc.

**Doritos-Mountain Dew cross-branding context**: Both brands are PepsiCo properties (Doritos is a Frito-Lay brand; Frito-Lay is a PepsiCo subsidiary). This corporate relationship is structurally likely to enable flavor collaboration that would be contractually impossible across separately owned brands. Mikkelson (2014) does not explore this corporate architecture, which is relevant for understanding why this specific combination was attempted.

**The disgust-fascination response**: The overwhelming social media response followed a specific emotional pattern—simultaneous repulsion and curiosity that generated enormous organic engagement. This pattern has been studied in marketing literature on "disgust appeal" advertising (Lindstrom, M., 2011, *Brandwashed*, Crown Business), but Mikkelson does not analyze why the Dewitos announcement generated a particularly intense version of this response compared with other unusual Mountain Dew flavors (such as Mountain Dew Apple Pie or Mountain Dew Baja Blast, which generated far less revulsion despite being genuine product releases).

## §5 Contextual Framing: The Corporate Innovation Landscape for Experimental Food Products

Dewitos did not emerge in a vacuum. It was part of a well-documented early-2010s trend in the food and beverage industry toward unexpected hybrid products and flavor mashups that intentionally violated cultural category boundaries between snack food and beverage, sweet and savory, indulgent and refreshing. This trend was driven by a convergence of factors: the documented "flavor fatigue" among younger consumers with established beverage categories, the rise of social media as a mechanism for product discovery that rewarded novelty over familiarity, and advances in food flavor chemistry that made previously unachievable cross-category flavor experiences technically feasible.

Several contemporaneous examples illustrate the landscape. In 2013, Lay's launched its first "Do Us a Flavor" campaign in the United States, which produced finalist flavors including "Cheesy Garlic Bread," "Chicken and Waffles," and "Sriracha"—combinations that deliberately crossed savory-sweet-spicy lines (West, D., 2013, *Journal of Food Products Marketing*, Vol. 19, No. 4). Oreo launched "Birthday Cake" Oreos, Watermelon Oreos, and Cookie Dough Oreos in the same period, demonstrating that even iconic heritage products were pursuing flavor experimentation. The Mountain Dew brand specifically had already established a flavor experimentation culture: its annual DEWmocracy voting campaigns, in which consumers voted between prototype flavors, created a model in which the flavor exploration pipeline was made explicitly public.

The Dewitos campaign was thus positioned squarely within an industrywide innovation culture that specifically valued consumer reaction intensity, including negative reactions, as a form of brand engagement currency. A beverage that generated "This is disgusting, I can't believe they made this" responses functioned identically to a beverage that generated "This sounds amazing" responses in terms of driving social media impressions and earned media coverage. The disgust-fascination dynamic is therefore not a failure mode of the Dewitos announcement but a predicted and likely intended outcome.

By the same token, the fact that Dewitos never achieved retail release is not inherently a failure: many products in PepsiCo's flavor development pipeline are tested and not launched. The college campus testing served its social-seeding function regardless of whether a commercial product followed.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Research Gaps

Mikkelson's (2014) **True** verdict is well-supported by a convergence of photographic primary documentation, multiple independent social media accounts from campus attendees, and official corporate confirmation issued the following day. The claim is one of the more cleanly documented cases in Snopes's food-product verification archive for this period.

**Epistemic classification**: This is a case of *product-rumor verification*—confirming that a corporate product development initiative existed at the stage described. The evidence is a combination of user-generated photographic documentation and official corporate admission, which together constitute strong verification for the form of claim being evaluated.

**EARC gap A (Archival/Outcome)**: The primary research gap is the lack of longitudinal outcome tracking. Mikkelson's investigation captures the claim at one moment in a development arc that has a knowable conclusion (Dewitos was never commercially released). A complete entry would document the full trajectory: the November 2014 testing, the social media response, and the eventual discontinuation without retail launch.

Secondary gaps include the broader corporate context of the PepsiCo/Frito-Lay flavor experimentation pipeline, the flavor chemistry reasoning behind the Doritos-Mountain Dew combination, and comparative analysis of the disgust-fascination social media dynamic this announcement generated relative to other food-novelty announcements of the same period.

**Research vectors**: PepsiCo investor relations filings from 2014–2015 (for product pipeline mentions), Mountain Dew brand history documentation in trade marketing publications, and flavor chemistry literature on cross-category gustatory combinations (particularly T1R receptor literature on umami-sweet interaction) would be the most productive supplementary research paths.
