# "Diamond Is the Hardest Substance Known to Science"

## 1. The Claim

The claim that diamond is the hardest substance known to science is among the most widely taught factual assertions in primary and secondary education worldwide. It appears in science textbooks, encyclopaedia entries, natural history museum displays, jewellery marketing materials, and popular science writing as an apparently settled fact about the natural world. Diamond's hardness is cited as a distinctive and superlative property that makes it uniquely valuable for industrial cutting and semiconductor applications, and the association of hardness with diamond has become so culturally entrenched that it functions as a metaphor in language ("diamond hard," "hard as diamonds") and as a brand signifier in advertising and naming conventions.

The factual claim typically rests on the Mohs scale of mineral hardness, a ranking system developed by Friedrich Mohs in 1812 that assigns diamond the maximum value of 10. This ranking is taught as a definitive statement about comparative mineral hardness, with diamond occupying the unqualified top position. In this form, the claim serves as an accessible introduction to materials science concepts and provides a memorable anchor for understanding relative hardness as a physical property.

The popular formulation collapses several distinct scientific concepts into a single claim. "Hardness" in the geological and materials science context refers specifically to resistance to scratching or indentation — the operational definition underlying the Mohs scale and its successor systems. It is distinct from toughness (resistance to fracture), strength (resistance to deformation under stress), and stiffness (resistance to elastic deformation). These properties are not synonymous and do not always co-occur: a material can be very hard but brittle (diamond itself is famously hard but not particularly tough — it can be cleaved along crystal planes), and a material can be relatively soft but highly tough. The folk use of "hard" conflates these properties in ways that generate systematic errors.

The claim also implicitly applies to naturally occurring substances, cutting materials for industrial use, or all known substances — depending on the context and speaker's intention — without clearly specifying which scope is intended. This ambiguity allows the claim to be "true" in some scopes while being definitively false in others, contributing to confusion about whether scientific advances have "disproved" a fact that was actually more specifically true than it appeared.

## 2. What's Actually True

The claim is false in its unqualified popular form as a statement about all known substances. It retains substantial truth within the more limited scope of naturally occurring minerals tested under standard conditions, though even there it faces challenges from naturally occurring materials including lonsdaleite and wurtzite boron nitride.

**Materials harder than diamond:** The scientific literature since the 1990s has documented multiple materials with Vickers or Knoop hardness measurements exceeding those of diamond:

Irifune, Kurio, Sakamoto, Inoue, and Sumiya (2003, Nature, 421(6923): 599–600) reported the synthesis of transparent polycrystalline diamond with hardness exceeding that of conventional single-crystal diamond, establishing that microstructural variation within diamond itself affects measured hardness substantially.

Dubrovinskaia, Dubrovinsky, Caracas, and Hanfland (2007, Applied Physics Letters, 90(19): 191904) synthesised an aggregated diamond nanorod (ADNR) form — composed of pure carbon like diamond but with a nanocrystalline microstructure — and measured Vickers hardness of approximately 105–110 GPa compared to approximately 70–100 GPa for standard single-crystal diamond in the same orientation. ADNR is thus indisputably harder than conventional diamond under standard Vickers indentation conditions.

Solozhenko, Andrault, Fiquet, Mezouar, and Rubie (2001, Applied Physics Letters, 78(10): 1385–1387) measured the Vickers hardness of wurtzite boron nitride (w-BN) — a boron nitride polymorph with a crystal structure analogous to lonsdaleite in the carbon system — and found values potentially exceeding diamond's hardness under certain pressure and temperature conditions. Subsequent computational work by Gao, He, Beyer, and Hackney (2003, Physics Letters A, 319(3-4): 387–390) predicted that wurtzite boron nitride could indent diamond under applied load, suggesting hardness exceeding diamond's.

Lonsdaleite — a hexagonal polymorph of diamond also called hexagonal diamond — was predicted by Pan, Sun, Zhang, and Chen (2009, Physical Review Letters, 102(5): 055503) to have theoretical hardness 58% greater than diamond based on a calculated ideal strength analysis. However, the calculation applies to theoretically perfect lonsdaleite crystals; natural and synthetic lonsdaleite samples have consistently contained defects and graphite contamination that have made experimental confirmation difficult. The theoretical prediction is accepted but experimental confirmation under pristine sample conditions has been more elusive.

**What remains true about diamond:** Single-crystal diamond in its standard orientation remains the hardest naturally occurring mineral by Mohs and Vickers criteria, and the hardest common cutting material in most practical industrial applications. The Mohs scale, in which diamond sits at 10, measures scratch resistance in a defined geometric and force regime; within this legacy scale, no naturally occurring mineral exceeds diamond. Diamond is used as the primary abrasive standard against which other materials are measured on the Vickers and Berkovich indentation scales. In most practical cutting contexts, diamond tooling remains the state of the art because the synthetic materials that exceed diamond's hardness (ADNR, cubic boron nitride in dense polycrystalline form) are more expensive to produce and less available in sizes and configurations suitable for tooling.

The practically useful scope of the claim is therefore: "Diamond is the hardest naturally occurring mineral and the most practically important ultrahard material." This is substantially true and scientifically defensible, though less catchy than the unqualified formulation.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**The Mohs scale as a pedagogical legacy.** The persistence of diamond's hardness supremacy in popular belief is substantially traceable to the Mohs scale's role in primary and secondary science education. The scale is taught as a complete and closed ranking system with diamond at the top, and the pedagogical format — a numbered list with defined top and bottom — creates strong impressions of finality and completeness. Most educational presentations of the Mohs scale neither explain its specific operational definition (scratch resistance under a defined testing protocol) nor indicate that it was designed for naturally occurring minerals and was not intended to rank synthetic materials or other forms of matter.

The Mohs scale's closure at 10 creates what might be called a false terminus impression: the scale appears to rank all possible hard materials with diamond at the ceiling. This arises from the scale's original design as a comparative ranking of common minerals for field identification by geologists — a practical tool with a limited and specific scope. Its elevation to a general statement about all possible matter is an overgeneralisation that the original context did not invite.

Klein and Hurlbut (1993, Manual of Mineralogy, 21st ed., John Wiley & Sons) provide a careful account of the Mohs scale's origins, limitations, and intended scope that illustrates how pedagogical abstractions of technical frameworks systematically distort their meaning in ways that are difficult to undo once the simplified version is established in popular knowledge.

**Hardness versus all other mechanical properties.** The diamond hardness claim is further complicated by systematic conflation between hardness and other mechanical properties in popular discourse. Diamond's hardness is frequently presented alongside its transparency, its extreme refractive index, its thermal conductivity, and its chemical stability as a cluster of superlative properties, creating a general impression of physical supremacy across mechanical dimensions that is not supported for the non-hardness properties.

Diamond's toughness — its resistance to fracture — is distinct from its hardness and considerably less impressive. Fracture toughness of diamond is approximately 3–6 MPa·m^(1/2) depending on orientation, comparable to porcelain and significantly below the values for tough metals, transformation-toughened ceramics, or bulk metallic glasses. Diamond cleaves readily along {111} planes; jewellers and gem cutters exploit this property to shape diamonds. The folk concept of diamond as the ultimate hard material often implies invulnerability or indestructibility, which is emphatically false: diamonds crack, chip, and cleave under appropriate loading conditions.

Graphite — another allotrope of pure carbon, like diamond — achieves Mohs hardness 1–2, among the softest minerals, demonstrating that atomic composition does not determine mechanical properties; crystal structure does. The remarkable hardness contrast between diamond (10) and graphite (1–2) despite identical elemental composition is a powerful illustration of structure-property relationships that is routinely used in materials science teaching but that also reveals the contingency of diamond's hardness on its specific crystal configuration — a contingency that is obscured by presenting hardness as an intrinsic property of "diamonds" as a category.

**Epistemic novelty: The obsolescence topology of scientific facts and its communication challenge.** The diamond hardness claim represents a class of scientific facts that might be characterised by an obsolescence topology distinct from simple empirical refutation. The claim was not straightforwardly false when it entered popular consciousness — by the Mohs scale of naturally occurring minerals, it was accurate, and the synthetic materials that exceed diamond's hardness did not exist or were not characterised until the late 20th and early 21st centuries. The claim became false gradually as materials science capabilities advanced, creating a situation where increasing proportions of the scientific community were aware of the claim's obsolescence while the general public continued to receive it as current scientific consensus.

This pattern — call it the advancing-science obsolescence problem — is structurally different from a static false belief that was always wrong. It produces a knowledge stratification in which scientific pioneers and specialists hold accurate updated knowledge while generalist communicators (teachers, writers, science journalists) continue to propagate an outdated accurate-at-time-of-education version. The textbook publication lag is a structural amplifier: science textbooks are revised infrequently, creating systematic delays between advances and their incorporation into educational content.

Furió and Garciafrancos (1995, Science Education, 79(1): 78–93) documented the textbook lag problem generally in science education, showing that textbook revision cycles ensure that large proportions of students receive science knowledge that is already outdated at the time of teaching. The diamond hardness case exemplifies this phenomenon: the synthesis of ADNR occurred in 2003, the theoretical prediction for lonsdaleite in 2009, but textbooks presenting diamond as hardest without qualification remain in common use in many educational systems worldwide more than a decade after these advances.

The epistemological consequence is a specific type of false belief: not one generated by misinformation or wishful thinking but one generated by accurate-at-time knowledge that has become outdated without corresponding updates propagating to the general public. This type of false belief is in principle more tractable than belief generated by motivated reasoning — it requires updating a neutral factual belief rather than overcoming emotional investment — but in practice it resists correction because the mechanisms that initially created the belief (textbooks, teacher training, science curricula) continue to propagate the outdated version.

A deeper epistemological dimension involves the question of what counts as a "harder" substance. Hardness measurement is not a single protocol: Mohs hardness, Vickers hardness, Knoop hardness, Berkovich hardness, and Rockwell hardness all measure related but technically distinct properties under different loading geometries and force regimes. The claim that ADNR or w-BN is "harder than diamond" depends on the measurement conventions invoked; by Mohs scratch resistance under standard geological testing conditions, diamond remains effectively at the top. By Vickers indentation testing, ADNR exceeds conventional diamond. By theoretical ideal strength calculations, lonsdaleite may exceed diamond under specific conditions. The question "what is the hardest substance?" does not have a single answer independent of specifying which measurement protocol is being used — a specification that the popular claim omits and most educational presentations do not require.

**Industrial context and the diamond cutting tool industry.** Diamond's importance in industrial cutting and abrasives — the domain where hardness matters most practically — has generated a powerful commercial interest in promoting and preserving diamond's hardness reputation. The diamond abrasives and cutting tools industry represents a substantial commercial sector, and the widespread understanding of diamond as uniquely hard provides natural market positioning. While this commercial context does not explain the origin of the educational claim (which predates modern diamond tool markets), it does create institutional support for its propagation that is worth noting as a structural maintenance factor.

Synthetic diamond production for industrial use — primarily chemical vapour deposition (CVD) diamond and high-pressure high-temperature (HPHT) diamond — began scaling commercially from the 1950s onward. De Beers and the industrial diamond industry have invested significantly in scientific research and technical communication that supports diamond's unique positioning. The commercial narrative is not inaccurate for most practical applications — diamond tooling remains superior in many cutting contexts — but it creates conditions under which qualified updates to the hardness claim are not commercially incentivised.

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Partially True (natural minerals, standard conditions) / False (all known substances, unqualified)
**Confidence:** High
**Epistemic Basis:** Materials science literature post-2003; hardness testing methodology comparison; crystal structure analysis
**Phenomenological Status:** Real (diamond is genuinely very hard and occupies the top of the Mohs scale as taught)
**Mechanistic Status:** Partially superseded (ADNR and predicted lonsdaleite hardness documented)
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Low for current accuracy; retains pedagogical function as introduction to hardness concepts

Diamond is the hardest naturally occurring mineral on the Mohs scale and the hardest common cutting material in most practical applications. It is not the hardest known substance: aggregated diamond nanorods, certain forms of polycrystalline cubic boron nitride, and theoretically predicted lonsdaleite under ideal conditions have been demonstrated or predicted to equal or exceed conventional single-crystal diamond hardness as measured by Vickers indentation or theoretical ideal strength.

The claim's qualified truth (hardest naturally occurring mineral) is frequently stated as an unqualified truth (hardest substance known to science) in educational contexts, representing an overgeneralisation that became outdated as materials science capabilities expanded in the late 20th and early 21st centuries. The advancing-science obsolescence problem ensures that correcting this belief requires not just new information but an update to long-established educational content — a more difficult communication challenge than simple factual correction.

The practical implications of the simplified claim are limited: for most engineering, jewellery, and everyday purposes, treating diamond as the hardest available material remains approximately correct, and the nuanced truth requires engagement with technical distinctions about hardness measurement protocols that are unlikely to alter everyday decision-making. The claim's educational importance, however, argues for accurate treatment even when the practical stakes are modest.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**The Mohs scale in historical context.** Friedrich Mohs published his mineral hardness scale in 1812 as a field identification tool for geologists, designed to allow rapid comparative assessment of minerals without laboratory instrumentation. The scale assigns integer values from 1 (talc) to 10 (diamond) based on a simple operational criterion: a mineral higher on the scale will scratch a mineral lower on the scale. The scale is not linear, meaning that the difference in absolute hardness between consecutive scale values is not constant — the difference in indentation hardness between corundum (9) and diamond (10) is much greater than the difference between adjacent lower positions.

Mohs himself was explicit that the scale was ordinal, not cardinal, and was designed for minerals specifically. Subsequent adoption of the scale as a general hardness ranking and its incorporation into science curricula worldwide as a statement about physical hardness generally represents a pedagogical overgeneralisation that the scale's designer did not invite. The Vickers, Knoop, and Berkovich scales — which use measured indentation to produce a true cardinal scale with physical units (GPa) — replaced the Mohs scale for scientific measurement purposes in the 20th century, though the Mohs scale remains in use for field geology and general educational purposes.

Tabor (1954, Proceedings of the Physical Society, Section B, 67(3): 249–257) provided an important analysis of the relationship between different hardness scales, showing that they measure related but not identical properties and that no single scale can be declared definitive across all materials and applications. Johnson (1985, Contact Mechanics, Cambridge University Press) provides a comprehensive treatment of contact mechanics underlying hardness testing that contextualises the operational diversity of hardness measurement.

**Superhard materials: the research frontier.** The scientific investigation of superhard materials — materials with Vickers hardness exceeding approximately 40 GPa — is an active research field with both fundamental and applied dimensions. Diamond itself, at approximately 70–100 GPa depending on crystal orientation and purity, defines the practical upper end of available tool materials. The quest for materials exceeding diamond's hardness is driven by the desire for cutting tools capable of machining diamond itself, by semiconductor applications requiring extremely hard coatings, and by fundamental interest in the limits of hardness as a material property.

Veprek (1999, Journal of Vacuum Science & Technology A, 17(5): 2401–2420) reviewed superhard nanocomposite coatings, describing materials with hardness approaching 100 GPa fabricated through nanostructural design principles rather than compositionally. Sung and Sung (1996, Materials Chemistry and Physics, 43(1): 1–18) reviewed cubic boron nitride (c-BN) as an alternative to diamond for cutting applications, noting that c-BN, while slightly softer than diamond, is more resistant to oxidation at high temperatures — making it superior to diamond for some machining applications despite being less hard by standard metrics.

This practical example illustrates a broader principle: "hardest substance" does not automatically translate to "best cutting material" because other properties — oxidation resistance, thermal stability, adhesion to workpiece materials, reactivity with iron-group metals — also determine cutting performance. Diamond, despite its hardness, performs poorly when machining ferrous metals because it is reactive with iron at cutting temperatures, forming iron carbide and degrading rapidly. Cubic boron nitride, slightly less hard but much less reactive with iron, is preferred for ferrous machining. The practical wisdom of the tooling industry therefore already implicitly recognises that "hardest" does not mean "best" across all applications.

**The lonsdaleite controversy and theoretical limits of hardness.** Lonsdaleite, discovered in 1967 by Hanneman, Strong, and Bundy (Smith, Wedepohl, Taylor, and Wedepohl, 1967, Science, 155(3765): 995–997 — original identification in Canyon Diablo meteorite iron) has a hexagonal crystal structure compared to diamond's cubic structure, with the same carbon-carbon bonding arrangement but different coordination geometry. The theoretical prediction by Pan et al. (2009) that ideal lonsdaleite would be 58% harder than diamond applies to the theoretically perfect hexagonal structure; natural and laboratory lonsdaleite samples consistently contain structural defects and graphite lamellae that reduce measured hardness far below the theoretical prediction.

Mineralogical work by Németh et al. (2014, Nature Communications, 5: 5447) raised questions about whether what had been identified as lonsdaleite in meteoritic and impact samples is actually a structurally disordered intermediate rather than pure hexagonal diamond, suggesting that some published lonsdaleite hardness measurements may have been made on impure or misidentified samples. The lonsdaleite hardness claim therefore remains under scientific discussion in a way that the ADNR result does not.

The controversy over lonsdaleite illustrates the general difficulty of establishing hardness claims near the limits of current synthesis and characterisation capabilities. Preparation of phase-pure, large-grained, defect-free samples of predicted superhard materials is often technically extremely challenging; hardness measurements on samples with unknown or significant defect content cannot reliably validate theoretical predictions.

**Material hardness in astrophysical and geological context.** The popular claim "the hardest substance known to science" implicitly applies to Earth-surface conditions and human-scale synthesis. However, materials under extreme pressure conditions in planetary interiors may exist in forms dramatically harder than surface-condition diamond. Ab initio calculations of carbon under ultra-high-pressure conditions (Grochala, Hoffmann, Feng, and Ashcroft, 2007, Angewandte Chemie International Edition, 46(20): 3620–3642) suggest that metallic carbon phases exist under conditions found in the deep interiors of carbon-rich exoplanets. "Diamond planets" — hypothetical carbon-rich exoplanets with diamond mantles — are a topic of active astronomical discussion.

Starchenko (2002) and subsequent work on high-pressure materials synthesis has produced multiple novel phases of carbon and boron nitride at conditions accessible in diamond anvil cells. The hardness of these exotic phases, where measured, varies widely. The general principle is that the hardness maximum in carbon-based materials has not been definitively established theoretically, and different structural topologies under different pressure and temperature conditions continue to yield surprising results. The claim that diamond is the "hardest substance" is thus not just empirically superseded but conceptually bounded by the specific conditions under which it is evaluated.

**Cultural resonance and the metaphor economy.** Diamond's cultural significance extends far beyond its material properties and includes its scarcity value, its optical properties (dispersion and brilliance), its role in engagement ring traditions, and its association with permanence and durability through the famous De Beers marketing slogan "A Diamond Is Forever" introduced in 1947. The hardness claim functions within this cultural context as one component of a broader set of superlatives used to justify diamond's premium cultural and economic positioning.

The cultural investment in diamond's supremacy means that corrections to the hardness claim face not only the standard cognitive friction of factual updating but also the implicit challenge of questioning a set of cultural associations with economic and symbolic value. This is not to suggest that motivated reasoning drives individual resistance to the correction — most people are unlikely to care about diamond's precise standing on the Vickers scale — but rather that the cultural context ensures continued amplification of the strongest version of the hardness claim in commercial and cultural communication.

The metaphorical economy of "hardness" language further entrenches the claim: "diamond-hard determination," "hard as diamonds," and analogous expressions in multiple languages treat diamond's hardness as a superlative standard. This linguistic embedding creates a form of cultural infrastructure for the claim that reinforces its plausibility independently of any explicit scientific training, and that will remain after even successful factual corrections because the metaphors are not primarily about physical chemistry.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates the claim "False," noting that diamond is the hardest naturally occurring mineral but that synthetic materials including aggregated diamond nanorods, cubic boron nitride, and ultrahard fullerite have been found to equal or exceed diamond's hardness by various hardness measures. Their analysis references the synthesis of superhard materials and correctly identifies the distinction between natural minerals and all known substances as the key qualification that the popular claim omits.

The Snopes treatment is accurate in its core verdict and reasonably well-sourced for a general audience. Their entry notes the lonsdaleite and ADNR literature and provides appropriate qualifications about hardness measurement methods. The analysis is somewhat brief relative to the complexity of the materials science involved and does not engage deeply with the Mohs scale's specific limitations or the distinction between hardness and other mechanical properties that generates much of the confusion.

The broader fact-checking literature on this claim — including treatments by LiveScience, Scientific American, Physics.org, and popular science outlets — varies considerably in technical depth. The most useful treatments explicitly address the multiple hardness scales and what "hardness" means operationally in each context, distinguishing between the claim's truth under Mohs criteria and its falsity under absolute hardness measures. The least useful simply assert that diamond has been superseded without explaining the specific conditions and materials involved or acknowledging the partial continued validity of the everyday claim. This claim benefits particularly from contextualised rather than binary treatment, since its truth value is genuinely scope-dependent in a way that binary fact-check verdicts can obscure.
