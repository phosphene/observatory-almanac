# Diamond Is the Hardest Substance Known

## 1. The Claim

Diamond is the hardest substance in existence. It sits at the top of every hardness scale, scratches anything, and cannot itself be scratched. It is a symbol of permanence, indestructibility, and absolute physical supremacy. The phrase "hard as a diamond" has become cultural shorthand for the upper limit of solidity itself.

This claim circulates in science classrooms, popular culture, jewelry marketing, and casual conversation. It is stated as settled fact in countless encyclopedias and textbooks that have not been updated to reflect several decades of materials science research.

The claim is partially true, highly context-dependent, and misleading in ways that illuminate important distinctions about what "hardness" actually means and why the concept matters far beyond gemstones.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

### Diamond Is the Hardest Natural Mineral — Under the Mohs Scale

Friedrich Mohs, a German mineralogist, devised his hardness scale in 1812 as a practical field tool for mineral identification. The Mohs scale ranks minerals from 1 (talc) to 10 (diamond) based on a simple scratch test: a mineral that scratches another is harder; one that is scratched is softer. Diamond sits at 10 by definition on this scale.

That ranking is accurate within its domain. Diamond does scratch every natural mineral. In the context of geology and gemology, diamond's hardness at the top of the Mohs scale is not wrong.

The problem is the leap from "hardest natural mineral on the Mohs scale" to "hardest substance known." That leap conflates a practical field classification with a universal physical claim — and the physics does not support it.

### Hardness Is Not a Single Property

In materials science, "hardness" refers specifically to a material's resistance to permanent plastic deformation — usually measured by indentation. The most common laboratory measures are the Vickers, Knoop, and Rockwell hardness tests, which press a standardized indenter into the material surface and measure the impression left behind.

Mohs hardness and Vickers hardness are related but not identical. Diamond scores approximately 70–100 GPa on the Vickers scale, depending on crystal orientation and purity. This is exceptionally high. But it is not absolute.

### Materials That Exceed Diamond's Hardness

**Wurtzite Boron Nitride (w-BN)**

A 2009 study by Pan et al. in *Physical Review Letters* used first-principles simulations to calculate indentation strengths of wurtzite boron nitride and lonsdaleite (hexagonal diamond). Wurtzite BN, under <001> indentation, showed a predicted indentation strength of 114 GPa — approximately 18% greater than cubic diamond under the same conditions. The mechanism involves a stress-induced structural transformation that allows w-BN to redistribute load across its crystal lattice in ways cubic diamond cannot.

Wurtzite BN is not easily synthesized in pure form under laboratory conditions, which is one reason it does not appear in common hardness tables. But its predicted and experimentally-supported properties exceed diamond's in measurable ways.

**Lonsdaleite (Hexagonal Diamond)**

Lonsdaleite is a polymorph of carbon — like diamond, it is pure carbon, but arranged in a hexagonal rather than cubic crystal structure. It forms naturally in meteorite impacts when graphite is subjected to extreme shock pressure. The same 2009 study predicted lonsdaleite's indentation strength at 152 GPa — roughly 58% harder than cubic diamond in certain crystallographic directions.

Lonsdaleite has historically been difficult to isolate in pure form; natural samples are typically contaminated with cubic diamond and graphite. A 2020 study using electron microscopy at the Australian National University found strong evidence for pure lonsdaleite in meteorite samples from the Canyon Diablo impact. The question of its measurable hardness in practice, rather than in simulation, remains active research — but the theoretical prediction is not in serious dispute.

**Aggregated Diamond Nanorods (ADNR)**

A 2005 study by Dubrovinskaia et al. in *Physical Review Letters* synthesized aggregated diamond nanorods — a nanostructured form of diamond — and found it to be 11% harder than conventional diamond and 3% less compressible. ADNRs are diamond rearranged at the nanoscale rather than a distinct compound, which makes them a form of diamond rather than a separate substance. But the point remains: "diamond" is not a single hardness value but a range that depends on structure, purity, grain size, and orientation.

### Hardness vs. Toughness: The Critical Distinction

Diamond is famously not tough. Toughness — a material's ability to absorb energy without fracturing — is fundamentally different from hardness. Diamond's crystal structure has well-defined cleavage planes: directions along which atomic bonds are weaker and the material splits cleanly when struck with sufficient force along the right angle.

Diamond cutters exploit this. The process of cleaving a raw diamond involves finding the cleavage plane, positioning a blade, and striking it to split the stone. A material this easy to cleave is, by engineering standards, brittle.

Compared to materials like steel, tungsten carbide, or even some ceramics, diamond has poor fracture toughness — roughly 5–10 MPa·m^(1/2). Silicon carbide and tungsten carbide can approach or exceed this value while offering far better thermal and chemical stability for industrial applications.

This is why industrial cutting tools do not use diamond alone. Polycrystalline diamond composites (PDC), diamond-tipped carbide tools, and cubic boron nitride (CBN) tools are preferred for machining because they combine hardness with resistance to fracture — something monocrystalline diamond cannot provide.

### The "Hardest Substance" Framing Is Scientifically Outdated

The consensus among materials scientists is that the claim of diamond being the hardest substance has been superseded. Brazhkin et al.'s 2004 review in *Nature Materials* — titled "What Does 'Harder Than Diamond' Mean?" — explicitly surveyed experimental and computational evidence for superhard materials exceeding diamond's hardness, including dense boron carbide phases, rhenium diboride, and various boron-nitrogen compounds.

The paper's purpose was to clarify the conceptual confusion that arises when popular literature treats diamond's hardness as a ceiling rather than a benchmark.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### The Mohs Scale Was Designed as a Teaching Tool

Mohs intended his scale for practical mineral identification in the field. Its linear 1–10 structure with diamond anchoring the top made it easy to memorize and use without instruments. The scale became embedded in elementary science education worldwide.

The problem is pedagogical compression: "diamond is 10 on the Mohs scale, which is the top" became "diamond is the hardest substance." The scale's scope — natural minerals, scratch resistance, field identification — was quietly dropped from popular transmission.

### Marketing Has an Interest in the Claim

The diamond industry has spent enormous resources associating diamonds with superlatives. De Beers' mid-twentieth-century campaigns established the cultural grammar of diamonds as symbols of permanence ("A Diamond Is Forever," 1947). Hardness — indestructibility, endurance — was central to this branding.

There is a commercial incentive to keep "hardest substance" attached to diamond. This does not mean marketing invented the claim, but it has amplified and preserved it long past its scientific accuracy.

### The Alternatives Are Obscure

Wurtzite boron nitride and lonsdaleite are not household names. They are not bought, sold, or worn. They appear in materials science journals rather than textbooks or popular science writing. The gap between what specialists know and what circulates publicly is unusually wide here — there is simply no cultural vehicle for correcting the claim.

### Hardness Is Conflated with Indestructibility

In everyday language, "hard" often means "impossible to break or damage." Diamond's reputation benefits from this conflation. Even people who have been told that diamond can be cleaved tend to revert to treating it as generically indestructible. The technical distinction between hardness (scratch resistance) and toughness (fracture resistance) is not intuitive and requires deliberate learning to retain.

## 4. Verdict

**Refined:** Diamond is the hardest naturally occurring mineral as measured by the Mohs scratch scale. It is not the hardest substance known. Synthetic and naturally occurring materials including wurtzite boron nitride, lonsdaleite (hexagonal diamond), and aggregated diamond nanorods have been predicted and, in some cases, experimentally shown to exceed conventional diamond in hardness.

The claim is also importantly incomplete because it conflates hardness (scratch and indentation resistance) with toughness (fracture resistance). Diamond is relatively brittle — it cleaves easily along crystal planes — making it unsuitable for many industrial applications where tougher materials are preferred.

**EARC Classification: R — Refined**
The claim is not simply false. Diamond is genuinely extraordinarily hard, and in most everyday contexts the distinction between "hardest natural mineral" and "hardest substance" does not matter. But in scientific, engineering, and educational contexts, the unqualified claim "diamond is the hardest substance" is inaccurate, and the inaccuracy is consequential: it obscures both the existence of superhard synthetic materials and the conceptual distinction between hardness and toughness that engineers and materials scientists navigate constantly.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### The Materials Science of Hardness

Understanding why certain materials are hard requires looking at their atomic-level structure. Hardness in covalently bonded materials like diamond comes from the density and strength of carbon-carbon bonds in the crystal lattice. Diamond's cubic structure places each carbon atom in a tetrahedral arrangement with four neighbors, creating an exceptionally rigid, isotropic network.

The search for superhard materials is fundamentally a search for structures that maintain or exceed this bond density and directionality. Boron-nitrogen compounds are attractive candidates because the B-N bond has similar length and strength to the C-C bond but offers different mechanical responses under stress. The hexagonal arrangement in lonsdaleite creates different stress-distribution pathways than cubic diamond — pathways that, under certain indentation geometries, are more resistant to yielding.

### Industrial Implications

The development of superhard synthetic materials has significant industrial implications. Cutting, drilling, and grinding operations across manufacturing, mining, and oil exploration all depend on tool hardness. The transition from diamond to cubic boron nitride (CBN) tools for machining ferrous metals — diamond reacts chemically with iron at high temperatures — illustrates that the "hardest" material is not always the best tool.

Research into ultrahard thin films, nanocomposite coatings, and boride-carbide ceramics has produced a class of synthetic materials tailored for specific industrial applications. The hardest of these challenge and sometimes exceed diamond at the system level, even if individual crystals remain difficult to directly compare.

### The Pedagogical Problem

The diamond hardness claim is a useful case study in how simplified scientific facts become entrenched misconceptions. The original simplification — "diamond is 10 on the Mohs scale" — was accurate and appropriate for its educational context. The distortion occurred in transmission: the scope of the claim expanded while the qualifications were dropped.

This pattern appears across science education: a context-dependent fact loses its context and becomes an absolute. The corrective is not to teach more complex material earlier, but to make the context explicit in the original transmission — "the hardest natural mineral on the scratch-resistance scale used for mineral identification" rather than "the hardest substance."

### Diamond in Nature vs. Laboratory

Natural diamond forms under extreme heat and pressure in the Earth's mantle, typically at depths of 150–200 km, over billions of years. Its crystal structure reflects the conditions of its formation. Laboratory-grown diamonds (CVD and HPHT synthesis) can now be produced in weeks with comparable or superior properties.

The existence of laboratory synthesis has further disrupted the cultural associations that sustained the "hardest substance" mythology — diamonds are no longer uniquely rare, irreplaceable, or categorically supreme. The material science conversation has moved on to engineering materials at the nanoscale, creating structures that do not occur in nature and that outperform natural diamonds in measurable ways.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

### The Asymmetric Difficulty

Fact-checkers face an unusual challenge with the diamond hardness claim. It is not a viral hoax with a clear origin or a politically motivated distortion. It is a legacy simplification embedded in textbooks, dictionaries, and standard educational materials. Debunking it requires correcting official sources — a different kind of work than refuting a social media post.

### Source Availability and Quality

The relevant primary literature is in peer-reviewed materials science journals (*Physical Review Letters*, *Nature Materials*) rather than general-interest science publications. Fact-checkers without materials science backgrounds must navigate unfamiliar territory: understanding what indentation strength calculations mean, how crystal-direction-dependent results should be interpreted, and whether simulated results are reliable predictors of experimental measurement.

The honest characterization is that the primary evidence for w-BN and lonsdaleite exceeding diamond comes from simulation, with experimental verification complicated by the difficulty of synthesizing pure samples. Fact-checkers should note this uncertainty without treating it as exonerating the "hardest substance" claim — the directional evidence is consistent, even if absolute measurements vary.

### The "Natural vs. Synthetic" Hedge

Some fact-checkers and science communicators have attempted to preserve the claim by adding "natural" as a qualifier: "diamond is the hardest naturally occurring substance." This is more defensible than the unqualified claim, but lonsdaleite complicates it — it occurs naturally in meteorite impacts. The hedge works only if one restricts to commonly available natural materials, which is not the same as "naturally occurring."

### Calibration Language

For a claim in this category — technically superseded, practically irrelevant in most contexts, important in specialist ones — well-calibrated fact-checker language would note:

- The Mohs scale ranking is accurate.
- The "hardest substance" claim is not supported by current materials science.
- The distinction between hardness and toughness is real and important.
- The harder synthetic materials are not widely available and do not change everyday decisions about diamonds.
- Educational materials should be updated to qualify the claim.

The goal is to correct the record without implying that diamond is some ordinary material or that the claim was a lie. It was a simplification that outlasted its accuracy.
