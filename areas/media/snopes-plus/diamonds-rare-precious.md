# "Diamonds Are Rare and Precious Stones"


## 1. The Claim

The widespread belief that "diamonds are rare and precious stones" represents one of the most successful marketing campaigns in modern history. This claim appears in countless contexts: jewelry advertisements, cultural references to diamond engagement rings, investment advice treating diamonds as valuable assets, and general discourse that positions diamonds as naturally scarce gemstones worthy of premium pricing.

The claim encompasses two distinct assertions: first, that diamonds are geologically rare compared to other materials; and second, that this rarity justifies their status as precious stones commanding high market prices. Both components require careful examination, as they conflate natural occurrence with market valuation in ways that obscure the human systems creating diamond "preciousness."


## 2. What's Actually True

### Geological Reality

Diamonds are not geologically rare. They are composed of pure carbon arranged in a cubic crystal structure, formed under high pressure and temperature conditions that occur naturally in the Earth's mantle. These conditions exist across vast regions of the planet, and diamonds are continuously formed through ongoing geological processes.

In terms of actual scarcity relative to other gemstones, diamonds are significantly more abundant than rubies, emeralds, or sapphires. The Gemological Institute of America estimates that ruby deposits are approximately 1,000 times rarer than diamond deposits. Emeralds are similarly scarce, with high-quality specimens far exceeding diamond rarity. Even semi-precious stones like tanzanite (found only in a small region of Tanzania) are geologically rarer than diamonds.

The global diamond supply is substantial. According to industry data, approximately 130 million carats of rough diamonds are mined annually worldwide, with reserves estimated in the billions of carats across known deposits in Russia, Botswana, Canada, Australia, and other regions. Russia's Sakha Republic alone contains diamond reserves that could supply global demand for decades.

### Historical Market Construction

The "preciousness" of diamonds is not a reflection of natural scarcity but rather the result of deliberate market manipulation orchestrated primarily by De Beers Consolidated Mines, founded by Cecil Rhodes in 1888. De Beers achieved near-monopolistic control over diamond supply, owning or controlling an estimated 80-90% of rough diamond production by the mid-20th century.

This monopoly enabled unprecedented control over both supply and demand. De Beers deliberately restricted diamond supply to maintain artificially high prices, stockpiling millions of carats to prevent market flooding. Simultaneously, the company invested heavily in demand creation through sophisticated marketing campaigns that transformed diamonds from luxury items for the wealthy into "essential" symbols of love and commitment.

The pivotal moment came in 1938 when De Beers hired the N.W. Ayer advertising agency to address declining diamond sales during the Great Depression. The resulting campaign introduced the slogan "A Diamond Is Forever" in 1947, reshaping American (and subsequently global) marriage customs.

### Cultural Manufacturing

Before De Beers' intervention, diamond engagement rings were not standard practice. Historical records indicate that diamond engagement rings were rare before 1930, with most American engagements involving other stones or simple gold bands. The tradition of spending "two months' salary" on an engagement ring was another De Beers creation, later increased to "three months' salary" in marketing materials.

The success of this cultural engineering was remarkable. By 1990, over 80% of American brides received diamond engagement rings, compared to approximately 10% in 1930. This transformation occurred not through organic cultural evolution but through systematic manipulation of social expectations and consumer desires.


### The Persistence Problem

The diamond rarity myth persists despite available corrective information due to several reinforcing factors. First, the sunk cost fallacy affects consumers who have already invested significantly in diamond purchases—acknowledging the artificial nature of diamond value would require confronting the economic loss this represents.

Second, the mythology is embedded in major life rituals and social expectations. Challenging diamond rarity requires questioning widely accepted cultural practices around marriage, wealth display, and gift-giving. The social costs of deviating from diamond-centered traditions often outweigh the economic benefits of more rational gemstone choices.

Third, the industry continues to invest heavily in maintaining the scarcity narrative. Modern diamond marketing emphasizes "conflict-free" sourcing, "sustainable" mining, and "investment grade" stones—all language that reinforces assumptions about diamond value and rarity without directly addressing the fundamental supply manipulation.

### Institutional Validation

The diamond industry's integration into financial systems creates institutional validation for rarity claims. Insurance companies, banks, and appraisal services operate using valuation models that assume diamond scarcity. These institutions have economic incentives to maintain existing pricing structures, creating systemic resistance to market corrections.

Professional gemological organizations, while technically accurate in their descriptions of diamond properties, often avoid directly addressing the artificial scarcity issue. The Gemological Institute of America, for instance, provides extensive technical education about diamond grading and identification while remaining largely silent on market manipulation and supply control.


## 3. Why People Believe This

### The Manufactured Consensus

The diamond rarity myth demonstrates how repeated assertion can create epistemic consensus around factually incorrect claims. Generations of consumers have operated under the assumption that diamonds are naturally rare because this assumption undergirds major life decisions and cultural practices. The economic incentives supporting this belief are substantial, creating resistance to corrective information.

This phenomenon extends beyond consumer behavior into financial and investment advice. Many personal finance resources continue to reference diamonds as "precious stones" or "store of value" without acknowledging their artificial scarcity or poor resale performance. The assumption of rarity shapes insurance valuations, estate planning, and inheritance decisions across millions of households.

### Documentary Evidence

The construction of diamond mythology has been extensively documented. Edward Jay Epstein's groundbreaking 1982 Atlantic article "Have You Ever Tried to Sell a Diamond?" revealed the massive disconnect between diamond retail prices and resale values. Epstein demonstrated that diamonds typically lose 50-90% of their purchase price immediately upon sale, indicating that retail pricing reflects market manipulation rather than inherent value.

More recent investigations, including Kitco News analyses and academic studies of gemstone markets, have confirmed that diamond resale markets remain systematically depressed compared to other precious stones. This price behavior is inconsistent with genuine scarcity—truly rare materials maintain value across primary and secondary markets.

### Scientific Validation

Advances in diamond synthesis have provided additional evidence of diamond abundance. Laboratory-grown diamonds, produced using High Pressure High Temperature (HPHT) or Chemical Vapor Deposition (CVD) methods, are chemically, physically, and optically identical to mined diamonds. The success of synthetic diamond production—now accounting for a significant portion of industrial diamond use—demonstrates that diamond formation is reproducible and scalable.

The natural diamond industry's response to synthetic competition has been telling. Rather than competing on rarity or inherent value, marketing has shifted to emotional and ethical appeals ("natural" vs. "artificial"), further confirming that diamond value is constructed rather than intrinsic.


## 4. Verdict

**EPISTEMOLOGICALLY LOADED**: The claim that "diamonds are rare and precious stones" is epistemologically loaded because it conflates two distinct concepts—geological abundance and market valuation—in ways that obscure the human systems creating diamond "preciousness."

Diamonds are not geologically rare compared to other gemstones, and their "precious" status results from systematic market manipulation rather than natural scarcity. The claim's persistence reflects successful cultural engineering rather than factual accuracy about material properties or natural occurrence.

The diamond case demonstrates how economic incentives can create and maintain false beliefs about natural resources, with implications extending far beyond gemstone markets into questions of how societies understand value, rarity, and the relationship between natural properties and market prices.

While diamonds do possess certain desirable physical properties (hardness, optical characteristics), these properties do not justify claims of rarity or inherent preciousness. The gap between geological reality and cultural belief reveals the power of sustained marketing to reshape fundamental assumptions about the natural world.

Understanding the constructed nature of diamond value provides insights into broader patterns of how artificial scarcity operates in modern economies, making this seemingly narrow claim relevant to economic literacy and critical thinking about market-driven narratives.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### Comparative Gemstone Markets

Examining other gemstone markets reveals how genuine scarcity operates differently from manufactured scarcity. Ruby markets, for instance, show price volatility consistent with actual supply constraints. High-quality Burmese rubies command premium prices that persist across primary and secondary markets, reflecting authentic geological rarity.

Similarly, emerald pricing reflects genuine supply limitations and quality variations. Colombian emeralds maintain value over time in ways that diamond resale markets do not, indicating that real scarcity creates different market dynamics than artificial scarcity.

The jade market provides another instructive comparison. Imperial jade pricing reflects cultural value combined with genuine rarity, creating markets where resale values can exceed purchase prices—a pattern impossible under artificial scarcity systems.

### Economic Theory Applications

Diamond markets illustrate classic economic principles of monopoly pricing and demand creation. The De Beers model represents a textbook case of successful cartel operation, demonstrating how concentrated control over supply can create artificial scarcity for abundant materials.

The diamond case also exemplifies Veblen good behavior—products whose demand increases with price due to their status signaling function. However, this economic behavior has been deliberately cultivated rather than arising naturally from consumer preferences.

### Broader Implications for Market Literacy

The diamond example has broader implications for understanding how markets can be manipulated to create false beliefs about value and scarcity. Similar patterns appear in other industries where concentrated ownership enables supply control and demand manipulation.

The persistence of diamond mythology also illustrates the challenges of correcting economically motivated misinformation. When false beliefs underpin major financial commitments and cultural practices, rational correction faces substantial psychological and social obstacles.

### Technological Disruption

Laboratory-grown diamonds represent a technological disruption to artificial scarcity systems. As synthetic production costs continue declining and quality improvements advance, the natural diamond industry faces pressure to justify premium pricing based on factors other than rarity or inherent properties.

This technological challenge has already begun reshaping industry messaging, with increased emphasis on "natural origin" and "billion-year formation" stories that sidestep scarcity claims while attempting to maintain price premiums through emotional and romantic appeals.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed the comprehensive claim that "diamonds are rare and precious stones," though the fact-checking platform has tackled related diamond myths, including various claims about diamond durability and formation. The absence of a dedicated Snopes entry on diamond rarity reflects how deeply embedded this belief has become in popular culture—it sits below the threshold of "questionable claim" because it has achieved the status of assumed fact.

This represents a classic case where institutional fact-checking focuses on dramatic or politically charged misinformation while leaving foundational economic myths largely unexamined. The diamond rarity claim operates in the space of "common knowledge" that shapes consumer behavior and cultural practices without triggering skeptical scrutiny.
