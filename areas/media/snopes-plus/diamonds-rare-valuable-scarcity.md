# "Diamonds Are Rare and Valuable Because of Scarcity"


## 1. The Claim

The widespread belief that "diamonds are rare and valuable because of scarcity" represents one of the most successful instances of manufactured demand in modern commercial history. This claim suggests that diamonds derive their extraordinary market value—often costing thousands of dollars per carat—from their geological rarity and the difficulty of obtaining them from the Earth.

The claim typically manifests in several interconnected assertions: that diamonds are among the rarest gemstones found in nature, that their formation requires extraordinary geological conditions making them inherently scarce, that their high prices reflect this natural scarcity through basic supply and demand economics, and that their value as symbols of love and commitment stems from their precious rarity. These beliefs have become so deeply embedded in consumer culture that questioning them often meets with surprise or disbelief.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Geological Reality**: The foundational premise of the claim—that diamonds are geologically rare—is demonstrably false. Diamonds are composed of carbon arranged in a cubic crystal structure, and carbon is the fourth most abundant element in the universe. On Earth, diamonds form in the mantle under conditions of extreme pressure and temperature, then are brought to the surface through volcanic pipes called kimberlites. While these formation conditions are specific, they are not particularly rare in geological terms.

Comparative abundance data reveals the magnitude of this misunderstanding. Diamonds are approximately 1,000 times more common than rubies, which require the rare combination of aluminum and chromium. Emeralds, formed through the interaction of beryllium-rich pegmatites with chromium or vanadium-bearing rocks, are roughly 20 times rarer than diamonds. Sapphires, while more common than rubies or emeralds, are still significantly less abundant than diamonds in most geological contexts.

The Argyle mine in Australia alone produced over 90% of the world's pink diamonds before its closure in 2020, while simultaneously producing massive quantities of industrial-grade diamonds. The Jwaneng mine in Botswana processes millions of carats annually. These operations would be impossible if diamonds were truly geologically rare.

**Market Structure Analysis**: The diamond market's structure reveals the artificial nature of diamond scarcity. From approximately 1888 to the 1990s, De Beers Consolidated Mines controlled between 80-90% of global diamond production and sales through its Central Selling Organization (CSO). This monopolistic control allowed the company to restrict supply regardless of actual mining capacity, creating artificial scarcity that bore no relationship to geological availability.

De Beers' internal documents, revealed through antitrust litigation and investigative reporting, demonstrate conscious efforts to limit supply. The company maintained stockpiles of diamonds, delayed opening of productive mines, and coordinated with other producers to maintain pricing structures. This wasn't market response to natural scarcity—it was manufactured scarcity designed to maintain high prices for an abundant resource.

**Marketing Campaign Documentation**: The transformation of diamond demand represents perhaps the most successful advertising campaign in commercial history. In 1938, De Beers hired the N.W. Ayer advertising agency to address a fundamental problem: American diamond sales were declining, and there was no established tradition of diamond engagement rings in most cultures.

The "A Diamond Is Forever" campaign, launched in 1947, didn't just advertise diamonds—it created the cultural framework that made diamond purchases feel inevitable. Internal Ayer documents describe strategies to "create a situation where almost every person pledging marriage feels compelled to acquire a diamond engagement ring." The campaign specifically targeted the emotional and psychological associations of diamonds rather than their material properties.

Hollywood product placement became a key strategy. Ayer arranged for diamonds to appear prominently in films, often paying studios to feature diamond jewelry in romantic scenes. The agency tracked these placements meticulously, measuring their impact on sales and cultural attitudes. This wasn't organic cultural evolution—it was systematic demand engineering.

**Price Dynamics**: The disconnect between diamond retail prices and their intrinsic value becomes clear in resale markets. Unlike truly scarce commodities such as gold or silver, diamonds typically retain only 25-50% of their retail value when resold. This dramatic depreciation indicates that retail prices reflect marketing premiums and monopolistic pricing rather than underlying scarcity value.

The emergence of lab-grown diamonds provides another lens for understanding this dynamic. Laboratory-created diamonds are chemically, physically, and optically identical to mined diamonds—they are literally the same material. Yet lab-grown diamonds typically cost 60-80% less than mined diamonds of equivalent quality. This price differential cannot be explained by scarcity, as lab-grown diamonds can be produced in unlimited quantities. Instead, it reflects the marketing premium attached to the narrative of "natural" formation and rarity.


The epistemological foundation for rejecting the scarcity-based explanation of diamond value rests on multiple independent lines of evidence that converge on the same conclusion. Geological evidence demonstrates abundance, historical evidence documents artificial manipulation, economic evidence reveals pricing anomalies, and marketing evidence shows systematic demand engineering.

This convergence is particularly compelling because it involves evidence from distinct domains—natural sciences, business history, economics, and marketing—that align without coordination. The geological abundance of diamonds wasn't discovered to discredit marketing claims; it's simply what the rocks reveal. The documentation of market manipulation wasn't created to support geological findings; it emerged from legal and journalistic investigations into business practices.

The strength of this epistemological position lies in its falsifiability and the specificity of its predictions. If diamonds were truly rare, we would expect to see different patterns in geological surveys, mining operations, pricing dynamics, and market behavior. The systematic absence of these expected patterns across multiple domains provides strong evidence for the artificial nature of diamond scarcity.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Geological Evidence Base**: Our understanding of diamond abundance rests on robust geological evidence collected over more than a century of mining operations, geological surveys, and scientific research. Global diamond production data, maintained by organizations such as the Kimberley Process Certification Scheme and the Diamond Producers Association, provides comprehensive documentation of extraction volumes that would be impossible if diamonds were truly rare.

Peer-reviewed geological research on kimberlite formation, published in journals such as *Economic Geology* and *Ore Geology Reviews*, establishes the widespread nature of diamond-bearing geological formations. Systematic surveys conducted by geological agencies worldwide have identified thousands of kimberlite pipes, many containing commercially viable diamond deposits.

**Historical Documentation**: The manipulation of diamond markets is extensively documented through corporate records, government investigations, and journalistic exposés. Edward Jay Epstein's 1982 *Atlantic* article "Have You Ever Tried to Sell a Diamond?" provided the first comprehensive public examination of De Beers' market control, based on internal company documents and interviews with industry executives.

Subsequent antitrust investigations by the U.S. Department of Justice, the European Commission, and other regulatory bodies have generated thousands of pages of documentation detailing market manipulation strategies. These aren't theoretical claims—they're backed by corporate communications, financial records, and testimony from industry participants.

**Economic Analysis**: Academic economists have extensively studied the diamond market as a case study in monopolistic pricing and artificial scarcity. Research published in journals such as the *Journal of Economic Perspectives* and *Industrial Organization* provides quantitative analysis of price premiums, market concentration effects, and the relationship between production costs and retail prices.

Studies comparing diamond markets to other gemstone markets reveal the anomalous nature of diamond pricing. When controlling for factors such as beauty, durability, and processing costs, diamonds command price premiums that cannot be explained by underlying scarcity or material properties.


## 4. Verdict

**Classification: CONSTRUCTED**

The claim that diamonds are valuable because of their natural scarcity is a constructed narrative that misrepresents both geological reality and market dynamics. Diamonds are among the most abundant gemstones on Earth, significantly more common than rubies, emeralds, or sapphires. Their high retail prices result from a century of artificial supply restriction and systematic demand engineering, not from natural scarcity.

This represents a textbook case of epistemologically-loaded framing, where a false premise (geological rarity) is used to naturalize an artificial market structure (monopolistic pricing). The claim functions to obscure the commercial construction of value by attributing it to immutable natural forces.

The persistence of this belief despite abundant contradictory evidence demonstrates how sophisticated marketing can create and maintain false beliefs about material reality, particularly when those beliefs are embedded in emotionally significant cultural practices like engagement and marriage.

Understanding the true dynamics of diamond valuation provides crucial insight into how markets can manufacture scarcity, engineer demand, and use cultural narratives to sustain pricing structures that bear little relationship to underlying material constraints or production costs.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**Consumer Psychology and Cultural Construction**: The diamond phenomenon illustrates fundamental principles about how markets can shape cultural values rather than simply responding to them. Research in behavioral economics and consumer psychology demonstrates how repeated exposure to marketing messages can create genuine emotional attachments to products, even when those attachments are based on manufactured narratives.

The diamond case study has influenced academic understanding of "positional goods"—products whose value derives primarily from their social signaling function rather than intrinsic utility. Economist Fred Hirsch's concept of "social limits to growth" draws heavily on examples like diamonds, where artificial scarcity maintains hierarchical distinction.

**Implications for Media Literacy**: The diamond narrative's success despite contradictory evidence highlights crucial challenges in information environments. The claim persists not because evidence supports it, but because it's embedded in emotional contexts (romance, commitment, tradition) that make critical evaluation psychologically difficult.

This dynamic appears across many domains where commercial interests have incentives to promote beliefs that serve their business models. The diamond case provides a template for understanding how sophisticated marketing can create and maintain false beliefs about material reality, even in the face of contradictory evidence.

**Regulatory and Ethical Dimensions**: The diamond industry's market manipulation has raised ongoing questions about consumer protection and truth in advertising. While De Beers' monopoly has been partially dismantled through antitrust action, the cultural beliefs created during the monopoly period continue to support pricing structures that don't reflect underlying scarcity.

The emergence of lab-grown diamonds has created new regulatory challenges around disclosure and marketing claims. Industry efforts to maintain price differentials between mined and lab-grown diamonds often rely on appeals to "authenticity" and "natural formation" that echo the original scarcity narratives, even when the products are materially identical.

**Comparative Analysis**: Similar dynamics appear in other luxury markets where artificial scarcity supports premium pricing. The art market, luxury fashion, and certain collectibles markets all demonstrate how controlling supply and crafting narrative can create value that exceeds material justification.

However, the diamond case is distinctive in its scale and cultural penetration. Unlike niche luxury goods, diamond engagement rings became normalized across broad demographic groups, making the economic impact of the manufactured scarcity narrative far more significant than typical luxury market manipulation.
