# "Diamonds Are Rare and Valuable": A Snopes+ Review

## 1. Claim Statement

The claim that "diamonds are rare and valuable" operates as both a descriptive assertion about geological scarcity and an implicit argument about intrinsic worth. This belief pervades popular culture, from the phrase "diamond in the rough" to the expectation that engagement rings should feature diamond centerpieces. The claim encompasses several interlocking assumptions: that diamonds occur infrequently in nature, that this natural scarcity justifies high market prices, that diamonds retain their value as investments, and that their worth stems from inherent properties rather than market manipulation.

The claim appears in countless contexts: jewelry advertisements proclaiming diamonds as "forever" investments, geological texts describing them as "precious stones," and cultural narratives positioning diamond ownership as a marker of success and commitment. The assertion extends beyond mere market value to encompass cultural significance—diamonds are presented not merely as expensive objects, but as naturally rare treasures whose worth transcends temporary economic fluctuations.

## 2. Snopes Assessment

Snopes has not directly addressed the comprehensive claim that diamonds are rare and valuable, though the fact-checking site has examined related claims about diamond marketing and the De Beers monopoly. The absence of a dedicated Snopes entry on this topic reflects the claim's position as accepted common knowledge rather than a controversial assertion requiring fact-checking. However, this gap represents a significant oversight, as the diamond industry exemplifies how manufactured scarcity can become indistinguishable from natural rarity in popular understanding.

The claim's epistemological status as "obvious truth" has largely protected it from systematic debunking. When diamond industry practices are discussed in mainstream media, the focus typically remains on ethical concerns (conflict diamonds, labor conditions) rather than questioning the fundamental premise of scarcity and value. This framing accepts the industry's valuation framework while merely critiquing its methods—a classic example of how epistemologically loaded claims can redirect critical attention toward peripheral issues.

## 3. Claim Fidelity Audit

The geological reality contradicts the scarcity claim decisively. Diamonds are not rare gemstones—they are the most common gemstone on Earth. According to the United States Geological Survey, diamond deposits exist on every continent except Antarctica and Europe. Industrial diamond production exceeds 100 million carats annually, dwarfing the production of genuinely rare gemstones like emeralds or rubies. The confusion arises from conflating "gem-quality" diamonds with diamonds generally, but even gem-quality stones are far more abundant than their market positioning suggests.

The rarity perception stems from controlled supply rather than geological scarcity. De Beers, through its Central Selling Organization, controlled approximately 80-85% of the global rough diamond market by the 1980s. This monopolistic control enabled the company to maintain artificial scarcity by stockpiling diamonds and releasing them strategically to maintain price levels. Internal De Beers documents, revealed during antitrust litigation, explicitly described strategies for "managing supply" to maintain the perception of rarity.

The valuation claim fails even more dramatically under scrutiny. Unlike gold or silver, diamonds have virtually no resale value for consumers. Industry insiders acknowledge that diamonds typically lose 30-50% of their retail value immediately upon purchase. This depreciation pattern contradicts the "store of value" narrative promoted in diamond marketing. The Rapaport Diamond Report, the industry's primary pricing guide, reflects wholesale prices that bear little relationship to retail markups, which often exceed 100-300%.

## 4. Review Epistemology

The epistemological structure of diamond valuation reveals the constructed nature of "natural" value. Edward Jay Epstein's seminal investigation "The Rise and Fall of Diamonds" (1982) documented how De Beers systematically created the modern diamond market through three complementary strategies: supply control, demand stimulation, and cultural programming. The company's internal documents described diamonds as having "no intrinsic value" while simultaneously funding massive advertising campaigns to establish cultural associations between diamonds and eternal love.

The N.W. Ayer advertising agency's "A Diamond Is Forever" campaign, launched in 1947, represents perhaps the most successful example of manufactured desire in advertising history. Before this campaign, only 10% of American engagement rings featured diamonds. By 1990, this figure exceeded 80%. The campaign succeeded not merely by promoting diamonds, but by creating a new cultural norm that positioned diamond engagement rings as necessary expressions of authentic commitment.

The epistemological innovation lay in presenting culturally constructed value as natural fact. The campaign avoided explicit claims about rarity or investment value, instead fostering emotional associations that made such questions seem irrelevant. The phrase "A Diamond Is Forever" suggested permanence without promising financial return, creating a framework where questioning diamond purchases appeared to question love itself.

Contemporary gemological education perpetuates these constructed categories. The "Four Cs" system (Cut, Color, Clarity, Carat) provides a scientific-seeming framework for valuation that obscures the arbitrary nature of diamond pricing. Laboratory-grown diamonds, which are chemically and optically identical to mined stones, typically sell for 30-40% less than "natural" diamonds—a price differential that has no basis in material properties and exists purely to maintain the constructed scarcity of mined stones.

## 5. Conclusion Epistemology

The diamond industry's success demonstrates how economic interests can reshape fundamental categories of value and rarity. The transformation of diamonds from industrial abrasives to symbols of eternal love required coordinating supply control with cultural engineering on an unprecedented scale. This coordination succeeded so completely that questioning diamond value now appears to question romantic commitment itself—a brilliant example of how economic claims can become epistemologically protected by embedding them within intimate social relationships.

The industry's response to laboratory-grown diamonds reveals the constructed nature of "natural" value. Despite being chemically identical to mined diamonds, synthetic stones are marketed as inferior specifically because they lack the "journey" narrative of geological formation and mining. This positioning acknowledges that diamond value depends not on material properties but on storytelling—yet the industry maintains price differentials as if the stories reflected natural facts.

The environmental and ethical critique of diamond mining has actually reinforced the scarcity narrative by positioning diamonds as so valuable that their extraction justifies significant social and ecological costs. Conflict diamond campaigns, while highlighting real harms, implicitly accept the premise that diamonds are worth fighting over. The Kimberley Process certification system treats diamond smuggling as a regulatory problem rather than questioning why rocks should command such prices in the first place.

## 6. The Wider Field

The diamond case illuminates broader patterns in how markets construct scarcity and value. Similar dynamics appear across luxury goods markets: Swiss watches, designer handbags, premium wines, and collector cars all employ controlled distribution and cultural programming to maintain price premiums disconnected from production costs or material properties. The diamond industry pioneered techniques now standard across luxury marketing: creating artificial scarcity, establishing cultural significance through advertising, and embedding products within identity narratives that make price comparisons seem inappropriate.

The success of manufactured scarcity depends on what economists call "Veblen goods"—products where higher prices actually increase demand by signaling status. Diamonds function as pure Veblen goods: their primary value lies in their expense, creating a circular logic where high prices justify themselves. This dynamic explains why laboratory-grown diamonds struggle to achieve price parity despite identical properties—their lower cost undermines their signaling function.

The digital economy has democratized similar scarcity-creation techniques. Non-fungible tokens (NFTs) represent perhaps the purest expression of manufactured scarcity, creating uniqueness and value through technological rather than geological constraints. Cryptocurrency markets employ "halving events" and "burning" mechanisms that explicitly manipulate supply to influence valuation. These contemporary examples reveal how the diamond model has become a template for value creation across diverse markets.

The psychological foundations of manufactured scarcity connect to deeper cognitive biases around loss aversion and social proof. Behavioral economics research demonstrates that people value objects more highly when they perceive them as scarce, regardless of intrinsic properties. The diamond industry exploited these cognitive patterns decades before they became formalized in academic literature, suggesting that successful market manipulation often precedes scientific understanding of the mechanisms involved.

Educational institutions play a crucial role in perpetuating constructed scarcity. Gemological programs teach diamond evaluation as technical expertise while rarely examining the historical construction of diamond markets. Business schools present De Beers as a case study in successful brand building without critically analyzing the social costs of monopolistic practices. This institutional legitimation transforms marketing success into educational orthodoxy, creating new generations of professionals invested in maintaining existing valuation frameworks.

## 7. Snopes+ Verdict

**ENGINEERED TRUTH**: The claim that diamonds are rare and valuable represents a masterpiece of cultural engineering that has successfully transformed manufactured scarcity into perceived natural fact.

The geological evidence decisively refutes diamond rarity—they are the most common gemstone on Earth, with industrial production exceeding 100 million carats annually. The economic evidence equally refutes intrinsic value—diamonds lose 30-50% of retail value immediately upon purchase and have virtually no resale market for consumers. Laboratory-grown diamonds, chemically identical to mined stones, sell for 30-40% less purely to maintain artificial scarcity narratives.

Yet the claim persists because it operates within an epistemologically protected framework that conflates market price with natural value. The De Beers monopoly's supply control, combined with the "A Diamond Is Forever" cultural programming campaign, created a belief system where questioning diamond value appears to question romantic commitment itself. This protection mechanism has proved so effective that the diamond industry continues to maintain premium pricing even as synthetic alternatives achieve technological parity.

The diamond case reveals how successful market manipulation can become indistinguishable from natural truth across multiple generations. Children growing up with diamond engagement ring expectations perceive this tradition as ancient and universal, rather than as a mid-20th century marketing creation. The industry's success lies not merely in controlling supply or stimulating demand, but in making its own historical construction invisible.

The persistence of diamond valuation demonstrates the power of embedding economic claims within intimate social relationships and identity narratives. Once purchasing decisions become expressions of love, commitment, or social status, rational evaluation of material properties becomes culturally inappropriate. The diamond industry pioneered techniques for creating "sacred" markets where economic logic is suspended in favor of symbolic meaning—techniques now standard across luxury goods, collectibles, and digital assets.

Understanding the constructed nature of diamond value provides crucial insight into how markets shape perception and how successful cultural engineering can outlast its original economic justifications. The diamond industry's achievement represents both a cautionary tale about manufactured scarcity and a demonstration of how thoroughly human societies can transform arbitrary cultural practices into seemingly natural truths.

The claim that "diamonds are rare and valuable" thus stands as engineered truth—technically false in its factual assertions, yet functionally true within the cultural and economic systems that have adopted diamond valuation as foundational assumption. Recognizing this engineered status neither eliminates diamond markets nor resolves the ethical questions surrounding diamond mining, but it does provide essential clarity about the difference between geological facts and market constructions—a distinction with implications extending far beyond gemstone evaluation.
