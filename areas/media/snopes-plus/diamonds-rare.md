# "Diamonds Are Extremely Rare"


## 1. The Claim

The widespread belief that "diamonds are extremely rare" encompasses several interconnected assertions: that diamond formation requires exceptionally rare geological conditions, that diamonds are among the scarcest gemstones found in nature, and that their high market value reflects this fundamental geological scarcity. This narrative underlies much of the diamond industry's marketing and consumer psychology, suggesting that purchasing a diamond means acquiring something genuinely precious due to its rarity in the Earth's crust.

The claim manifests in multiple forms across popular culture and commercial messaging. Jewelry advertisements routinely emphasize diamonds as "rare gifts of the Earth" or "treasures millions of years in the making." Educational materials often describe diamond formation as requiring "extraordinary conditions found only in specific locations." Wedding industry messaging positions diamonds as appropriate symbols of enduring love precisely because of their supposed geological rarity and irreplaceability.

This rarity narrative connects to broader cultural assumptions about value and meaning. The belief that "rare equals valuable" drives consumer psychology across luxury markets, but the diamond case represents one of the most successful applications of this principle to a mass-market commodity. The claim implies that owning a diamond connects the purchaser to geological processes so uncommon that the resulting crystals deserve reverence and significant financial investment.

The geological component of the claim suggests that the specific pressure and temperature conditions required for diamond formation occur only in highly unusual circumstances, making natural diamonds scarce relative to other carbon forms or other gemstones. This technical assertion provides the foundation for all subsequent marketing and pricing strategies.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim fails geological scrutiny on multiple levels. Diamonds are carbon crystals formed under high pressure and temperature conditions that, while specific, are not exceptionally rare in geological terms. The Earth's mantle contains vast quantities of diamonds, with estimates suggesting billions of carats exist in kimberlite pipes alone. Industrial diamond production has operated at massive scales for decades, indicating substantial natural deposits.

Kimberlite pipe surveys across Africa, Canada, Russia, and Australia have identified hundreds of diamond-bearing formations. The Argyle mine in Western Australia, operational from 1983 to 2020, produced over 865 million carats during its lifetime—roughly one-third of global natural diamond production by volume. The Jwaneng mine in Botswana processes approximately 15 million carats annually, indicating massive underground reserves.

Comparative gemstone analysis reveals diamonds are significantly more abundant than emeralds, rubies, or sapphires. High-quality colored gemstones require far more specific trace element conditions during formation. A ruby needs precise chromium concentrations within aluminum oxide crystal structures, while diamonds require only carbon under pressure and heat—conditions that exist throughout much of the Earth's upper mantle.

The formation process itself contradicts rarity claims. Diamonds crystallize at depths of 140-190 kilometers below the surface, where temperatures reach 900-1300°C and pressures exceed 4.5 gigapascals. These conditions exist extensively throughout the continental lithospheric mantle, the stable portions of tectonic plates that have remained undisturbed for billions of years. Unlike rare gemstones that require specific chemical environments, diamonds form wherever carbon-bearing materials encounter standard mantle conditions.

Geological surveys using seismic analysis and deep drilling have revealed diamond indicator minerals across vast geographic regions. The presence of pyrope garnets, chrome diopsides, and olivine crystals with specific chemical signatures indicates diamond-bearing kimberlites across enormous areas of continental shields. These indicator mineral fields suggest diamond deposits far exceed current mining operations.

The De Beers cartel controlled approximately 85% of global rough diamond distribution throughout most of the 20th century, deliberately restricting supply through stockpiling and exclusive dealing arrangements. Internal De Beers documents from the 1980s, revealed during antitrust litigation, explicitly described strategies for maintaining "price discipline" through artificial scarcity management.

Company archives detail specific quota systems, territory allocations, and inventory management practices designed to prevent market flooding despite abundant natural supplies. The Central Selling Organization, De Beers' distribution arm, maintained massive stockpiles of rough diamonds in London vaults, releasing inventory at controlled rates regardless of production capacity or natural availability.


The convergence of geological, economic, and historical evidence creates an exceptionally strong epistemic foundation for rejecting the "extremely rare" narrative. Unlike many commercial claims that exist in evidential grey areas, diamond abundance enjoys robust support across multiple independent lines of inquiry.

Geological abundance is directly measurable through mining output data, kimberlite surveys, and mantle studies. The Kimberley Process Certification Scheme tracks global rough diamond production, revealing annual outputs of approximately 130-150 million carats from natural mining operations. This volume indicates substantial natural availability rather than genuine scarcity.

Economic manipulation is documented through corporate records, legal proceedings, and market analysis. The combination eliminates reasonable doubt about the artificial nature of diamond scarcity. De Beers' own strategic documents describe diamond abundance as a challenge requiring active management to maintain price levels, contradicting public messaging about natural rarity.

Synthetic diamond technology provides additional confirmation of abundance claims. Laboratory-grown diamonds, produced using the same pressure and temperature conditions as natural formation, demonstrate that diamond creation requires readily available materials and reproducible processes. The ability to manufacture diamonds at industrial scale using standard equipment confirms that natural formation occurs under common geological conditions.

Market behavior patterns support abundance conclusions. Unlike genuinely rare commodities that appreciate over time due to genuine scarcity, diamonds depreciate rapidly after purchase. Retail diamonds typically lose 60-80% of purchase price immediately upon sale, indicating artificial retail markup rather than scarcity-driven pricing.

The epistemological confidence level for this conclusion approaches certainty due to the direct measurability of key variables. Diamond abundance can be quantified through mining output, geological surveys, and deposit analysis. Cartel manipulation can be documented through business records and market data. The convergence of independent evidence streams creates exceptionally strong grounds for rejecting rarity claims.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemological framework for evaluating diamond rarity involves multiple intersecting domains: geological science, economic history, and marketing analysis. Geological evidence from kimberlite pipe surveys, mantle xenolith studies, and deep drilling operations provides direct physical evidence of diamond abundance. Economic analysis of cartel behavior and market manipulation offers documented evidence of artificial scarcity creation.

The scientific consensus on diamond abundance emerges from convergent evidence across multiple geological subdisciplines. Geochemical analysis of mantle samples, seismic studies of kimberlite formations, and direct mining data all support similar conclusions about diamond prevalence in appropriate geological contexts.

Petrological studies of xenoliths—mantle rocks brought to the surface by volcanic activity—consistently contain diamond inclusions when sourced from appropriate depths. These natural samples provide direct evidence that diamond formation occurs routinely in mantle environments, not as exceptional events requiring rare conditions.

Experimental petrology laboratories have successfully synthesized diamonds using standard high-pressure, high-temperature equipment, demonstrating that formation conditions are reproducible and well-understood. The HPHT (High Pressure High Temperature) and CVD (Chemical Vapor Deposition) processes used for synthetic diamond production mirror natural formation mechanisms, confirming that diamond crystallization requires common geological conditions rather than extraordinary circumstances.

Historical documentation of De Beers' market manipulation comes from company archives, antitrust case files, and investigative journalism. Edward Jay Epstein's "The Diamond Invention" (1982) utilized internal company documents and executive interviews to expose cartel operations. Subsequent academic research by economists like Timothy Green and Jeffrey Sachs confirmed these findings through independent analysis.

Court records from United States v. De Beers (1994-2004) and European Commission competition investigations provide additional documentation of cartel coordination. These legal proceedings revealed extensive evidence of price-fixing, territory allocation, and supply restriction agreements among major diamond producers.

The epistemological strength comes from the independence and convergence of these evidence streams. Geological abundance conclusions emerge from physical measurements and samples, while cartel documentation comes from business records and legal proceedings. Neither evidence stream depends on the other, yet both support identical conclusions about the artificial nature of diamond scarcity.


## 4. Verdict

**CONTRAST**: The claim that diamonds are extremely rare represents a fundamental epistemological inversion of geological and economic reality. Natural diamonds are among the more abundant gemstones, formed under common mantle conditions and available in vast quantities through established mining operations. The perception of rarity results from deliberate market manipulation by the De Beers cartel, which controlled global supply for nearly a century through stockpiling, exclusive dealing, and coordinated marketing campaigns.

The "A Diamond Is Forever" marketing strategy successfully transformed a relatively abundant industrial material into a symbol of romantic commitment and luxury status. This transformation required suppressing public awareness of geological abundance while creating artificial scarcity through cartel coordination. The campaign's success demonstrates how coordinated narrative construction can override empirical evidence in shaping public consciousness and consumer behavior.

The epistemological gap here is "loaded"—the false scarcity narrative serves specific commercial interests while contradicting measurable physical evidence. Unlike cases where scientific understanding evolves or where measurement challenges create genuine uncertainty, diamond abundance is directly observable through mining output, geological surveys, and comparative gemstone analysis.

Geological evidence from multiple independent sources confirms diamond abundance: kimberlite pipe surveys across multiple continents reveal extensive diamond-bearing formations; mantle xenolith studies demonstrate routine diamond presence in appropriate geological contexts; industrial mining operations process diamonds at scales incompatible with genuine rarity. The formation process itself requires only carbon under pressure and heat—conditions that exist throughout continental mantle regions, not exceptional circumstances requiring unique geological events.

Economic evidence documents systematic market manipulation designed to create artificial scarcity despite natural abundance. De Beers' internal documents explicitly describe diamond abundance as a challenge requiring active inventory management to maintain pricing. The company's Central Selling Organization maintained massive stockpiles, releasing inventory at controlled rates regardless of production capacity or natural availability.

The contrast reveals how successfully manufactured narratives can override empirical evidence in public consciousness, particularly when supported by coordinated marketing campaigns and industry-wide messaging. The diamond case demonstrates that perceived rarity, not actual scarcity, drives luxury market psychology in modern consumer culture.

Consumers operating under the genuine rarity assumption make economic decisions based on false premises about natural abundance and intrinsic value. This represents a systematic information asymmetry that has persisted despite widespread availability of contradictory geological and economic evidence. The persistence of the myth despite contrary evidence illustrates the power of coordinated narrative construction in shaping market reality.

The success of synthetic diamond technology—which produces chemically identical diamonds at fraction costs—further confirms the artificial nature of natural diamond scarcity. Laboratory-grown diamonds demonstrate that diamond formation requires readily available materials and reproducible processes, not rare geological phenomena. Market discrimination against synthetic diamonds despite identical properties confirms that perceived scarcity, not material characteristics, determines commercial value.

This epistemological contrast has profound implications for understanding how commercial narratives shape public knowledge about natural phenomena. The diamond case demonstrates that coordinated marketing can successfully override scientific evidence when economic incentives align with psychological biases about rarity and value. The persistence of false scarcity beliefs despite abundant contradictory evidence reveals the power of institutional narrative control in modern consumer culture.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The diamond scarcity myth operates within broader patterns of manufactured luxury and artificial value creation in consumer markets. Similar dynamics appear in other luxury goods industries where marketing narratives diverge significantly from production realities.

The engagement ring tradition, now considered ancient and universal, was largely created by N.W. Ayer's advertising campaign for De Beers beginning in 1938. The "A Diamond Is Forever" slogan, introduced in 1947, is widely regarded as the most successful advertising campaign in marketing history. Before the 1930s, diamond engagement rings were uncommon in American culture, with many couples choosing other gemstones or simple gold bands.

Market research conducted by De Beers in the 1930s revealed that American consumers viewed diamonds as luxury items for wealthy elites rather than essential romantic symbols. The advertising campaign specifically targeted middle-class consumers, creating new cultural associations between diamond ownership and emotional commitment. Campaign materials explicitly instructed men that diamond quality should reflect their love intensity and financial capability.

Synthetic diamond technology, which produces chemically and physically identical diamonds at 70-80% lower costs, demonstrates the disconnect between natural abundance and market pricing. Laboratory-grown diamonds require the same carbon, pressure, and heat as natural formation, but in controlled environments. Their market rejection by traditional retailers indicates that rarity perception, not actual scarcity, drives consumer behavior.

The Chemical Vapor Deposition process can produce gem-quality diamonds with superior clarity and fewer inclusions than natural stones. Despite identical chemical composition and superior quality metrics, synthetic diamonds face systematic market discrimination. This discrimination confirms that perceived scarcity, rather than intrinsic material properties, determines commercial value.

The resale value of diamonds typically ranges from 20-40% of retail purchase price, unlike genuinely rare commodities that maintain or appreciate in value. This dramatic depreciation pattern reflects the artificial nature of retail diamond pricing rather than true scarcity economics.

International gemstone markets reveal the relative abundance disparity clearly. High-quality emeralds, rubies, and sapphires command prices per carat that often exceed comparable diamonds, reflecting their genuine geological rarity. Kashmir sapphires or Padparadscha sapphires can sell for $50,000+ per carat, while comparable diamonds rarely exceed $20,000 per carat in wholesale markets.

The environmental and social costs of diamond mining, including ecosystem disruption and human rights violations, occur partly because diamond abundance enables large-scale extraction operations. Genuinely rare gemstones typically require smaller, more selective mining approaches due to limited deposits.

Open-pit diamond mining operations like Jwaneng in Botswana or Diavik in Canada process millions of tons of ore annually, indicating massive diamond-bearing formations. These industrial-scale operations would be impossible if diamonds were genuinely rare geological phenomena. The Argyle mine processed over 20 million tons of ore during peak production years, extracting diamonds at rates incompatible with genuine scarcity.

Technological applications of diamonds in industrial settings—drill bits, cutting tools, semiconductors, and scientific equipment—rely on diamond abundance and consistency. These applications consume far more diamonds annually than the jewelry market, indicating substantial natural and synthetic availability. Industrial diamond consumption exceeds 4 billion carats annually, approximately 30 times the jewelry market volume.

The diamond cutting and polishing industry employs hundreds of thousands of workers across India, Belgium, Israel, and other centers. This massive labor force processes rough diamonds at scales incompatible with genuine scarcity. Surat, India, alone employs over 500,000 diamond workers, processing approximately 80% of global rough diamond inventory through large-scale industrial operations.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed the geological rarity of diamonds as a specific fact-check item, focusing instead on related claims about diamond mythology and marketing practices. However, their coverage of De Beers marketing campaigns and diamond industry practices provides relevant context for understanding how perceptions of diamond rarity have been shaped by commercial interests rather than geological reality.

The platform has examined claims about diamond engagement ring traditions, debunking the assertion that this custom has ancient origins. Their analysis revealed that diamond engagement rings became common only after coordinated marketing campaigns in the mid-20th century. This indirect approach to diamond mythology provides useful context for understanding how manufactured narratives shape public perception of natural abundance.

Snopes has also addressed various diamond-related urban legends, including claims about diamond rain on other planets and the supposed discovery of massive diamond deposits that would crash global markets. These entries demonstrate awareness of the disconnect between public perceptions of diamond scarcity and astronomical or geological evidence of diamond abundance.

The absence of direct fact-checking on geological rarity claims may reflect the technical complexity of mineralogical evidence and the institutional momentum behind diamond scarcity narratives. Traditional fact-checking platforms often avoid highly technical scientific claims that require extensive geological expertise to evaluate properly.

However, Snopes' analysis of De Beers' historical marketing practices and cartel operations indirectly supports conclusions about artificial scarcity. Their documentation of how diamond engagement ring traditions were manufactured through advertising campaigns demonstrates the company's success in creating cultural practices based on false premises about natural abundance.
