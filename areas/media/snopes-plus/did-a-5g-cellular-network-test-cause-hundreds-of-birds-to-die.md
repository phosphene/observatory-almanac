# A 5G Network Test Caused Hundreds of Birds to Die

## 1. The Claim

In late 2018 and early 2019, a striking narrative began circulating online: during a test of 5G cellular network technology in The Hague, Netherlands, hundreds of starlings had suddenly dropped dead from the sky, their bodies littering Huijgenspark, a small urban park in the Dutch administrative capital. The claim was vivid, specific, and terrifying. Depending on the version, the death toll ranged from 150 to 297 to as many as 500 birds, all allegedly struck down simultaneously by the invisible hand of next-generation wireless radiation.

The story first appeared on Dutch-language blogs and alternative health websites in November and December 2018. Early versions cited unnamed "residents" who reportedly witnessed the birds falling mid-flight, with some accounts describing the starlings tumbling out of their famous murmuration formations — those mesmerizing aerial ballets that starling flocks perform at dusk. The visual power of this imagery was immense: nature's most beautiful collective behavior interrupted and destroyed by human technology.

By January 2019, the claim had leapt from its Dutch-language origins into English-language anti-5G Facebook groups, where it was presented as documentary evidence — often accompanied by genuine photographs of dead starlings — that 5G technology poses a mortal threat to wildlife. The narrative framework was simple and compelling: the Dutch government had conducted a secret 5G test; the birds in the immediate vicinity had died; therefore, 5G killed the birds. The implicit follow-up was even more alarming: if 5G can kill hundreds of birds instantly, what is it doing to human beings?

From anti-5G Facebook groups, the claim spread to YouTube, where it was featured in videos with titles like "5G KILLS BIRDS — Exposed" and "The Hague 5G Bird Massacre — What They Don't Want You to Know." These videos accumulated hundreds of thousands of views. Twitter accounts with large followings in the alternative health and conspiracy communities amplified the claim further. By mid-2019, the story had been translated into French, German, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Russian, and Arabic, appearing on websites and social media platforms worldwide.

The claim was presented not as speculation but as established fact that the mainstream media was deliberately suppressing. Some versions included fabricated quotes from unnamed Dutch officials allegedly "covering up" the connection. Others referenced entirely fictional scientific reports. The core assertion was always the same: 5G radiation is so powerful that it kills birds on contact, and the incident in The Hague proved it.

## 2. What's Actually True

The bird deaths were real. That is the first and most important thing to establish, because the effectiveness of this particular piece of disinformation rested on the anchoring of a fabricated causal claim to a genuine, documented event.

A series of starling die-offs did occur in and around Huijgenspark in The Hague. However, the timeline was wrong in almost every version of the viral claim. The die-offs occurred primarily in **June 2018**, not October or November 2018 as the anti-5G narratives stated. Birds were found dead in the park over a period of several days, with the largest single event involving approximately 150 starlings. Additional smaller die-offs were reported in subsequent days. The total number of dead birds recovered during the entire episode was in the low hundreds — within the range cited by the claim, but spread across multiple events over weeks rather than a single catastrophic moment.

Dutch authorities investigated the die-offs. The Netherlands Food and Consumer Product Safety Authority (NVWA) collected bird carcasses for necropsy. Stichting DierenDetective (the Animal Detective Foundation), a Dutch organization that investigates unusual animal deaths, also examined the incidents. The NVWA investigation found no evidence of poisoning, infectious disease, or environmental contamination that would explain the deaths. Some of the birds showed signs of internal hemorrhaging and bruising consistent with traumatic impact — leading investigators toward the hypothesis that the starlings had experienced a collision event, possibly involving a predator (such as a raptor) scattering a flock into trees or structures during a murmuration. Other possible explanations included ingestion of toxic yew berries, which are present in the park and are known to be fatal to some bird species when consumed in sufficient quantities.

What the investigation emphatically did **not** find was any connection to electromagnetic radiation of any kind. And critically, **no 5G network test was documented at or near Huijgenspark during the period of the die-offs.** The Netherlands was indeed in the early stages of 5G spectrum allocation and planning in 2018, but the specific claim that a 5G mast was being tested in the immediate vicinity of the park was unsupported by any evidence — no permits, no equipment records, no operator confirmations, nothing. The 5G element of the story was added retroactively, grafted onto the real bird deaths without evidentiary basis.

This matters because the scientific literature on radio frequency (RF) electromagnetic fields and biological effects is extensive, and it does not support the mechanism the claim implies. The World Health Organization's International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified RF electromagnetic fields as **Group 2B** — "possibly carcinogenic to humans" — in 2011. This classification is frequently cited by anti-5G activists as evidence that wireless technology is dangerous. However, Group 2B is the weakest positive classification in the IARC system. It means that there is limited evidence of carcinogenicity in humans and less than sufficient evidence of carcinogenicity in experimental animals. For context, Group 2B also includes pickled vegetables and talcum powder. It does not mean "probably causes cancer"; it means "we cannot entirely rule it out based on current evidence." The classification was made primarily on the basis of a single epidemiological study (the Interphone study) showing a possible association between heavy mobile phone use and glioma, an association that subsequent larger studies have not consistently replicated.

Research specifically on birds and RF fields provides no support for the claim of acute mass mortality. Wiltschko et al. (2015), published in the *Journal of Experimental Biology*, demonstrated that electromagnetic fields can interfere with the magnetic compass orientation of European robins — the birds' ability to sense the Earth's magnetic field for navigation purposes. However, this effect was observed at field intensities far exceeding anything produced by a 5G base station or any cellular network infrastructure. The power levels involved in the Wiltschko experiments were orders of magnitude above what birds would encounter from telecommunications equipment. Moreover, the effect was on navigation behavior, not survival — the birds became disoriented, not dead.

No peer-reviewed study published in any scientific journal has documented acute mass mortality of wild birds caused by 5G or any other generation of cellular network RF exposure. The specific biophysical mechanism by which sub-thermal RF fields at base station power levels could cause instantaneous death in birds has never been proposed in the scientific literature, let alone demonstrated. The energy levels are simply too low to cause the kind of acute tissue damage that would result in immediate death.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The persuasive power of the 5G-bird-death claim illustrates a cognitive mechanism that we might call **causal proximity grafting**: the rhetorical and psychological process by which two real but unrelated events are placed within the same geographic and temporal frame, creating the perception of a causal link where none exists. The bird die-off was real. The development of 5G technology in the Netherlands was real. Both occurred in The Hague. Both occurred in 2018. The claim simply asserted that the first was caused by the second, and for many people, the spatial and temporal proximity was sufficient to make the connection feel true.

This technique is particularly effective when both source events are obscure, foreign, or inaccessible to the audience. Most people who encountered the claim had no independent knowledge of Huijgenspark, no access to Dutch-language investigative reports, no ability to verify whether 5G equipment was present in a specific location in The Hague, and no expertise in avian pathology or RF biophysics. The claim could not be quickly checked by the average social media user, so it persisted and spread in the gap between assertion and verification.

Causal proximity grafting exploits a well-documented cognitive shortcut: the human tendency to infer causation from correlation, particularly when the proposed cause-and-effect relationship aligns with prior beliefs or anxieties. Psychologist Ziva Kunda's influential work on **motivated reasoning** (Kunda, 1990, *Psychological Bulletin*) demonstrated that people evaluate evidence in a biased manner when they have a directional goal — when they *want* a particular conclusion to be true. For individuals already concerned about wireless radiation, already suspicious of telecommunications companies, or already embedded in communities organized around opposition to 5G, the bird-death claim did not need to meet a high evidentiary standard. It confirmed what they already believed, and motivated reasoning ensured that confirming evidence was accepted uncritically while disconfirming evidence was dismissed or ignored.

The anti-5G movement provided the social infrastructure — the Facebook groups, YouTube channels, websites, and real-world protest networks — through which the claim could propagate. This was not a claim invented by a lone crank; it entered a pre-existing ecosystem of belief and activism that had both the motive and the means to spread it globally. Within these communities, sharing and amplifying the claim served social functions beyond information transmission: it signaled group membership, demonstrated vigilance against perceived threats, and reinforced collective identity.

The claim also drew strength from a recurring historical pattern that scholars of technology anxiety have documented extensively: **new technology panic cycles**. Virtually every generation of wireless technology has been greeted with claims of catastrophic biological effects. In the 1990s, early mobile phones were said to cause brain tumors (the scientific evidence for this remains inconclusive at best). During the 3G rollout in the early 2000s, health activists claimed that new base stations were causing headaches, nausea, and sleep disturbances in nearby residents — claims that were not supported when tested in double-blind provocation studies. The 4G/LTE rollout in the early 2010s generated similar concerns. By the time 5G arrived, the template was well established: new wireless generation → health panic → specific claims of harm → scientific investigation finding no evidence → persistence of claims regardless.

Perhaps most importantly, the claim benefited from what might be called **epistemic scaffolding** — the existence of genuine scientific uncertainty that could be conscripted to support an unfounded specific claim. The IARC 2B classification is real. The Wiltschko research on birds and electromagnetic fields is real. The ongoing scientific debate about whether long-term, low-level RF exposure has any biological effects is real. These legitimate elements of scientific discourse gave the bird-death claim a veneer of plausibility that it would not have possessed if the scientific literature were entirely clear. The claim's propagators could point to the IARC classification and say, "Even the WHO admits this radiation is dangerous" — a distortion of what Group 2B means, but a rhetorically effective one. This technique of exploiting genuine uncertainty to launder fabricated specifics is one of the most common and most dangerous disinformation strategies in circulation.

## 4. Verdict

**FALSE.**

The specific claim — that a 5G network test in The Hague caused the sudden death of hundreds of starlings — is fabricated. It fails on every factual element:

- **The timing was wrong.** The bird die-offs occurred primarily in June 2018, not during the October-November 2018 period alleged in the viral claim.
- **The cause was wrong.** Dutch authorities investigated the die-offs and found no evidence of electromagnetic radiation involvement. Leading explanations pointed to collision events or possible toxic berry ingestion.
- **The 5G test never happened.** No 5G infrastructure was documented as operational or under test at or near Huijgenspark during the period of the die-offs. The 5G element was retroactively grafted onto a real but unrelated event.
- **The mechanism is unsupported.** No peer-reviewed scientific study has documented acute mass mortality of wild birds from exposure to 5G or any cellular network RF fields. The power levels involved in 5G base station transmissions are insufficient to cause the kind of acute biological damage that would result in immediate mass death.

The bird deaths were real. The 5G causation was not. The claim constitutes a fabricated event — not in the sense that nothing happened, but in the sense that the causal story connecting a real event to a fictitious cause was manufactured without evidence and propagated deliberately.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Hague bird-death claim did not exist in isolation. It was one node in a sprawling network of anti-5G narratives that grew through 2019 and reached its most dangerous peak in 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic provided new fuel for the movement.

The anti-5G panic of 2019-2020 was remarkable in its scope and its real-world consequences. In the United Kingdom, between March and May 2020, at least 77 mobile phone towers were physically attacked — set on fire or vandalized — by individuals who believed that 5G technology was either causing COVID-19 directly, weakening immune systems to make people susceptible to the virus, or was being used as cover for a mass surveillance or population-control agenda. Similar attacks occurred in the Netherlands, Belgium, Ireland, Italy, Australia, New Zealand, and Canada. Telecommunications engineers were harassed and threatened. In some cases, the damaged towers were not even 5G-capable; they were older 3G and 4G equipment, but the attackers either did not know or did not care.

The intellectual pathway from "5G killed birds in The Hague" to "5G caused COVID-19" may not be immediately obvious, but it follows a clear escalation logic within conspiratorial thinking. The bird-death claim established, for those who believed it, that 5G technology was biologically lethal — capable of killing living creatures on contact. Once that premise was accepted, it required only a modest additional step to believe that 5G could cause illness in humans. When COVID-19 emerged — a novel illness with no immediately intuitive explanation for many people — the anti-5G framework was ready to absorb it.

This escalation illustrates how individual disinformation claims function as **nodes in a belief network** rather than as isolated assertions. Each claim, once accepted, lowers the epistemic threshold for accepting the next. The bird-death claim was a gateway — relatively modest in its implications but serving as proof-of-concept for the broader proposition that 5G is biologically dangerous. From there, the movement could escalate to claims about human health effects, government suppression of evidence, and ultimately the incorporation of 5G conspiracism into the much larger QAnon and "Great Reset" conspiracy ecosystems.

The economic dimension is also significant. The anti-5G movement was not purely a grassroots phenomenon. Research by organizations including the Oxford Internet Institute and the EU DisinfoLab has documented how anti-5G content has been amplified by state-affiliated media outlets, particularly RT (formerly Russia Today), and by commercial actors selling "EMF protection" products — crystals, pendants, stickers, fabric, and devices that claim to block or neutralize electromagnetic radiation, none of which have demonstrated efficacy. The 5G-bird-death claim, along with similar stories, served the commercial interests of an "alternative health" industry estimated to generate billions of dollars annually. Fear of wireless technology is, for some actors, not a belief but a product line.

The scientific community's response has been consistent and clear but has struggled to compete with the virality of the claims. Major reviews — including the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection (ICNIRP) guidelines updated in 2020, the comprehensive review by the Scientific Committee on Emerging and Newly Identified Health Risks (SCENIHR) of the European Commission, and multiple national reviews by agencies in Sweden, Australia, the UK, and elsewhere — have all concluded that exposure to RF electromagnetic fields at levels below established guidelines does not cause adverse health effects. The 5G frequencies in use (primarily in the sub-6 GHz and millimeter wave bands) do not introduce novel biological mechanisms compared to previous generations of wireless technology.

But the disinformation ecosystem does not require scientific consensus to be on its side. It requires only that scientific consensus be imperfect, incomplete, or poorly communicated — which it always is, because that is the nature of science. The exploitation of genuine uncertainty to manufacture unfounded certainty remains one of the most effective disinformation techniques in operation.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated this claim **FALSE**, and their fact-check remains one of the most comprehensive treatments of the specific bird-death narrative. The organization investigated the timeline, contacted Dutch authorities, reviewed the investigative reports on the Huijgenspark die-offs, and verified that no 5G test had been conducted at the alleged location.

The fact-check process worked well in this case largely because the claim was attached to a **specific, datable, locatable event**. Claims about particular incidents — a specific number of birds, in a specific park, on a specific date — can be verified or falsified against documentary records. This is the easiest category of misinformation for fact-checkers to address: the claim makes testable predictions, and the tests come back negative.

Contrast this with vaguer anti-5G claims like "5G weakens the immune system" or "5G causes cancer over time" — claims that are not attached to specific events and that invoke mechanisms operating over timescales too long for easy verification. These claims are much harder to fact-check definitively because they do not make falsifiable short-term predictions. The bird-death claim's specificity was, paradoxically, both its rhetorical strength (it felt concrete and evidence-based) and its epistemic weakness (it could be definitively checked and found false).

Other major fact-checking organizations also addressed the claim. Full Fact (UK), AFP Fact Check, Reuters Fact Check, and PolitiFact all published analyses reaching the same conclusion. The Dutch Radiocommunications Agency (Agentschap Telecom) explicitly stated that no 5G experiments had taken place in the area at the time of the bird deaths. The convergence of multiple independent fact-checking organizations on the same verdict is noteworthy and provides one of the clearest examples of fact-checking consensus in the anti-5G domain.

However, the debunking did not stop the claim. This is perhaps the most important lesson from the 5G-bird-death episode: **fact-checking and counter-narrative do not automatically neutralize viral misinformation.** The claim continues to circulate years after it was thoroughly debunked. It has become what researchers call a **"zombie claim"** — a piece of misinformation that has been killed by evidence but refuses to stay dead, rising again in new contexts, on new platforms, and before new audiences who never encountered the original fact-check.

The persistence of zombie claims reflects several structural features of the information environment. First, fact-checks rarely reach the same audience as the original claim. The people most likely to encounter and believe the bird-death claim are also the people least likely to follow Snopes or Reuters Fact Check. Second, within anti-5G communities, fact-checking organizations are themselves perceived as part of the cover-up — agents of the telecommunications industry or the government tasked with suppressing the truth. Debunking by Snopes can actually increase belief in the claim among motivated audiences, a phenomenon known as the **"backfire effect"** or more precisely described in Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler's work on motivated reasoning and corrections (2010, *Political Behavior*). Third, social media platforms have historically been slow to act on health misinformation, allowing debunked claims to continue circulating and accumulating engagement long after corrections are published.

The 5G-bird-death claim also reveals the limitations of the individual-claim fact-checking model. Even when a specific claim is thoroughly debunked, the underlying belief system — that 5G is dangerous, that authorities are covering up health effects, that wireless technology kills — remains intact. Debunking one claim within a belief network does not collapse the network; it simply redirects adherents to other claims. The fact-checking community has increasingly recognized this limitation and has begun developing approaches that address the structural features of misinformation — media literacy education, platform design interventions, and pre-bunking strategies — rather than relying solely on post-hoc individual claim verification.

For the Truth Vault, this case is instructive: a well-constructed, vivid, specific false claim built on a real event, amplified by a motivated community, resistant to correction even after comprehensive debunking, and serving as a gateway to more extreme conspiratorial beliefs. It is a textbook example of modern disinformation architecture.
