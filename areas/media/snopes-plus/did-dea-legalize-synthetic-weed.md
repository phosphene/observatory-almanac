# Did the DEA Legalize Synthetic Marijuana?


## 1. The Claim

In November 2017, a cluster of articles from cannabis culture and political commentary websites reported that the Drug Enforcement Administration had approved "synthetic marijuana" as a Schedule II controlled substance — a classification that permits medical prescribing — while simultaneously maintaining plant-based marijuana as a Schedule I substance with "no currently accepted medical use." The framing in popular coverage emphasised apparent regulatory hypocrisy: the government was treating a synthetic proxy for marijuana as medically legitimate while continuing to criminalise the natural plant.

The most shared iteration of this claim came from Herb.co, a cannabis culture website, under the headline "The DEA Approves Synthetic Marijuana as Medicine While the Real Thing Remains Illegal." The article appeared in November 2017, shortly after the DEA issued its final ruling — published in the *Federal Register* on 22 November 2017 — placing Syndros, an oral solution formulation of the synthetic compound dronabinol, into Schedule II of the Controlled Substances Act. The story was picked up by Vice News, Motherboard, and other outlets covering the marijuana policy space. It circulated widely in pro-marijuana-legalisation circles where the DEA's apparent double standard served as a pointed political argument.

The embedded political argument was not trivial. Critics of U.S. drug policy have long argued that the Schedule I classification of cannabis — which legally requires a finding of "no currently accepted medical use and a lack of accepted safety for use under medical supervision" — is scientifically indefensible given the accumulating evidence base for therapeutic uses of cannabinoid compounds. The Syndros ruling offered a fresh illustration of the contradiction: if a synthetic molecule chemically identical to a component of a Schedule I plant can be classified as medically acceptable, the rationale for maintaining the plant's Schedule I status is significantly weakened.

Snopes.com rated the claim a Mixture in a fact-check by Alex Kasprak, published 30 November 2017. This entry unpacks what was true, what was misleading, and what the important distinctions are.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The factual core.** On 5 July 2016, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved a New Drug Application from Insys Therapeutics for Syndros — an oral liquid formulation of dronabinol, a pharmaceutical compound that is a synthetic version of delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC), the primary psychoactive ingredient in cannabis. Syndros was approved for treating anorexia associated with weight loss in AIDS patients, and for nausea and vomiting associated with cancer chemotherapy in patients who had not responded adequately to conventional antiemetic treatments. Following FDA approval, the DEA is required to schedule any new pharmaceutical. The DEA issued an interim final rule on 23 March 2017, placing Syndros in Schedule II — a classification reserved for substances with high potential for abuse but accepted medical use — and confirmed this placement without changes in its final rule published 22 November 2017 (Federal Register, 2017).

**The hypocrisy argument has genuine substance.** The DEA's own final ruling explicitly addressed the contradiction. The ruling states: "The DEA notes that FDA-approved products of oral solutions containing dronabinol have an approved medical use, whereas marijuana does not have an approved medical use and therefore remains in schedule I." This reasoning is circular and has been noted as such by drug policy scholars and advocates. The DEA's position depends on treating plant-originated and synthetically produced THC as categorically different — a distinction that does not map onto chemical identity or pharmacological action. Dronabinol is, at the molecular level, the same compound as the THC found in the cannabis plant.

**What the reporting got wrong: the K2/Spice conflation.** The Herb.co article — and several subsequent reports drawing on it — illustrated the "synthetic marijuana" story with photographs captioned as depicting emergencies caused by K2, Spice, and similar products sold in gas stations and convenience stores primarily in the 2010s. These products are generically described as "synthetic cannabis" in popular usage, but they are not the same as dronabinol. K2 and Spice contain synthetic cannabinoid compounds — typically powerful agonists of the CB1 receptor — that are not chemically identical to THC. These compounds (catalogued by researchers; see Castaneto, M. et al., 2014, "Synthetic Cannabinoids: Epidemiology, Pharmacodynamics, and Clinical Implications," *Drug and Alcohol Dependence*) were specifically manufactured to be structurally novel enough to evade existing drug scheduling, and they often have dramatically different and more dangerous pharmacological profiles than natural THC, including a much higher binding affinity and greater risk of psychotic episodes, cardiovascular events, and death.

The DEA did not schedule K2, Spice, or any similar product as medicine. Using images of K2 poisoning victims alongside a story about dronabinol created a serious and misleading conflation between two categories of substance that share only approximate popular vocabulary. Dronabinol is a chemically pure, pharmaceutical-grade molecule. K2 and Spice are uncharacterised mixtures of synthetic compounds sold without quality control. The Herb.co report's use of street "synthetic cannabis" imagery in a story about a pharmaceutical dronabinol ruling was misleading by juxtaposition.

**The 1985 history the report buried.** The second major omission in the viral reporting was historical. Dronabinol in capsule form — marketed as Marinol — was first approved by the FDA in 1985, more than thirty years before the Syndros ruling. Marinol in capsule form was initially classified as Schedule II, and was then moved to the less restrictive Schedule III in 1999 (Federal Register, 1999). This means that synthetic THC has been a legally prescribable medicine in the United States for over three decades. The "new" Syndros ruling extended this approval to a liquid oral formulation — a meaningful regulatory event, but not a precedent-setting one, much less a sudden reversal of drug policy. The implied newness of the DEA's position was false: the DEA had been treating synthetic THC as medically acceptable since before most of the people who shared the 2017 articles were born.

The reason for the distinction in scheduling between Marinol (Schedule III) and Syndros (Schedule II) was not ideological inconsistency but a pharmacokinetic assessment: the Department of Health and Human Services advised the DEA that the oral liquid form of dronabinol could be more easily manipulated to produce concentrated extracts for inhalation, giving it greater abuse potential than the harder-to-process capsule form (Federal Register, March 2017).


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Genuine merit in the underlying critique.** The fact that the claim is partly misleading should not obscure the fact that the underlying policy critique has genuine merit. The Schedule I classification of cannabis — maintained despite decades of accumulating research supporting therapeutic applications — does sit in uncomfortable tension with the approval of medicines that derive their active ingredients from the same plant. The scheduling contradiction is real; it was the Herb.co reporting's characterisation of the contradiction (framing it as new, and conflating dronabinol with K2/Spice) that introduced the inaccuracies. The claim's persistence in drug policy circles partly reflects the fact that people with legitimate grievances about U.S. drug policy find the underlying argument useful, even in its imprecise form.

**Regulatory complexity creates information asymmetry.** The DEA scheduling system, the FDA drug approval process, the Controlled Substances Act's classification criteria, the distinction between interim and final rulings, and the history of specific pharmaceutical approvals over decades constitute a dense regulatory landscape that most people — including many journalists — cannot be expected to navigate with precision on a deadline. The complexity of the actual regulatory history (dronabinol approved 1985, moved from Schedule II to Schedule III in 1999, then Syndros as a new formulation scheduled at II in 2017) is not easily summarised in a headline, which creates structural pressure toward simplification that collapses important distinctions.

**The term "synthetic marijuana" does too much work.** Popular usage of "synthetic marijuana" encompasses at least three meaningfully different categories: (1) dronabinol, a pharmaceutical-grade synthetic molecule chemically identical to natural THC; (2) synthetic cannabinoid receptor agonists like those found in K2/Spice, which are not identical to THC but activate the same receptor; and (3) various semi-synthetic manipulations of natural cannabinoids. These categories have radically different risk profiles, pharmacological mechanisms, legal statuses, and social contexts. A single popular label applied to all three produces systematic confusion. Castaneto et al. (2014, *Drug and Alcohol Dependence*) document the pharmacological diversity of synthetic cannabinoid compounds and their clinical distinctions, but this scholarly precision does not translate into the popular vocabulary.

**Political utility amplifies imprecision.** For audiences deeply invested in cannabis legalisation, the narrative of DEA hypocrisy is politically valuable and emotionally satisfying. The story confirms a prior belief — that drug policy is arbitrary rather than evidence-based — and provides a specific concrete example to cite. This positive reinforcement creates strong motivation to share the story and reduced motivation to examine its accuracy. Politically useful claims share this property across the ideological spectrum: they are shielded from scrutiny by the social cost of undermining a narrative that one's community finds important.


## 4. Verdict

**Mixture.** As Kasprak (2018) correctly establishes, the claim is partially true at the core (the DEA did place dronabinol-based Syndros in Schedule II while marijuana remains in Schedule I, and there is genuine policy inconsistency here) but misleading in its framing (dronabinol is not the same as the K2/Spice products that prior reporting associated with "synthetic marijuana," and the scheduling of synthetic THC has been ongoing since 1985, not since 2017). The political argument about DEA hypocrisy is defensible and supported by the DEA's own statements, but it was embedded in reporting that misidentified the substance category and presented a thirty-year regulatory history as a sudden reversal.

A more accurate version of the underlying claim would read: "The DEA has scheduled pharmaceutical-grade synthetic THC (dronabinol) as a legally prescribable medicine since 1985, while maintaining plant-based cannabis as a Schedule I drug with no accepted medical use. A 2017 ruling extending this approval to a new oral liquid formulation of dronabinol is the latest iteration of this long-standing inconsistency, not a new policy departure."


## 5. The Wider Picture

The dronabinol case illuminates a broader set of tensions in U.S. drug policy that extend beyond the specific scheduling question. The Controlled Substances Act of 1970, which established the current scheduling system, created a framework that was designed primarily as a law enforcement tool and was not optimised for integration with the iterative, evidence-based processes of pharmaceutical regulation. The two systems have existed in increasing friction since the late twentieth century as the evidence base for therapeutic cannabinoid applications has expanded.

Research on cannabinoid pharmacology has grown substantially since the 1990s. The discovery and characterisation of the human endocannabinoid system — the set of receptors (CB1 and CB2) and endogenous ligands (anandamide, 2-arachidonoylglycerol) that natural and synthetic cannabinoids interact with — has provided a robust mechanistic framework for understanding both the therapeutic and adverse effects of these compounds (Di Marzo, V., 1998, "Endocannabinoids and other fatty acid derivatives with cannabimimetic properties: Biochemistry and possible physiopathological relevance," *Biochimica et Biophysica Acta*). Subsequent clinical research has supported medical applications including pain management, nausea suppression, appetite stimulation, and the treatment of certain forms of childhood epilepsy.

The legal treatment of cannabis at the federal level has not kept pace with this expanding evidence base, producing the contradictions that stories like the dronabinol scheduling regularly expose. The FDA's approval of Epidiolex (a purified CBD oil product for certain epilepsy indications) in 2018 continued the pattern: another cannabinoid compound, again chemically derived from or identical to components of the Schedule I plant, approved for medical use while the plant itself remained Schedule I.

Drug policy scholars including Mark Kleiman, Jonathan Caulkins, and Angela Hawken have argued that the scheduling system's rigidity creates a systematic mismatch between scientific evidence and legal classification that produces exactly the kind of inconsistency the Syndros story highlighted (Kleiman, M.A.R., Caulkins, J.P., & Hawken, A., 2011, *Drugs and Drug Policy: What Everyone Needs to Know*, Oxford University Press). The political resolution of this mismatch has been ongoing at state level (medical cannabis programs, recreational legalisation) but has been structurally impeded at the federal level by the Controlled Substances Act framework itself.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Kasprak (2018) fact-check demonstrates the value of the Mixture rating in capturing a claim that is neither simply true nor simply false, but where different components have different truth values. The headline claim (DEA legalised synthetic marijuana while keeping cannabis illegal) is directionally accurate in its hypocrisy criticism but substantively misleading in its characterisation of both the substance (conflating dronabinol with K2/Spice) and the novelty (a first since 1985 this was not). Rating it Mixture rather than False preserves the genuine policy point while correcting the framing errors.

The methodological approach in this type of fact-check requires decomposing a compound claim into its constituent components and evaluating each separately. What was the DEA action? (Factual: Syndros placed in Schedule II.) What did the DEA action mean in context? (Historical: dronabinol has been approved since 1985.) What substance is at issue? (Chemical: dronabinol is not the same substance as K2/Spice products.) Does the policy hypocrisy argument hold? (Substantive: yes, with caveats about the regulatory distinction between plant and pharmaceutical forms.) Each of these questions requires different sourcing: the Federal Register for the regulatory action, pharmaceutical history databases for the 1985 approval, pharmacology literature for the chemical distinctions, and drug policy commentary for the normative evaluation.

For readers who encounter drug policy claims, the key practice is tracing the specific regulatory action and the specific substance involved before accepting any characterisation of what a ruling means. Drug policy journalism is particularly prone to generalising from specific regulatory actions to broad characterisations of policy direction, and the technical vocabulary (Schedule I vs. II vs. III, the distinction between pharmaceutical-grade compounds and unregulated street products) is both important and not widely known. Checking the primary source — the Federal Register in this case — is time-consuming but definitively clarifying.
