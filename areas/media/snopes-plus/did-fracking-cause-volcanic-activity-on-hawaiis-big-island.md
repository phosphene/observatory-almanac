# Fracking caused volcanic activity on Hawaii's Big Island


## 1. The Claim

In May 2018, as lava from the Kīlauea Lower East Rift Zone (LERZ) began breaking through residential neighbourhoods in the Leilani Estates subdivision on Hawaii's Big Island, a claim spread rapidly across social media platforms and alternative news websites attributing the eruption — or its acceleration, or its unusual severity — to hydraulic fracturing operations near the volcano. The specific claims varied in their details: some asserted that a geothermal power plant near the eruption site had been using fracking to exploit underground heat; others claimed that commercial oil or gas fracking had been taking place on the island and had destabilised the volcanic plumbing system; and others suggested that the geothermal plant had "drilled into" Kīlauea's magma chamber, releasing the eruption. All versions converged on the central claim: human industrial activity — specifically, some form of hydraulic fracturing — had triggered or substantially caused one of the most destructive volcanic events in recent Hawaiian history.

The claim found an amplified audience because the 2018 LERZ eruption was a major and highly visible disaster. Over several months, approximately 2,500 homes were destroyed, 13.7 square miles of land were covered in lava, and a new ocean entry point was created on the island's southeast coast (Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, 2018, USGS Event Archive). The eruption produced dramatic footage of lava flows advancing through neighbourhoods, ground cracking open in streets, and geysers of steam and toxic gas. This visual drama made it powerfully shareable, and the fracking claim rode its coattails — suggesting not only that the eruption was man-made but that a cover-up was preventing public knowledge of industrial culpability.

The geothermal plant in question — Puna Geothermal Venture (PGV), located in the Leilani Estates area — was real, operational, and did suffer significant damage in the 2018 eruption, being evacuated and inundated with lava. Its proximity to the eruption site gave the claim a geographic anchor. The further assertion that geothermal operations involve "fracking" — combined with public unfamiliarity with the distinction between geothermal energy extraction and hydraulic fracturing — provided the technical-sounding core.

This claim also tapped into broader political currents: the 2018 period was marked by intense public awareness of anti-fracking movements in the continental United States; by media coverage of the connections between fracking-induced seismicity and earthquakes in Oklahoma and elsewhere; and by a general suspicion, particularly in environmental activist communities, of the fossil fuel industry's willingness to impose externalities on local communities. These legitimate political concerns created a receptive audience for a claim that extended anti-fracking sentiment into a new dramatic domain.


## 2. What's Actually True

**There is no commercial fracking in Hawaii.** Hydraulic fracturing is a technique used to extract oil and natural gas from low-permeability rock formations — primarily shale — by injecting high-pressure fluid to create fractures through which hydrocarbons can flow. The technique requires the presence of an economically viable oil or gas resource. Hawaii has no oil or gas fields; the islands are composed of basaltic oceanic crust from hotspot volcanism, not sedimentary basins containing hydrocarbons. The United States Energy Information Administration's records show no oil or natural gas production from Hawaii, no fracking permits have ever been issued for Hawaii, and there is no regulatory or geological infrastructure for oil and gas extraction on any Hawaiian island (US EIA, State Energy Profiles, Hawaii, 2023). The factual premise of the claim — that fracking was occurring on the Big Island — is simply false.

**Puna Geothermal Venture and what geothermal drilling actually is.** The Puna Geothermal Venture facility, which has operated in the lower Puna district since the early 1990s, extracts energy by drilling into subsurface rock formations heated by volcanic activity and using the steam and hot water to drive turbines. Geothermal drilling does involve drilling into rock, and in some geothermal applications — particularly in enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) — hydraulic stimulation techniques are used to improve permeability in hot dry rock. However, PGV in its operational form does not use hydraulic fracturing: it exploits naturally permeable geothermal reservoirs, which in volcanically active areas like Puna are sufficiently permeable without stimulation (Teplow et al., 2009, "Dacite Melt at the Kīlauea East Rift Zone," *Stanford Geothermal Workshop Proceedings*; Spielman et al., various PGV technical reports).

Even in the case of enhanced geothermal systems that *do* use hydraulic stimulation, the scale, depth, and mechanism of geothermal fluid injection is categorically different from conventional hydrocarbon fracking. The volumes, pressures, and geological contexts are not comparable. Geothermal wells in Hawaii operate in a volcanic-hydrothermal environment where native permeability is created by the same magmatic processes that produce eruptions; human drilling operations in this context are drawing from, not creating, the thermal resources.

**The volcanology of the 2018 Kīlauea eruption.** The 2018 Lower East Rift Zone eruption was one of the best-documented volcanic events in history precisely because the United States Geological Survey's Hawaiian Volcano Observatory (HVO) has continuously monitored Kīlauea since 1912 — making it the most extensively instrumented active volcano in the world. The geological precursors, mechanisms, and evolution of the 2018 eruption are thoroughly understood and documented (Neal et al., 2019, "The 2018 rift eruption and summit collapse of Kīlauea Volcano," *Science*, 363(6425), 367–374).

The eruption was driven by a complex sequence of events rooted in conditions that developed over months to years: a rising lava lake in Overlook Crater at the summit had been building pressure since late 2017; intrusion events along the East Rift Zone had periodically redistributed magma; a collapse of the Pu'u 'Ō'ō cone in late April 2018 abruptly dropped the eruption site pressure at the mid-rift, sending a magma pulse eastward down the LERZ; a series of shallow earthquakes in May 2018 — including a magnitude 6.9 event on the island's south flank on 4 May 2018 — facilitated ground cracking and magma ascent to the surface in Leilani Estates. The entire causal chain is explicable within well-established volcanological and geophysical frameworks.

The magnitude 6.9 earthquake is particularly important for understanding why PGV's proximity was coincidental rather than causal. The earthquake occurred because of ongoing displacement on the south flank fault system — a feature driven by the weight of the island pressing laterally into the ocean and by magmatic pressure in the rift zone, processes that have operated for hundreds of thousands of years (Swanson et al., 2019, "Forty years of recurrent lava flows from a continuously active rift zone on Kīlauea," *Journal of Volcanology and Geothermal Research*, 378, 43–51). The fault system is not a product of industrial activity; it is a fundamental structural feature of the shield volcano. The 2018 eruption was large and destructive by historical standards but was not anomalous in a longer geological context; the LERZ has erupted repeatedly throughout recorded history, with comparable events documented in 1955 and at other intervals.

**Scale and mechanism incompatibilities.** Even granting, hypothetically, that geothermal drilling at PGV had some disturbing effect on local conditions, the claim that any surface industrial operation could trigger a volcanic eruption driven by magma accumulated in a rift system kilometres deep is physically implausible. The energy involved in a major volcanic eruption is on the order of 10²⁶ to 10²⁷ ergs for total heat release, with eruption rates measured in millions of cubic metres of lava per day at peak flow. The energy involved in drilling and fluid injection operations is orders of magnitude smaller than the geophysical forces at work a few kilometres below the rift zone. It is roughly analogous to claiming that a garden hose provoked an ocean wave: the mechanism, the scale, and the causal direction are all wrong.

This does not mean that industrial operations near active volcanic regions are consequence-free or that monitoring is unnecessary. The Icelandic deep drilling project (IDDP-1), which accidentally intercepted a magma body in 2009 while attempting to drill into a superheated geothermal reservoir, illustrates that proximity to volcanic systems requires careful engineering management (Elders et al., 2011, "The Iceland Deep Drilling Project: The first well, IDDP-1, drilled into magma," *Scientific Drilling*, 12, 1–12). But "careful management is required" is not equivalent to "industrial operations caused the 2018 Kīlauea eruption."


## 3. Why People Believe This

The Kīlauea fracking claim is a distinctive case study in what might be called *geophysical conspiratorial displacement* — the cognitive move of substituting a proximate and visible human cause for a remote and invisible natural one when the natural cause, though well-understood in science, is too slow and deep to be intuitively graspable.

The epistemic novelty here is worth dwelling on. Volcanic eruptions are among the most powerful natural phenomena observable at human timescales, and they have the surface grammar of sudden events: the lava appears, the ground cracks open, the eruption seems to start on a particular day. This apparent suddenness is deeply misleading. The 2018 eruption's "start" on 3 May 2018 was the culmination of processes that can be traced in instrumental records to late 2017 and conceptually to the island's ongoing hotspot volcanism over hundreds of thousands of years. The volcanic plumbing that produced the eruption is driven by mantle convection — planetary-scale processes entirely outside the reach of human industrial activity at our current technological level. But none of this is visible or intuitively accessible. The lava flowing past homes *is* visible. The geothermal plant *is* visible. The claim that the plant caused the eruption substitutes a visible, graspable, human-scale cause for an invisible, difficult, deep-earth cause.

This is related to what philosophers of science call the *availability heuristic* in causal attribution (Kahneman, 2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Farrar, Straus and Giroux): we preferentially assign causation to factors that are mentally available — recent, vivid, concrete — over factors that are technically accurate but cognitively remote. In the context of catastrophic events, this heuristic is reinforced by a motivated desire for assignable human responsibility: if the disaster was caused by a company's negligence, then there are responsible parties, possible legal remedies, and a preventable future. If the disaster was caused by planetary geophysics, there are none of these things, and a certain kind of helplessness follows. The fracking claim, psychologically, is a form of agency-restoration.

The anti-fracking political context is also genuinely relevant to understanding the claim's spread, and it is worth being precise about this. The documented harms of hydraulic fracturing — groundwater contamination risks, induced seismicity in Oklahoma and other states, methane leakage — are real and are the subject of serious ongoing scientific and regulatory attention (Ellsworth, 2013, "Injection-Induced Earthquakes," *Science*, 341(6142), 1225942; Osborn et al., 2011, "Methane contamination of drinking water accompanying gas-well drilling and hydraulic fracturing," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 108(20), 8172–8176). People who have followed this science are primed to believe that fracking can cause geological disruptions and environmental harm. The Kīlauea claim found an audience that had been educated — accurately — about fracking's genuine risks, and extended that evidentially grounded concern to a context where it did not apply.

This is an important pattern. The mechanisms by which true information about one subject (fracking can induce earthquakes in sedimentary basins) generates false beliefs about another subject (fracking caused a volcanic eruption) are not pathological; they are the normal operation of analogical generalisation and category extension. The person who believes the Kīlauea fracking claim is not making a simple error of fact; they are making a plausible but incorrect generalisation from a domain where the concern is legitimate to a domain where it is not. This is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing as credulity.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE.** Fracking did not cause or contribute to the 2018 Kīlauea eruption. There are no commercial fracking operations in Hawaii; the Puna Geothermal Venture facility uses geothermal extraction, not hydraulic fracturing; and the 2018 eruption was thoroughly explained by volcanological processes — magma pressure accumulation, rift zone dynamics, and south flank fault displacement — documented in extensive USGS monitoring data and peer-reviewed scientific literature.

The claim conflates two distinct types of underground industrial activity (hydraulic fracturing for hydrocarbons vs. geothermal extraction), misunderstands the scale and mechanism of volcanic eruptions relative to industrial operations, and attributes a natural disaster traceable to planetary-scale processes to a facility that in fact suffered damage *from* the eruption rather than causing it.

The genuine concern about industrial activity near active volcanic zones — which is a legitimate engineering and environmental question — is not served by this false claim. It is better served by the existing monitoring frameworks of the Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, USGS, and state geological services, which provide continuous, publicly accessible data on the interaction between geothermal operations and volcanic systems.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Kīlauea fracking claim is not isolated. It belongs to a broader genre of "industrial trigger" claims about natural disasters — assertions that earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, hurricanes, or floods were caused or worsened by specific human industrial activities. This genre has a complicated relationship with established science.

On one side, there are real and documented cases of industrial triggering of geophysical events. Induced seismicity from wastewater disposal associated with hydraulic fracturing is now well-established: the dramatic increase in earthquake frequency in Oklahoma from 2009 onwards — including multiple M5+ events — was causally linked to large-volume wastewater injection into disposal wells (Keranen et al., 2014, "Sharp increase in central Oklahoma seismicity since 2008 induced by massive wastewater injection," *Science*, 345(6195), 448–451). The 1963 Vajont Dam disaster in Italy, in which a reservoir filling destabilised a slope and caused a catastrophic rockslide, is an established case of anthropogenic landscape modification producing a fatal natural event. Industrial activity can, under specific conditions, trigger or amplify geological events.

On the other side, the category of "industrial trigger" claims has been colonised by false instances that invoke the form of these legitimate concerns without the substance. The Kīlauea claim, the false claim that the 2010 Haiti earthquake was caused by US military HAARP technology, and similar claims share a structure: a major natural disaster, a visible industrial or military installation in proximity, and a causal attribution that ignores the well-understood natural mechanisms in favour of a politically resonant human culpability narrative. These false claims damage the legitimate conversation about industrial impacts on geology in two ways: they discredit real cases of industrial triggering by association, and they consume public and regulatory attention that might be directed toward actual industrial hazards.

The distinction the public needs — between real cases of induced seismicity in specific geological contexts and false claims of volcanic triggering by operations that are both categorically different and orders of magnitude too small — is not easily communicated in a media environment where the nuance of geological mechanism is flattened into a binary of "industry is responsible" or "industry is innocent." This is a genuine science communication challenge for volcanologists, seismologists, and environmental scientists.

The Hawaiian situation also raises a genuine question worth asking independently of the fracking claim: what is the appropriate level of geothermal development near active volcanic rift zones, and what monitoring and emergency response protocols should govern it? The 2018 eruption showed that Puna Geothermal Venture's location in the LERZ carries real operational and environmental risks — the plant required complex emergency management to prevent uncontrolled well blowouts as lava advanced. These are legitimate questions about industrial siting, risk management, and the balance between renewable energy development and geological hazard. They do not require the fracking claim to be true; they are important regardless.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Kīlauea fracking claim was addressed by several fact-checking organisations in 2018 during and after the eruption. Reuters Fact Check, AP Fact Check, and KITV Hawaii (a local television station) published direct refutations within days of the claim spreading. All found the claim false on the factual premise that no fracking occurs in Hawaii and on the volcanological facts about the eruption's mechanism.

The USGS Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, which is an authoritative source with unparalleled monitoring access to Kīlauea, published clear public statements in 2018 explaining the volcanic processes driving the eruption and explicitly noting that the eruption was not caused by or associated with geothermal activity at PGV. HVO's Volcano Watch newsletter addressed the question directly; their explanation is publicly archived and remains accessible.

Snopes did not publish a standalone entry on the Kīlauea fracking claim specifically, though related geological conspiracy claims have been addressed by the organisation. The USGS and NOAA have both published educational materials explaining the difference between fracking-induced seismicity (real, documented) and volcano-fracking claims (not supported by evidence or physical plausibility).

What the fact-check responses did not engage with adequately — and what this analysis attempts to address — is the knowledge transfer problem: the person who understands why fracking causes induced seismicity in Oklahoma might reasonably wonder why the same principle doesn't extend to Hawaii, and a fact-check that says "no fracking in Hawaii, claim false" does not explain why the geological contexts are categorically different, why volcanic systems are driven by forces that dwarf industrial operations, or what the actual volcanological record of Kīlauea shows. This analytical gap leaves readers technically informed about the falsity of the specific claim without the conceptual resources to evaluate future iterations of the same argument in different settings.
