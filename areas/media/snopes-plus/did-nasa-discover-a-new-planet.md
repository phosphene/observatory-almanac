# NASA discovered a new planet that could support life


## 1. The Claim

Since the early 2010s, a reliable drumbeat of headlines has announced that NASA—or, in variant forms, "astronomers" or "scientists"—has discovered a new planet that could support life. The claim recurs because new candidate exoplanets are regularly identified, and because the phrase "could support life" provides perpetually renewable dramatic content for science reporting. Each successive announcement draws on the same narrative structure: a new planet has been found, it meets certain criteria that make it plausible as a host for life, and humanity is therefore one step closer to answering the fundamental question of whether we are alone in the universe.

The specific version that Snopes fact-checked in April 2021 concerned Kepler-1649c, an exoplanet found in old data from NASA's retired Kepler Space Telescope during a reanalysis conducted by professional astronomers. The discovery was real and genuinely significant within the field of exoplanet science. Kepler-1649c was identified as 1.06 times the size of Earth — exceptionally close to Earth's dimensions, making it a "near-Earth-size" candidate by the standards the field uses — and it receives approximately 75 percent of the light that Earth receives from the Sun, a figure that places it within the parameters used to define the "habitable zone" around its host star. NASA's own press materials described it as "the most similar to Earth in size and estimated temperature of any planet ever found outside our solar system" at the time of announcement.

The variant claiming that NASA "discovered a planet that could support life" circulates not just around Kepler-1649c but as a recurring formula applied to multiple exoplanet discoveries across more than a decade: Kepler-452b (the "Earth's cousin" of 2015), Proxima Centauri b (2016), the TRAPPIST-1 system planets (2016–17), TOI-715b (2024), and LHS 1140b (2026), among others. Each discovery cycle produces a variant of the same claim, with media outlets competing to express the finding in the most human-interest terms available.

The claim is not a lie. In each case, the space agencies and research institutions whose findings are being reported have indeed identified real planets meeting real criteria that scientists use to discuss habitability. The question is about what those criteria actually established, what the gap is between the headline formulation and the underlying science, and what risks attach to routine overpromising in the communication of exoplanet discoveries. The claim's persistence and the regularity of "True" verdicts for individual instances of it create a situation in which the overall pattern of science communication is arguably misleading even while each individual instance is technically accurate.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Kepler-1649c and the concept of discovery.** The finding announced in April 2021 was the result of a reanalysis of archived data from the Kepler Space Telescope, which operated from 2009 until 2018 when it exhausted its fuel supply. The original automated analysis pipeline used to process Kepler data had flagged Kepler-1649c's signal as a "false positive" — the algorithm had misclassified the periodic dimming of the host star that indicates an orbiting planet as a spurious signal. Human review of the data by Andrew Vanderburg of the University of Texas at Austin and collaborators working with the Kepler False Positive Working Group corrected this misclassification.

The planet orbits a red dwarf star (spectral type M) about 300 light-years from Earth. It has an orbital period of approximately 19.5 Earth days — it completes a full orbit of its host star roughly every three weeks, which, given the smaller and cooler nature of M-dwarf stars, still places it within the habitable zone defined by stellar energy flux calculations. The "1.06 times Earth size" dimension refers to the planet's estimated radius based on transit depth measurements; its mass, density, and composition are not directly measured and are inferred from statistical relationships between radius and mass established from prior exoplanet studies.

**The habitable zone: what it actually means.** The central scientific concept underpinning the "could support life" claim is the habitable zone, sometimes called the Goldilocks zone — the range of orbital distances from a star at which a planet with a suitable atmosphere could maintain liquid water on its surface. The habitable zone is a useful heuristic and a genuinely important parameter in the search for potentially life-hosting planets. It is not, however, a confirmed indicator of habitability in any individual case.

The habitable zone calculation requires only stellar luminosity and orbital distance — information derived from transit observations — to produce an energy flux estimate. It does not incorporate: whether the planet has an atmosphere; what that atmosphere's composition and pressure might be; whether the planet has a magnetic field to protect surface conditions from stellar radiation; whether the planet's surface has geological activity capable of driving the carbon-silicate cycle that regulates Earth's long-term temperature stability; or numerous other factors that habitability assessments at any more granular level require. A planet can be squarely within the habitable zone and have no atmosphere at all — Mars is within (or near the outer edge of) the Sun's habitable zone and has a surface pressure less than one percent of Earth's. Venus is within the habitable zone and has surface temperatures of roughly 465 degrees Celsius due to a runaway greenhouse effect.

The habitable zone framework is therefore a necessary but radically insufficient condition for the kind of habitability that "could support life" suggests to general audiences. A planet within the habitable zone of its star is a planet that scientists regard as worth investigating further, which is very different from a planet that is likely to support life or has been established as capable of supporting life.

**Atmospheres and the next step.** The full scientific assessment of potential habitability requires atmospheric characterisation: determining whether the planet has an atmosphere and what it contains. This is an extremely technically demanding measurement for planets the size of Earth orbiting within the habitable zones of other stars. For the gas giants and Neptune-size planets that constitute most confirmed exoplanet detections, atmospheric spectroscopy is more tractable because their larger size produces stronger transmission signals. For smaller, rocky, Earth-sized planets, atmospheric characterisation requires the collecting power of very large space-based telescopes and many hours of observation time.

The James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), launched in December 2021 and reaching operational status in 2022, represents a step functional advance in this capacity. Several Earth-sized planets in habitable zones — including the TRAPPIST-1 planets, which have been prioritised for JWST observation time — have been placed under intensive atmospheric investigation. Results as of 2025–2026 include the significant announcement of atmospheric detection around LHS 1140b, a rocky planet 48 light-years away that generated headlines about being the "closest we've gotten to finding a planet that could support life" — the same claim structure as before, now with the additional anchoring of direct atmospheric detection.

However, even atmospheric detection does not confirm habitability or the presence of life. Confirming the presence of an atmosphere tells scientists that the surface conditions are not as extreme as a bare-rock airless world, but the specific composition — carbon dioxide dominated versus nitrogen-oxygen-water vapour dominated, for instance — makes an enormous difference to surface temperatures and chemistry. The identification of biosignature molecules (molecular signatures associated with life, such as the simultaneous presence of methane and oxygen in a planetary atmosphere) remains the next major scientific threshold, and that threshold has not been reached as of the current date.

**What scientists actually claim.** A consistent pattern in exoplanet discovery announcements is that the researchers themselves are considerably more cautious than the headlines their work generates. NASA researcher Vanderburg, commenting on Kepler-1649c, described it as "intriguing" and noted that it "gives us even greater hope that a second Earth lies among the stars, waiting to be found." This is an expression of scientific optimism and motivation for continued research, not a claim that Kepler-1649c itself is confirmed as capable of supporting life. The gap between "gives hope" and "could support life" in the headline is substantial.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The exoplanet "could support life" claim travels so easily because it occupies a category of scientific information that activates deep human narrative needs without requiring audiences to accept implausible premises. Unlike claims about alien visitation, cold fusion, or homeopathy, the claim that we have discovered a planet that might support life is consistent with mainstream scientific consensus about the prevalence of planets in the galaxy, the conditions required for life as we know it, and the statistical likelihood that some fraction of the approximately four billion rocky planets estimated to exist in habitable zones in the Milky Way would meet the full set of habitability conditions.

The claim is therefore not obviously irrational to believe. It sits in an epistemologically comfortable space: extraordinary in its implications but warranted by the basic premises of mainstream planetary science. People who accept the scientific consensus on cosmology, planetary formation, and astrobiology have good reasons to believe that somewhere in the galaxy there is a planet supporting life, and "could support life" headlines feel like they are reporting progress toward confirming that belief rather than making an overreach.

The recurring nature of the claim also normalizes it. By the time a reader encounters their fifteenth or twentieth "planet that could support life" headline, they have either become entirely habituated to the formulaic announcement cycle and discounting it entirely, or they have internalized a progressively stronger prior that life-hosting planets are being found regularly. Neither response accurately tracks the scientific reality: these discoveries are genuinely incrementally significant, adding statistical weight to the case that Earth-like conditions are achievable elsewhere, but none of them has established that a specific planet actually hosts or could host life.

Science journalism's structural incentives push toward maximum-impact framing. Exoplanet stories compete with all other science coverage for a limited amount of attention and editorial space; a story headlined "Rocky Planet Found In Potentially Habitable Zone, Atmospheric Characterisation Pending" will be read less often than "Scientists Find Planet That Could Support Life." Editors and headline writers who know this optimize accordingly, and science communications officers at funding agencies like NASA have learned that bolder formulations drive more media coverage, which supports visibility in congressional appropriations debates. The incentive structure of science communication in competitive media environments reliably produces overstatement.

The specific case of Kepler-1649c gained additional traction from its positioning as a coda to the Kepler mission. Kepler had retired in 2018 after an extraordinarily productive operational life — it confirmed over 2,600 exoplanets and contributed statistical estimates suggesting that roughly one in five Sun-like stars hosts an Earth-sized planet in the habitable zone. A major discovery emerging from reanalysis of Kepler's archive data, announced in 2021 after the mission's formal end, fit a narrative of post-retirement productivity and scientific legacy that media outlets found inherently appealing. The story was not just about an exoplanet; it was about scientific thoroughness, archival value, and a famous space telescope continuing to contribute after its operational life ended.


## 4. Verdict

**TRUE with systematic epistemic overreach in the popular framing.** Snopes correctly rates the specific Kepler-1649c claim as True: NASA did discover (more precisely, confirm through reanalysis) an Earth-sized exoplanet in the habitable zone of its host star, meeting the standard scientific criteria for a "potentially habitable" planet candidate. The planet is real, the measurements are real, and the scientific description of it as a potentially habitable candidate is defensible within the conventions the exoplanet science community uses.

The overreach is in the phrase "could support life," which implies a closer proximity to confirmed life-hosting capacity than the science establishes. Being within the habitable zone of an M-dwarf star is a necessary but entirely insufficient condition for life-hosting potential. The planet's atmospheric composition is unknown; its magnetic field status is unknown; its geological activity is unknown; its water inventory is unknown; and its host star's flare activity — M-dwarfs are known to be frequently and intensely flare-active, which can strip atmospheres from close-orbiting planets — is a known risk factor that specific assessments would need to address.

The claim is true as a description of a confirmed scientific finding; it is epistemically overloaded as a claim about life-hosting potential. The recurring nature of very similar claims across more than a decade of exoplanet discovery announcements means that "NASA discovers planet that could support life" has become a routinely recycled formula that individual instances of true discovery cannot individually sustain across the full periodic cycle.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Kepler-1649c announcement and the broader pattern it exemplifies sit at the heart of a wider challenge in communicating contemporary astronomy and astrobiology to public audiences. The scientific infrastructure for exoplanet discovery has developed to a point of remarkable sensitivity — the Kepler, K2, and TESS missions have collectively confirmed thousands of planets, and the James Webb Space Telescope has opened the door to the atmospheric characterisation that will be necessary for any meaningful confirmation of habitability. This is a genuine heroic achievement of human scientific and engineering capability.

But the pace at which discoveries that meet the "habitable zone, Earth-sized" criteria have arrived has created a situation where the framing used to communicate each successive finding must either escalate to maintain audience interest — each planet is "the most Earth-like yet," the "best candidate for life we've found," the "closest we've come" — or it must accept diminishing news value as audiences habituate to what are by now a familiar class of announcements. The escalating framing is what journalism systematically produces; the habituating audience response is what many engaged science followers report experiencing after years of such announcements.

The TRAPPIST-1 system discovered in 2016–17 illustrates this dynamic particularly clearly. The announcement that a system of seven roughly Earth-sized planets — three of them in the habitable zone — had been found orbiting a dim M-dwarf star only about 40 light-years away was perhaps the single most significant exoplanet discovery since the first confirmation of exoplanets in the 1990s. The system is close enough, and has enough candidate planets, that it became an immediate priority target for follow-up observations. JWST has spent substantial observing time on the TRAPPIST-1 planets, and the results have been scientifically valuable but cosmetically disappointing: early JWST measurements found no evidence of thick atmospheres around several of the inner TRAPPIST-1 planets, suggesting they may lack the retained atmospheric envelopes necessary for surface habitability.

These negative findings are themselves scientifically important — they constrain which types of planets around M-dwarfs are likely to have atmospheres, contributing to the field's understanding of atmospheric retention under conditions of intense stellar radiation. But they do not support "could support life" headlines, and they receive substantially less public attention than the original TRAPPIST-1 discovery announcement. The asymmetry between discovery coverage and follow-up constraint coverage is a structural feature of science journalism that systematically builds public expectations and then fails to deflate them.

The LHS 1140b case breaking in 2026 represents the more positive end of the update cycle: an actual atmospheric detection around a rocky habitable-zone planet, which is genuinely the next significant threshold after "planet identified in habitable zone." If LHS 1140b's atmosphere proves to contain water vapour in significant quantities, and if subsequent observations can characterise its atmospheric temperature profile, it would represent real progress toward the kind of evidence that would warrant more confident "could support life" claims. The scientific community's measured excitement about LHS 1140b reflects exactly this: it is the case where the chain of evidence has advanced furthest toward meaning "could support life" in a sense that departs from the "habitability zone placement only" floor that most prior claims rested on.

Astrobiology as a discipline operates under particularly acute versions of the overstatement problem because its central question — whether life exists elsewhere in the universe — is one of the most profound questions humans ask, and any progress toward answering it will necessarily be reported as epochal regardless of whether the specific finding is actually epochal. Individual discoveries that are genuinely significant incremental steps in a multi-decade research program get reported as near-answers to ultimate questions, creating a cycle of modest expert excitement and major popular excitement that reliably generates disappointment when the full picture of what was actually established becomes apparent.

The search for life outside Earth also operates in a political economy with its own pressures. NASA's exoplanet and astrobiology research programs compete for congressional funding against other scientific and engineering priorities; compelling public communication about the proximity of life detection is not just a desirable communication outcome but an institutional survival strategy. The agency's communications about each new finding are therefore not purely scientific assessments but also advocacy for continued programmatic investment, a context that inflects what is emphasized and what is hedged in every announcement from mission control.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's April 2021 fact-check of the Kepler-1649c claim is exemplary in its provision of accurate scientific context while appropriately affirming the core finding's truth. The piece correctly describes what the planet was found; accurately conveys the star type, orbital period, and size estimates; correctly defines the habitable zone concept; and quotes both the primary researchers and NASA communications appropriately. The "True" rating is defensible.

What the Snopes treatment does not fully interrogate is the epistemological gap between "True" and "meaningful." Every iteration of the "NASA discovers planet that could support life" cycle receives its own individual "True" verdict when the core discovery is confirmed, but the cumulative pattern of recurrent claims — each individually true, each individually rated True — creates a misleading overall picture in which the public could reasonably infer that life-hosting planets are being regularly confirmed rather than that the field is making steady incremental progress toward a very distant scientific threshold.

A more comprehensive meta-analysis of the recurring claim would note that: the same headline structure has been applied to dozens of discoveries over fifteen years; the "could support life" formulation has a consistent baseline meaning (habitable zone placement) that is far weaker than popular audiences interpret it to mean; and direct atmospheric characterisation, which is the actual necessary next step, is only now becoming possible for the first time with JWST and will take years to decades to yield results confident enough to meaningfully change the epistemic situation.

Fact-checkers evaluating astronomical habitability claims would benefit from a standard contextualising framework: Is this planet's atmospheric composition known? What does "habitable zone" actually require versus what it guarantees? What would need to be established before a "could support life" claim was scientifically robust? Has this research team made similar strong claims about prior planets that did not pan out? Applying this framework consistently would produce a richer and more epistemically accurate picture than individual "True/False" verdicts for each new announcement in an ongoing series.

For media literacy education, the NASA exoplanet case provides an ideal study in an underappreciated phenomenon: the truth-with-systematic-overreach category, where individual claims are technically correct but the recurring formula in which they are embedded creates cumulative epistemic distortion. Teaching the distinction between "true claim" and "accurately scoped claim" — between the fact that a planet is in a habitable zone and the implication that it hosts or could host life — equips readers to engage with science journalism at a level of interrogation that single-verdict fact-checks do not typically model.
