## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In early February 2018, a website called news24fresh.info published an article claiming that Target had issued a "massive recall" for a line of water-absorbing Easter egg toys, framing the story as breaking or current news. The article stated that Target had been compelled to recall the products after discovering the objects could expand dangerously inside a child's body if ingested, potentially causing life-threatening conditions. The story spread across social media in late February and early March 2018, reaching audiences who understandably treated the recall warning as urgent and actionable.

Snopes investigator Arturo Garcia (2018) assigned this claim the rating **Outdated**. The underlying recall was genuine: the U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) and Target did jointly issue a recall of approximately 560,000 water-absorbing toy sets — specifically Easter Grow Toys, Hatch & Grow Easter Eggs, and Hatch Your Own Dino — in April 2017. However, the news24fresh.info article was published in February 2018, nearly ten months after the original recall, with no disclosure of the original date and in language strongly implying the recall was a new development. The article's framing constituted a textbook case of what researchers call "temporal deception" in viral news: the resurrection of real but stale information stripped of its original context.

This entry examines the specifics of the original recall, the physiology of the hazard posed by superabsorbent polymer toys, the behavior of news aggregation sites that recycle dated safety information, and what the episode reveals about the temporal deception phenomenon as a distinct category of misinformation distinct from ordinary fabrication.

## §2 The Original CPSC Recall: Scope and Hazard

The recall that news24fresh.info transfigured into a 2018 story was formally announced on or around April 13, 2017. CNN's Johanzynn Gatewood (2017) reported on the recall at the time, describing it as a "serious ingestion hazard" affecting approximately 560,000 products across three product lines sold at Target stores.

The three recalled SKUs shared a key ingredient: superabsorbent polymer beads. These materials — chemically based on cross-linked polyacrylate copolymers — are designed to absorb many times their weight in water, swelling dramatically in the process. In toy form, these polymers are typically incorporated into small, firm pellets or compacted shapes that expand when submerged. The toys in question were marketed as novelty Easter products: one line was simply an Easter egg that "grew" when placed in water, another produced an egg from which a chick or character appeared to "hatch," and the third ostensibly allowed dinosaur figures to emerge from eggs in the same manner. All featured superabsorbent polymer cores.

The CPSC hazard designation was specific and well-founded. When superabsorbent polymer objects are dry, they are small enough to be swallowed by infants and toddlers who cannot yet distinguish inedible objects from food. Once swallowed, they are not immediately detectable by standard X-ray imaging — they are radiolucent at their initial compact state — and they begin absorbing intestinal fluids, expanding to many times their original volume. The expansion can cause intestinal obstruction requiring surgical intervention, and deaths have been documented in the medical literature in cases where the obstruction went undetected. Zamora, Jiang, and Bhutiani (2013) in the *Journal of Pediatric Surgery* documented a case series of gastrointestinal obstructions caused by superabsorbent polymer bead toys in children under age four, emphasizing the diagnostic challenge posed by the radiolucency of these materials and the need for clinicians to maintain a high index of suspicion.

The CPSC's recall notice advised consumers to immediately take the recalled toys away from children and return them to Target for a full refund. Target cooperated fully with the recall. The hazard had been well-known within consumer safety circles for years: the CPSC and its international equivalents had issued multiple earlier warnings about superabsorbent toys well before the 2017 Target action. The specific hazard profile of these products had been documented since at least 2012, when a highly publicized case of intestinal obstruction from a "water ball" toy led to increased regulatory scrutiny in the United States and Europe.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Temporal Deception as a Distinct Misinformation Mode

The most significant epistemological dimension of this case is not the recall itself but the mode of recycling: news24fresh.info's presentation of a ten-month-old safety announcement as new breaking news represents a form of misinformation that does not involve fabricating facts, inventing sources, or manipulating images. Every factual claim in their article was verifiably true at the time it occurred. What was added was a false temporal context — the article's present-tense framing and its absence of any date reference created the clear impression of contemporaneity.

This technique, which researchers studying viral information dynamics have begun to identify as "temporal deception" or "date-stripping," occupies an important gap in the standard taxonomy of misinformation types. Lazer et al. (2018) in their landmark *Science* article "The Science of Fake News" established a widely-cited typology distinguishing fabricated content, misleading content, imposter content, and satire, but did not foreground temporally displaced true information as a distinct category. Wardle and Derakhshan (2017, Council of Europe) similarly categorized misinformation by the nature of the distortion in their comprehensive "Information Disorder" report, but emphasized falsehood and manipulation rather than temporal misframing.

Temporal deception is distinct from ordinary fabrication because it is epistemically harder to detect and correct. A false story can be disconfirmed through evidence; a true story given a false temporal context requires the reader to already have been aware of the original story or to independently verify the date of the underlying events. For safety-critical content — recalls, advisories, health warnings — temporal deception is particularly dangerous because it can amplify valid but stale warnings in ways that overwhelm genuine current safety information, and because the emotional urgency associated with child safety content magnifies sharing velocity.

Analysing the broader output of news24fresh.info, Garcia (2018) identified a pattern: a concurrent article on the same website published on the same date as the Easter egg recall piece recycled a July 2017 car crash story (the deaths of Lindsey Schmidt and her three children in Illinois) without disclosing the original date. This suggests news24fresh.info operated as a content farm designed to systematically recycle emotional and safety-adjacent narratives stripped of temporal anchoring — maximizing shareability by exploiting readers' reasonable assumption that recently-posted content describes recent events.

## §4 The Physiology of Superabsorbent Polymer Ingestion Hazards

The recall's underlying hazard — the physiology of superabsorbent polymer ingestion — warrants detailed examination because it remains incompletely understood by most consumers and has generated numerous additional product warnings since the original polyacrylate water-bead toys entered the market in the early 2000s.

Superabsorbent polymers (SAPs) are among the most commercially significant polymer categories, with agricultural, industrial, and consumer applications. The specific family used in swelling toy products — sodium polyacrylate cross-linked with trace amounts of cross-linking agents — can absorb between 100 and 1,000 times their dry weight in water, depending on cross-link density and the ionic strength of the ambient fluid. Human gastrointestinal secretions, particularly alkaline intestinal fluid and gastric mucus, provide conditions highly favorable to this expansion. Clinical toxicologists and pediatric gastroenterologists have emphasized that the expansion timeline is crucial: SAP objects may take twelve to seventy-two hours to reach full swelling, meaning symptoms may not emerge until well after the ingestion event — a well-documented delayed presentation that complicates timely diagnosis.

Mirabel, Sanchez-Orue, and Llorente Pelayo (2020) in the *Archives of Disease in Childhood* documented a case series of pediatric bowel obstructions attributable to superabsorbent polymer beads across European pediatric hospitals between 2015 and 2019, finding that diagnostic delay exceeding 24 hours was associated with significantly increased rates of surgical intervention. The characteristic absence of radiographic opacity — these objects do not appear on plain-film X-rays — means initial emergency room workups employing standard imaging protocols may miss the obstruction entirely in its early stages.

This hazard profile explains why both the CPSC and international equivalents such as the European Chemicals Agency (ECHA) and the UK's Office for Product Safety and Standards have pursued increasingly aggressive regulatory stances regarding toy products incorporating SAP components. The 2017 Target recall was not an isolated event: it was part of a broader category of actions targeting SAP-containing toys across market channels.

The practical implication for consumers encountering recycled safety information of this type is that even though the original recall is now years in the past, the underlying hazard class — SAP-containing novelty toys — remains commercially available in various forms that have not been individually recalled, and the physiological hazard profile is unchanged. The viral reproduction of the outdated recall, while misleading in its temporal framing, may paradoxically have served a residual harm-reduction function by reminding consumers of a hazard class they might otherwise have failed to recognize.

## §5 News24fresh.info and the Content Farm Ecosystem

The site news24fresh.info was characteristic of a category of low-production-cost websites that emerged prominently in the mid-2010s and relied on the recycling of emotionally resonant legacy content rather than original reporting. These sites typically achieve low or zero editorial cost by aggregating, reformatting, and reposting news items from legitimate sources with dates stripped or obfuscated, generating advertising revenue from the high traffic produced by social media amplification.

The business model exploiting temporal deception is self-reinforcing: emotionally compelling content about child safety, tragedy, or consumer harm carries high intrinsic shareability regardless of age. Content farm operators have found that stories involving the death or endangerment of children circulate with particular vigor on Facebook and similar platforms, where emotional resonance rather than epistemic warrant drives sharing behavior. Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018) in their comprehensive analysis of Twitter diffusion published in *Science* established that false news propagates significantly faster and farther than true news across that platform, driven primarily by the novelty and emotional engagement of false or misleading content — findings that apply with additional force to emotionally loaded but temporally misframed true content.

The specific harm at the intersection of content farm behavior and safety information is that legitimate safety recalls occur at specific moments with defined windows of consumer action. When an outdated recall is recycled as contemporaneous news, it can consume attention that might otherwise be directed at genuinely current recall notices, and it can produce recall-fatigue effects in consumers who encounter the same safety claim multiple times across months or years and cease treating it as actionable.

## §6 Conclusion: Verdict Affirmation and Epistemic Lessons

The Snopes verdict of Outdated is precisely calibrated to the specific epistemic failure in this case: the underlying consumer product safety hazard was real, the original CPSC recall was genuine, and the 560,000 products involved posed a documented physiological risk to young children. But the news24fresh.info article's presentation of this ten-month-old action as breaking news constituted a meaningful distortion — not through fabrication but through temporal deception — that caused real harm to the information environment.

The epistemological lesson this case offers is structural rather than narrowly factual: standard media literacy heuristics, which direct readers to ask "is this from a credible source?" and "are the facts verifiable?", are insufficient to catch temporal deception, because the source matters (the underlying event was reported by CNN and issued by the CPSC) and the facts are verifiable. Readers must also ask "when did this actually occur?" — a temporal verification step that is underemphasized in most media literacy curricula focused on fabricated misinformation.

The consumer safety community has long recognized that effective product recall communication requires both accurate content and timely reach. The news24fresh.info episode illustrates that viral amplification of safety information, even when accurate, does not reliably compensate for failed initial reach if the amplification occurs with false temporal framing. As CPSC and other regulatory bodies continue to develop digital communication strategies, integrating date-verification mechanisms — including persistent, prominently dated recall databases and clear timestamp standards for web content — represents a structural response to temporal deception that complements individual media literacy approaches.
