# Old-timey cough syrup really contained morphine and cannabis


## 1. The Claim

One of the internet's more popular categories of historical trivia involves the earnest-to-incredulous revelation that pharmaceutical products widely available in nineteenth and early twentieth century America and Britain contained substances that are today controlled, scheduled, or banned. The specific form involving cough medicines circulates as a combination of vintage advertisement images — often photographed from actual period packaging or catalogues — paired with captions expressing amused disbelief that such products were sold openly, marketed to children, and presumably authorised by respectable medical opinion of the day.

The typical visual format shows an authentic-looking Victorian or Edwardian advertisement for a cough mixture, children's teething preparation, or "soothing syrup." The product claims to relieve coughs, colds, bronchitis, asthma, "consumption" (tuberculosis), or infant irritability. Somewhere in the ingredients list, printed in period typeface, one finds: laudanum (tincture of opium), morphine sulphate, cannabis indica, heroin hydrochloride, chloroform, cocaine, or some combination thereof. The accompanying commentary — "they really said 'heroin for kids!'" or "no wonder everyone was 'soothed'" — frames the discovery as both funny and representative of a more freewheeling pharmaceutical past.

The underlying claim is: yes, this was real. Patent medicines and over-the-counter preparations in this period routinely and legally contained what are now Schedule I and Schedule II controlled substances, administered to adults and children alike, purchased without prescription, and advertised with claims that would constitute serious regulatory violations under modern standards. The medicines were not fringe products; they were mainstream commercial commodities, sold in pharmacies, advertised in newspapers and magazines, and recommended by some physicians.

The claim has also been extended in more substantive form in drug policy debate: the historical ubiquity of opium-containing medicines — and the fact that many people who consumed them did not become addicted by the standards of the time — is sometimes invoked as evidence against prohibition regimes, or as context for understanding modern opioid policy. This broader deployment makes the factual accuracy of the historical claim more consequential than its meme-currency alone would suggest.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The core claim is historically accurate.** Patent medicines in the United States, Britain, and other Western countries from approximately the 1840s through the first decade of the twentieth century did routinely contain opium, morphine, cannabis, cocaine, heroin, alcohol, and other pharmacologically potent substances. The evidence for this is extensive, well-documented by historians, and preserved in the primary source record of period advertisements, pharmacy catalogues, recipe books, and eventually in the early investigative journalism and regulatory proceedings that led to the first serious oversight legislation.

**The specific products and their contents.** Several products have become historically prominent examples:

*Mrs. Winslow's Soothing Syrup* was among the most widely sold children's preparations of the Victorian era, marketed specifically for teething and infantile irritability. It contained morphine sulphate (approximately 65 mg per fluid ounce in typical formulations), alcohol, and sodium carbonate. Hoffert SR (2003, *Private Matters: American Attitudes Toward Childrearing and Infant Nurture in the Urban North, 1800–1900*, University of Illinois Press, Urbana) documents the product's marketing and use in a broader study of nineteenth-century American child-rearing norms. The American Medical Association investigated the product in the early 1900s as part of its Nostrums and Quackery campaign; their analysis confirmed the morphine content and its potential for infant harm.

*Bayer Heroin* is among the most frequently cited examples, and its history is entirely factual. The Bayer pharmaceutical company — then Friedrich Bayer & Co. of Elberfeld, Germany — introduced diacetylmorphine under the trade name "Heroin" in 1898, initially as a supposedly non-addictive morphine substitute and cough suppressant. Bayer's internal development of heroin was documented by chemist Heinrich Dreser, whose pharmacological testing suggested that heroin could suppress cough without the dependence liabilities of morphine — a conclusion that proved dramatically incorrect. Musto DF (1999, *The American Disease: Origins of Narcotic Control*, third edition, Oxford University Press, Oxford) provides the canonical scholarly account. Bayer's marketing included both adult cough preparations and recommendations for general respiratory conditions. Heroin was sold over the counter in the United States until the Harrison Narcotics Tax Act of 1914 restricted its sale.

*Cocaine-containing preparations* were widespread. The Sears, Roebuck & Co. catalogue of 1897 offered a "Sears' Soothing Syrup" containing cannabis and hydrochloride of morphine. Various "coca wines" combining cocaine-containing coca leaves with wine alcohol — of which Vin Mariani was the most celebrated, endorsed by Pope Leo XIII and numerous celebrities — were fashionable beverages. Freud's famous advocacy of cocaine as a treatment for morphine dependence and depression in *Über Coca* (Freud S, 1884, *Über Coca*, Verlag von Moritz Perles, Vienna; translated and discussed in Byck R, ed., 1974, *Cocaine Papers*, Stonehill Publishing, New York) contributed to its medical legitimacy, though Freud's subsequent experience with the product's actual dependence liability eventually revised his view.

*Cannabis-containing preparations* were listed in the United States Pharmacopeia from 1851 through 1942 (Zuardi AW, 2006, "History of Cannabis as a Medicine: A Review," *Revista Brasileira de Psiquiatria*, 28(2), 153–157). Cannabis indica (the dried flowering tops of *Cannabis sativa*) was used in preparations for neuralgia, rheumatism, tetanus, cholera, convulsions, and — relevant to the cough syrup context — asthma and bronchitis. The Irish physician William Brooke O'Shaughnessy's treatise introducing cannabis into Western medicine following his work in India (O'Shaughnessy WB, 1843, "On the Preparations of the Indian Hemp, or Gunjah," *Transactions of the Medical and Physical Society of Bombay*, 8, 421–461) became foundational for subsequent Victorian-era prescribing. Cannabis tinctures by companies including Parke-Davis, Squibb, and Eli Lilly were commercially available through the early twentieth century.

*Chloroform and other dangerous agents.* Beyond the plants-derived narcotics and stimulants, many patent medicines contained chloroform (a general anaesthetic and organ toxin at the concentrations used), strychnine (a stimulant at low doses, a lethal poison at higher ones), arsenic compounds, mercury, and alcohol concentrations exceeding those of commercial spirits. The composition varied enormously by product and era, and patent medicine manufacturers were under no obligation to disclose their formulas — indeed, the "patent" in "patent medicine" often referred not to a pharmaceutical patent but to the designation "letters patent" indicating royal licence to sell, which predated any disclosure requirements.

**The regulatory void and the Pure Food and Drug Act.** The absence of regulatory oversight that allowed these products to flourish was not an oversight; it was the policy default in liberal markets of the period. The first major American federal intervention was the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906, passed after an extended campaign by reformers including Harvey Washington Wiley, chief chemist of the US Department of Agriculture, and journalists associated with the muckraking movement — including Samuel Hopkins Adams, whose 1905–1906 series "The Great American Fraud" in *Collier's Weekly* documented the contents and marketing claims of specific patent medicines in damning detail (Adams SH, 1905–1906, "The Great American Fraud," *Collier's Weekly*, serialised 7 October 1905 through September 1906). The 1906 Act required that products containing alcohol, opiates, cocaine, or cannabis be labelled as such, but it did not ban these substances from sale. It was the Harrison Narcotics Tax Act of 1914 that imposed federal registration and taxation on opiates and cocaine, effectively restricting sales to prescription settings.


## 3. Why People Share This

**The meme functions as historical amnesia therapy.** The "old-timey drug cough syrup" format achieves wide circulation primarily because it performs a single clean rhetorical move: it makes the present seem less extreme by revealing that the past was also extreme, in ways that have been forgotten. The contemporary audience receives these images with a sense of having been shown something transgressive and hidden — a secret history that contradicts the official story of drug prohibition as a response to substances inherently dangerous and previously unknown. The meme implies: people were doing this before and it was fine; maybe our current legal apparatus is excessive.

This rhetorical function means the meme often omits or minimises the significant accompanying historical context that would complicate the nostalgic reading. Patent medicines were not a charming Victorian eccentricity; they were the product of a regulatory vacuum that caused considerable harm — most evidently in widespread iatrogenic opioid addiction among middle-class women, who were the primary marketing target for morphine-containing "soothing" and "tonic" preparations.

**The epistemic novelty claim: what advertising and regulation concealed from each other.** The genuinely underappreciated dimension of this history is not the mere fact that opioids were in cough medicines — that is now moderately well-known as a piece of internet-era historical trivia — but rather the sophisticated marketing machinery that operated in the absence of disclosure requirements, and the character of dependence that resulted.

Manufacturers of morphine-containing products explicitly marketed them for conditions — nervous tension, female complaints, pain, restlessness — where a calming, pleasurable pharmacological effect would produce satisfied customers and ensure repeat purchases, without naming the mechanism. The opioid effect was the product's selling point, but it was sold under symptom relief language. When customers returned because they felt worse without the product, the manufacturer interpreted this as evidence of efficacy rather than dependence. This is not charming naivety; it is a recognisable commercial logic that reappears in the marketing of OxyContin by Purdue Pharma in the 1990s and 2000s, down to specific phrases about addiction risk that were systematically understated.

Courtwright DT (2001, *Dark Paradise: A History of Opiate Addiction in America*, expanded edition, Harvard University Press, Cambridge) documents that the population of opioid-dependent Americans in the late nineteenth century was characterised by its demographic composition: predominantly white, middle-class, and female, with dependence typically acquired through medical or self-medication pathways rather than recreational drug use. The patient was a regular customer for morphine-containing "women's tonics" and "soothing compounds," not a stereotypical opium den habitué. Courtwright estimates the opiate-addicted population at somewhere between 250,000 and 1 million by the 1890s — a prevalence rate not dramatically different from some estimates for the early twenty-first century opioid crisis.

This historical continuity is the most important piece of context missing from the meme: the "old-timey cough syrup with morphine" was not an aberration later corrected by wise regulation and replaced by better products; it was the same phenomenon of commercially motivated underselling of addiction risk that recurred a century later, with the same demographic dynamics of a professional-class population initially encountering opioids through respectable medical channels.

**Why the claim needs context, not just fact-checking.** The straightforward factual content of the meme — yes, these products contained opioids — is accurate. The implicit framing — that this was amusing, mildly transgressive, and retrospectively vindicates a more permissive view of drug regulation — deserves substantial complication. The regulatory history matters: the Harrison Act and subsequent Controlled Substances Act did not emerge from moral panic alone; they emerged from a documented public health problem with an identified commercial cause. The question of whether schedule-based prohibition is the optimal policy response is a legitimate one in drug policy scholarship, but it is not answered by pointing at Victorian patent medicine advertisements.


## 4. Verdict

**True but contextualised.** The claim that old-fashioned cough syrups and patent medicines contained morphine, cannabis, heroin, cocaine, and other now-controlled substances is historically accurate. This is documented in pharmaceutical histories, preserved in primary source advertisements and catalogue records, confirmed by chemical analyses conducted by early twentieth-century reform investigations, and embedded in the regulatory history that produced the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906 and the Harrison Narcotics Tax Act of 1914.

The claim is true. The framing in which it typically circulates — nostalgic, amused, implicitly suggesting a more innocent and permissive medical past — is incomplete and somewhat misleading. These products caused widespread opioid dependence, were aggressively marketed to parents for use on children, and were priced and formulated to ensure repeat purchase through pharmacological dependence. The regulation that ended their sale was a public health response to a real crisis, not an arbitrary prohibition.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The history of patent medicines in America and Britain provides one of the most instructive case studies in the history of pharmaceutical regulation, and it is under-discussed outside specialist history of medicine circles.

**The regulatory evolution.** The arc from unregulated patent medicines to modern pharmaceutical oversight was not a simple linear progression but a series of contested political interventions. The 1906 Pure Food and Drug Act was itself a compromise: it mandated ingredient disclosure but not restriction, and it did not give the government authority to pre-approve products for safety or efficacy. The Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 — passed in the wake of the sulfanilamide disaster, in which approximately 107 people were killed by a drug dissolved in diethylene glycol (a solvent also used in antifreeze) — significantly expanded FDA authority and introduced the new drug application process. The 1962 Kefauver-Harris Amendment, passed following the thalidomide crisis, added the requirement to prove efficacy as well as safety. The regulatory framework that today governs pharmaceutical products was built incrementally in response to successive crises caused by regulatory gaps.

**The cannabis-in-medicine dimension.** Cannabis's removal from the United States Pharmacopeia in 1942 and its Schedule I classification under the Controlled Substances Act of 1970 occurred against the background of the pharmaceutical reforms that followed the patent medicine era, but cannabis's scheduling was driven by different political dynamics than opioid restriction — including the Marihuana Tax Act of 1937, whose passage was influenced by a range of factors including concerns about Mexican immigrant communities and the interests of the cotton and paper industries, as well as documented recreational use. The contrast between cannabis's century of legal medical use and its subsequent prohibition has become a standard element of arguments for contemporary medical cannabis legalisation, and the patent medicine history provides legitimate historical grounding for this argument, though it does not in itself resolve questions about appropriate regulatory frameworks for any specific substance.

**Historical parallels to the contemporary opioid crisis.** The parallels between the late nineteenth-century patent medicine opioid epidemic and the late twentieth and early twenty-first century prescription opioid epidemic are striking enough to constitute a genuine historical lesson. In both cases: pharmaceutical manufacturers marketed opioid products to professional-class consumers through medical channels; addiction risk was systematically understated in marketing materials; regulatory frameworks were inadequate to identify the problem before widespread dependence had developed; and when regulatory responses came, they were politically contested by industry interests. The 1906 and 1914 regulatory responses followed the pattern of the 2017 federal opioid legislation and the 1978 FDA black-box warning extensions that characterise modern period responses.

Quinones S (2015, *Dreamland: The True Tale of America's Opiate Epidemic*, Bloomsbury Press, New York) and Keefe PR (2021, *Empire of Pain: The Secret History of the Sackler Dynasty*, Doubleday, New York) document the contemporary crisis with journalistic depth. Courtwright DT's *Dark Paradise* provides the historical frame that places both episodes in continuity. The historical parallel is not merely rhetorical; it suggests that the structural conditions enabling both crises — commercially motivated minimisation of dependence risk, capture of regulatory processes by regulated interests, physician reliance on manufacturer-provided safety data — are recurrent patterns that new regulatory interventions have repeatedly failed to permanently close.

**The global dimension.** British patent medicines followed a similar trajectory. The British Pharmacopoeia and related documents included cannabis, opium, and their derivatives in legitimate therapeutic listings throughout the nineteenth century, and patent medicines including Godfrey's Cordial (containing opium) were sold in British working-class communities specifically as children's quieteners — a practice documented in the reformist literature of the 1860s through 1880s and eventually addressed in the Pharmacy Act of 1868, which was Britain's first move to restrict sales of certain substances. The broader global dimension includes the opium trade between Britain and China — the context in which British authorities used military force to maintain the trade against the Chinese government's attempts to restrict it — which provides essential context for understanding why opium regulation was politically contentious in ways that extended far beyond domestic pharmaceutical markets.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Major US fact-checking organisations have not published formal fact-checks of the "old-timey cough syrup with morphine" claim in the standard Snopes/PolitiFact format, which reflects the claim's unusual epistemic status: it is true, and is not typically the subject of active denial. It circulates primarily as interesting historical information rather than as contested political claim, and fact-checking infrastructure is optimised to correct false claims rather than to contextualise true ones.

The closest adjacent coverage appears in: (1) history of medicine writing that contextualises patent medicines within the broader regulatory history (the Smithsonian Institution, the FDA's own historical exhibits, and academic medical histories have all covered this territory); (2) drug policy journalism that uses the patent medicine history as prologue to contemporary opioid policy discussions; and (3) occasional corrections of specific factual errors in viral versions of the claim — for example, verifying which specific products existed and whether a specific advertisement image is authentic rather than a modern parody.

The fact-checker's role with a claim like this is less about correcting factual error than about providing contextual enrichment that allows the audience to understand the significance of the true claim. The viral format — "look at these hilarious Victorian drug ads" — accurately represents the existence of the products while obscuring the harm context. A responsible media literacy approach to this material would preserve the genuine historical interest while adding: what the social conditions enabling these products were; what harms followed from the regulatory vacuum; how the regulatory response developed; and what historical continuities connect the patent medicine era to contemporary pharmaceutical policy debates.

The most practically useful framing for general audiences is that the patent medicine history is neither purely funny nor purely horrible — it is a detailed case study in what happens when commercially motivated actors operate pharmaceutical markets in the absence of safety, efficacy, and labelling requirements, and it provides a historically grounded foundation for understanding why those requirements exist. Treating it only as amusing trivia about the past risks losing the historical lesson that it most clearly contains.
