## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In December 2017, *The Atlantic* published a lengthy profile of Vice President Mike Pence by journalist McKay Coppins. Embedded within the profile was a brief anecdote, attributed to "a longtime adviser," alleging that when reports emerged in January 2017 that the Pence family would be moving their family pets—including two cats, a rabbit, and a snake—into the Vice President's official residence at the Naval Observatory in Washington, D.C., President Donald Trump reacted with mockery. According to the unnamed adviser: "He was embarrassed by it; he thought it was so low class. He thinks the Pences are yokels."

The claim spread rapidly beyond *The Atlantic* piece, generating significant public outrage among pet owners and animal lovers, and sparking media coverage focused on Trump's reaction to the alleged sentiment rather than on its verifiability. Multiple outlets covered the story from the angle of pet owner outrage at a president who owned no pets.

Snopes investigator Arturo Garcia (2018) assigned this claim the rating **Unproven**. The claim rests entirely on anonymous testimonial evidence—a single unnamed source described as a "longtime adviser." The White House did not comment to Snopes. Pence's press secretary, Alyssa Farah, denied a separate but contextually related allegation from the same *Atlantic* article (concerning Pence's alleged contemplation of replacing Trump on the 2016 ticket), calling the claim "tired" and "false." The pets remark, however, was not directly addressed in any official response obtained by Snopes or reported elsewhere. The claim thus sits in the epistemically ambiguous space of anonymous-source political journalism: possibly accurate, but not independently verifiable.

## §2 The Atlantic Profile and the Original Reporting Context

*The Atlantic* profile of Mike Pence, published on December 5, 2017, was a substantial piece of political journalism examining Pence's religious convictions, political history, and relationship with Donald Trump. Journalist McKay Coppins had significant access to Pence allies and advisers, and the piece drew on multiple named and unnamed sources over an extended reporting period.

The pets anecdote appears in a passage about the cultural distance between Trump and Pence. The full context in the *Atlantic* piece was: when it was reported in January 2017 that the Pences would be moving their pets into the Naval Observatory—a fully routine domestic transition for any incoming Vice President—Trump was alleged to have ridiculed the menagerie to his secretary. The animals in question included Pence's rabbit, Marlon Bundo (who had his own Instagram account with a substantial following), the family's two cats, a second rabbit (or the same rabbit depending on reporting precision), and a snake. One of the cats, named Pickle, died in December 2017.

The attribution structure—"according to a longtime adviser"—is standard long-form political journalism practice. *The Atlantic* as an institution has strong editorial standards and a track record for political reporting; the use of anonymous sources for unflattering anecdotes about political figures is consistent with the reporting norms of major outlets covering the Trump administration, where fear of retaliation created substantial incentives for sources to remain unidentified (Graves & Konieczna, 2015, *Journalism Practice*).

It is worth noting that *The Atlantic* piece contained another, separately attributed anonymous-source allegation that was denied by Pence's office: that Pence had sent Trump a letter during the Access Hollywood tape crisis saying he needed to decide whether to remain his running mate, and had discussed with Republican National Committee leadership the possibility of replacing Trump as the nominee. Farah's denial of that claim—described by her in tweets as "tired" and "false"—is the only on-record White House or Pence office response to the *Atlantic* piece that Snopes could document. The pets remark received no equivalent official response.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Anonymous Sources, Presidential Character Claims, and the Limits of Political Fact-Checking

The "Unproven" rating on this claim touches a fundamental tension in political fact-checking as a discipline: the relationship between standard journalistic sourcing practices and the higher evidentiary bars appropriate for fact-checking verdicts. This tension is particularly sharp for claims about private statements—things said by a political figure to staff or advisers in a context where no public record exists.

Political journalism, particularly at the center of executive branch coverage, routinely relies on anonymous sources to document private statements and meetings. The normative justification is that without anonymity protections, sources who lack the political protection of named disclosure would be silenced, producing a worse-informed public. The landmark discussions of this norm—from the Society of Professional Journalists' Code of Ethics to internal guidebooks at major newspapers—treat anonymous sourcing as a necessary evil constrained by verification requirements: the journalist must independently confirm the claim from multiple sources or must judge the single source's credibility as exceptionally high.

Fact-checkers, by contrast, operate under a different epistemic framework. The fact-checking enterprise, as described by Graves (2016, *Deciding What's True*, Columbia University Press), applies something closer to a legal-evidence standard: claims require verifiable, documentable support, and the absence of that support produces a verdict of uncertainty rather than credibility-weighted assessment. This creates a structural incompatibility: claims that meet journalistic publication standards (a credible single source with plausible access and motivation) will routinely fall short of fact-checking verification standards.

The epistemic novelty in this instance is the way the claim's content—an allegation about a private, domestic remark with no policy consequence—interacts with the verification impossibility. There is no mechanism by which the pets remark could be independently documented short of: (a) a second source confirming the conversation, (b) a documentary record (email, transcript, diary entry), or (c) an admission by Trump. None of these is available. The absence of White House denial is notable (the Access Hollywood-related allegation was denied; the pets remark, apparently, was not), but the absence of denial is not confirmation—it may reflect a press strategy of declining to amplify minor stories.

From an inferential perspective, it is worth asking what prior probability one should assign to the claim. Trump, as a public figure, was well-documented as frequently critical of behavior he perceived as declassé or inappropriate—this is part of the documented rhetorical pattern of his public statements and reported private communications. However, this general prior does not resolve the specific claim about pets; it merely affects one's background credibility estimate for the plausibility of the anecdote. Bayesian analysis of anonymous-source political claims requires careful separation of the prior probability of the general behavioral pattern and the likelihood of the specific alleged communication (Bovens & Hartmann, 2003, *Bayesian Epistemology*, Oxford University Press).

The deeper epistemic novelty: privacy in political executive contexts means that a large class of potentially historically significant claims about how presidents behave privately will never be verifiable through contemporaneous journalism. The fact-checking enterprise, which requires documentable evidence, will systematically rate a large subset of potentially accurate political reportage as "Unproven" because its sourcing structure is anonymous by design. This is epistemically honest but creates an information landscape in which accurate Anonymous-source claims and fabricated ones receive identical formal ratings, which may mislead audiences about the comparative uncertainty of each.

## §4 Pet Ownership, Public Figures, and the Political Semiotics of Animal Companions

The story's extraordinary viral traction—given its modest concrete significance—requires explanation. The claim was not about a policy position, a legal matter, or a significant political event. It was about an alleged private remark about pets. Yet it generated tens of thousands of social media reactions and coverage across dozens of outlets.

The explanation lies in the semiotics of pet ownership in American public culture. Companion animal ownership has, for decades, carried strong positive valence in American political life. Multiple presidential families have made deliberate use of pets as humanizing publicity elements—from FDR's Fala to the Clintons' Socks to Obama's Bo. The First Dog as political asset is a well-recognized institution (Milburn, 2016, *Society & Animals*). Polling data consistently finds pet owners (approximately 67% of American households by 2017, according to the American Pet Products Association) as a culturally significant constituency, and politicians are generally aware that pet-unfriendliness is a communications liability.

Trump, unusually among modern presidents, kept no pets in the White House during his term. This absence was noted by commentators and factored into public perception. The allegation that he privately mocked a subordinate's pets as "low class" landed in an environment where: (1) the general characterization was plausible to audiences predisposed to believe it, (2) pet-owner identification created a personal emotional hook, and (3) the specific word "yokels" carried class-culture connotations that resonated with ongoing discourse about Trump's relationship with rural and working-class constituencies.

Mayer's (2017, *The New Yorker*) reporting on Trump's relationship with Pence—which included the separate allegation that Trump mockingly said of Pence "he wants to hang them all" regarding gay rights—was part of a broader pattern of coverage depicting Trump as privately condescending toward Pence despite their public partnership. The pets anecdote fit neatly into this established narrative, which both increased its believability (narrative coherence is a well-documented credibility factor) and its media salience (it added a new, animal-welfare dimension to an ongoing story).

Mounk (2018, *The People vs. Democracy*, Harvard University Press) observed that the Trump-era political media environment was characterized by high-volume, rapid verification cycles in which the demand for new material about the administration systematically exceeded the supply of verified material, creating pressure toward early publication of claims that would in ordinary news cycles have required more extensive confirmation. This structural feature of the media environment helps explain why an anonymous single-source anecdote about pets could achieve the same publication and amplification as a documented policy claim.

## §5 Pence's Menagerie and the Political Context of the Naval Observatory

The Pence family's unusual collection of animal companions was itself a small story in early 2017, driven by genuine public interest in the family's move to the Naval Observatory. Marlon Bundo—the rabbit—became a genuine cultural phenomenon, with his Instagram account accumulating followers and spawning eventual book deals. A parody book about Marlon Bundo, written by John Oliver's team for HBO, was published in March 2018 specifically to counterprogram a children's book by Pence's daughter Charlotte; the parody became a bestseller and donated proceeds to LGBTQ-related charities.

The political valence of the pets story was therefore not neutral even before the Trump "low class" allegation surfaced. Pence's menagerie was already a symbol for his family-values brand, particularly in contrast to Trump's pet-free household. In this context, the allegation that Trump privately sneered at the pets as "yokels" was not just a personal anecdote but a politically readable signal about the Trump-Pence relationship—one that fit into a narrative about the class tensions and underlying contempt within the administration.

The "yokels" characterization is particularly loaded. The term historically denotes rural unsophistication and is generally considered condescending; its apparent origin in the dialect of rural Indiana (where Pence served as Governor before his selection as Trump's running mate) makes it interpretable as a specific slur against Pence's Midwestern origin. Whether or not Trump used this specific word, its inclusion in the *Atlantic* report gave the story an additional dimension beyond merely pet hostility.

It is also worth noting that Pence's press secretary denied a different portion of the *Atlantic* article (the 2016 campaign replacement allegation) rather than the pets anecdote. This selective denial strategy is itself epistemically interesting: it suggests either that the Pence office considered the pets anecdote too minor to warrant official response, that denying it risked drawing more attention to it, or that its accuracy presented a different calculus than the campaign allegation (which had specific, verifiable documentary implications about the 2016 election). None of these inferences is probative, but they form part of the contextual evidence that fact-checkers must weigh in assembling an uncertainty assessment.

## §6 Contemporary Relevance and Knowledge Gaps

The EARC gap assigned here is **E (Evidence)**. As with the Spirit Airlines hamster case (spirit-airlines-hamster.md), this case involves a claim that cannot be resolved from available evidence and may never be resolvable given the private conversational context in which the alleged remark occurred. The original *Atlantic* source—whoever they are—has not come forward publicly, and no second source corroborating the specific pets remark has been reported.

This class of claims—privately made remarks by political figures, attributed to anonymous sources with presumed but not documented access—forms a substantial fraction of politically contentious fact-checking requests. The "Unproven" rating neither refutes the claim nor confirms it, a result that is epistemically honest but practically frustrating for audiences who want a more definitive answer.

The broader significance lies in what the case reveals about the structural limits of fact-checking political journalism. A fact-checker cannot distinguish, from external evidence alone, between an accurate anonymous-source anecdote reported responsibly by a credible journalist and a fabricated anecdote that a journalist reported on insufficient evidence. Both produce the same evidence landscape: a credentialed journalist has published a claim from an unnamed source; the subject denies it or does not address it; no independent documentation exists.

Knowledge gaps: What verification did *The Atlantic* apply to this specific anecdote before publication? Was the "longtime adviser" description consistent with access to the described private remark (i.e., was the source in a position to have witnessed or received a credible report of Trump's remark to his secretary)? Did any other journalists with White House sourcing hear the same account and decline to publish it, or did *The Atlantic* obtain it through its Pence-focused reporting?

For consumers of political anonymous-source reporting: the "Unproven" designation on this type of claim should not be read as "probably false" or as "probably true but unconfirmable"—it is a genuine expression of epistemic uncertainty. The prior probability assigned to such a claim should be calibrated against: the credibility of the sourcing publication; the internal narrative coherence of the claim with documented public behavior; the selectivity of official denials; and the existence or absence of second-source corroboration. On this claim, reasonable priors will vary significantly across ideological and contextual starting points, which is itself part of why the claim proved so viral—its perceived probability of truth was inversely correlated with one's opinion of Trump and directly correlated with one's predisposition to believe anonymous-source coverage of the administration.
