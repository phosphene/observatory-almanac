## §1 Claim & Verdict Summary

The viral claim examined in this entry originates from a January 2024 post on X (formerly Twitter) by user @Ragland1836, asserting that the Transportation Security Administration (TSA) "can see your c*ck and b*lls whenever you walk through the X-ray scanner." The tweet accumulated hundreds of thousands of views before the user hid it; an archived version remains accessible. Snopes, in an article by Madison Dapcevich (2024), rated this claim **Outdated**.

The rating is consequential in its precision. The claim is not false as a historical description — TSA backscatter X-ray units deployed at U.S. airports between 2010 and 2013 did produce images with sufficient anatomical detail that agency documentation described the output as resembling "a chalk etching" of the human body, and critics characterized the devices as "digital strip searches" (EPIC, Electronic Privacy Information Center, archived report). However, the devices were fully removed from all U.S. airports by 2013, replaced by millimeter-wave Advanced Imaging Technology (AIT) units that produce a generic stick-figure "avatar" display rather than an anatomically detailed body image. The present-tense framing of the 2024 claim is therefore inaccurate: no TSA scanner currently in use reveals anatomical detail of the kind described.

The word "Outdated" encodes a specific epistemological position: that a claim can be historically accurate yet misleading when stripped of its temporal context and presented as current. This rating category is one of the more epistemically sophisticated in the Snopes vocabulary, reflecting the fact-checker's recognition that misinformation often consists not of outright fabrication but of decontextualized truth.

## §2 Evidence Inventory

**2.1 The backscatter technology deployment (2010)**

TSA began rolling out backscatter X-ray Advanced Imaging Technology units to U.S. airports in 2010 in a major expansion of the agency's checkpoint technology program (TSA, Transportation Security Timeline, undated). Backscatter technology operates by projecting very low-energy X-ray beams at the surface of the body; the beams scatter back to detectors positioned around the scanning booth, and the reflected radiation is processed to produce a two-dimensional surface image. The TSA's own 2010 documentation described the output as producing "an image that resembles a chalk etching" — a euphemism that acknowledged the body-surface detail of the image while minimizing its prurient implications.

The Environmental Protection Agency characterized the radiation exposure from a single backscatter scan as equivalent to approximately two minutes of background cosmic radiation exposure during flight — a very low dose (EPA, Radiation and Airport Security Scanning, 2017). This framing was intended to address health concerns but sidestepped the privacy concern, which was the more prominent driver of public opposition.

By the end of 2010, hundreds of backscatter units had been installed at major U.S. airports, supplementing and in some cases replacing traditional metal detectors. TSA materials distributed at the time indicated that scanned images were viewed by officers in a remote room to maintain separation between the screener and the passenger, and that images were not saved or stored — they were deleted after review and resolution of any anomaly (TSA guidance documents, archived 2010).

**2.2 Privacy controversy and EPIC litigation**

The Electronic Privacy Information Center (EPIC) characterized the backscatter units as enabling "digital strip searches" and filed a lawsuit against the Department of Homeland Security arguing that the AIT deployment violated the Administrative Procedure Act, the Privacy Act, the Religious Freedom Restoration Act, and the Fourth Amendment. EPIC obtained and published sample backscatter images demonstrating the level of anatomical detail captured, which provided the evidentiary basis for widespread public and Congressional alarm.

Multiple civil liberties organizations supported the position that the images constituted an unreasonable invasion of bodily privacy, particularly for travelers who had no practical alternative to scanning — refusal triggered a mandatory opt-out pat-down procedure that many travelers found equally intrusive.

**2.3 Removal of backscatter units (2012–2013)**

In October 2012, TSA began removing backscatter units from major airports, citing the inability of the manufacturer (Rapiscan Systems) to meet a Congressional mandate to develop generic-avatar "Automated Target Recognition" software that would eliminate the anatomically detailed image (Grabell, 2012, *ProPublica*). The mandate required all AIT systems to produce only a generic human outline indicating approximate threat location, rather than an operator-readable body image.

Rapiscan failed to deliver compliant software within the required timeline. TSA terminated its contract with Rapiscan and removed approximately 250 backscatter units from airports, completing the removal by June 2013 (Duffy, 2013, *NPR*). Millimeter-wave AIT units from L-3 Communications (later part of Leidos) remained in service because L-3 had successfully implemented the generic avatar software.

**2.4 Current millimeter-wave technology**

Millimeter-wave scanners currently in use at U.S. airports operate on a fundamentally different physical principle than backscatter X-ray. They emit non-ionizing radiofrequency energy at millimeter wavelengths (approximately 70–80 GHz); the body surface reflects this energy back to receivers, and the reflected signal is processed to create a three-dimensional body-surface map. However, the output sent to the TSA officer is not a photographic or chalk-etching-like image of the body. It is a generic cartoon avatar — a schematic stick figure — with any detected anomaly flagged on the avatar's corresponding body location. The officer sees only the generic figure and the flag, not any anatomically individuated image of the specific passenger.

This design choice was specifically mandated by Congress and implemented in response to the privacy controversy surrounding backscatter technology. It effectively eliminates the "digital strip search" concern as it applied to the original backscatter units, though critics have raised questions about data retention policies for the underlying detection algorithms and raw signal data.

**2.5 Image provenance: Yandex reverse-image search**

A reverse-image search using Yandex confirmed that the image included in the 2024 viral post — showing backscatter scan outputs — had been present on the internet since 2010, when it appeared on TSA's own official website in a "How It Works" explainer page. The image thus authentically depicted the backscatter technology precisely as described: it was a genuine historical document of equipment that had been fully retired more than a decade before the post. Its re-circulation in 2024 as evidence for a present-tense claim is a textbook case of "outdated illustration" repurposed to imply current relevance.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty

**3.1 The "Outdated" verdict as epistemological category**

The Snopes verdict of "Outdated" — rather than "False" or "Misleading" — deserves examination as an epistemic instrument. The category reflects a recognition that temporal context is a constitutive dimension of empirical claims, not merely an optional modifier. A claim that was accurate in 2010 and is presented without temporal qualification in 2024 is not simply "false" — it is displaced in time in a way that carries different implications for how the misinformation functions.

When a claim is rated "False," the implied intervention is replacing it with the correct claim. When a claim is rated "Outdated," the implied intervention is both temporal correction and a more complex narrative: the thing described was real, it was genuinely controversial, it was changed in response to that controversy, and the change matters. The outdated rating thus contains within it an acknowledgment of valid historical grievance, which a simple "False" rating would obscure.

This has implications for how fact-checking interventions are likely to be received. Research on correction effects in misinformation (see Nyhan & Reifler, 2010, *Political Behavior*; Walter et al., 2020, *Communication Monographs*) suggests that corrections are more credible when they acknowledge what was true in the original claim before identifying what has changed. The "Outdated" category structurally implements this best practice at the level of the rating itself.

**3.2 Technology policy as temporal misinformation driver**

The TSA backscatter case is an instance of a broader pattern: claims about technology capabilities that were accurate at a specific moment in policy time become misinformation when technology policy changes but the claims continue to circulate. This pattern is particularly acute for surveillance and security technology, where:
- The general public has limited visibility into deployment and retirement decisions
- Official documentation is often opaque or archived rather than actively communicated
- Civil liberties organizations maintain historical documentation that remains surfaceable online
- Bad-faith actors can selectively surface older documentation to imply current relevance

The challenge for media literacy in this domain is that historical sources are not inherently unreliable — they may be accurate records of a real past state — but their accuracy at an earlier time does not transfer to present-tense claims. Distinguishing between "this was true then" and "this is true now" requires temporal metadata that is often stripped in social media re-sharing.

**3.3 Privacy by design as a contestable norm**

A further epistemic dimension is the question of whether the generic-avatar replacement technology constitutes genuine privacy protection or merely a privacy theater performance. EPIC and subsequent academic critics have argued that even if the viewer-facing display is a cartoon avatar, the underlying signal processing chain necessarily produces high-resolution body-surface data internally — and the privacy protections depend entirely on whether that underlying data is retained, accessible, or vulnerable to breach (Hasbrouck, 2012, *Papers, Please!* documentary analysis).

This creates an epistemological gap between the official description of current technology ("only a generic figure is shown") and the full privacy implications of the system. Fact-checking the viral claim that current TSA scanners show anatomical detail is tractable — they do not, as viewer-facing display. But fact-checking whether the underlying data pipeline constitutes a privacy risk is a harder, more contested empirical question that the "Outdated" verdict does not resolve.

## §4 Contextual Analysis

**4.1 The post-9/11 security ratchet**

The deployment of backscatter X-ray technology in 2010 was part of a broader post-9/11 airport security expansion that systematically traded privacy for security assurance. The TSA was created by the Aviation and Transportation Security Act of 2001, signed 19 November 2001, in response to the September 11 attacks. Its authority to deploy new screening technologies is grounded in that Act and subsequent Congressional appropriations. The backscatter rollout was specifically accelerated following the failed "Underwear Bomber" attack of December 2009 (Umar Farouk Abdulmutallab attempted to detonate PETN explosive concealed in his underwear aboard Northwest Airlines Flight 253), which demonstrated that explosive materials could evade metal detectors. AIT technology was specifically designed to address the detection gap exposed by that near-miss.

**4.2 Congressional intervention and the algorithm mandate**

An important institutional dimension of the backscatter removal is that it resulted from a Congressional mandate — not from administrative discretion or market forces. The FAA Modernization and Reform Act of 2012 required that all body scanners deployed at airport checkpoints use Automated Target Recognition software producing only a generic avatar display, rather than an operator-readable anatomical image, by a specified deadline. This was a notable instance of Congress acting on a civil liberties concern to constrain an executive agency's technology deployment, responding to sustained advocacy by EPIC and allied organizations.

The fact that Rapiscan failed to deliver compliant software and that TSA terminated the contract rather than seek an extension illustrates how policy mandates can drive technology deployment outcomes. L-3's millimeter-wave units survived because L-3 had already developed compliant software, suggesting that the technology selection outcome was in part a regulatory compliance competition.

**4.3 The opt-out pat-down alternative**

Throughout the AIT deployment period, TSA offered travelers the option to opt out of AIT screening and receive a manual pat-down instead. This choice was characterized by critics as a false option — the pat-down procedure is physically more intrusive for many people than a passive scan, and the practical social friction of requesting an opt-out in a busy airport checkpoint was sufficient to deter most travelers from exercising the option. TSA data showed opt-out rates of well under 1% at most airports.

The opt-out provision became itself the subject of political controversy, with pilots' and flight attendants' unions opposing AIT screening for crew members on grounds of cumulative radiation exposure and dignity. TSA eventually created expedited crew screening lanes with modified procedures, representing another instance of stakeholder-specific policy modification that further complicates the simple "current TSA scanners do/do not show everything" framing.

**4.4 International comparison**

The United States was not alone in deploying and then modifying body scanning technology. The European Union undertook a similar regulatory trajectory: the European Commission initially authorized backscatter X-ray AIT deployments but in 2011 issued guidance recommending against backscatter units pending further review of health and privacy concerns, and in 2013 adopted regulations requiring that only generic avatar software be used in EU airport AIT deployments. The UK and Germany conducted their own assessments, with Germany's Federal Police (Bundespolizei) running a millimeter-wave pilot at Hamburg Airport that produced sufficiently high false-positive rates to be considered operationally unacceptable, leading to program suspension (Bundespolizei, 2011, evaluation report). The cross-national pattern suggests that the privacy concerns were not uniquely American but reflected a broadly shared democratic discomfort with mandatory body imaging in civil transportation contexts.

## §5 Broader Implications

**5.1 The half-life of surveillance claims**

The TSA scanner case illustrates what might be called the "half-life problem" of surveillance misinformation: claims about surveillance capabilities circulate on social media long after the underlying technology policy has changed, because the original concern was genuine and the documentation remains accessible. Unlike fabricated claims, which have no authentic documentary foundation and can in principle be fully refuted, outdated-technology claims are supported by real historical evidence that makes them perpetually resurfaceable. The Snopes "Outdated" rating is a partial solution, but the rating itself requires ongoing maintenance — the claim may need to be rerated if TSA technology changes again.

**5.2 Privacy advocacy, documentation, and resurfacing**

One structural feature of the TSA backscatter controversy is that EPIC's litigation produced publicly accessible documentation — including actual scan images — that was never fully removed from the internet. This documentation was accurate evidence of a real privacy concern in 2010–2013. Its perpetual internet accessibility means it will continue to be resurfaced in contexts where it implies current relevance. This creates a systematic challenge for media literacy: the internet's memory is longer than policy change timescales, and the resurfacing of historically accurate documentation in misleading contemporary contexts is a class of misinformation for which there is no clear technical solution.

**5.3 Design as policy: avatar replacement as a model**

The replacement of anatomically detailed backscatter images with generic avatar displays is a case study in privacy-by-design as a policy instrument. Rather than relying on procedural safeguards (remote officer, image deletion policy), the 2012 Congressional mandate required an architectural change that made the privacy-threatening functionality technically impossible at the user-facing level. This "privacy by architecture" approach — eliminating rather than regulating the problematic capability — is recommended by privacy engineering researchers (Cavoukian, 2009, "Privacy by Design: The 7 Foundational Principles") as superior to procedural controls, which depend on consistent human compliance. The TSA case is thus a real-world policy experiment in privacy by design, with measurable outcomes: the revival of the "TSA sees everything" narrative in 2024 as a viral claim, despite over a decade since the technology was retired, suggests that procedural memory in public discourse is longer than technical reality.

**5.4 Radiation exposure and health literacy**

A subsidiary dimension of the backscatter controversy was public concern about X-ray radiation exposure. The EPA's characterization of backscatter exposure as equivalent to two minutes of cosmic radiation during flight was accurate in dosimetric terms. However, research on health communication indicates that ratio-based comparisons of low-dose radiation exposures are systematically misunderstood by general audiences, who tend to use a zero-threshold model — any deliberate X-ray exposure is perceived as riskier than equivalent background exposure — rather than the probabilistic risk model used by radiation health scientists (Slovic, 1987, *Science*). This health literacy gap contributed to public opposition to backscatter scanning even among people whose primary concern was radiation rather than privacy.
