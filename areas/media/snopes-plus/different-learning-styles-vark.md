# People learn best through their preferred learning style (visual, auditory, kinesthetic)

## 1. The Claim

Walk into almost any classroom, corporate training session, or professional development workshop and you will encounter some version of the following idea: people are fundamentally different kinds of learners. Some of us are visual learners who grasp information best through charts, diagrams, and images. Others are auditory learners who retain material most effectively when they hear it spoken. Still others are kinesthetic learners whose understanding blossoms through physical activity and hands-on experience. According to this model—often presented through frameworks like VARK (Visual, Auditory, Reading/Writing, Kinesthetic) or related systems such as VAK, the Kolb Learning Styles Inventory, or the Myers-Briggs-adjacent approaches to classroom design—effective teaching requires identifying each student's dominant style and adapting instruction accordingly. Present a visual learner with charts. Explain concepts aloud to the auditory learner. Give the kinesthetic learner something to build or manipulate. Fail to do this, and you are teaching against the grain of human cognitive diversity.

This idea has become deeply embedded in educational culture. Surveys have found that roughly 90 percent of teachers in several countries believe in learning styles as a pedagogically meaningful concept. Learning-styles assessments are administered in schools, universities, and workplaces around the world. Entire professional development industries have grown around the premise. Teachers are evaluated on their ability to differentiate instruction by style. Students label themselves as "visual" or "kinesthetic" learners and use the identity to explain academic difficulties or justify preferences about how they want to be taught.

The claim, at its core, is not merely that people have preferences—that seems true and unremarkable. The pedagogically consequential claim is the *meshing hypothesis*: that learning is improved when instructional format is matched to the learner's dominant style. This is the claim that the scientific evidence does not support.

## 2. What's Actually True

The meshing hypothesis has been subjected to rigorous experimental scrutiny and has repeatedly failed to find support. The most comprehensive review of the evidence, conducted by Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork and published in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* in 2008, concluded that the evidence for learning-styles-based instruction is inadequate. Their analysis identified what a proper test of the meshing hypothesis would require: a crossover experiment in which learners identified as visual and auditory are taught the same material in both visual and auditory formats, and their performance is compared. If the meshing hypothesis is correct, visual learners should outperform auditory learners when taught visually, and auditory learners should outperform visual learners when taught auditorily. What the researchers found was that existing studies almost never meet this standard, and the few that do fail to show the predicted interaction.

Cedar Riener and Daniel Willingham, writing in *Change: The Magazine of Higher Learning* in 2010, put the issue plainly: "There is no credible evidence that learning styles exist." Their analysis distinguished between two things commonly conflated in the learning-styles literature: the observation that people *prefer* certain instructional formats (well-documented and real) and the claim that matching instruction to preference improves learning (not supported). Riener and Willingham further pointed out that instruction matched to learning style may actually impede learning by limiting exposure to diverse representational formats. A student identified as a kinesthetic learner who is always given hands-on activities rather than diagrams or text may never develop the ability to extract information from a graph—a skill that matters for that student's future.

It is worth understanding why learning-styles instruction might make things *worse*. Cognitive science has established that learning is generally enhanced by what Bjork and colleagues call "desirable difficulties"—challenges that slow initial acquisition but strengthen long-term retention and transfer. Forcing a self-identified visual learner to extract meaning from a verbal explanation may feel harder in the moment but may produce deeper encoding. Tailoring instruction to preference removes the desirable difficulty and optimizes for immediate comfort rather than durable learning.

What does the evidence support instead? Several findings hold up well. First, *multimodal instruction*—presenting information through multiple formats, including visual, verbal, and hands-on elements—is broadly beneficial for most learners. This is not because it matches anyone's learning style but because redundant encoding across modalities strengthens memory consolidation. Second, *expertise* shapes what instructional format works best. A novice learning chess benefits from explicit verbal rules and labeled diagrams; an expert benefits more from pattern exposure. Third, the *nature of the content itself* determines the optimal instructional format. Mathematics taught purely through auditory lecture would be perverse; kinesthesia trained exclusively through reading would be equally so. The material has affordances that should drive instructional design far more than hypothetical student style profiles.

The VARK framework specifically, developed by Neil Fleming in 1987, was never grounded in experimental evidence for the meshing hypothesis. Fleming himself has noted in interviews that learning styles are preferences, not prescriptions. Yet the framework proliferated, was reified into assessments, and generated an industry before the evidentiary gap received sustained attention. A 2018 study published in *Anatomy & Cell Biology* examined medical students who identified their VARK learning style and compared study strategies to academic outcomes. Students who adopted study strategies aligned with their stated VARK style did not outperform those who did not. Style identification and style-matched study simply did not predict performance.

The directionality here matters: it is *not* that teaching should ignore individual differences or that all students are identical. Individual differences in prior knowledge, working memory capacity, motivation, and subject-specific background knowledge are real and pedagogically significant. But these differences are not best captured by visual/auditory/kinesthetic style categories. Domain-specific preparation, attention, and practice account for far more variance in learning outcomes than style preferences.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of the learning-styles myth in educational culture has multiple interlocking causes that are worth examining carefully, because the belief is not irrational given the information most people encounter.

**Personal experience feels confirmatory.** When a student who considers herself a visual learner is given a well-designed diagram and immediately grasps a concept she had struggled to understand from text, the experience feels like evidence. The problem is that good diagrams help *most* learners—their benefit is not evidence that they helped *this* learner specifically because she is visual. The experiment that would distinguish these interpretations (comparing the visual learner's performance to a non-visual learner's performance under the same instruction) is not running in everyday educational experience. What we notice is the effective teaching moment; we attribute it to style match.

**The concept has face validity.** Anyone who has encountered a student who clearly engages better with hands-on activities than lectures, or who struggles with verbal instructions but excels when shown a demonstration, can generate dozens of informal examples. These observations are real. What is not established is that the *category* doing the explanatory work is a fixed, diagnostic learning style rather than prior experience, interest, subject matter, or the quality of the specific instructional instance.

**Commercial and institutional investment.** Learning-styles assessment tools have been sold to schools, corporations, and governments at scale. The professional development industry that grew around differentiated instruction by style created constituencies—consultants, trainers, curriculum designers—whose business model depends on the premise. Once an idea is embedded in professional certification standards and evaluation rubrics, it becomes self-reinforcing regardless of evidence.

**Misapplied neuroscience.** In the 1980s and 1990s, claims about left-brain/right-brain differences and hemisphere specialization were popularized in ways that significantly overreached the actual science. Learning-styles theories rode this wave of pop neuroscience, absorbing its rhetorical authority. By the time hemisphere-dominance claims were widely recognized as oversimplified, the learning-styles framework had already established institutional roots. More recent claims have sought to ground learning styles in neuroimaging differences, but these studies have not demonstrated that identified neural differences translate into differential learning from style-matched instruction.

**The kernel of truth.** Learning styles theory survives in part because the observations that initially motivated it are real: people vary in how they engage with material, some learners respond better to some formats, and multimodal instruction genuinely helps. The mistake is not in observing that people differ, but in the specific theoretical framework used to explain and act on that observation. When learning styles explanations capture something real, they feel valid even if the mechanism is wrong.

**Identity and self-understanding.** Being a "kinesthetic learner" or a "visual learner" gives students a vocabulary for understanding their own experience and advocating for themselves in educational settings. This can be valuable as an advocacy tool even if the underlying theory is weak. Removing the label without providing an alternative framework for thinking about individual learning differences feels like dismissing the real experiences the label was explaining.

## 4. Verdict

**ACCURATE IN LETTER, MISLEADING IN PRACTICE.** People do have genuine preferences for how they engage with educational material. These preferences are real, vary across individuals, and can affect motivation and engagement. Multimodal instruction—presenting information through visual, verbal, and experiential channels—is broadly effective and educationally sound. However, the pedagogically consequential claim embedded in learning-styles instruction—that matching instructional format to each student's identified learning style improves learning outcomes—is not supported by experimental evidence. The *meshing hypothesis* has failed controlled tests. Teaching to identified styles may actually limit learning by reducing exposure to diverse representational formats and removing beneficial cognitive challenge. Effective instruction is driven by the nature of the content, students' prior knowledge, and principles of cognitive load and encoding—not by style diagnoses.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The learning-styles myth is part of a broader family of educational neuromyths—claims about the brain and learning that have proliferated in teacher training and curriculum design without adequate evidence. Others in this family include the "10 percent of the brain" claim, the idea that listening to Mozart increases intelligence (the "Mozart effect"), the notion that different hemispheres of the brain are responsible for different cognitive "styles," and the claim that optimal learning windows close irreversibly in early childhood. Neuroscientist and science educator Tanya Luhrmann, among others, has documented how these myths persist in educational culture long after being refuted in the research literature. The pattern is consistent: a finding from cognitive psychology or neuroscience is overextended, simplified, and commercialized, and the resulting simplified version enters teacher training while the corrective literature remains confined to academic journals.

The gap between educational research and educational practice is a structural problem, not merely a communication failure. Teachers are trained in pedagogy, not cognitive science literature review. Professional development is often delivered by consultants with commercial interests in particular frameworks. Accreditation standards and state education requirements sometimes codify specific theories before evidence has been established. Once a concept like learning styles becomes embedded in how teachers are evaluated and certified, it acquires a kind of institutional momentum that evidence alone cannot dislodge.

The learning-styles question also illuminates a deeper issue in educational research: the challenge of conducting proper experiments in naturalistic classroom settings. Educational interventions interact with teacher skill, student motivation, subject matter, time of day, class size, and dozens of other variables. Running the controlled crossover experiment that a proper test of the meshing hypothesis requires is logistically difficult and ethically complex. The absence of proper tests sustained the myth, because advocates could always say that the definitive experiment had not been run. When properly controlled experiments were finally conducted and analyzed, they did not support the hypothesis.

This does not mean that individual differences in learning are unimportant. They are very important—but they are better characterized through constructs like prior knowledge, working memory capacity, motivation, metacognitive awareness, and domain-specific aptitude. These constructs are supported by robust research traditions and do translate into actionable pedagogical guidance. A student with limited prior knowledge in mathematics needs explicit instruction, worked examples, and gradual progression toward complexity. A highly motivated student with strong metacognitive skills can benefit from more open-ended inquiry. These differences matter. Learning style category membership does not.

The practical implication for educators is not nihilism about individual differences but a reorientation toward what the evidence actually supports. Design instruction around the demands of the content. Use multiple representational formats not because students have styles to match but because diverse encoding strengthens memory. Attend to motivation and engagement, which are shaped by autonomy, competence, and relevance rather than by style accommodation. Monitor what each student actually knows and adjust accordingly. These principles are supported by decades of cognitive and educational research.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Learning styles occupy an unusual position in the fact-checking landscape: the claim is not a straightforward hoax or political disinformation but a longstanding belief in professional and educational culture. Most major fact-checking organizations have not treated it as a "fact-check" target in the traditional sense, because it does not take the form of a verifiable claim with a clear true/false adjudication point. Instead, the debunking has occurred primarily in scientific review literature, science journalism, and education policy commentary.

The most influential debunking has come from peer-reviewed cognitive psychology, particularly the Pashler et al. (2008) review in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, which functions as the primary authoritative source for journalists and educators addressing the topic. This review has been cited widely in science communication outlets including *Scientific American*, *The Atlantic*, and *Education Week*. Neuroscientist Yana Weinstein and colleagues have produced accessible summaries distinguishing supported from unsupported educational practices, placing learning-styles-matched instruction firmly in the unsupported column.

The British Psychological Society and the American Psychological Association have both issued statements noting the lack of evidence for learning-styles-based instruction as an effective practice. Several national educational ministries, including those in the UK and Australia, have updated teacher training frameworks to de-emphasize learning-styles-matched instruction, though adoption is uneven.

The nuance that responsible communicators maintain is the distinction between preferences (real, acknowledged) and meshing-hypothesis efficacy (not supported). Good science communicators are careful to avoid overcorrecting into the claim that all students are identical or that individual differences don't matter—because they do, just not along the axes that VARK-style frameworks propose. The challenge for fact-checkers is helping audiences understand that the problem with learning styles theory is not that it observes individual differences (correct) but that it uses the wrong framework to categorize and act on those differences (not supported). This is a more nuanced message than a simple true/false verdict, and it requires more context than most fact-checking formats easily accommodate.
