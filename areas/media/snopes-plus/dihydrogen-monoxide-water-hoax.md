# "Dihydrogen Monoxide" Is a Dangerous Chemical That Should Be Banned

## 1. The Claim

Circulating since at least the early 1990s — and proliferating across early internet message boards, email chains, and later social media — a document appears warning the public about the dangers of a chemical called "dihydrogen monoxide" (DHMO). The document is either presented deadpan as a genuine public health warning, or forwarded by someone who encountered it in a form designed to appear credible.

The warning typically includes claims like these: Dihydrogen monoxide has been found in concentrations dangerous to human survival. It is a component of acid rain. It can cause severe burns in its gaseous form. Prolonged exposure to its solid form causes tissue damage. Accidental inhalation can be fatal. It is present in tumours and cancerous tissue. Industrial plants use it in enormous quantities. It is implicated in erosion, flooding, and thousands of deaths each year. The chemical is not regulated by federal authorities, and efforts to restrict its use have repeatedly failed.

The document often concludes with a call for regulation or outright ban, and sometimes asks the reader whether they think DHMO should be banned.

Dihydrogen monoxide is, of course, water. H₂O: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom. The chemical name, rendered in systematic IUPAC-adjacent nomenclature, is technically accurate. Every factual claim in the DHMO document is also, in a specific sense, accurate. Steam burns. Ice causes frostbite. Inhaling water causes drowning. Water is found in tumours, in acid rain, in industrial cooling systems, in floodwaters and eroding riverbeds. All of it is true.

So is this a hoax? A joke? A real concern dressed up in legitimising scientific language? It is all three, depending on the context in which it appears — and what it reveals about science literacy, risk communication, and the mechanics of manufactured fear is genuinely important.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

Let's start by confirming what the DHMO materials assert, because most of it is factually accurate statement presented in maximally alarming framing.

**The chemistry is correct.** Dihydrogen monoxide is a valid, if unusual, way to refer to water. "Di" = two, "hydrogen" = hydrogen atoms, "monoxide" = one oxygen atom. H₂O. It is not the most common systematic name (that would be "oxidane" under IUPAC 2005 recommendations), but it is not wrong. The slight awkwardness is intentional in the hoax format — it sounds industrial and alarming in a way that "water" does not.

**The hazardous properties are real.** Every danger catalogued in standard DHMO documents is a genuine property of water:

- Steam (water in gaseous form) causes severe thermal burns — this is why pressure-cooker accidents are serious and why industrial steam systems require safety protocols.
- Ice (water in solid form) causes frostbite upon sustained contact with skin.
- Aspiration of liquid water blocks oxygen uptake in the lungs and causes drowning — a leading cause of accidental death worldwide, with approximately 236,000 deaths reported annually by the World Health Organization.
- Water is a significant component of acid rain, which does constitute an environmental and structural hazard.
- Water is present in tumour tissue — as it is in all human tissue, given that the human body is approximately 60% water by weight.
- Industrial cooling systems, nuclear reactors, and chemical plants use water in enormous quantities, and leaks or failures can have serious consequences.
- Flooding causes thousands of deaths annually and enormous economic damage globally.
- Coastal and riverine erosion driven by water is an ongoing geological and human infrastructure challenge.

So the claim that "DHMO is dangerous" is not false. It is technically defensible. What makes the hoax a hoax is not the inaccuracy of its individual facts but rather the decontextualised presentation of those facts, stripped of the information that would allow a reader to calibrate the actual risk profile of the substance. A hazard that kills thousands per year when aspirated sounds alarming until you learn that the substance in question is water, and the deaths are drownings.

**What the hoax teaches through its own mechanics.** This is what makes the DHMO experiment particularly interesting as a piece of critical thinking pedagogy. The hoax was designed not primarily to deceive but to demonstrate how easily deception is accomplished through selective framing, unfamiliar nomenclature, and absence of context. When surveys have been conducted asking respondents whether dihydrogen monoxide should be banned — before informing them it is water — significant proportions have said yes.

A now-famous example: in 1997, fourteen-year-old Nathan Zohner of Eagle Rock Junior High in Idaho conducted a science fair project in which he presented fifty classmates with the DHMO document and asked their opinions. Forty-three of the fifty said DHMO should be banned. Zohner's project, which he titled "How Gullible Are We?", won first prize in his regional science fair in the environmental science category. The episode was widely reported and the word "Zohnerism" was briefly proposed (by scientific sceptic James Randi's associate David Emery) to describe the practice of using factually accurate but misleading information to reach false conclusions.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The DHMO hoax works because it exploits precise and durable features of human cognitive architecture. It is not a crude scam relying on general gullibility. It is a sophisticated piece of rhetoric that succeeds because of how people think about chemical names, risk, and authority.

**The unfamiliar name manufactures unfamiliarity.** This is the core mechanism. When we encounter a chemical name we do not recognise, we have no prior evaluative framework to apply. We cannot assess whether "dihydrogen monoxide" is dangerous from first principles unless we already know organic chemistry nomenclature. For most people, unfamiliar chemical names activate a precautionary heuristic: "I don't know what this is, and someone is warning me about it, so perhaps it's worth worrying about." This heuristic is not irrational — it often serves us well — but it can be gamed.

**The specific facts overwhelm the evaluative frame.** The DHMO document is structured as a list of specific, individually verifiable facts, presented without comparative context. This mimics the structure of legitimate risk documents and scientific safety data sheets (SDSs), which also list hazards exhaustively without always providing comparative calibration. Readers accustomed to seeing this structure in legitimate safety materials extend to DHMO documents the credibility they would normally apply to SDS information. The document feels authoritative because it has the shape of authority.

**Risk communication is asymmetric by design.** Hazard identification is structurally easier than risk contextualisation. It is possible to write a technically accurate and genuinely alarming-sounding document about almost any common substance, including table salt (sodium chloride can be lethal in sufficient quantities, is used in industrial processes, and features in the composition of nuclear bomb-production facilities), oxygen (supports combustion, can cause oxygen toxicity above certain concentrations, is stored at high pressure in cylinders that present explosion risk), and carbon dioxide (present in all carbonated beverages, can cause asphyxiation in confined spaces, a major greenhouse gas linked to climate change). The DHMO framing is extreme but structurally identical to dozens of legitimate-seeming risk presentations that surround us.

**The internet amplifies the decontextualisation.** By the time many people encounter the DHMO warning, it has been forwarded multiple times, often stripped of whatever commentary or context accompanied the original. What began as a clearly satirical document, or a deliberate pedagogical exercise, arrives in a new reader's inbox or feed as an apparently earnest warning. The iterative stripping of context across forwarding chains is a well-documented feature of viral misinformation.

**Authority signals work even when fake.** DHMO documents often include official-sounding organisational names, committee reports, and governmental study references that are either entirely invented or artfully ambiguous. The appearance of bureaucratic infrastructure — the trapping of institutionalised concern — lends credibility that the underlying argument could not sustain on its own.

## 4. Verdict

**The hoax is a demonstration, not a deception — but it demonstrates something real.** The factual claims in DHMO documents are accurate. Dihydrogen monoxide is a valid chemical name for water. Water does have all the properties described. Water does kill people under specific conditions.

What is false, or at least profoundly misleading, is the implicit meta-claim: that these facts constitute grounds for considering water a chemical of exceptional danger warranting regulatory attention or a ban. That meta-claim is only sustainable if the audience lacks the contextualising knowledge to recognise the subject as water.

For audiences that do lack that knowledge — which is most audiences, because most people do not hold chemistry degrees — the DHMO exercise is a functional demonstration that accurate facts can produce false impressions. The hoax is pedagogically valuable precisely because it does not require the authors to lie. It shows that the selective presentation of truth can be just as misleading as fabrication.

The verdict on the core claim — "dihydrogen monoxide is a dangerous chemical that should be banned" — is: **FALSE (as a policy proposal) while being grounded in factually accurate statements**. This places it in the "distorted-but-grounded" gap category: the individual facts are real, but the conclusion drawn from them is not.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The DHMO hoax is usually encountered as a joke or a clever classroom exercise, but it sits within a much broader tradition of using scientific terminology and selective fact assemblage to manufacture alarm — or to sow doubt.

The same structural techniques used in DHMO documents are deployed, often far less transparently, in a range of real-world misinformation contexts:

**Vaccine ingredient lists.** A widely circulated rhetorical strategy in anti-vaccine communication involves presenting vaccine ingredients by their full chemical names — formaldehyde, polysorbate 80, thimerosal — stripped of their quantities and contextualised risk profiles. Formaldehyde, present in trace amounts in some vaccines, is also produced naturally by the human body in far greater quantities during normal metabolic processes. The individual facts are accurate; the impression created — that vaccines contain uniquely dangerous industrial chemicals — is not.

**GMO ingredient labelling discourse.** Similar framing appears in some anti-GMO rhetoric, where the presence of specific chemicals in genetically modified crops is described using technical names and industrial contexts without comparative data from non-GMO equivalents.

**Chemical scare journalism.** Investigative-style journalism about environmental contamination sometimes reproduces this structure inadvertently: naming compounds found in consumer products or water supplies at measured concentrations, without adequate comparison to naturally occurring background levels or established harm thresholds.

The DHMO hoax, created in 1990 by Eric Lechner and later popularised through various internet channels, is sometimes attributed specifically to a 1994 Internet posting by Craig Jackson or to earlier student publications, and the history of authorship is somewhat tangled. But its creation was explicitly satirical and pedagogical — designed not to deceive in the long term but to expose how easily deception is achieved. In this it succeeded arguably beyond its creator's intentions.

The hoax has since been deployed in media literacy curricula, critical thinking workshops, and science communication training as a canonical example of what is sometimes called "Zohnerism" or "statistically accurate misleading" (a subset of what researchers call "technically true but misleading" claims). It has become its own piece of scientific folklore — a story about how easily scientific-sounding language can be used to produce false impressions of risk.

The deeper lesson is about what we might call the nomenclature gap: the distance between how scientists name and discuss substances and how non-specialists hear those names. Chemical nomenclature is precise and internally consistent, but it is not designed for risk communication to non-specialist audiences. DHMO lives in that gap.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The DHMO hoax presents an unusual challenge for fact-checkers: the target claim is both technically accurate and fundamentally misleading, and the "reveal" (it's water) is well-known enough now that few newsrooms treat it as a live deception requiring investigation.

Snopes has not issued a formal detailed entry on DHMO as of the time of this writing (snopes_url: not-addressed), but the claim has been addressed across numerous science journalism and media literacy publications. The broad contours of fact-checker treatment are consistent: the hoax is identified as a deliberate piece of satirical science communication, the factual accuracy of the individual claims is confirmed, and the pedagogical value is highlighted.

The more interesting fact-checking challenge arises when DHMO framing is applied not to water but to real substances that people legitimately should be cautious about. The structural techniques of DHMO — accurate facts, alarming names, no context — appear in authentic misinformation about pesticides, pharmaceutical side effects, food additives, and environmental toxins. Distinguishing between a DHMO-style theatrical exercise and a DHMO-style genuine distortion requires the fact-checker to actually evaluate the substance being described, not just the structure of the presentation.

This highlights a limitation of format-based misinformation detection. Identifying a claim as "structured like the DHMO hoax" tells you something about presentation strategy but does not settle whether the underlying substance claim is accurate. A legitimate warning about a genuinely dangerous industrial chemical will sometimes have the same surface structure as a DHMO-style deception. Context, quantitative data, established scientific consensus, and regulatory status are the relevant tools.

For media literacy instruction, the DHMO hoax remains a high-value teaching artefact. It is specific, memorable, and verifiable. The reveal is satisfying precisely because it is so simple — it's water — and the reaction it produces in many students ("How did I not see that?") is a productive moment of epistemic self-awareness. Understanding how that moment of confusion was produced is among the more useful things a scientifically literate person can carry forward.
