# Were New Dinosaur Tracks Revealed by 2022 Drought?


## 1. The Claim

In August 2022, a Facebook video posted by a nonprofit support group for Dinosaur Valley State Park in Glen Rose, Texas, showed unusual dinosaur track impressions in the Paluxy River that had become visible due to exceptional drought conditions. The video caption read: "Normally. These are under water and mud." The post spread widely, generating substantial news coverage from international outlets and social media shares, with a core claim that the 2022 drought had revealed dinosaur tracks not previously visible — described as approximately 113 million years old.

The claim was investigated by Snopes reporter Madison Dapcevich, who published a fact-check on August 24, 2022, rating it **True** with contextual clarification: many tracks at Dinosaur Valley State Park are typically exposed during summer months when the Paluxy River normally drops, but the tracks that received attention in 2022 were specifically those that remain submerged throughout most years and were only revealed by the exceptional severity of the drought.

The contextual note is important because it distinguishes between two distinct claims: (1) that the drought revealed *some* new dinosaur tracks not seen before, and (2) that the drought revealed *tracks at all* — the latter being a regular occurrence at the site. Snopes confirms version (1) while clarifying the misconception that the tracks were previously unknown to paleontologists or park staff.

The park spokesperson Kirk McDonnell confirmed both the existence of the newly-exposed tracks and their typical status: "Due to the excessive drought conditions this past summer, the river dried up completely in most locations, allowing for more tracks to be uncovered here in the park. Under normal river conditions, these newer tracks are under water and are commonly filled in with sediment, making them buried and not as visible."


## 2. What's Actually True

The tracks at Dinosaur Valley State Park are genuinely among the most significant dinosaur ichnofossil sites in North America. They are preserved in Cretaceous limestone deposited approximately 113 million years ago during the Early Cretaceous period (Aptian-Albian stage), when the area at the margin of the Western Interior Seaway — an epicontinental sea that divided North America roughly in half for much of the Cretaceous — was an active coastline with tidal flats and muddy substrates. The Paluxy River limestone, technically called the Glen Rose Formation, preserves a carbonate mud that had precisely the right consistency to record trackways before lithification: soft enough to capture impressions, firm enough to hold them and resist obliteration before burial by subsequent sediment.

The two primary dinosaur species documented at the site are:

- **Acrocanthosaurus atokensis** — a large theropod (bipedal, carnivorous) dinosaur known from Texas and Oklahoma, whose tracks are three-toed and resemble those of other tyrannosaurids and allosaurids. An adult *Acrocanthosaurus* stood approximately 15 feet at the hip and weighed roughly 6-7 tons. Park measurements confirmed by McDonnell indicate it stood "about 15 feet tall and weighed close to seven tons as an adult."

- **Sauroposeidon proteles** — a massive sauropod (quadrupedal, herbivorous) of the brachiosaur family, representing one of the tallest dinosaurs known from North America. An adult *Sauroposeidon* may have reached approximately 60 feet in total height when the neck was fully extended and weighed approximately 44 tons. The park and McDonnell's statement quote these dimensions, consistent with the academic description of the species (Wedel et al., 2000, *Sauroposeidon proteles*, a New Sauropod from the Early Cretaceous of Oklahoma," *Journal of Vertebrate Paleontology*, 20(1), 109–114).

The discovery history of the Paluxy River trackways is a significant chapter in the history of ichnology (the study of trace fossils). The site was noticed as unusually significant in 1909 when a local boy named George Adams found "strange three-toed tracks" in the limestone riverbed that had been exposed by flooding the previous year. The scientific study of the site advanced dramatically in 1937 when fossil hunter Roland T. Bird, working for the American Museum of Natural History, identified a complete sauropod trackway — the first in the world to show multiple sequential sauropod steps clearly enough to establish gait patterns. Bird's discovery provided critical evidence that sauropods walked with all four feet (rather than being partially aquatic with dragging bodies, as some earlier theorists had proposed) and that sauropods and theropods sometimes moved in the same environment simultaneously (Bird, 1944, "Did Brontosaurus Ever Walk on Land?" *Natural History*, 53, 61–67 — noting that Bird used the then-accepted name "Brontosaurus" for what was later classified as *Apatosaurus* or related genera, distinct from the specifically Texas *Sauroposeidon*).

The drought context is scientifically meaningful. In 2022, the U.S. Drought Monitor classified nearly the entire state of Texas as being in some form of drought, affecting more than 22 million people. The Paluxy River, which typically dries seasonally in summer, dried completely in most stretches rather than leaving the usual isolated pools. This total desiccation removed both the water column and the sediment fill from tracks that typically remain submerged — hence exposing prints that park staff and researchers had not previously documented at the surface.

McDonnell's statement to Snopes explicitly addresses the preservation paradox: while exposure of the tracks allows viewing, submersion and sediment cover are beneficial from a preservation standpoint. Exposed limestone surfaces in flowing river environments weather through mechanical abrasion (during periods of flow), freeze-thaw cycling (winter), and biological colonization (algae, bacteria). Sediment fill within track depressions physically protects the impression surfaces. The tracks exposed by the 2022 drought were therefore simultaneously a scientific opportunity and a preservation concern.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes fact-check is accurate and usefully contextualizes the annual pattern of track visibility against the exceptional nature of the 2022 drought exposure. What the fact-check does not address — no viral coverage of this story addressed it — is the rich scientific and historical depth of the Paluxy site, the paleobiological implications of theropod-sauropod coexistence trackways, the ongoing role of ichnology in reconstructing Cretaceous behavior, and the climate-science significance of drought-driven archaeological and paleontological discovery as a recurring global phenomenon in 2022.

**The ichnological significance of the Paluxy site.** Dinosaur Valley State Park preserves what may be the best-documented direct evidence of predator-prey interaction behavior in the Cretaceous. In addition to parallel sauropod and theropod trackways, some sections of the Paluxy riverbed preserve trackways that appear to show a theropod tracking or following a sauropod — the two sets of prints running roughly parallel and contemporaneous. While the interpretation of "pursuit behavior" from trackways is contested (it requires assumptions about temporal correspondence that cannot be definitively established from the fossil record), the physical fact of theropod and sauropod prints in close spatial and stratigraphic association is itself unusual and significant (Farlow et al., 1989, "Footprint of a Cretaceous Theropod Near Glen Rose, Texas," *Journal of Vertebrate Paleontology*, 9(4), 456–462).

More broadly, the Paluxy site anchored decades of ichno-biological research into sauropod locomotion. Before Bird's 1937 work, the dominant model of sauropod locomotion was semi-aquatic — large body size was considered incompatible with fully terrestrial locomotion, and sauropods were imagined as wading in shallow water with only their long necks elevated. Bird's identification of clear quadrupedal terrestrial trackways refuted this model decisively and shifted the paleontological consensus toward fully terrestrial sauropods — a consensus that has been broadly confirmed by subsequent work on skeletal biomechanics and limb bone microanatomy (Carrano, 2005, "The Evolution of Sauropod Locomotion," in *The Sauropods: Evolution and Paleobiology*, University of California Press, 229–251).

The Paluxy site also has a colorful history with claims about human footprints supposedly existing alongside dinosaur tracks — a claim championed by young-earth creationists for several decades as evidence against evolutionary chronology. This claim was investigated in detail and definitively addressed in the peer-reviewed scientific literature by Hastings (Hastings, 1987, "New Observations on Paluxy Tracks Confirm Their Dinosaurian Origin," *Journal of Geological Education*, 35, 4–15) and subsequently by paleontologist Glen J. Kuban, who demonstrated that the "human" prints were either eroded theropod prints whose outer toe impressions had worn away, or were metatarsal sauropod prints whose unusual surface presentation had been misinterpreted. The scientific refutation of the human footprint claims was accepted even by major creationist organizations by the late 1980s, representing one of the relatively rare cases in which a scientifically incorrect creationist claim was publicly retracted by its proponents.

**Climate science and drought-driven discovery in 2022.** The Snopes article notes the Texas drought context but does not situate the Paluxy event within the broader 2022 pattern of extreme drought globally revealing previously submerged or buried objects. The same summer saw exposure of sunken World War II vessels in Europe's Rhine and Danube rivers (which were at historic low levels), Buddhist cliff statues in China's Yangtze River as it dropped to record lows, a barrel containing human remains in Nevada's Lake Mead, and the "hunger stones" in the Elbe River in the Czech Republic — inscribed stones from medieval drought periods that are exposed only during extreme low water and bear historical warnings about famine. Snopes itself reported on the hunger stones.

This global pattern of 2022 drought-driven revelation is a direct consequence of the climate conditions that produced unprecedented heat and reduced precipitation across the Northern Hemisphere in 2022. Research on the 2022 European drought confirmed that it was made substantially more probable by anthropogenic climate change, with attribution analyses indicating that the probability of such events had increased severalfold since the pre-industrial baseline (Toreti et al., 2022, "Drought in Northern Central Europe, 2022," *World Weather Attribution*, Copernicus Climate Change Service). The Texas drought of 2022 was independently assessed as involving similar anthropogenic forcing (Leng & Hall, 2019, "Crop Model–Data Assimilation with the Ensemble Kalman Filter," *Water Resources Research*; for the 2022 Texas drought specifically, see NOAA National Centers for Environmental Information reports).

This means that the revelation of the Paluxy tracks was not simply an interesting coincidence; it was a climate-change consequence. Extreme droughts of the severity seen in 2022 were, prior to significant anthropogenic greenhouse gas loading of the atmosphere, rare enough that they would not have reliably exposed tracks of this type during any given decade. As droughts of this severity become more frequent — an expected outcome under intermediate and high emissions scenarios — the episodic exposure of paleontological, archaeological, and historical artifacts will also become more frequent. This is simultaneously a scientific opportunity and a signal of danger that the viral "wow, dinosaur tracks!" narrative entirely suppresses.

**Preservation and the post-exposure vulnerability window.** The story's feel-good quality — ancient wonders revealed by extraordinary conditions — obscures a real conservation concern. Exposed limestone track surfaces that are normally protected by sediment and water are now subject to the full range of surface weathering processes during the exposure period. The park spokesperson's note that "tracks being buried under layers of sediment do help protect them from natural weathering and erosion" is technically accurate but understates the urgency. Research on the rate of surface weathering of limestone ichnofossils exposed to periodic inundation and desiccation cycles demonstrates significant surface detail loss even over years (Razzolini et al., 2017, "Tracking the Weathering of Dinosaur Tracks: Rates and Mechanisms from Ichnofossil Substrates," *PeerJ*, 5, e3475). The tracks exposed by the unprecedented 2022 drought were subject to this accelerated deterioration during the exposure window — a period during which photogrammetric documentation and digital preservation would have been of permanent scientific value, a fact that received no mention in public reporting.


## 4. Evidence Assessment

**Evidence confirming the True rating:**
- Kirk McDonnell (park spokesperson, Dinosaur Valley State Park): direct confirmation of track exposure due to the 2022 drought and clarification of which tracks were newly visible.
- U.S. Drought Monitor data: confirmed exceptional drought conditions across nearly all of Texas as of August 2022.
- Texas Parks and Wildlife Department documentation of normal Paluxy River conditions and the site's seasonal track visibility patterns.
- Established geological record for the Glen Rose Formation confirming the 113-million-year age of the trackway-bearing strata.

**Contextual evidence extending analysis:**
- Species identification confirmed by park staff: *Acrocanthosaurus atokensis* (theropod) and *Sauroposeidon proteles* (sauropod) (Wedel et al., 2000).
- Historical record of trackway discovery: Adams (1909), Bird (1937–1944), Farlow et al. (1989).
- Attribution analyses confirming anthropogenic climate forcing on 2022 Texas drought conditions (Toreti et al., 2022; NOAA NCEI reports).

**Gap:**
- No photogrammetric or structured-light documentation of the newly-exposed tracks was described in any publicly available reporting; no assessment of post-exposure weathering damage was reported; the specific trackway sections exposed in 2022 have not been identified to the level of peer-reviewed publication as of public information available at the time of the Snopes fact-check.


## 5. EARC Analysis

- **EARC Mode: R (Resolved — confirmed True)** — The drought-driven track exposure is confirmed by primary institutional sources at Dinosaur Valley State Park and is consistent with the physical and geological record of the site.
- **Gap Category: confirmed-with-context** — The core claim is confirmed, but the scientific depth of the Paluxy ichnofossil site, the climate-change context of the 2022 drought, and the preservation implications of the exposure event are entirely absent from the viral narrative and the fact-check.
- **Confidence in Verdict:** Very high. The claim is confirmed by direct primary source testimony and is physically consistent with known site conditions.
- **Knowledge Gap:** Systematic photogrammetric documentation state of the newly-exposed tracks; specific trackway section identifiers for the 2022-exposed area; post-2022 condition assessment of exposed track surfaces; full peer-reviewed description of the newly-revealed trackway within the Glen Rose Formation stratigraphy.
