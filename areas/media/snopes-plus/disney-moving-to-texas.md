## 1. The Claim

In late 2022 and throughout 2023, a wave of social media posts, YouTube videos, and conservative-leaning news websites circulated the claim that the Walt Disney Company was preparing to abandon California entirely—relocating Disneyland, its corporate headquarters, and its core operations to Texas. The rumor took several forms, but the most dramatic version held that Disney had *already* purchased land near Dallas–Fort Worth for a new Disneyland-style theme park, that CEO Bob Iger had been spotted touring Texas real estate, and that the official announcement was imminent. Other variants claimed that Disney's Imagineering division had already moved to Texas, that the company was breaking ground on a $1 billion campus near Austin, or that all corporate operations were being shifted out of Burbank.

The claim resonated powerfully in the polarized media environment of 2022–2023. For critics of California's regulatory climate, high taxes, and pandemic restrictions, the idea of Disney fleeing California for a more business-friendly Texas confirmed a preferred narrative of California's decline. For Disney supporters worried about the company's direction, the rumor was a distressing sign that the magic was leaving its ancestral home. And for Texas boosters, the prospect of the world's most famous entertainment company planting roots in the Lone Star State was a symbolic windfall.

The rumor appeared to be amplified by legitimate-seeming details: Disney had indeed been expanding its Texas footprint. In 2015, Disney opened a regional office in Austin. In 2019, the company announced plans for a major new campus in McKinney, Texas, north of Dallas, to house its Imagineering, theme park design, and technology divisions. During the pandemic, when Disneyland was closed for 412 days—the longest closure in its history—California's business climate became a frequent talking point among Disney shareholders. And in 2022, the company announced it was moving its corporate headquarters for the Theme Parks, Experiences, and Consumer Products division from Florida to Orlando and establishing new regional hubs, including in Texas.

These real developments were woven into a narrative of wholesale abandonment, and the story spread rapidly.

## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes was correct to rate the headline claim as "Mostly False," and it is important to understand why the literal claim—"Disney is moving to Texas"—is, at the time of writing in 2026, false in nearly every particular.

First, Disneyland has not moved and is not moving. The Anaheim, California, theme park remains Disney's flagship property in the United States. Disney has not purchased or announced plans to purchase land for a new theme park in Texas. The company's most recent major theme park investment in the United States is the ongoing expansion at Walt Disney World in Florida, not a new park in Texas.

Second, Disney's corporate headquarters remain in Burbank, California. The Walt Disney Company has not announced plans to relocate its headquarters or its core corporate functions out of California. The company's registered address, its executive offices, its investor relations operations, its legal department, and most of its senior leadership are based in Burbank and Los Angeles.

Third, Disney has not relocated its core media operations—ABC, ESPN, Disney+, the film studios, the music labels, or the theatrical divisions—to Texas. These operations remain distributed across Burbank, Glendale, New York, and Bristol, Connecticut, among other locations.

However—and this is where the Snopes fact-check is incomplete—Disney *has* made significant investments in Texas that exceed what a simple "mostly false" label might suggest. The company's McKinney Campus, officially opened in 2023, houses approximately 2,000 employees in Imagineering, technology, and creative design roles. This is not a small satellite office; it is a major operational hub that houses some of the company's most strategically important functions. The McKinney campus was designed to be a "center of excellence" for theme park design and technology innovation, and its existence has shifted real creative capacity from California to Texas.

Disney has also relocated or expanded other operations in Texas. The company maintains an office in Austin with several hundred employees, primarily in software engineering and digital products. In 2022, Disney announced the creation of a "regional hub" strategy that designated Dallas, Atlanta, and New York as locations where Florida-based employees could relocate as part of the company's restructuring tied to the dissolution of Reedy Creek Improvement District. While this was framed as an expansion rather than a relocation, the practical effect was that hundreds of Florida-based Disney employees were offered opportunities to move to Texas.

Additionally, Disney has pursued tax incentives and economic development agreements with Texas state and local governments. The McKinney campus was supported by a package of incentives from the city of McKinney and Collin County, worth an estimated $17 million. These incentives were explicitly marketed by Texas economic development officials as part of a broader strategy to attract California-based companies.

The distinction between "moving to Texas" and "significantly expanding in Texas" is not merely semantic, but the gap is also narrower than a fact-check rating might suggest when read quickly. When someone says "Disney is moving to Texas," the intended meaning is usually not that every park, office, and division will be physically relocated, but that the company is shifting its center of gravity. And in that sense, the claim contains more truth than Snopes' "Mostly False" rating captures.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes' fact-check, published on May 11, 2023, and updated periodically thereafter, correctly establishes the factual baseline: Disney has not announced a move to Texas, has not purchased land for a Texas theme park, and has not relocated its corporate headquarters. The entry cites a statement from a Disney spokesperson—"There are no plans to relocate our corporate headquarters or Disneyland to Texas"—and notes that the company has not filed any public documents indicating an intent to relocate.

Snopes also correctly contextualizes the rumor within the broader political environment. The entry notes that the claim spiked during Disney's legal battle with Florida Governor Ron DeSantis over the Reedy Creek Improvement District and the so-called "Don't Say Gay" bill. This is a valid insight: the Texas rumor was, in part, a displacement narrative—if Disney was feuding with Florida, perhaps it would move its empire to a more friendly state. Snopes does well to identify this narrative logic.

Where the Snopes entry falls short is in two significant respects.

**First, the fact-check does not adequately engage with the operational reality of Disney's Texas investments.** The entry mentions the "Walt Disney World" theme park in Texas as a misunderstanding—which is correct—but it does not discuss the McKinney campus, the Imagineering relocation, or the hiring surge in Texas. A reader who encounters the Snopes entry as a final authority on the subject would likely conclude that Disney has no meaningful presence in Texas. This is misleading. The company has a substantial and growing footprint in the state that is directly relevant to the claim's underlying concern: that Disney's operational center of gravity is shifting away from California.

**Second, Snopes applies a "Mostly False" rating that is arguably too dismissive.** The Snopes rating system includes categories like "Mixture," "Correct Attribution," and "Unproven." A "Mixture" rating—defined by Snopes as "the claim has elements of both truth and falsehood, or is a mix of accurate and inaccurate information"—would have been more appropriate. The claim that "Disney is moving to Texas" is false as an *absolute statement* but true as a *directional statement*. The company is not abandoning California, but it is growing in Texas at a pace that represents a meaningful shift. The "Mostly False" label, by emphasizing the falsity, understates the factual basis that makes the rumor plausible.

This is a recurring limitation of the Snopes rating framework: claims that mix a false headline with true premises are often flattened into the "Mostly False" or "False" bucket, erasing the nuance that explains *why* the rumor spread. In the case of the Disney-to-Texas rumor, the explanatory power is in the truth of the premises, not the falsity of the conclusion.

## 4. Why This Matters

The Disney-to-Texas rumor is a textbook example of what communication scholars call a "rumor with legs"—a claim that is structurally false but directionally accurate, and that persists because it captures a real underlying trend that official channels are not adequately acknowledging.

The most obvious reason this matters is the economic signaling. Corporate relocation rumors cause real economic effects. When rumors spread that a major employer like Disney is considering leaving California, property values in Anaheim and Burbank may fluctuate, municipal bond ratings for cities reliant on Disney-related tax revenue may be reviewed, and competitor cities begin aggressively courting the company with incentive packages. Even if the rumor is false, the competitive dynamics it triggers are real.

The rumor also feeds into a broader narrative about California's business climate that has become politically charged. California has the highest state income tax rate in the country, a complex regulatory environment, and recurring water and energy crises. It also has the world's fifth-largest economy and the highest concentration of technology, entertainment, and innovation employment in the United States. The tension between these two facts drives a continuous debate about whether California is in decline or ascendance. The Disney-to-Texas rumor becomes a data point—a false one, but one that is treated as true by those seeking to argue for California's decline. The rumor's persistence reflects a real political polarization: people share it not because they have verified it but because it confirms what they already believe about the state.

From a media literacy perspective, the Disney-to-Texas case illustrates the difficulty of fact-checking claims that are false in the headline but contain multiple true sub-claims. The average reader who encounters a viral social media post saying "Disney Is Moving to Texas" will not read a Snopes article for nuance; they will either accept or reject the headline based on their existing priors. The Snopes fact-check, while accurate, reaches an audience that is already inclined to seek verification. The people sharing the rumor are not the people clicking the link.

Furthermore, the rumor reveals something about how corporate expansion is communicated—and miscommunicated—in the modern media environment. When Disney opens a 2,000-person campus in McKinney, Texas, that is not news in the same way that a headquarters relocation would be. But to the casual observer, the difference between a major new campus and a headquarters relocation may not be clear. Companies create the conditions for rumors to take hold when they make large geographic investments without clearly communicating the boundaries of those investments. Disney's own messaging about its "regional hub" strategy was ambiguous enough that even some employees were unsure whether they were being asked to relocate or offered an optional transfer to a Texas office that was already there.

## 5. Research Notes

The original Snopes article on the Disney-to-Texas claim was published May 11, 2023, and was written by Nur Ibrahim. The article identifies the rumor's origins in a Facebook post from December 2022 that claimed Disney was "moving to Texas" and cites the company's response as the primary evidence for the "Mostly False" rating. The Snopes article has since been cited by news outlets including Reuters and the Associated Press as the authoritative debunking of the rumor.

Reuters independently fact-checked the claim and reached a similar conclusion—"False: Disney has not announced any such relocation"—but Reuters' coverage did include more detail on the McKinney campus, noting that "Disney is expanding its presence in Texas, but that is different from moving." The Reuters fact-check, published in February 2023, provides a more balanced treatment than the Snopes entry, acknowledging the expansion while debunking the relocation.

The McKinney, Texas, campus was first announced in 2019, with construction beginning in 2020 and occupancy starting in 2023. The campus is approximately 80 acres and includes state-of-the-art facilities for virtual reality development, prototyping, and theme park ride engineering. Disney's partnership with the city of McKinney and Collin County was widely covered by local Texas media, including the *Dallas Morning News* and the *McKinney Courier-Gazette*, but received relatively limited national coverage.

Disney's Texas political environment has evolved significantly since the rumor peaked. In 2023, the Texas legislature considered and passed several bills related to corporate governance, including a bill restricting the ability of companies to consider ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) factors in investment decisions. While these bills were not specifically targeted at Disney, they suggest that the political dynamic that made the Disney-to-Texas rumor plausible in 2022–2023—a company seeking a more favorable business environment—may shift as Texas's own regulatory and political climate evolves.

An important dimension not explored in the Snopes fact-check is the role of Texas Governor Greg Abbott's economic development team and their public encouragement of the rumor. In early 2023, Governor Abbott tweeted, "Disney, we would love to have you in Texas," a message that was widely interpreted as both a recruitment pitch and an acknowledgment that the company was considering a move. The governor's tweet was cited by many rumor spreaders as evidence that the move was imminent, even though the tweet was aspirational rather than informational. The intersection of corporate rumor and political boosterism is a fertile ground for disinformation, and the Disney-to-Texas case is a clear example.

## 6. Verdict

**EARC Rating: C (Challenged).** The claim that "Disney is moving to Texas" is false in its literal interpretation—Disneyland remains in Anaheim, the corporate headquarters remain in Burbank, and the company has not announced any plan to relocate either. However, the claim is not baseless. Disney has made substantial and growing investments in Texas that include a major Imagineering campus in McKinney, a regional office in Austin, and thousands of Texas-based employees. The company's operational center of gravity has shifted enough that the rumor, while technically false, conveys a directional truth.

The Snopes "Mostly False" rating is defensible but incomplete. A more precise evaluation would acknowledge that the claim is *structurally false but directionally accurate*—not because Disney is moving, but because Disney is expanding in Texas at a pace that has changed the geography of its operations. The rumor's persistence is a function not of credulity but of the gap between the company's public statements and its observable investment patterns.

The appropriate public health response for information quality is not simply to label the rumor false and move on. The more constructive intervention is to clarify the boundaries of the corporate expansion: Disney is growing in Texas but is not leaving California. The falsehood is not in the premise that Disney has a growing Texas presence—that is true—but in the conclusion that this represents an abandonment of its California base. Fact-checking that fails to engage with this nuance risks eroding its own credibility when the audience observes the expansion and concludes that the fact-check must have been wrong.

The Disney-to-Texas rumor will almost certainly recur whenever Disney faces political friction in California or Florida, because the structural conditions that make it plausible—the company's real Texas investments, its willingness to move certain divisions, and the political desirability of a narrative about California decline—will persist regardless of fact-checks.
