## §1 — The Claim and Its Vernacular Life

"Centipede" is one of those words that carries its own self-confident biology lesson: *centi-* means hundred, *-pede* means foot, therefore the creature has a hundred of them. The logic feels airtight in the way that folk etymologies often do — clean, memorable, and wrong. The claim that centipedes have exactly 100 legs circulates as casual trivia on playground tables, in pub quizzes, and on social media posts that aggregate "weird animal facts." A representative Reddit post from May 2022 in r/todayilearned — pulling from Wikipedia — restated the correction that "no species of centipede has exactly 100 legs," earning thousands of upvotes, which tells us something important: the myth is persistent enough that its debunking still counts as news.

Snopes rated the claim **False** in a June 2022 fact-check by Nur Ibrahim. The verdict is unambiguous and supported by multiple independent sources. But the interesting question is not whether the claim is true (it isn't) and not merely why the name misleads (Latin numerology applied to a creature that hadn't been carefully counted), but rather *why* it is developmentally and evolutionarily impossible for any centipede to have an even number of legs at all — let alone precisely 100. That puzzle sits at the intersection of developmental genetics, evolutionary biology, and the odd persistence of vernacular misnaming in popular zoology.

This entry examines all three threads: the factual record on centipede leg counts, the developmental explanation for the odd-number constraint, and the epistemological problem of name-based beliefs about nature that resist correction even when authoritative sources have been loudly saying otherwise for decades.

## §2 — The Factual Record: Leg Counts Across Chilopoda

Centipedes belong to the class Chilopoda, itself one of four classes within the subphylum Myriapoda — a grouping that also includes millipedes (Diplopoda), symphylans, and pauropods. Chilopoda is divided into five recognised orders, each with characteristic leg counts.

The National Park Service notes in its Everglades educational materials that centipedes "can have fewer than 20 legs to more than 300 legs, but they always have an odd number of pairs of legs." This upper bound requires clarification: the maximum reliably documented is 191 pairs (382 legs) in certain geophilomorph centipedes. All counts in between are possible, but *only* if they are odd numbers multiplied by two.

A 2017 peer-reviewed study by Kenning, Matthes, and colleagues, published in *PeerJ* (volume 5, e4023), provides the most comprehensive recent morphological review. The paper states: "Contrary to the trivial name centipedes, literally suggesting the presence of 100 legs, adult representatives of scutigeromorph, lithobiomorph, and craterostigmomorph species possess 15 post-forcipular pairs of legs." The paper further documents that Scolopendromorpha possess 21 or 23 pairs, with unusual cases of 39 or 43 pairs recorded in *Scolopendropsis duplicata* (Chagas-Júnior, Edgecombe & Minelli, 2008), while Geophilomorpha may reach up to 191 pairs. Kenning et al. (2017) explicitly observe that males in geophilomorph species typically have fewer leg-bearing segments than females of the same species, adding intraspecific variation to an already complex picture.

The Australian Museum, Sydney, has long maintained educational materials stating that the name centipede is a misnomer "because the number of pairs of legs in centipedes is always an odd number and the known range is between 15 and 191 pairs." This independent corroboration from a natural history institution in the Southern Hemisphere (where centipede biodiversity is high) strengthens the global claim.

By arithmetic: if every centipede has an odd number of leg *pairs*, no centipede can have 100 legs total. 100 ÷ 2 = 50, and 50 is an even number. An even number of pairs is excluded by the developmental rule. Therefore a centipede with exactly 100 legs is impossible. The claim is not merely unverified — it is structurally prohibited.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Two-Segment Periodicity Rule and Why Odd Numbers Are Compulsory

The most intellectually rich finding here is not the leg count per se but the developmental-genetic mechanism that enforces the odd-pair constraint. A 2004 study published in *Current Biology* (Cell Press) by researchers from the University of Cambridge Department of Zoology, reported by ScienceDaily, investigated the embryonic segmentation of the centipede *Strigamia maritima* using gene expression analysis. The study identified a series of segmentation genes that initially establish a **two-segment periodicity** in the forming trunk of the embryo.

This is a crucial insight. Segmentation in centipedes is not assigned one segment at a time — it is parcelled out in pairs. The molecular patterning cascade defines two segments simultaneously before resolving them into individual units. If evolutionary changes add or remove segments, they do so in pairs, by the logic of the underlying patterning system. This means the number of leg-bearing segments is constrained to values reachable by adding or subtracting two — which, starting from an odd number, always returns an odd number.

But the study's authors leave a further puzzle: why *odd* rather than *even* numbers of pairs? Their proposed resolution concerns the forcipules — the distinctive "poison claws" that characterise the first post-head segment of all centipedes. According to the Cambridge team, these forcipules are almost certainly modified legs from an ancestral leg-bearing trunk segment. In the evolutionary transition from leg to venom-delivery weapon, the segment carrying these modified appendages was shifted anteriorly and reassigned a different function. If we count the forcipule-bearing segment as a "trunk segment" in the ancestral body plan, then centipedes *effectively* have an even total number of trunk segments — but because in the derived condition one pair is re-purposed into poison claws rather than walking legs, the counted walking-leg pairs remain odd.

This is a clean example of what evolutionary developmental biology calls **co-option**: a structure evolved for one function (locomotion) is repurposed to another (prey capture and venom delivery), with secondary consequences for the counting arithmetic of the remaining structure. The study illustrates that the folk belief ("centipedes have 100 legs") fails not merely at the level of empirical observation but at the level of the generative rule: the developmental programme does not *allow* a centipede substrate on which 100 walking legs could develop.

The phylogenetic depth of this constraint matters. The two-segment periodicity and odd-pair result have been conserved across all five orders of Chilopoda, suggesting the constraint was fixed very early in centipede evolutionary history — likely in the Upper Cambrian or Lower Ordovician, when myriapod lineages are first evidenced in the fossil record. This means the folk name has always been wrong — not just in the present but for hundreds of millions of years of centipede existence.

## §4 — The Poison Claw Architecture and What It Means for Leg Counting

The Encyclopedia of Toxicology (ScienceDirect, agricultural and biological sciences topics) describes centipede morphology: "Centipedes are nocturnal multisegmented elongated arthropods known for the distinct feature of having a pair of legs for each body segment except for the last. The first pair of legs on the cranial segment is, in actuality, modified into a pincerlike apparatus, known as forcipules, used to inject venom into prey."

This distinction between legs and forcipules is critical for correct counting protocol. When lay observers count appendage pairs on a centipede, they often fail to distinguish forcipules from walking legs. The forcipules are visually conspicuous — they are positioned near the head and often seen managing prey — but they are not locomotory. A count that includes forcipules would be miscounting the "legs" even by the colloquial standard.

Moreover, centipede venom is not a curiosity but an operationally sophisticated chemical system. Scolopendra species — the large tropical centipedes most often photographed for viral content — possess venom containing compounds including serotonin, cardiotoxic proteins, and various enzymatic factors that paralyse invertebrate prey and can cause significant pain in humans. The Missouri Department of Conservation lists geophilomorph soil centipedes as possessing venom glands in their forcipules even though soil centipedes are generally too small to penetrate human skin effectively.

For the leg count question: a large *Scolopendra* specimen with 21 pairs of walking legs plus one pair of forcipules has 21 × 2 = 42 walking legs plus 2 forcipules = 44 visible appendages. A count of 44 "legs" would be wrong in multiple ways simultaneously: wrong in total number, wrong in category, and wrong in comparison to the folk claim of 100. The gap between the vernacular claim and the anatomical reality is therefore not a matter of a few missing or miscounted appendages — it is a complete structural mischaracterisation of the organism.

## §5 — Why the Myth Persists and the Epistemology of Name-Belief

Naming an animal is an act with epistemic consequences that typically outlast the namers' intentions. The Latin taxonomic tradition is rich with organisms whose names encode outdated or incorrect beliefs: the starfish (Asteroidea) is not a fish; the jellyfish is neither jelly nor fish; the seahorse (Hippocampus) is not a horse; and the centipede, apparently, is not a hundred-footed creature.

The centipede case is epistemically interesting because the name does not encode a deliberate misclassification but an aspirational folk taxonomy — the name was presumably given by speakers who believed the creature *roughly* had a hundred legs, or who chose "hundred" as an impressionistic large number term (much as "myriad" originally meant "ten thousand" but now generically means "many"). The BBC reported in 2012 on the discovery of a millipede with more legs than any previously recorded, noting that the upper bound on observed leg counts in the myriapod phylum is repeatedly being revised. Yet the name "centipede" has not shifted.

This reflects a general phenomenon in popular zoology: common names, once coined, are nearly impossible to revise even when they encode factual errors. The common name functions as an identity token, not a description. Telling someone that centipedes never have 100 legs produces surprise — often repeated surprise — because the name continuously re-activates the wrong belief. Every time someone reads "centipede," they unconsciously reconstruct the etymology, and the reconstruction suggests 100.

The Reddit correction cycle is instructive here. The r/todayilearned post that Snopes cited received extensive engagement. But the comment patterns in such threads typically show that many commenters who upvote the correction will, in a different context days later, describe a centipede as having "about a hundred legs" — the correction does not stick against the constant low-level priming of the name itself.

This is relevant for science communication strategy. "No species of centipede has exactly 100 legs" is a clean, factual debunking statement. But data from the cognitive science of misconception correction suggests that fact-only corrections are less durable than corrections that include a coherent alternative narrative (Lewandowsky, Ecker, Seifert, Schwarz, & Cook, 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*). The alternative narrative here — the developmental two-segment periodicity story, the evolutionary co-option of forcipules — is, fortunately, more interesting than the myth it replaces.

## §6 — Verdict, Residual Gaps, and Lines for Further Inquiry

**Snopes verdict: False.** The claim that centipedes have 100 legs is false. No centipede species has ever been documented with exactly 100 legs; the structural reason is that all centipedes possess an odd number of leg pairs, and 100 is an even number. The range of documented walking-leg pairs across all Chilopoda is 15–191 (Kenning et al., 2017, *PeerJ*; Australian Museum educational materials), and the mechanism enforcing odd-pair counts is the two-segment periodic developmental patterning of the centipede trunk (Cambridge developmental biology research, 2004, *Current Biology*).

**EARC assignment: R (Refuted).** The empirical record is consistent and the mechanistic explanation is well-supported. This is not a contested claim, a matter of definition, or a simplification — it is straightforwardly incorrect.

**Residual gaps for future entries:**

1. *Species-level documentation gaps.* The geophilomorph order shows the widest range of leg counts but remains poorly surveyed in tropical forest habitats; new species with unusual pair counts are regularly described.
2. *Developmental genetics in non-model centipede species.* The 2004 Cambridge study focused on *Strigamia maritima*; molecular work on Scolopendromorpha (the large, medically significant species) is comparatively under-resourced, leaving the segment-addition mechanism partially inferred.
3. *Venom ecology.* The ecological role of centipede venom in structuring invertebrate communities — especially in soil ecosystems — is beginning to receive research attention but remains underrepresented in mainstream popular science.
4. *Inaccessibility of primary sources.* The Kenning et al. (2017) *PeerJ* paper is open-access; the 2004 *Current Biology* paper is paywalled, making it largely invisible to the public audience most likely to hold the misconception.

The centipede leg myth is a small but instructive case study in how words embed false models of nature, how folk naming conventions acquire the force of fact, and how developmental biology can provide a more compelling corrective than simple enumeration. The creature, it turns out, is constrained by its own evolutionary history to remain forever mathematically unable to achieve the number embedded in its name.
