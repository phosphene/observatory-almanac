# Do Hurricanes with Female Names Cause More Deaths Because People Don't Take Them Seriously?

*A Truth Vault analysis of a study that made exactly the splash its hypothesis predicted, then partially drowned in the reanalysis flood — a case study in how politically resonant findings interact with peer review, media amplification, and scientific correction.*

## The Claim

In June 2014, the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (PNAS) published a study by Jung et al. (2014) with the title "Female hurricanes are deadlier than male hurricanes." The finding was as immediately arresting as its framing was deliberately provocative: using historical hurricane data from 1950 to 2012, the authors claimed that hurricanes assigned female names caused significantly more deaths than those assigned male names, and that this disparity was explained by gender stereotypes — specifically, that people perceived female-named storms as less threatening and therefore failed to evacuate or take protective measures at appropriate rates.

The study received extraordinary media coverage upon publication. *Time*, *The Washington Post*, *The Guardian*, *NPR*, and dozens of other outlets ran the finding under headlines that foregrounded the gendered explanation: "Sexism Kills," "Lady Hurricanes Are Deadlier Because of Sexism," "Sexist Attitudes May Make Female Hurricanes More Deadly." The story fit a recognizable cultural narrative about implicit gender bias with remarkable precision. It was also specific, empirical, and published in one of the world's most prestigious scientific journals. It seemed almost too perfect.

The subsequent scientific history is instructive about how findings that resonate with pre-existing narratives move through the scientific and media ecosystem — and about how that ecosystem handles correction.

## What's Actually True

The methodological critiques of Jung et al. (2014) appeared rapidly and were substantial. They do not simply re-litigate the finding; they identify structural problems with the analysis that, when corrected, cause the effect to disappear entirely.

**The naming convention history.** The most consequential confound in the Jung et al. analysis is one that the original paper minimizes: the naming history of Atlantic hurricanes is not a random or gender-balanced assignment protocol. Atlantic hurricanes were given exclusively female names from 1953 to 1979. During this period, the most catastrophic storms in the historical record occurred — including storms that killed hundreds or thousands of people. Retaining records from this era in an analysis comparing "female-named" versus "male-named" storms guarantees that female-named storms will have higher death tolls on average, not because of gender stereotypes but because of survivorship and era effects. The early decades had worse building codes, less warning infrastructure, less reliable meteorological tracking, and far fewer resources for evacuation.

Christensen and Christensen (2014, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, Letter) made this point directly and quantitatively after the original publication. Smith (2014, in a widely-circulated analysis) demonstrated that removing the era in which all storms had female names eliminated the effect. Malter (2014, *PNAS* letter) showed that the statistical model was highly sensitive to a handful of outlier events — specifically, a very small number of catastrophically deadly storms with female names.

**The outlier problem.** Jung et al.'s original dataset contained 94 storms meeting their inclusion criteria over six decades. Of these, a handful of catastrophically deadly storms — primarily those from the all-female naming era — exerted enormous leverage on the regression estimates. Elaine Showalter's analysis (reported in multiple independent reanalyses) demonstrated that removing as few as three or four major outliers (most significantly, Hurricane Katrina and Hurricane Audrey) changed the estimated effect radically. A finding that is not robust to the inclusion or exclusion of a tiny fraction of the sample is not a reliable finding. This fragility is precisely what the biological sex of storm names should not produce under the study's hypothesis — gender bias at the population level should generate a diffuse, small-but-consistent effect across many storms, not an effect concentrated in a handful of catastrophic outliers.

**The laboratory experiments problem.** Jung et al. did not rely on historical data alone. They also conducted laboratory experiments in which participants were asked to rate their willingness to evacuate from hypothetical storms assigned male or female names. The paper reported that participants rated female-named storms as less severe and showed lower evacuation intent. This experimental component appeared to provide the mechanistic chain the observational data required: the historical mortality difference was explained by the experimental finding about individual risk perception. Independent reanalyses showed that the experimental effects were small, the samples were not representative of hurricane-vulnerable populations, and most importantly, the translation from laboratory willingness-to-evacuate ratings to actual mortality differences in the field involved inferential leaps that the data did not support. The laboratory scenario used hypothetical storms (non-named, with only the name manipulated) without realistic meteorological information such as storm tracks, intensity forecasts, or local authority evacuation orders — all of which, in real hurricane contexts, swamp any potential name-salience effects.

**The post-2012 data.** Jung et al.'s dataset ended in 2012. Subsequent analyses extending the dataset to include more recent hurricane seasons have consistently failed to find the predicted effect. Katz (2016, *Weather, Climate, and Society*) analyzed data through 2015 and found no significant effect of storm name gender on mortality when controlling for storm intensity and landfall characteristics. The absence of the predicted effect in newer data is precisely what would be expected if the original finding was an artifact of the historical naming convention, not a genuine behavioral phenomenon.

**The NOAA naming protocol.** Since 1979, Atlantic hurricane names have alternated boy/girl in a pre-assigned list maintained by the World Meteorological Organization. The list is gender-balanced and the assignment of any particular year's storms to names in the list is determined by order of formation, not by intensity or any other characteristic correlated with lethality. Under this protocol, there is no mechanism by which gender-associated names could predict mortality through the storm-severity channel (i.e., more intense storms being consistently assigned gendered names). Post-1979 data alone shows no significant effect.

**What the study did find.** To be fair to Jung et al., their analysis did find a statistically significant association in their dataset under their model specification. This was replicated within the same dataset using their methods. The problem is not that the mathematics was wrong but that the model was misspecified: it did not adequately account for era effects, it did not use appropriate outlier-robust methods, and it attributed a historical artifact to a psychological mechanism that was tested only indirectly. The laboratory experiments found effects that, while directionally consistent with the hypothesis, were of uncertain practical significance and measured in populations unlikely to be directly relevant (Amazon Mechanical Turk participants who are presumably not all in hurricane evacuation zones).

## Why People Believe This

The gender-hurricane hypothesis is a case study in how the interaction of scientific credentialing, a politically resonant hypothesis, and media amplification dynamics can produce a claim that is widely believed despite methodological fragility.

**PNAS publication carried substantial credibility weight.** The *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* is one of the highest-impact general science journals in the world. A finding published in PNAS carries an institutional imprimatur that news organizations and the public treat as provisional guarantee of quality. This credibility transfer happens even when PNAS published the paper under a system (now reformed) that allowed National Academy members to facilitate the review of papers by their own disciplines under non-standard review processes. The Jung et al. paper was communicated to PNAS by a member, which meant its review path differed from standard externally peer-reviewed submissions. This procedural detail was not widely reported in the media coverage.

**The hypothesis was maximally resonant.** The claim that gender bias contributes to preventable death is both politically important (if true) and emotionally compelling. It connects hurricane science to the broader national conversation about implicit bias, diversity, and systemic sexism that was particularly acute in 2014. A finding that implicit gender stereotypes kill people is the kind of finding that advocates for gender equality will amplify, because it provides empirical support for claims that would otherwise be made on normative grounds alone. This amplification is not cynical or dishonest — the people sharing the finding believed it was true. But the structural incentive for amplification means that pro-amplification forces are much stronger than correction forces when the claim is politically resonant for an identifiable audience.

**The correction received far less coverage than the finding.** The series of published letters and reanalyses that collectively undermined the original finding appeared in *PNAS*, in *Weather, Climate, and Society*, and in several statistical blogs. They received modest coverage in science journalism outlets with specialist audiences. They received essentially no coverage in the general press outlets that had prominently featured the original finding. This asymmetry is well-documented across the science communication literature: initial findings are newsworthy; corrections and reanalyses are not. The result is a persistent public belief that tracks the media cycle of initial reporting rather than the slower scientific process of correction and consensus formation.

**The epistemic novelty here is the meta-finding about publication and correction incentives.** The gender-hurricane case is unusual in that it provides a relatively controlled test of several claims about science communication. The hypothesis was specific, the data were available for independent reanalysis, the reanalyses were published in the same journal, and the media reaction to both the finding and its critique was tracked in real time. What the case demonstrates is that the scientific correction process functioned more or less as designed — independent reanalyses appeared rapidly, substantial critiques were published, and the scientific community came to understand the finding as at best unproven and at worst a statistical artifact. What failed was the second-stage correction: the translation of scientific reanalysis back into public understanding. The journals performed their epistemic function. The media ecosystem did not perform a corresponding corrective function because corrections are not news.

## Verdict

The claim that female-named hurricanes cause more deaths because gender stereotypes lead people to underestimate their threat is **Unproven to Likely False**.

**The original finding (Jung et al., 2014, *PNAS*)** reported a statistically significant association between female hurricane names and higher mortality in a dataset running from 1950–2012. This association was real within their dataset and model specification.

**The finding does not survive methodological scrutiny.** It does not survive removal of the all-female-naming era (1953–1979), in which many of the deadliest storms in the historical record occurred under conditions entirely unrelated to gender stereotypes. It does not survive outlier-robust statistical methods. It does not survive extension to more recent data. The laboratory experiments suggesting a behavioral mechanism used non-representative samples and hypothetical scenarios not representative of real hurricane decision-making environments.

**The most parsimonious explanation** for the original dataset's association is confounding by historical era and a small number of high-leverage outlier events — not a gender-bias mechanism operative at the population level. This is the scientific consensus as represented in the subsequent literature.

**Our rating: UNPROVEN to LIKELY FALSE** as a causal claim about gender stereotypes driving differential mortality. The finding is an artifact of dataset construction, not evidence of the proposed mechanism.

## The Wider Picture

The gender-hurricane case is valuable beyond its verdict as a case study in several intersecting problems in contemporary science and science communication.

### The Lifecycle of a Resonant Finding

Social scientific findings that align with culturally salient narratives follow a predictable lifecycle in the contemporary media ecosystem. The initial publication generates a wave of uncritical amplification, driven by outlets whose audiences are pre-receptive to the narrative frame. The amplification precedes peer critique, which is a normal feature of the scientific process but is poorly suited to the media cycle's timing requirements. When critique appears — usually within weeks to months of a high-profile publication — it receives substantially less coverage than the original, for structural reasons: corrections are less emotionally compelling, confirm no intuition, and cannot be headlined simply.

The net result is a public knowledge state that lags behind scientific knowledge by months to years for major findings, and in some cases never catches up. A 2020 survey study would need to ask specifically about the hurricane name finding to assess public belief, but media monitoring suggests the original claim is more widely recalled than any of its critiques. The practical implication for fact-checking and science communication is that correction is not simply a matter of publishing the right answer. It requires narrative framing, emotional resonance, and institutional amplification roughly comparable to what the original false finding received — conditions that are structurally difficult to achieve because corrections do not have advocates with the same emotional stake in the finding that the original amplifiers had.

### Publication Bias and the File Drawer Problem

The gender-hurricane paper is one high-profile example of a more systematic problem. Publication bias — the tendency of academic journals to publish positive, statistically significant findings while declining null or negative results — creates a published literature in which effect sizes are systematically inflated and the frequency of published effects exceeds the true underlying prevalence. Simmons, Nelson, and Simonsohn (2011, *Psychological Science*) demonstrated through simulation that standard research practices (flexible sample sizes, selective outcome reporting, covariates) can produce a nominally significant p-value on completely random data with disturbing frequency — a phenomenon they termed "p-hacking" or "researcher degrees of freedom." The Open Science Collaboration (Nosek et al., 2015, *Science*) found that only 36 to 39 percent of 97 psychology studies reproduced the original finding at comparable effect sizes.

Jung et al.'s analysis involved substantial researcher degrees of freedom: the choice of time period, the treatment of outliers, the specification of covariates, and the design of the laboratory experiments all involved judgment calls that could have been made differently and that, when made differently (as subsequent reanalyses showed), produced different results. This does not imply deliberate dishonesty; it reflects the normal operation of scientific research in a publication environment that rewards positive findings. The solution — pre-registration of analysis plans, registered reports, open data to enable independent reanalysis, and explicit accounting for researcher degrees of freedom — is well understood but incompletely implemented.

### Implicit Bias Research: Insights and Overclaims

The gender-hurricane paper belongs to a broader literature on implicit bias whose scientific standing is complicated. The concept of implicit bias — that people hold unconscious associations that influence their behavior even when their explicit attitudes are non-discriminatory — is supported by substantial evidence, primarily from the Implicit Association Test paradigm developed by Greenwald, McGhee, and Schwartz (1998, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*). IAT scores reliably correlate with various behavioral outcomes, including hiring decisions, clinical decision-making, and interpersonal interactions. The claim that gender stereotypes operate implicitly and can produce life-and-death consequences is not inherently implausible.

However, the relationship between IAT scores and actual discriminatory behavior in real-world contexts has proven weaker and more variable than early advocates claimed. Oswald et al. (2013, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) conducted a meta-analysis of the IAT's predictive validity and found effect sizes substantially smaller than widely assumed. More recently, Forscher et al. (2019, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) meta-analyzed interventions designed to reduce implicit bias and found that while many interventions successfully reduced IAT scores, evidence for corresponding reductions in discriminatory behavior was weak. The mechanism by which an IAT-measured implicit association would translate into a behavioral change (failure to evacuate from a hurricane) under conditions of significant threat, official warning, and social coordination is not specified in Jung et al., and the experimental evidence they provide is not adequate to establish it.

This does not mean gender stereotypes are irrelevant to hurricane behavior. It means that the specific causal chain posited in the paper — implicit gender associations → differential risk perception → differential evacuation behavior → differential mortality — is not established by the evidence, and the evidence suggests at least two links in the chain are weaker than assumed.

### The NOAA Hurricane Naming History

The history of hurricane naming is itself instructive about how institutional practices encode cultural assumptions in ways that later generate unintended analytical confounds. The U.S. National Hurricane Center began using female names for storms in 1953, following a practice started informally during World War II when military meteorologists named storms after women (a practice some historians attribute to the influence of a 1941 novel by George Stewart, *Storm*, in which storms are named after women, though direct lineage is uncertain). Male names were added in 1960 in experimental usage and alternated fully from 1979 onward, following pressure from feminist organizations including the National Organization for Women, which had long objected to the exclusively female naming convention.

The feminist objection was precisely that naming dangerous, destructive, and chaotic phenomena exclusively after women was itself an exercise in gendered association — attaching negative connotations to female names. The advocacy succeeded: the naming convention changed to alternating gender. The unintended statistical consequence, four decades later, was that a dataset spanning the full naming history created a confound between "female name" and "historical era" that the Jung et al. analysis failed to adequately address. The feminists who changed the naming convention in 1979 created, inadvertently, the conditions for a 2014 paper to claim that female-named storms were deadlier — a claim whose mechanism reproduced some of the same gender-association logic that motivated the original naming change. The recursive irony is complete.

### Risk Communication and Evacuation Behavior

The behavioral mechanism proposed in Jung et al. — that gender stereotypes attenuate perceived storm threat and reduce evacuation compliance — is a question of real practical importance for emergency managers, regardless of whether the historical mortality data support the specific claim. Risk communication is a mature field with a substantial empirical literature (Fischhoff et al., 1978, *Policy Sciences*; Mileti & Sorensen, 1990 *International Journal of Mass Emergencies and Disasters*) examining the factors that drive evacuation decisions, shelter-in-place behavior, and warning response. This literature finds that evacuation intent and behavior are driven primarily by storm severity cues (forecast track, predicted surge, hurricane category), social cues (whether neighbors are evacuating, whether trusted authorities have issued orders), prior experience with evacuation outcomes, and structural barriers (transportation access, economic resources, housing precarity).

The role of the storm's name in this decision environment is not zero — research on brand names and gendered associations in consumer psychology suggests some potential for name-based framing effects. But the effect size plausible from this literature is small relative to the primary drivers, and it is further attenuated in real hurricane contexts by high-information environments (continuous media coverage, official forecasts with named storm tracks, emergency management communications) that provide ample cues to override any name-based priming. The practical implication for emergency management is clear regardless of the study's flaws: focus communication on intensity measures, storm surge predictions, and evacuation orders from authorities with established credibility, rather than assuming that name assignment exercises significant independent leverage on behavior.

### Science by Press Release and the Role of Institutional Communications

The extraordinary media coverage of Jung et al. (2014) was not entirely organic. The paper was accompanied by an institutional press release from the University of Illinois (one of the authors' institutional affiliations) that highlighted the gender-bias angle prominently. The press release framing "Female Hurricanes Are Deadlier Than Male Hurricanes" was picked up nearly verbatim by wire services and distributed to hundreds of outlets. The role of institutional science communications in shaping media coverage of research findings is substantial and underappreciated: institutional PR offices have strong incentives to generate coverage and strong incentives to frame findings in ways that maximize newsworthiness. Accuracy and epistemic conservativeness are secondary concerns. The result is a systematic selection pressure for findings to be described in more striking, more certain, and more socially resonant terms by PR offices than the underlying research typically warrants.

This is not a malevolent conspiracy. It is a structural outcome of misaligned incentives between scientific epistemology and institutional reputation management. The solutions — more conservative PR practices, greater scientist involvement in science communication, journalist training in interpreting statistical findings, and media organizational policies privileging accuracy over novelty — are well-understood but implemented unevenly across the ecosystem.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The gender-hurricane case represents one of the more challenging categories for contemporary fact-checking: the claim was published in a peer-reviewed journal and was subsequently undermined by published critique, but neither publication nor critique produced a clean resolution of the type that "True/False" verdicts require. Snopes' "Unproven" rating is appropriate and epistemically honest — it reflects the genuine uncertainty in the scientific literature rather than forcing a binary verdict.

But the Snopes treatment, like most fact-check treatments of contested scientific findings, is unable to convey the directionality of the evidence. The claim is not "Unproven" in the sense that we lack evidence. It is "Unproven" in the sense that the evidence that exists is inconsistent with the proposed mechanism and the original finding does not survive methodological scrutiny. This is closer to "Likely False" than to "We don't know." The distinction matters for how readers update their beliefs: "Unproven" invites continued suspension of judgment, while "Likely False as Stated" invites active revision of a memorably misleading claim. Fact-checking frameworks that lack granularity between these states sometimes leave the public in a state of unjustified uncertainty that serves the original false claim as well as no correction at all.

The gender-hurricane case is also a test case for fact-checking timescales. The original finding was published in 2014. Important reanalyses appeared within months. By 2016, the scientific community had substantially revised its view of the finding. If a reader encounters the original claim in 2026, the most relevant fact-check is not the one published in 2014 but one that incorporates a decade of subsequent scientific development — including the post-2012 data extensions that fail to find the predicted effect. Fact-checking organizations face structural incentives to produce timely verdicts on new claims, not to maintain updated assessments of old findings as the scientific record develops. The gender-hurricane case illustrates the need for living fact-checks that are revised as the scientific consensus evolves.
