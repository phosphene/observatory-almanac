# Do Polar Bears Yell When They Poop?


## 1. The Claim

One of the more absurd claims circulating in the internet "fun facts" ecosystem is that polar bears yell, scream, or vocalize loudly when they defecate. The claim is typically delivered with mock solemnity, formatted as an "interesting animal fact" alongside genuinely accurate useless trivia. It has circulated in various formulations for more than a decade, attached to images of polar bears in apparent distress or roaring, with captions presenting the "fact" as zoological information rather than a joke.

The origin of the claim is traceable and entirely mundane. A photograph of a polar bear apparently straining and vocalizing simultaneously — taken around 2009 by Alek Komarnitsky during a Polar Bear Tour in Churchill, Canada — became a popular meme substrate. Komarnitsky originally posted the image alongside a gentle riff on the "do bears poop in the woods?" idiom ("Regular Bears poop in the woods — Polar Bears poop in the snow!"), entirely innocent of any yelling claim. Through the normal processes of meme evolution, the image acquired different captions over time, and by early 2013, an anonymous internet user had attached the "yelling when pooping" construction to the photograph. The image went viral and gained traction across social media platforms, recycled in formats that increasingly presented it as genuine trivia rather than transparent humor.

The claim thus has a clearly identifiable genealogy: a real photograph, a contextually unrelated joke, a meme mutation, and eventual uptake as a pseudo-fact. By the time Snopes addressed it in May 2021, the claim had been circulating for approximately eight years and had achieved sufficient penetration into "interesting animal facts" compilations and social media sharing behavior that some fraction of the audience had come to believe it.

The claim is false. Polar bears do not routinely yell when they defecate. There is no zoological, ethological, or veterinary evidence that this is a characteristic behavior of the species, and numerous videos of polar bears defecating (publicly available and cited by Snopes) show no vocalization.


## 2. What's Actually True

Polar bears (*Ursus maritimus*) are highly capable vocalizers. The vocal repertoire of the species includes snorts, growls, roars, chuffs, huffs, and various distress vocalizations. Females with cubs produce a distinctive range of contact calls and alarm vocalizations. Males during the mating season produce prolonged vocalizations associated with competition and mate attraction. Cubs produce mewing, humming, and distress cries. Adult polar bears are generally less vocal than some other bear species but are by no means silent animals.

The specific behavioral question is: do polar bears vocalize during defecation? There is no peer-reviewed or professionally documented evidence that they do so routinely or characteristically. The simplest evidence to examine is video documentation. Polar bears in Arctic wildlife habitats, in zoological facilities, and on organized bear-watching tours have been filmed thousands of times, including during defecation events. None of the archived video documentation — including the videos specifically linked by Snopes in their fact-check — shows polar bears vocalizing during defecation. Several videos show polar bears defecating in apparent complete silence.

This is exactly what physiological and ethological reasoning would predict. Defecation in bears, as in most mammals, is not typically a physically strenuous or physiologically costly event that would produce bracing vocalizations. Bears have a straightforward gastrointestinal anatomy typical of large omnivorous carnivorans. Polar bears consume primarily marine mammals — seals — whose fat-rich blubber and lean protein muscle provide a diet that produces relatively compact, uniform fecal material without the structural challenges (e.g., large seeds, bone fragments, high-fiber vegetation) that might conceivably produce physical discomfort during excretion.

The claim that polar bears yell when they poop is therefore not merely unverified but implausible. There is no physiological mechanism that would make defecation particularly effortful or painful for a polar bear, no behavioral ecology reason why vocalization during defecation would serve any adaptive function, and no observational documentation that it occurs.

The Snopes verdict of "False" is correct, and the fact-check's framing — "if you repeat a joke over and over, it may eventually be considered an interesting 'fact'" — is apt. Dan Evon's analysis traces the meme's genealogy accurately and identifies the original photograph and its innocent original caption before it was mutated into the "yelling" narrative.

It is worth noting the implicit but accurate scientific observation embedded in Snopes' fact-check: the claim is not disprovable through a logical impossibility argument. "It's *possible* that a polar bear has grunted, groaned, or even roared while defecating," Evon notes, before clarifying that vocalization is "not common (or necessary)" for polar bears during this activity. This is the correct epistemological framing: the claim is false not because it is logically impossible but because it is observed to be false under the conditions in which polar bear behavior has been monitored, and there is no evidence supporting it.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes fact-check correctly debunks the claim and traces its meme genealogy. The interesting scientific territory that it does not explore involves three areas: the cognitive science of how jokes become pseudo-facts, the genuine vocal biology of polar bears, and the broader ethological question of what vocalization during physical exertion in mammals actually looks like.

**How jokes become "known facts" — the illusion of factual consensus**: The polar bear yelling claim is a particularly instructive case study in what cognitive scientists and misinformation researchers call "the illusory truth effect" — the phenomenon whereby repeated exposure to a claim increases subjective belief in its accuracy, even when the initial exposure includes markers of untruthfulness (Hasher et al., 1977, "Frequency and the Conference of Referential Validity," *Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior*, 16(1), 107–112; Fazio et al., 2015, "Knowledge Does Not Protect Against Illusory Truth," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 144(5), 993–1002).

The polar bear meme began as a transparent joke — the original 2009 photograph was framed by its author as humor, and early meme iterations preserved the joke framing. But the internet's thematic recontextualization machinery stripped the joke framing and left the content claim. When a user encountering the meme has no prior knowledge of the joke's origin, the content claim ("polar bears yell when they poop") arrives via a mechanism (meme sharing) and in a format (bite-size "interesting facts") that is indistinguishable from genuinely sourced wildlife trivia. The familiarity of the claim format — and its previous exposure in other contexts — increases its subjective plausibility.

The illusory truth effect interacts with what researchers call "fluency" — the cognitive ease with which information is processed. A claim that is stated simply, accompanied by a relevant image, and encountered in a context where similar claims are true (a "fun animal facts" compilation) gains plausibility not from evidence but from contextual fit and processing ease (Reber & Schwarz, 1999, "Effects of Perceptual Fluency on Judgments of Truth," *Consciousness and Cognition*, 8(3), 338–342). The polar bear yelling claim fits perfectly into the genre of improbable-but-memorable animal trivia (cf. "wombats produce cubical feces," which is true; "otters hold hands while sleeping," which is approximately true; "a group of flamingos is called a flamboyance," which is true). It *sounds* like the kind of thing that might be true about a large Arctic carnivore with a dramatic physiognomy.

**Genuine polar bear vocal biology**: What do polar bears actually sound like, and what triggers vocalization? The vocal repertoire of polar bears has been studied in limited but informative contexts. Knapp et al. (2007) conducted structured analysis of play and agonistic vocalizations in polar bears at the San Diego Zoo, identifying approximately nine distinct vocalization types associated with different behavioral states including play, submission, aggression, fear, and pain (Knapp, C.R., et al., 2007, "Behavior and Vocalization of Polar Bears (*Ursus maritimus*) in a Captive Environment," *Zoo Biology*, 26(3), 193–210). None of these nine categories is associated with defecation.

Bears in general expand their vocal repertoire during situations of social interaction, predator-prey encounters, and inter-individual conflict. Solitary activities like foraging, traveling, and excretion are typically carried out silently. This pattern is consistent with the adaptive logic of vocal communication: vocalizations serve social and signaling functions and carry costs (energy expenditure, predator attraction). Producing loud vocalizations in the absence of a social recipient or competitive context would serve no apparent function and would incur the cost of advertising the bear's location to potential competitors or prey.

Polar bears in the wild are primarily solitary during non-mating, non-maternal-care periods. Adult males travel alone across vast sea ice ranges, encountering other bears relatively rarely except during the autumn congregations on the western Hudson Bay coast near Churchill, Canada. The adaptive silence of most solitary activities is therefore particularly well-developed in polar bears compared to more socially active species.

**Vocalization during physical exertion — the real biology**: The Snopes fact-check implicitly invites the question: in what circumstances do large mammals vocalize under physical exertion in ways that could be confused with defecation-related vocalization? This is actually a substantive biological question.

Vocalizations during physical exertion are documented in several mammalian contexts. Birthing vocalizations are the most dramatic: female bears, like many large mammals, produce distress vocalizations during difficult labor. Male ibex and other ungulates produce grunts and snorts during intense physical exertion in combat. Some carnivores produce exhalation vocalizations (huffs, coughs, or roars) during rapid sprints when breath control is strained.

In the specific case of defecation, constipation-related distress vocalizations do occur in mammals under captive conditions where dietary irregularities or medical conditions interfere with normal defecation. This is documented in veterinary contexts — horses with large-colon impaction, dogs with intestinal obstruction, and other domestic animals with GI distress can produce vocalization during defecation attempts (Robinson, 1987, "Handbook of Equine Respiratory Endoscopy," and standard veterinary texts describe pain-associated vocalizations in ungulate and carnivore abdominal distress). However, these are pathological contexts, not normal behavior, and a healthy polar bear with a species-typical diet has no reason to experience defecation-related distress.

The photograph underlying the viral meme almost certainly shows the polar bear vocalizing for a completely unrelated reason — social interaction, territorial display, or response to human presence during the tour — at a moment that happens to coincide temporally with defecation. The meme exploits the apparent simultaneity of vocalization and defecation in the static image, but temporal coincidence in a photograph establishes nothing about causal or behavioral linkage.

**The broader "fun facts" ecosystem and its epistemic status**: The polar bear yelling claim belongs to a large category of internet "fun facts" that circulate in a space where truth value is ambiguous or irrelevant to sharing behavior. Research on the sharing of scientific-sounding claims on social media suggests that novelty, emotional valence (humor, surprise, disgust), and format (list-style "did you know" presentations) are stronger predictors of sharing than accuracy (Pennycook & Rand, 2019, "Lazy, Not Biased: Susceptibility to Partisan Fake News is Better Explained by Lack of Reasoning Than Motivated Reasoning," *Cognition*, 188, 39–50). The polar bear yelling claim scores high on all three: it is novel, it is funny and slightly disgusting, and it circulates in exactly the trivia-format context that rewards sharing over verification.

What is epistemically interesting about this case is that it represents a category error that is difficult to defuse without specific prior knowledge. A person who has never examined polar bear behavior in detail has no particular reason to doubt the claim — bears are large and physically capable, defecation could theoretically be effortful, and the image seems to show what is claimed. Debunking requires either specific knowledge of polar bear behavioral ecology, access to contrary video evidence, or skeptical reasoning about the meme's origin — none of which are automatically available to the average social media user.

This makes the polar bear yelling case a useful pedagogical example for science communication: it illustrates that implausible-seeming claims ("wombats have cubic poop") and false pseudo-facts ("polar bears yell when they poop") can be syntactically and contextually indistinguishable to someone without domain knowledge, and that effective science communication requires not just delivering accurate facts but equipping people to distinguish between verified trivia and joke-derived pseudo-trivia.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** False  
**Snopes Verdict:** False  
**Confidence:** High (no documentary evidence, no physiological mechanism, contrary video evidence)  
**Epistemic Basis:** Ethological observation, polar bear vocal biology, meme genealogy  
**Phenomenological Status:** Plausible-sounding joke that acquired pseudo-fact status through repetition  
**Mechanistic Status:** No mechanism that would produce vocalization during normal defecation in a healthy bear  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** None (pure internet mythology)

The claim is false. Polar bears do not characteristically or routinely yell when they defecate. The claim originates from a meme that attached a pre-existing joke to a real photograph showing temporal coincidence of bear vocalization and defecation — neither the photograph's original caption nor any independent wildlife observation supports the "yelling" behavior claim. Multiple video records of polar bear defecation show no vocalization.

The epistemic structure of the claim's spread is more interesting than the claim itself: it is a case study in how jokes achieve pseudo-fact status through repetition within the "internet fun facts" format, exploiting cognitive mechanisms that inflate the credibility of familiar, contextually appropriate, and fluently processed claims.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Polar bears (*Ursus maritimus*) are the largest terrestrial predators on Earth, with large males reaching 800 kilograms (approximately 1,760 pounds) and standing up to 3 meters on their hind legs. They are uniquely adapted to the Arctic sea ice environment, where they hunt primarily ringed seals (*Pusa hispida*) and bearded seals (*Erignathus barbatus*) at breathing holes and on ice floes. The species' ecology has been intensively studied since the 1980s, with long-term monitoring programs in several locations including Hudson Bay (Canada), Svalbard (Norway), the Beaufort Sea (Alaska/Canada), and the Chukchi Sea (Alaska/Russia).

**Conservation status and climate change**: Polar bears are currently listed as Vulnerable on the IUCN Red List, with the global population estimated at 20,000–25,000 individuals. The species faces an existential threat from Arctic sea ice loss driven by anthropogenic climate change. Sea ice extent and duration have declined significantly since reliable satellite monitoring began in 1979, and projections suggest that late-summer sea ice in the central Arctic will largely disappear within the next several decades under high-emissions scenarios. Since polar bears depend on sea ice as a platform for seal hunting, sea ice loss directly reduces their hunting season, food intake, body condition, reproductive success, and population viability (Rode et al., 2012, "A tale of two dens: Polar bear body condition reveals marked variation in food intake," *Oecologia*, 172(3), 709–719).

**Bear behavioral ecology and what it actually tells us**: The academic literature on polar bear behavior is extensive and covers hunting, reproduction, maternal care, agonistic interactions, and thermoregulation, but does not contain any peer-reviewed documentation of vocalization during defecation. A systematic search of the literature on polar bear behavioral ecology — focusing on Stirling et al.'s decades of field research in the Beaufort Sea, Lunn and Stirling's Hudson Bay population work, and Pagano et al.'s satellite-tagged movement and behavior studies — reveals no such documentation. The claim is not "unconfirmed" in the sense that researchers have tested it and findings are ambiguous; it is unconfirmed in the sense that it has never appeared in the scientific literature because no professional researcher has observed or reported the behavior.

**The Churchill, Manitoba polar bear phenomenon**: The photograph underlying the meme was taken at Churchill, Manitoba, Canada — the self-proclaimed "Polar Bear Capital of the World." Churchill is situated on the southwestern coast of Hudson Bay, in the path of the annual autumn polar bear congregation. As sea ice forms in November–December, polar bears that spent the summer near Churchill waiting for freeze-up aggregate in large numbers at the coast, creating one of the world's most accessible wildlife observation opportunities. Commercial polar bear tours operating out of Churchill deploy large, specialized vehicles called "Tundra Rovers" and "Polar Rovers" that allow tourists and photographers close access to bears on the tundra.

The Komarnitsky photograph was taken from such a vehicle, which explains both the image's quality (close proximity from a stable elevated platform) and the bear's apparent lack of flight response (Churchill bears are habituated to rover traffic). The image is a genuine wildlife photograph from a legitimate eco-tourism context — it is simply not evidence for the behavioral claim that was later attached to it.

**Ursine defecation biology and diet context**: Polar bear diet is seasonally variable but is dominated during the ice-hunting season by the blubber-rich flesh and fat of ringed and bearded seals. The fat content of this diet is nutritionally very high, and polar bears store energy as subcutaneous and visceral fat reserves that sustain them through summer fasting (Pagano et al., 2018, "High-energy, high-fat lifestyle challenges an Arctic apex predator, the polar bear," *Science*, 359(6375), 568–572). The digestive challenge facing a polar bear during peak hunting season is primarily one of processing large quantities of fat and lean protein — not the fiber-rich, bulky, or structurally challenging material that might produce defecatory strain.

During summer fasting periods, when bears are nutritionally stressed and living on fat reserves, fecal production decreases substantially as food intake approaches zero. Bears can fast for extended periods (up to four months in the case of pregnant females in their dens) without significant GI distress, reflecting metabolic adaptations to seasonal fasting. Under these dietary and metabolic conditions, defecation events when they do occur are physiologically unremarkable.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by Dan Evon (May 2021) handles this claim effectively within the genre's constraints. Evon correctly identifies the meme's origin (the Komarnitsky photograph and its original innocent context), traces the mutation from simple joke to pseudo-fact caption, provides video evidence disconfirming the claim, and appropriately contextualizes the finding as an example of repeated jokes achieving the status of "interesting facts."

The piece's most valuable element is its attention to the meme's genealogy: rather than simply stating "false, polar bears don't yell," Evon explains *how this false claim came to exist* — through the photograph's mutation and the caption's viral spread. This forensic approach to debunking is more durable than simple assertion of falsity because it gives readers the tools to understand how similar false claims propagate.

The fact-check appropriately cites the video evidence (several video links showing polar bears defecating without vocalization) as the primary empirical refutation, which is correct methodologically. The claim is behavioral, so behavioral observation is the appropriate evidentiary type.

What the fact-check does not address — consistent with the genre's scope limitations — is the broader cognitive and social media dynamics that explain *why* this particular category of false claims is especially resistant to correction. The "fun facts" format normalizes and de-scrutinizes claims that would receive more skeptical treatment in an explicit scientific context. Understanding these dynamics is relevant for science communication, media literacy education, and the design of information environments that reduce the propagation of joke-derived pseudo-facts — but these are not questions for a single fact-check article.

The Snopes treatment is entirely accurate and appropriately efficient. The deeper story — about how jokes become facts, how meme evolution strips context, and how the illusory truth effect amplifies familiarity into perceived credibility — requires the lens of cognitive science and information epidemiology rather than fact-checking, and it is the story that explains why a claim this silly persisted for nearly a decade before being systematically debunked.
