# Throwing rocks into the ocean contributes to sea level rise


## 1. The Claim

A recurring piece of pop-physics reasoning holds that throwing rocks into the ocean literally contributes to sea level rise, because the rocks displace water. The argument is typically presented as a counterintuitive insight: climate activists and journalists focus on ice melt, thermal expansion, and greenhouse gas emissions as the drivers of sea level rise, but anyone can add to the sea level simply by dropping a stone off a pier. In some iterations the claim is presented humorously — "technically everything you throw in the ocean raises sea levels" — while in others it is deployed as a genuinely intended gotcha against climate science: "if your garbage raises sea levels, why do you blame CO₂?"

The physics framing invokes Archimedes' principle, which states that an object immersed in a fluid displaces a volume of fluid equal to the object's own volume. A rock thrown into the ocean occupies space that was previously occupied by water. The water must go somewhere; it cannot compress significantly. Therefore, the water is pushed outward and upward. Therefore, sea level rises. The logic is clean, the principle is real, and the conclusion is technically correct — at some level of precision.

The claim also circulates in more serious educational contexts. Popular science communicators have used it to illustrate how Archimedes' displacement works interactively with sea level. Physics teachers have posed it as a thought experiment. A variant involving very large-scale rock addition — imagining the entire continent of Africa being moved into the ocean, or all of Mount Everest being dropped in — scales the thought experiment up to make the displacement intuitive. These educational uses are reasonable and do not misrepresent the physics.

The more problematic use is the implicit or explicit rhetorical move: "if anything can raise sea level, then raising sea level is meaningless as a concern specific to climate change." This is a category conflation between physical truism and policy-relevant scale, deployed to produce doubt about the significance of anthropogenic sea level change. Whether this deployment is intentional or arrives organically through social transmission of a physics joke is not always clear, but the rhetorical effect is to muddy rather than clarify.

A further complicating dimension is the claim's use as a reductio in conversations about waste. "Every piece of plastic you throw in the ocean contributes to sea level rise" is sometimes found in environmental communication, using technically correct physics to mobilise concern about ocean pollution. Here the same logical structure — displacement of water — is deployed in the opposite rhetorical direction: not to diminish climate concern but to expand it. The underlying physics is identical in both cases. What differs is which aspects of the claim's meaning are emphasised and which are suppressed.

Whether the claim is felt as innocuous physics trivia, educational illustration, policy deflection, or environmental communication depends almost entirely on context and framing. Understanding it requires taking the physics seriously but then asking what it actually means at the relevant scales, and what additional physical principles complicate the simple displacement story.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Archimedes' principle applies and displacement is real.** A rock thrown into the ocean does displace water, and in the most precise theoretical sense this incrementally raises the mean water level of the global ocean. The global ocean basin is not a closed rigid container: its boundaries are the world's coastlines, and any addition of volume to the ocean system pushes water against those boundaries. The Archimedes argument is not wrong as physics; it is wrong as a claim about meaningful contribution to the phenomenon known as sea level rise.

**The scale discrepancy is enormous.** Church & White (2011, *Surveys in Geophysics*) provide a comprehensive analysis of global mean sea level change since 1880, finding a total rise of approximately 195 mm (roughly 19.5 centimetres) over the twentieth century and an accelerating trend in the early twenty-first century reaching approximately 3.2 mm per year. Nerem et al. (2018, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) demonstrated, using satellite altimetry data from the TOPEX/Poseidon, Jason-1, Jason-2, and Jason-3 missions, that global mean sea level rise has been accelerating and that the rate has increased from approximately 1.8 mm/year in the early twentieth century to over 3 mm/year in the 2010s, with continued acceleration projected. Approximately half of this rise comes from the thermal expansion of ocean water as it warms (thermosteric sea level rise) and approximately half from the addition of mass via ice melt from glaciers and ice sheets (barystatic sea level rise) (Church et al., 2013, *Climate Change 2013: The Physical Science Basis*, IPCC Working Group I).

A typical large rock might weigh 10 kilograms. With a density of approximately 2,700 kg/m³ (typical for granite), this rock occupies a volume of approximately 3.7 litres, or 0.0037 cubic metres. The global ocean surface area is approximately 361 million square kilometres, or 3.61 × 10¹⁴ square metres. By Archimedes' principle, adding this rock underneath the sea surface raises global mean sea level by approximately 0.0037/361,000,000,000,000 = approximately 10⁻²⁰ metres, or approximately 0.01 attometres. This is roughly 10 billion times smaller than the diameter of a hydrogen atom. The universe is approximately 13.8 billion years old; at the rate rocks would need to be thrown to produce 1 millimetre of sea level rise, the time required would far exceed the age of the universe. The scale comparison is not a rhetorical flourish; it is the physically relevant context within which the "contribution" operates.

**The displacement analysis is more complex than Archimedes' principle alone captures.** The full story requires asking where the rock was before it entered the ocean. Archimedes' principle describes what happens when a foreign object displaces water in a fluid system. But if the rock was previously sitting above sea level on land, then its mass was already gravitationally affecting the Earth-ocean system. Land-based mass exerts a gravitational pull on nearby ocean water, creating local sea level elevations near high-mass land bodies. This effect is described by the gravitational-rotational-deformational (GRD) component of sea level change and has been described in detail by Mitrovica et al. (2001, *Nature*), who showed that the gravitational fingerprint of ice sheet mass loss causes sea level changes that are spatially non-uniform in counterintuitive ways — sea levels fall near melting ice sheets and rise more strongly than the global mean at distant coastlines precisely because of gravitational and isostatic effects. A rock on land near a coastline contributes fractionally to this gravitational pull. When it is moved into the ocean, it stops contributing its tiny gravitational pull to the land system and begins displacing water. Whether sea level at a specific distant coastline goes up or down depends on the precise geometry — though both changes are orders of magnitude below detection thresholds.

**The question of whether Earth materials enter the ocean continuously is also real.** Rivers carry billions of tonnes of sediment into the ocean annually. Milliman & Farnsworth (2011, *River Discharge to the Coastal Ocean: A Global Synthesis*) estimate global fluvial sediment discharge at approximately 18 billion metric tonnes per year before accounting for reservoir trapping. This natural rock-to-ocean transfer is enormous compared to any humanly plausible rock-throwing activity. The addition of terrestrial sediment to the ocean has historically led to delta progradation — coastlines advancing outward as sediment deposits accumulate — which is in some physical sense the inverse of sea level rise at those locations. Modern dam construction has reduced sediment delivery by approximately 25-30% globally (Walling, 2006, *Geomorphology*), contributing to shoreline erosion and effective relative sea level rise in delta regions, in a process that is the opposite of the rock-throwing scenario. The naturally occurring version of "rocks going into water" has historically fought sea level rise rather than contributing to it, at least at shoreline scales.

**Thermal expansion and ice melt are not commensurable with rock displacement.** The dominant mechanisms of current sea level rise — thermosteric expansion and barystatic input from land ice — are thermodynamic processes driven by energy accumulation in the climate system. The ocean is currently absorbing the vast majority of the excess heat being retained by the atmosphere due to elevated greenhouse gas concentrations. Cheng et al. (2020, *Advances in Atmospheric Sciences*) found that the ocean had absorbed 228 × 10²² joules of heat between 1955 and 2019, producing measurable expansion. This process is ongoing, self-amplifying, and driven by a physical forcing (radiative imbalance) that will continue unless atmospheric CO₂ concentrations fall. Rock displacement is a one-time volume addition without physical forcing; thermal expansion is continuous heating with ongoing radiative drivers. The phenomena belong to entirely different physical categories. "Contributing" framing implies commensurability that does not exist.


## 3. Why People Believe This

This claim works as a persuasive device, even for people who understand the basic physics, because it exploits a structural feature of how humans evaluate comparative magnitude. The formal structure of the argument — displacement of water occurs; sea level is affected — is correct. The problem is that evaluating whether a genuinely true physical statement constitutes a meaningful "contribution" to a phenomenon requires quantitative comparison across scales that are not intuitive. Humans are systematically poor at intuitive comparison across extreme scale differences; the difference between a contribution of 10⁻²⁰ metres and 3 millimetres per year does not feel like a meaningful distinction in the same way that the difference between 1 kilometre and 2 kilometres does.

**The scale fallacy operates as motivated reasoning protection.** Once a listener accepts that any rock-throwing "contributes" to sea level rise, they have access to a schema in which the scale of contribution is merely quantitative rather than categorical. From here, the tacit inference is: "well, lots of things contribute at small scales, so singling out CO₂ as the cause is arbitrary." This inference is structurally invalid — the scale difference between rock displacement and greenhouse-driven thermal expansion is genuinely categorical in the sense that human civilisation has no realistic capacity to address sea level rise by not throwing rocks, while greenhouse gas reduction is a feasible mechanism — but the invalid inference is available from the technically-true claim. The claim functions as a permission structure for dismissing climate-specific concern without making an explicitly false statement.

**Archimedes' principle is vivid and satisfying.** Physical principles that explain something directly visible in everyday experience have a quality of obviousness that makes them satisfying to apply. Archimedes' principle explains something everyone has observed: dropped objects in water displace the water. The step from "I've seen displacement" to "therefore displacement applies globally" is short, intuitive, and formally correct. The further step to "therefore my rock contributes to sea level rise" preserves the Archimedean structure while losing the scale context that makes the global claim meaningless. The satisfaction of the formal derivation conceals the loss of scale context — a common mechanism by which technically correct reasoning generates meaningless conclusions.

**The claim trades on first-principles appeals.** Climate skepticism in its sophisticated forms often takes the structure of first-principles physical derivations that challenge consensus claims not by denying them outright but by appearing to demonstrate internal tensions or undermining axioms. The rock-throwing claim participates in this rhetorical form: it appears to derive a climate skeptic-friendly conclusion (sea level rise is not unique to climate forcing) from unimpeachable physics (Archimedes' principle). This form is more resistant to debunking than flat denial, because it requires showing that the derivation leads somewhere practically irrelevant — which is a longer and more effortful argument than simply citing a contrary source.

**The educational and comic context makes critical distance harder.** Because the claim also appears in legitimate educational and comic contexts — science teachers illustrate displacement, comedians observe that technically you're contributing to sea level rise every time you take a bath — encountering it does not automatically trigger the critical scrutiny that flagged misinformation would. People have positive associations with the claim from prior encounters in non-adversarial contexts that reduce their readiness to engage skeptically when it appears in a policy context.


## 4. Verdict

**TECHNICALLY TRUE — practically meaningless — potentially misleading at scale.** The statement that throwing rocks into the ocean contributes to sea level rise is physically correct in the most precise technical sense: displacement of water is real, and any volume addition to the ocean incrementally raises global mean sea level. The contribution of a typical rock is approximately 10⁻²⁰ metres — roughly ten billion times smaller than the diameter of a hydrogen atom — against a background of climate-driven sea level rise of approximately 3–4 millimetres per year and accelerating.

The claim is distorted-but-grounded in that it draws on genuine physics but applies "contribution" in a way that suppresses the quantitative context that would make the claim meaningful. A contribution at 10⁻²⁰ scale to a phenomenon driven at millimetre-per-year scales by entirely different physical processes does not constitute a "contribution" in any policy-relevant sense of that word. The physical truth is real; the implied equivalence to climate-driven factors is not.

The claim additionally omits the complexity introduced by gravitational-rotational-deformational effects (land-based mass affects local sea level), the sediment cycle (natural rock addition to the ocean has historically offset erosion and sea level change in delta regions), and the thermodynamic character of thermal expansion as a process driven by continuous forcing rather than one-time displacement.


## 5. The Wider Picture

This claim belongs to a family of technically-correct-but-misleading arguments that have played a recurring role in public science communication, particularly in contexts where scientific consensus has clear policy implications. The structure — derive a correct conclusion from genuine physics, then allow that conclusion to imply a sceptical framing — appears in arguments about CO₂'s small atmospheric concentration (0.04% seems tiny, therefore CO₂ forcing is small), about human emissions relative to natural volcanic emissions, and about the small absolute warming from a given doubling of atmospheric CO₂ if considered without feedback processes. In each case, the physical claim is technically correct and the implied conclusion that it undermines climate concern is not.

Understanding this pattern requires a kind of epistemic vigilance distinct from ordinary fact-checking: not "is this statement true" but "does the amount of truth in this statement support the inferences it appears to licence?" This is a harder question and requires contextual quantitative reasoning that most members of the public do not have the domain-specific knowledge to apply quickly. The inferential gap between a technically true statement and a policy-relevant conclusion is where much of the most damaging scientific misinformation operates.

Oreskes & Conway (2010, *Merchants of Doubt*, Bloomsbury) documented how technically-grounded but misleading claims about climate science were systematically deployed in public communication by well-resourced groups seeking to delay regulatory responses. The rock-throwing claim does not appear in their account — it is too peripheral — but the rhetorical structure they describe maps clearly onto the logic of the displacement argument. Creating the impression of scientific debate or internal contradiction through selective application of genuine science principles is a documented strategy in the history of science communication, and the formal structure of rock-throwing argument participates in it whether or not any specific instance of the claim is deployed intentionally.

The natural sediment system is also a valuable context for this claim. Rivers carry approximately 18 billion tonnes of sediment to the ocean annually, constituting an enormous natural rock-to-ocean transfer. This material does not meaningfully contribute to sea level rise; it contributes to delta formation and has historically been the mechanism by which major river deltas have grown into the sea. The interruption of natural sediment transport by dam construction, which has reduced fluvial sediment delivery by approximately 25-30%, is one of the drivers of delta erosion and effective relative sea level rise in delta cities including Ho Chi Minh City, the Nile Delta, and the Mississippi Delta. The "natural rocks in ocean" version of this scenario is therefore, if anything, sea-level-stabilising rather than sea-level-raising — a nuance that the rock-throwing claim does not engage with.

Physical intuition about displacement is a genuine contribution to scientific literacy when it is connected to quantitative scale and physical context. Archimedes' principle is important, useful, and worth understanding. The displacement thought experiment is a valid way to introduce it. The step from displacement principle to sea level rise claim becomes misleading only when it loses the scale context that determines whether the displacement is meaningful relative to the phenomenon under discussion. Maintaining that context — keeping scale present as an explicit part of physical reasoning — is one of the most important skills in science literacy and one that is systematically undertaught in contexts where vivid physical principles are introduced without quantitative grounding.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim sits in an unusual position in the fact-checking ecosystem: it is technically true and presents no clear false statement that can be rated. Standard fact-checking verdicts — True, False, Misleading, Unproven — map poorly onto a claim whose physical content is correct and whose problem is the scale and inferential context in which it is deployed. Some fact-checking organisations have partial tools for this: PolitiFact's "Mostly True" or "Half True" ratings, Snopes's "Correct Attribution / Mostly False" split verdicts, and similar contextualised ratings acknowledge that a claim can be technically accurate while being substantially misleading. The rock-raising-sea-levels claim would fit the "technically true but missing important context" category in most rubrics that have such a category.

The deeper challenge the claim poses for fact-checkers is one of completeness versus efficiency. A thorough evaluation requires explaining Archimedes' principle, working through the scale arithmetic, explaining the difference between displacement and thermosteric/barystatic mechanisms, acknowledging the claim's legitimate uses in physics education, and distinguishing the educational version from the policy-deflection version. This is a 2,000-word explanation for a claim that was transmitted in one sentence. Fact-checking organisations operating in a high-volume, attention-scarce media environment face structural incentives against producing this kind of deep contextual evaluation.

Science communicators and media literacy educators who encounter this claim in the wild should centre two questions: What is the implied significance of the contribution — i.e., does the person making the claim believe it implies that human rock-throwing is a relevant driver of sea level rise? And is scale context present in the claim as stated? If a claim about sea level uses technically correct physics to imply that climate-driven factors are no more significant than rock displacement, the claim is misleading regardless of the formal correctness of the displacement physics. If it is used to illustrate Archimedes' principle in an educational context with clear scale context, the same physical content is informative rather than misleading. The evaluation that matters is not whether the physics is correct but whether the framing accurately represents the quantitative relationship between the described effect and the phenomenon it implies relevance to.

Teaching people to ask "how much does this actually contribute relative to the thing we are discussing?" is a durable media literacy skill that applies far beyond this particular claim. Scale reasoning — developing intuition for orders of magnitude and the conditions under which a "contribution" is meaningful versus negligible — is one of the most widely applicable tools for navigating quantitative scientific claims in public discourse.
