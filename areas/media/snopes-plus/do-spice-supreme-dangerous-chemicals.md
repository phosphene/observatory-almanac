# "Spice Supreme Brand Spices Contain Dangerous Chemicals"


## 1. The Claim

In late April 2018, a photograph began circulating rapidly across Facebook. It showed a retail shelf stocked with Spice Supreme brand seasoning products—a widely distributed budget spice line sold at discount stores across the United States—accompanied by a prominent card reading: "Spice Supreme spices contain chemicals known to the State of California to cause cancer and birth defects or other reproductive harm."

The post generated significant alarm. Replies ranged from immediate declarations that the product would never enter the household again, to questions about whether it had already harmed people who had used it for years, to more sceptical voices wondering whether the warning was legally meaningful or simply a precautionary legal response. The photograph spread well beyond the initial California grocery context, reaching audiences nationally and internationally who had no immediate access to the specific retailer or the specific regulatory framework the warning referenced.

The plain-language reading of the accompanying card—particularly the phrase "chemicals known to cause cancer"—was terrifying to many consumers and not obviously limited in scope. The implicit question the image raised was simple and alarming: Does Spice Supreme contain chemicals that will hurt me? Do its products cause cancer? And why, if this is known, is the product still on sale?

Snopes received enough reader queries about the photograph to treat it as requiring a formal investigation, publishing their assessment on April 30, 2018. Their conclusion—that the claim was "unproven," not "false"—reflected the genuinely complicated evidentiary situation the image inhabited.


## 2. What's Actually True

The warning sign in the viral photograph was almost certainly a Proposition 65 compliance measure. Understanding what that means requires understanding Proposition 65—formally, the Safe Drinking Water and Toxic Enforcement Act of 1986—as a piece of California consumer protection legislation with some unusual features.

Proposition 65 requires California businesses to provide "clear and reasonable" warnings before knowingly exposing anyone to a substance the state has identified as potentially causing cancer, birth defects, or reproductive harm. The list of such substances, maintained by the California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment (OEHHA), currently includes over 900 chemicals. The list functions as a precautionary catalogue: a substance's presence on the list does not mean that normal consumer exposure necessarily poses significant risk—it means California has determined the substance has some demonstrated carcinogenic or reproductive toxicity potential. Many common substances appear, including coffee (added and later removed based on further analysis), alcohol, acrylamide (formed when starchy foods are cooked at high temperatures), and a range of industrial chemicals.

A distinctive feature of Proposition 65 is its enforcement mechanism: private citizens and advocacy organisations can sue on behalf of the public over Proposition 65 violations, without needing to demonstrate actual harm. Businesses face a statutory penalty of up to $2,500 per day per violation and must reform their labelling or reduce exposure to below threshold levels to settle.

In the case of Spice Supreme, the warning sign was not a government regulatory determination—it was a retailer's response to legal pressure in an unresolved litigation process. Here is what was actually established by the time the photograph went viral:

**The 2016 Recall** — In July 2016, the US Food and Drug Administration announced a voluntary recall by Gel Spice, Inc.—the parent company of the Spice Supreme brand—of one specific lot of turmeric powder sold under the Fresh Finds brand. The recall followed routine sampling by New York State Department of Agriculture and Markets food inspectors, whose analysis showed elevated lead levels in that batch. The recall was subsequently expanded to encompass all Gel Spice turmeric products across its brand portfolio, which included Spice Supreme branded turmeric.

Lead in turmeric is a documented problem in the global spice supply chain. A subsequent investigation by Consumer Reports and peer-reviewed research in academic food-safety journals identified a supply chain contamination pattern: turmeric roots are sometimes intentionally coated with lead chromate (a bright yellow pigment) in South Asian processing facilities to enhance the visual vibrancy of the finished product. This practice adds lead at the source before the turmeric reaches international distributors. The problem is not unique to Gel Spice—it has affected multiple brands and supply chains.

**The Class Action Lawsuit** — Following the recall, a class action complaint was filed against Gel Spice Co. in the Northern District of California in December 2016, alleging that the company had sold turmeric with toxic lead levels in violation of federal and state law, and had failed to provide the Proposition 65 warnings required by California law.

**The Consumer Advocacy Group Litigation** — The Consumer Advocacy Group, Inc. (CAG), a California-based organisation that files private Proposition 65 enforcement actions, sent multiple 60-day notices of intent to sue to Gel Spice and the retailers stocking their products. A 60-day notice is a legally required pre-litigation step under Proposition 65—it gives potential defendants time to remedy their compliance or negotiate a settlement before formal lawsuits are filed. CAG's 2016 notices targeted specific Gel Spice products (cinnamon in a July 2016 notice, cumin in August 2016). The April 2018 notices, filed just days before the viral photograph appeared, targeted "ground spices containing lead and lead compounds" more broadly—suggesting the entire Spice Supreme product line rather than specific ingredients.

Critically—and this is what made the Snopes investigation genuinely difficult—the scientific data underlying the April 2018 CAG notices had not been made public. When Snopes reporter Alex Kasprak contacted the attorney representing CAG, he confirmed that the organisation's lead testing had been conducted by registered laboratories, but that test results were sealed pending litigation outcome. Neither the methodology, the specific concentrations found, nor the products tested were publicly available. The warning signs appearing next to Spice Supreme products in California retailers were thus a retailer's pre-litigation compliance response to a legal notice based on data nobody outside the litigation had seen.

What was established: a documented recall for elevated lead in Gel Spice turmeric products, a class action lawsuit, and active Proposition 65 litigation based on private laboratory testing. What was not established: the specific lead concentrations in the broader Spice Supreme product range, whether those concentrations exceeded safe exposure thresholds, and whether any products other than turmeric posed a demonstrated risk.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The viral photograph was alarming because it looked like official, authoritative information. Consumers are accustomed to safety warnings on products reflecting government determinations—warnings about allergens, pharmaceutical side effects, electrical hazards. When a warning sign in a shop says "contains chemicals known to cause cancer," the natural reading is: a government body has tested this product and found it dangerous.

That reading is not entirely unreasonable as a heuristic. Many Proposition 65 warnings do ultimately reflect government-determined health risks. But the specific California regulatory framework works differently from most food safety warnings consumers are familiar with. Proposition 65 warnings can be placed on products by retailers acting proactively in response to litigation threats—before any court determination, before any confirmed public test data, and sometimes even before the 60-day notice period has resolved into a formal lawsuit. The warning in the viral photograph may have reflected exactly this kind of pre-emptive compliance action.

There is also a genuine underlying concern that makes the claim credible even to careful readers: lead contamination in spices is a real and documented food safety problem, not a fabricated one. The 2016 FDA recall of Gel Spice turmeric products was real. Lead in turmeric has been identified as a supply chain problem by academic researchers and consumer advocacy organisations. The class action lawsuit was real. For a budget-priced spice brand primarily serving lower-income consumers—the demographic most likely to be shopping at Dollar Tree and similar discount retailers where Spice Supreme products are stocked—the contamination concern echoes broader patterns of environmental injustice in which cheaper products sometimes carry disproportionate safety risks.

The alarm also reflects accurate intuition about the general toxicology of lead. Unlike many substances that generate Proposition 65 warnings on precautionary grounds, lead is a well-established heavy metal toxin with no known safe level of exposure. Any credible evidence of elevated lead in a commonly consumed food product is legitimately alarming. The question is not whether lead is dangerous—it unambiguously is, particularly for children and pregnant women—but whether Spice Supreme products in the broader range contained lead at levels posing meaningful risk.


## 4. Verdict

**UNPROVEN** (Snopes rating): The Snopes assessment of this claim as "unproven" rather than "true" or "false" is carefully calibrated to the actual evidentiary situation, and it remains accurate.

What was genuinely established: Gel Spice, Inc., the parent company of Spice Supreme, issued FDA-confirmed recalls in 2016 for elevated lead in turmeric products. Class action litigation followed. Active Proposition 65 litigation based on private laboratory testing was underway in 2018 when the photograph went viral, and the warning signs in California stores reflected retailer compliance responses to that litigation.

What remained genuinely unresolved: the specific lead concentrations across the full Spice Supreme range, whether products beyond turmeric were contaminated, and whether any contamination exceeded legally meaningful thresholds beyond California's unusually precautionary Proposition 65 limits.

This is not a "nothing to see here" verdict. The underlying lead contamination concern—documented for the turmeric products, plausibly extending to other ground spices—is a legitimate food safety issue. But the claim as circulated—"Spice Supreme spices contain dangerous chemicals"—went beyond what was publicly established at the time, and consumers who saw the viral photograph and concluded that every Spice Supreme product had been confirmed dangerous by government testing were drawing conclusions the evidence did not yet support.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Spice Supreme incident illuminates several intersecting issues in food safety, consumer protection law, environmental justice, and the communication of risk.

**Lead in Spices: A Systemic Problem**

The specific contamination mechanism identified in the 2016 Gel Spice recalls—lead chromate adulteration in the turmeric supply chain—is not an isolated incident specific to one company. A 2019 study published in the journal Environmental Research, led by researchers at Stanford University, identified lead-contaminated turmeric as a significant contributor to blood lead levels in Bangladesh. The researchers confirmed that lead chromate was being deliberately added during turmeric processing to enhance the visual appearance of the product. Subsequent research has identified similar practices at multiple points in the South Asian and South American spice supply chains.

The problem is compounded by detection challenges: spice fraud and adulteration frequently involve adding materials that are visually indistinguishable from the legitimate product, making contamination difficult to detect without chemical testing. The FDA's spice safety programme has limited sampling capacity relative to the volume of imported spice products entering the US market annually.

**Proposition 65 and Risk Communication**

Proposition 65 warnings have a complex public health legacy. The law's advocates argue that its precautionary approach—erring on the side of warning—gives consumers meaningful information and creates market pressure for manufacturers to reduce chemical exposures. Its critics argue that the prevalence of Proposition 65 warnings across an enormous range of ordinary products has generated warning fatigue, training consumers to ignore the labels and therefore defeating their informational purpose.

Both concerns have merit. Studies on warning labels generally find that their effectiveness depends on specificity and novelty: warnings that distinguish between significant and minimal risks are more useful than uniform warnings applied at every level. Proposition 65 does not currently distinguish in its consumer-facing labels between a substance present at levels orders of magnitude above any plausible threshold for harm and a substance present at the regulatory limit with minimal confirmed risk at those concentrations.

For the Spice Supreme situation, this meant that a warning sign generated by pre-litigation compliance activity—before any confirmed public data—looked identical to a warning that might follow a government determination of significant risk. The visual language of consumer safety warnings was doing multiple types of work simultaneously without indicating which type applied.

**Environmental Justice Dimensions**

Spice Supreme and its parent brand portfolio are primarily sold at discount retailers and dollar stores, serving lower-income consumers. This demographic pattern is not incidental. Research on food safety and environmental contamination consistently shows that lower-income communities face higher exposures to food and environmental contaminants, partly because budget-priced products sometimes substitute cheaper ingredients or receive less rigorous quality oversight, and partly because lower-income communities have less access to the premium-priced alternatives that sometimes (though not always) carry lower contamination risks.

The Spice Supreme situation echoes concerns raised about lead paint in affordable housing, about environmental pollution near industrial facilities in low-income areas, and about pesticide residues in produce marketed to price-sensitive consumers. The food safety system's reliance on voluntary recalls, private litigation under Proposition 65, and limited FDA sampling capacity creates a regulatory landscape that places the burden of identifying contamination frequently on watchdog organisations and class-action attorneys rather than on systematic government surveillance.

**What Happened After the Viral Moment**

The litigation initiated by Consumer Advocacy Group ultimately proceeded through the California courts. The specific terms of any settlement or court outcome are not fully in the public record at the time of this writing, and the underlying laboratory data that triggered the 2018 notices was not released publicly during the period covered by the Snopes investigation. Continued vigilance in the spice supply chain—across all major manufacturers, not only Gel Spice—remains warranted based on the documented existence of lead adulteration as a supply chain practice.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published a formal assessment on April 30, 2018, written by investigative journalist Alex Kasprak. The assessment rated the specific claim—that Spice Supreme products "contain dangerous chemicals"—as "Unproven." The rating was based on the finding that while the underlying litigation and concern were real, the specific data supporting the broader contamination allegation had not been made public and had not been verified by any independent testing that Snopes could access.

Kasprak's reporting was careful to distinguish between what was confirmed (the 2016 turmeric recall; the class action lawsuit; the active Proposition 65 litigation) and what remained genuinely contested (whether the broader product range was contaminated; what concentrations the private laboratory testing had found; whether any proven contamination exceeded meaningful safety thresholds). This distinction is precisely the kind of careful evidentiary reasoning that substantive fact-checking requires and that the viral photograph's plain-language framing entirely elided.

The "Unproven" rating reflects Snopes' well-established taxonomy for claims where the evidence is inadequate to reach a definitive conclusion in either direction. It is worth noting that "Unproven" is not a vindication and is not equivalent to "False." The rating means: the concern may be justified, but the evidence does not yet allow confirmation. In this case, the legitimate underlying concern—lead contamination in Gel Spice turmeric products—was already confirmed by FDA recall; what remained unproven was the broader claim about the full Spice Supreme range.

Other fact-checkers do not appear to have filed independent investigations into this specific claim, which is perhaps unsurprising given how technically specific the Proposition 65 litigation context was. Understanding the claim's real status required familiarity with California's private enforcement mechanism, the specific litigation timeline, and why a Proposition 65 warning sign in a shop is a legal compliance response rather than a government safety finding. Kasprak's report did this work effectively, and no competing analysis has materially revised his conclusions.

The broader lesson for fact-checkers engaged with food safety claims is the importance of distinguishing between different types of authoritative-looking consumer protection communications. Proposition 65 warnings, FDA recall notices, court filings, and class action complaints all carry different evidentiary weights and represent different stages of the evidence-gathering and litigation process. Claims that conflate these distinct mechanisms—treating pre-litigation compliance warnings as equivalent to confirmed government findings—need to be carefully unpacked rather than simply rated true or false.
