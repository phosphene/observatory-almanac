## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In early April 2021, photographs of a massive American alligator (*Alligator mississippiensis*) went viral across social media, and the detail that captured public imagination was not the animal's impressive bulk—12 feet long and 445 pounds—but what investigators found when the carcass was processed at Cordray's Butcher Shop in South Carolina. According to the Facebook post that initiated the viral spread, the alligator's stomach contained five dog tags, a bullet jacket, a spark plug, and an assortment of turtle shells and bobcat claws. Two of the five dog tags were still legible, and one phone number on them was still active. The owner of two of the animals whose tags were recovered reportedly said the tags were 24 years old.

Snopes investigator Madison Dapcevich (2021) assigned this claim the rating **True**. The facts were independently confirmed in direct communication with Cordray's Butcher Shop, the original source of the photographs and claims. The biological plausibility of these gastric contents is well-supported by the scientific literature on alligator feeding ecology, and the legal framework in South Carolina that permitted this animal to be hunted is a matter of public record.

This entry explores the verification methodology, the biology of alligator gastric accumulation and gastorlith behavior, and the broader questions the incident raises about apex predator record-keeping in managed landscapes.

## §2 The Facebook Post and Independent Confirmation

The viral origin of this story was a Facebook post published on April 8, 2021, by Cordray's Butcher Shop, a South Carolina establishment that offers game processing and taxidermy services. The post included photographs of the alligator, a layout of the stomach contents, and the specific details regarding the dog tags. This is not a nameless internet account; it is a named, locally known commercial business with a verifiable address and a long-standing presence in the South Carolina wildlife processing community.

Snopes reporter Madison Dapcevich (2021) made direct contact with Cordray's and received confirmation of all the details stated in the post. The animal was brought in by a man named Ned McNeely. The butcher shop confirmed both the stomach contents and the processing method (meat and taxidermy). This represents a clean verification chain: original post → identified primary source → direct phone confirmation.

The Snopes investigation also addressed the legal framework surrounding this kill, an important contextual element that some viral coverage omitted. South Carolina's *regular* alligator hunting season is typically open from mid-September through mid-October and requires special permits issued by the South Carolina Department of Natural Resources (SCDNR). However, the state maintains a separate private-land alligator management season that runs from September through May of the following year, permitting landowners to manage problem animals (SCDNR Private Lands Alligator Program, 2019–2020). South Carolina also makes provisions for "nuisance alligators" that pose documented threats to people or property, which creates additional removal pathways outside the regular season.

The April 2021 kill was therefore not illegal poaching; it fell within the legally permitted window for private-land alligator management, a fact supported by state-published documentation reviewed in the fact-check.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Gastric Archaeology and What Alligator Stomachs Record

The most epistemically novel aspect of this incident is not the news value of the stomach contents, but what it reveals about the function of the alligator's gastrointestinal system as an *inadvertent biological archive* of its landscape history.

American alligators are well known to accumulate gastroliths—stones and other hard objects ingested deliberately or coincidentally—in their stomachs. McIlhenny (1935), in his foundational field observations later synthesized by Platt and Brantley (1997) in *Copeia* (American Society of Ichthyologists and Herpetologists), documented that alligators swallow rocks and debris that may serve both digestive and buoyancy-regulation functions. Unlike strict gastroliths in crocodilians recorded in the fossil record (Wings & Sander, 2007, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*), the American alligator's inclination to ingest hard objects means that its stomach contents literally accumulate a record of the physical environment it has moved through over decades.

In the Cordray's case, this archival function is dramatically illustrated. The dog tags—at 24 years old at the time of recovery, placing their deposition in approximately 1997—indicate that this alligator had been roaming its territory for at least a quarter century. Alligators are long-lived; documented maximum longevity in wild populations exceeds 50 years, and the sexual maturity threshold is reached at 6–7 feet (about 10–12 years in favorable conditions) according to Joanen and McNease (1972) in *Proceedings of the Annual Conference of the Southeastern Association of Game and Fish Commissioners*. A 12-foot, 445-pound male alligator is almost certainly at least 30–40 years old, meaning the 1997 ingestion of dog tags (if they were freshly lost at that time) occurred during the animal's active adult foraging period.

The bullet jacket is also informative. Its presence without apparent injury to the animal suggests either a deflected or near-miss shot, or an ingested projectile recovered from a carcass the alligator consumed. The spark plug raises questions about whether the animal had access to vehicle disposal sites, flooded roadways, or trash-dumping zones—alligators in human-modified landscapes are documented opportunistic scavengers that investigate novel objects (Nifong et al., 2012, *Southeastern Naturalist*).

The turtle shells and bobcat claws are more straightforward: they represent prey and scavenge records. American alligators are major predators of freshwater turtles in the southeastern United States, and turtle shell fragments are among the most common hard debris recovered from alligator stomachs in published necropsies (Delany & Abercrombie, 1986, *Journal of Wildlife Management*). Bobcat *claws* in the stomach indicate that this alligator had consumed at least one *Lynx rufus* at some point—an unusual prey item that nevertheless fits within the broad opportunistic diet of a large adult male.

The epistemic novelty, then, lies in this claim: **viral incidents involving improbable-sounding lists of stomach contents from large, old alligators should be treated as *more* rather than *less* plausible, because the biology supports decades-long accumulation of diverse hard objects.** Public skepticism tends to apply a uniform prior—that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence—without accounting for whether the underlying biology provides its own independent prior-probability boost. In this case, it does.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Dapcevich's (2021) investigation competently verified the core claims. Several threads deserve additional attention:

**The legible tags and the living phone number**: Snopes reported that two of the five dog tags were still legible and that the phone number on one was still active, with the owner confirming the tags were 24 years old. This is arguably the most poignant element of the story—a pet owner receiving a call confirming their long-vanished dogs' likely fate. Whether the owner was willing to be publicly identified, and whether any further communication with wildlife authorities occurred, is not documented in available sources.

**Genomic sequencing potential**: A 12-foot adult male with at least 30–40 years of territorial history in a specific South Carolina watershed represents a significant data point for local alligator population genetics. There is no indication in available reporting that tissue samples were taken for DNA analysis, population assignment, or any genetic archive purpose. South Carolina's alligator management databases would benefit from systematic tissue banking of harvested animals.

**The "spark plug" question**: The presence of a spark plug invites but does not resolve the question of the animal's range and habitat use. South Carolina's coastal watersheds connect to boat launches, agricultural drainage systems, and developed floodplains. Whether this object originated from a marine or boat environment, or from terrestrial dumping, cannot be determined from the available evidence.

**Physical condition at harvest**: No information is available on the animal's body condition score, parasite burden, or any pathological findings from the internal organs. For a 445-pound animal, body condition data would substantially enrich the interpretation of this specimen.

## §5 Contextual Framing: South Carolina Alligator Ecology and Population Management

South Carolina has one of the densest alligator populations in the United States outside of Florida and Louisiana. The species was listed as threatened under the federal Endangered Species Act in 1967 following decades of unregulated hunting, and was delisted in 1987 after successful population recovery (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, 1987). Today, the southeastern United States population is estimated at well over one million animals, with South Carolina's coastal and freshwater wetland systems supporting robust densities.

The private-land management season referenced in the Snopes investigation reflects a pragmatic evolution in South Carolina wildlife law. Because alligators are expanding their range into suburban and agricultural landscapes as populations recover (Rootes & Chabreck, 1993, *American Midland Naturalist*), conflicts with domestic animals, livestock, and occasionally humans have increased. The nuisance alligator program allows property owners to remove animals that constitute documented threats without waiting for the general season, which falls in autumn.

The gastric contents of the Cordray's specimen—dog tags, a bullet jacket, turtle shells, bobcat claws, and a spark plug—collectively paint a portrait of an animal that had long experience navigating a mixed human-wildland landscape. Such animals, sometimes called "residential" alligators by wildlife managers (Nifong & Silliman, 2013, *PLoS ONE*), are of special management interest because they are simultaneously the most likely to come into conflict with humans and the most ecologically significant as apex regulators of their local wetland food webs.

The five dog tags in particular illustrate a real and recurring hazard: domestic dogs allowed to run freely near water bodies in alligator habitat are at significant predation risk. The American Veterinary Medical Association and state wildlife agencies in Florida, Georgia, South Carolina, and Louisiana routinely publish advisories urging pet owners to keep dogs leashed near water at dawn, dusk, and nighttime when alligator activity peaks (Stevenson et al., 2010, *Herpetological Review*).

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: TRUE**

Five dog tags—along with a bullet jacket, spark plug, turtle shells, and bobcat claws—were confirmed found in the stomach of a 12-foot, 445-pound alligator processed at Cordray's Butcher Shop in South Carolina in April 2021. The claim was independently confirmed through direct contact with the business. The legal framework for the animal's harvest is a matter of public record. The biological plausibility of the stomach contents is well-supported by published alligator ecology literature.

**EARC Classification: C (Contextual)**

The epistemic work required here is not debunking but contextualization. The unusual stomach contents, precisely because they sound extraordinary, require contextual anchoring in alligator biology to be properly understood. Once anchored—decades-long gastrolith accumulation, opportunistic scavenging, long-range territory use—the extraordinary-sounding claim becomes entirely predictable and unremarkable from a biological standpoint. The contextual gap is the missing biological frame that news coverage uniformly failed to provide alongside the photographs.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Identity of the dog owners who received the call about the 24-year-old tags
- Whether tissue samples were banked for population genetics
- Whether the individual had prior conflict records with wildlife authorities
- Source environment of the spark plug (aquatic vs. terrestrial origin)
- Full pathological examination record of the specimen
