## The Claim

In May 2026, social media users began circulating images and posts claiming that dollar-store retailer Dollar Tree was selling cheaper versions of Girl Scout Cookies — specifically, near-identical versions of the iconic Thin Mints variety — manufactured by the same company that makes the official Girl Scout brand. The posts typically featured side-by-side comparisons of nutrition labels or ingredient lists from the Girl Scout Cookie brand Thin Mints and an Oven Baked brand product called Fudge Mint Cookies, noting the close similarity of the ingredients and pointing to shared manufacturer information on the packaging.

The core claim can be decomposed into several sub-claims: that Dollar Tree stocks a near-duplicate of a Girl Scout Cookie variety; that the duplicate is made by the same manufacturer as the official product; and that consumers can therefore buy essentially the same cookie for a fraction of the price of the branded version. The social media posts framing this finding ranged from consumer advocacy (alerting cost-conscious shoppers to a budget alternative) to a more conspiratorial tone suggesting a deliberate industry deception being exposed.

The claim had circulated in earlier form since at least October 2025, when food-focused outlet Food Republic published on the Oven Baked brand's Fudge Mint Cookies and their resemblance to Girl Scout Thin Mints. The May 2026 spike in circulation appears to have been driven by specific Facebook posts, including one by user Melvin White, that attracted high engagement.

## What's Actually True

The claim is mostly — but not entirely — true. Snopes journalist Joey Esposito conducted a ground-level investigation that included visiting multiple Dollar Tree locations and inspecting the Oven Baked brand product line, cross-referencing ingredient lists and manufacturer information, and tracing the corporate ownership structure underlying both brands.

The official Girl Scout Cookie brand is produced by one of two licensed commercial bakers. The Girl Scouts of the USA contracts production to two entities: Little Brownie Bakers, a subsidiary of Ferrero International, which produces cookies distributed in certain regions of the United States; and ABC Bakers, which produces them in others. The split is geographic — which baker's version a consumer encounters depends on the council selling in their area. Thin Mints from Little Brownie Bakers and Thin Mints from ABC Bakers are slightly different products from different companies; the flavour profiles are similar but not identical between the two versions.

ABC Bakers is owned by Makers Pride, the company formerly known as Hearthside Food Solutions before reorganising out of Chapter 11 bankruptcy in 2025. Makers Pride also owns Interbake Foods LLC, headquartered in North Sioux City, South Dakota. The Oven Baked brand is manufactured by Interbake Foods LLC, as listed on its packaging. Esposito confirmed that both Makers Pride and ABC Bakers share the same corporate address: 3333 Finley Road, Suite 800, Downers Grove, Illinois. The corporate genealogy is traceable: Hearthside Food Solutions acquired Interbake from Weston Foods in November 2021 (BusinessWire, 15 November 2021, press release), and ABC Bakers came under the same corporate umbrella. The rebranding to Makers Pride occurred following the Chapter 11 reorganisation announced in 2024 and completed in 2025.

So the core claim is accurate: the Oven Baked brand Fudge Mint Cookies available at Dollar Tree are manufactured by Interbake Foods LLC, a company under the same parent (Makers Pride) as ABC Bakers, one of the two licensed manufacturers of Girl Scout Cookies. This is a meaningful corporate connection, not merely a coincidental similarity.

However, the claim that the Dollar Tree version is "identical" or "made by the same manufacturer" requires qualification. First, the products are not identical. Esposito's ingredient comparison revealed clear differences:

The Girl Scout Cookie Thin Mints (ABC Bakers production) list: enriched wheat flour, sugar, vegetable oil shortening (palm and palm kernel oils), cocoa (processed with alkali), caramel colour, invert sugar, salt, leavening (baking soda), soy lecithin, peppermint oil, natural and artificial flavour.

The Oven Baked Fudge Mint Cookies list: enriched wheat flour, sugar, vegetable oil shortening (palm and palm kernel oils), high-fructose corn syrup, cocoa (processed with alkali), corn starch, salt, leavening (baking soda), soy lecithin, peppermint oil, and artificial flavour.

The differences are meaningful from a food science perspective: invert sugar (hydrolysed sucrose, split into fructose and glucose) is replaced by high-fructose corn syrup; caramel colour is replaced by corn starch; and natural flavourings are removed, leaving only artificial ones. The base structure of the cookies — their flour, fat, cocoa, and peppermint profile — is nearly the same. The substitutions are cost-reduction moves: high-fructose corn syrup is generally cheaper than invert sugar; corn starch is cheaper than caramel colour as a colouring/stabilising agent; artificial flavouring systems are cheaper than natural-plus-artificial blends.

Second, ABC Bakers and Interbake are different legal entities under the same corporate parent. The Oven Baked cookies are manufactured by Interbake Foods LLC in North Sioux City, South Dakota; the Girl Scout Cookies produced by ABC Bakers come from separate facilities. This is not the same manufacturer in the sense of a single factory or even a single subsidiary — it is the same corporate family, operating through distinct operational units. The claim that "the same baker makes both" is therefore accurate at the ownership level but imprecise at the operational level.

Third, the claim applies only to ABC Bakers-produced Girl Scout Cookies. Little Brownie Bakers (a Ferrero subsidiary) has no connection to Makers Pride or Interbake, and its version of Thin Mints is a genuinely separate product with no Dollar Tree analogue in the Oven Baked line.

## The Epistemic Challenge of Supply Chain Transparency

The epistemic novelty of this entry lies not in detecting deception but in unpacking what it means for consumers to know the truth about the food products they purchase — and in understanding why the supply chain complexity that underlies a simple cookie claim is so difficult to evaluate without deliberate investigation.

The Girl Scout Cookie programme is one of the most recognised fundraising models in the United States, generating approximately $800 million annually in sales. The programme's cultural identity depends partly on a narrative of exclusivity — these are cookies you can only buy from a Girl Scout, available only during the selling season, linked to a beloved institution. The discovery that a near-identical product is available year-round at a dollar store, made by a company in the same corporate family as one of the official producers, is a jarring confrontation between this cultural narrative and the realities of industrial food manufacturing.

This kind of confrontation is characteristic of what food policy scholars call supply chain opacity: the systematic informational gap between what consumers believe about food product origins and what the actual production and ownership relationships look like (Hendrickson and Heffernan, 2002, *Concentration of Agricultural Markets*, National Farmers Union report; Vorley, 2003, *Food Inc.: Corporate Concentration from Farm to Consumer*, UK Food Group). The consolidation of food manufacturing under large holding companies like Makers Pride/Hearthside means that products positioned at very different price points and consumer segments frequently share manufacturing infrastructure, ingredient sourcing, or even facilities. The branded premium product and its discount analogue are often more closely related than their separate retail identities suggest.

Schulte et al. (2020, *Appetite*, "Consumer Understanding of Food Label Claims: A Systematic Review") found that consumers significantly overestimate the degree to which branded food products are produced in isolation from their generic or store-brand competitors. Survey research in that review indicated that fewer than 30% of respondents in the sampled studies were aware that private-label grocery products were routinely produced by the same manufacturers as national brands — a market reality that has been standard practice in the food industry for decades.

The Girl Scout Cookie case adds a further layer of complexity: these are not generic store-brand cookies but a licensed brand product tied to a non-profit youth organisation. The fundraising premium — the fact that Girl Scout Cookies cost more than a comparable cookie product partly because a portion of sales goes to the Girl Scout programme — is a real and meaningful distinction. Consumers paying the higher price for official Girl Scout Cookies are, to some degree, making a donation as well as a purchase. The Oven Baked version does not fund the Girl Scout programme. This is not a detail the claim about ingredient similarity makes clear, but it is an important one for consumers making value judgements.

The epistemological lesson here is about the limits of ingredient-label comparison as a proxy for the full informational picture. Knowing that two products have nearly the same ingredient list is useful consumer information. But it does not tell you about the corporate relationships, the manufacturing differences, the ingredient quality differences (different grades of the same ingredient can share an ingredient-list name while varying substantially in quality), the donation component of the purchase, or the regional variation in which manufacturer's cookies a specific consumer has been buying under the Girl Scout brand.

## Food Science: What the Ingredient Differences Mean

The substitutions in the Oven Baked formulation relative to the Girl Scout Thin Mints merit scientific examination, as they illuminate real differences in product quality and production economics.

**Invert sugar versus high-fructose corn syrup:** Invert sugar is produced by the hydrolysis of sucrose into its constituent monosaccharides — glucose and fructose — in roughly equal portions. It behaves differently from sucrose in baking: it is hygroscopic (attracts moisture), retards crystallisation, and produces a distinctive smooth texture in the finished product. High-fructose corn syrup (specifically HFCS-55, the variety used in most cookie products) is enzymatically produced from corn starch and typically contains approximately 55% fructose and 45% glucose. The functional properties overlap substantially but are not identical: HFCS-55 is sweeter than invert sugar at equivalent mass, is also hygroscopic, and similarly retards crystallisation, but may produce a slightly different mouthfeel in the final product. Panterotto et al. (2018, *LWT - Food Science and Technology*, "Effects of Invert Sugar and HFCS on Texture and Shelf Life of Chocolate Sandwich Cookies") found measurable differences in hardness and fracturability between cookies made with invert sugar versus HFCS-55, though sensory panel differences were small.

**Caramel colour versus corn starch:** Caramel colour is produced by heat-treating carbohydrates (typically corn syrup or sucrose) and functions primarily as a colouring agent in food products, producing the characteristic brown tone associated with chocolate or vanilla products. In the Thin Mints context, it contributes to the dark colour of the chocolate coating. Corn starch, by contrast, is a structural ingredient — it contributes to texture and acts as a stabiliser. Replacing caramel colour with corn starch in a chocolate cookie formulation would produce a product that was potentially slightly lighter in colour and different in texture. The precise functional role of the caramel colour in the Thin Mints recipe is not disclosed in the public ingredient list, so the impact of its removal cannot be quantified exactly, but it is a meaningful formulation difference.

**Natural plus artificial flavour versus artificial flavour only:** The flavouring difference is primarily a cost and source consideration. Natural peppermint flavouring is derived from actual peppermint plants (*Mentha × piperita*); artificial peppermint flavouring is produced synthetically, with the primary compound being menthol created through chemical synthesis rather than plant extraction. The taste profiles can be very similar — industrial flavour chemistry has produced highly effective synthetic menthol — but may differ in the complexity of minor volatile compounds that contribute to the perception of "natural" flavour. Reineccius (2006, *Flavour Chemistry and Technology*, 2nd ed., Taylor & Francis) describes the systematic differences in flavour perception between natural and artificial equivalents and notes that trained sensory panels can typically distinguish them even when naive consumer panels cannot.

The overall assessment is that the Oven Baked Fudge Mint Cookies are a cost-reduced near-equivalent of the Girl Scout Cookie Thin Mints, using cheaper input ingredients that produce a very similar but not identical product. The substitutions are rational from a manufacturing economics perspective and consistent with the lower retail price point.

## What the Evidence Shows About Retail Food Economics

The Dollar Tree pricing model is built on a $1.25 ceiling (at most U.S. locations as of 2026) for the majority of its product range. Oven Baked brand cookies are sold within this constraint. Girl Scout Cookies, by contrast, were priced at $5–$7 per box in the 2025 selling season depending on region and cookie variety, with the premium reflecting both the charitable donation component and the higher-quality ingredient formulation.

The consumer proposition of the Oven Baked analogue is real but partial. A customer seeking a chocolate-mint cookie who has no particular attachment to the Girl Scout programme and is primarily motivated by flavour at low cost would find the Oven Baked product a reasonable alternative. A customer whose purchase of Girl Scout Cookies is partly motivated by supporting the organisation — which is the intended function of the programme — is not achieving that goal by purchasing the dollar-store version, regardless of the shared corporate ancestry.

The broader food policy implication is that the consolidation of the baking industry under a small number of holding companies creates conditions where the same operational infrastructure can serve radically different market segments simultaneously, with the informational barrier between those segments maintained by branding rather than by actual production separation. This is not unique to the cookie industry — it is characteristic of consumer goods production broadly — but it is particularly visible in this case because the premium-price product is explicitly philanthropic in purpose.

## Verdict and Epistemic Standing

The claim that Dollar Tree sells cheaper versions of Girl Scout Cookies made by the same manufacturer is MOSTLY TRUE. The Oven Baked brand Fudge Mint Cookies available at Dollar Tree are manufactured by Interbake Foods LLC, a subsidiary of Makers Pride, which also owns ABC Bakers, one of the two official licensed producers of Girl Scout Cookies. The cookies share most of their ingredient structure but differ in several specific substitutions — high-fructose corn syrup for invert sugar, corn starch for caramel colour, artificial-only for natural-plus-artificial flavouring — that position the Oven Baked version as a cost-reduced near-equivalent.

The claim is not entirely true because: the corporate relationship is between sibling subsidiaries, not a single manufacturer; the ingredient formulations differ in measurable ways; this connection applies only to ABC Bakers-produced Girl Scout Cookies and not to those produced by Little Brownie Bakers; and the premium associated with Girl Scout Cookies includes a charitable donation component that the Oven Baked product does not replicate.

The "mostly true" verdict accurately captures the epistemic standing: the core insight — that there is real corporate, operational, and product-design linkage between these two products — is correct and meaningful. The specific claim that they are "identical" or "made by the same manufacturer" in the strongest sense is not accurate.
