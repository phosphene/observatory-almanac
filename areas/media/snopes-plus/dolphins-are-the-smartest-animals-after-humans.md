# Dolphins Are the Second-Smartest Animals After Humans


## 1. The Claim

The claim that dolphins are the second-most intelligent species on Earth — behind humans and ahead of all other animals — is among the most resilient animal myths in popular science culture. It appears in children's educational materials, nature documentaries, popular science journalism, and general conversation with a frequency that suggests settled fact rather than contested interpretive claim. The formulation has many variants: "dolphins are the most intelligent animals apart from humans," "dolphins are almost as smart as people," "only humans are smarter than dolphins," and, most precisely, "dolphins rank second after humans on the intelligence scale."

The claim has a cultural genealogy traceable to the 1960s research of neuroscientist John C. Lilly, whose work on dolphin communication and consciousness attracted extraordinary public attention and generated claims about dolphin intelligence that significantly outran the evidence. Lilly proposed, most influentially in *Man and Dolphin* (1961), that dolphins had language-like communication systems and were approaching or equalling human cognitive level. His research environment at the Communication Research Institute in Miami became a cultural locus for speculation about interspecies communication that was amplified enormously by the popular imagination. The 1963 film *Flipper* and its television adaptation (1964–1967) embedded the idea of the dolphin as an almost-human companion across a generation of American children.

The claim is reinforced by selective presentation of genuine cognitive research findings. Dolphins do demonstrate mirror self-recognition, suggesting some form of self-awareness; they use echolocation in sophisticated ways; they exhibit cultural transmission of behaviours; they have large complex brains; and decades of captivity research at facilities including the Marine Mammal Laboratory at the University of California Santa Cruz and the Kewalo Basin Marine Mammal Laboratory in Hawaii have documented impressive performance on cognitive tasks including artificial symbol comprehension, analogical reasoning, and imitation.

This accumulation of evidence is then packaged into the claim's comparative assertion: all of this is so impressive that only humans surpass it. The claim is not typically argued from a rigorous cross-species comparison — it is stated as common knowledge, warranted by general impressiveness.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Dolphin cognitive abilities are genuine and extensively documented.** This must be stated clearly before examining the comparative claim, because skepticism about the "second-smartest" ranking is sometimes misread as dismissiveness toward dolphin cognition.

Mirror self-recognition was demonstrated in bottlenose dolphins by Reiss and Marino (2001, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*), using a well-validated methodology adapted from earlier work with great apes. The study found that dolphins marked with non-toxic marks on their bodies used mirrors to inspect the marks, a finding consistent with self-referential mirror use. This places dolphins in a very small category of non-human animals that pass variations of the mirror test, which includes great apes, elephants (Plotnik et al., 2006, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*), European magpies (Prior et al., 2008, *PLOS Biology*), and some other corvids.

Cultural transmission in dolphins has been documented extensively. The sponge-carrying behaviour of bottlenose dolphins in Shark Bay, Australia — where some individuals learned to carry marine sponges on their rostra to protect against abrasion while bottom-feeding — is transmitted from mothers to daughters through direct observation, making it one of the most compelling documented cases of animal cultural inheritance outside of great apes (Krützen et al., 2005, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*).

Communication complexity in cetaceans, particularly bottlenose dolphins and sperm whales, has been an active research area. Tyack (2009, *Integrative and Comparative Biology*) reviewed evidence for learned vocalisation, individually distinctive signature whistles (analogous to names), and social learning of new calls. The discovery of sperm whale clan-level cultural dialects (Rendell and Whitehead, 2001, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*) added a comparative dimension suggesting cultural complexity across cetaceans.

Dolphins trained with artificial symbol lexicons at the Kewalo Basin laboratory by Louis Herman and colleagues (Herman et al., 1984, *Cognition*) demonstrated understanding of object-gesture relationships, elementary syntax-sensitivity in instruction sequences, and references to absent objects — findings that were headline-catching at the time and remain methodologically significant.

**Why "second-smartest" is not a scientific finding.** The comparative claim assumes several things that the comparative cognition field now largely rejects.

First, it assumes that intelligence is a single linearly rankable trait rather than a multidimensional profile. Neuroscientist and comparative cognition researcher Nicola Clayton (Clayton and Dickinson, 1998, *Nature*; Clayton et al., 2001, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*) demonstrated that western scrub jays show episodic-like memory — the ability to remember what, where, and when a specific past event occurred — a capacity previously assumed to require both episodic memory systems and a theory of mind. Jays also demonstrate prospective planning and cache deception strategies that are cognitively sophisticated by any metric. Under a "who is smartest" framework, how should jay episodic memory be weighed against dolphin mirror recognition?

Second, the claim implies that a single scale applies across species with profoundly different sensory and cognitive architectures. Dolphins evolved in a three-dimensional acoustic environment where echolocation is the primary sensory modality for prey detection and for navigating in low-visibility water. Their impressive cognitive abilities are substantially adaptations to this environment — spatial memory, acoustic object identification, group coordination in three dimensions. These are not just "smart" in the way a clever person is smart; they are smart in ways that solve problems human cognition does not face and would struggle with. Comparing dolphin sonar-based spatial cognition to human visual-symbolic cognition is comparing different kinds of problem-solving apparatus evolved for different niches.

Cognitive biologist Alex Taylor's work on New Caledonian crows (Taylor et al., 2010, *Science*; Taylor et al., 2012, *PLoS ONE*) documented multi-step tool manufacture and use, including the ability to compose sequences of tool operations to reach food — a capacity demonstrably present in a bird species with a brain the size of a walnut, with no neocortex in the mammalian sense, operating through a functionally analogous pallial structure. Under a brain-size-based intelligence proxy, crows should score low. Under behavioural performance criteria for flexible tool use and causal reasoning, they score remarkably high.

Cephalopod cognition represents perhaps the most dramatic challenge to vertebrate-centred intelligence rankings. Octopus problem-solving, including maze navigation, jar-opening, and observational learning (Fiorito and Scotto, 1992, *Science*), was documented decades ago. More recent work has examined octopus play behaviour, individual personality variation (Mather and Anderson, 1993, *Journal of Comparative Psychology*), and spatial memory. These capacities emerge from a distributed nervous system with approximately 500 million neurons, most of which are located in the arms rather than the centralised brain — an architecture with no clear homologue to vertebrate cognition and no principled way to compare to dolphin cortex.

**Brain size and complexity.** A common justification for dolphin intelligence rankings is the encephalisation quotient (EQ) — the ratio of brain size to the expected brain size for a mammal of that body weight. This metric was developed by Harry Jerison (1973, *Evolution of the Brain and Intelligence*, Academic Press) as a proxy for cognitive capacity. Bottlenose dolphins have an EQ of approximately 4.2, higher than chimpanzees (approximately 2.5) and surpassed among non-human animals only by some dolphin species and, in some calculations, domestic pigs. This is the empirical substrate for the "nearly as smart as humans" claim.

EQ has significant limitations as a cognitive predictor. Herculano-Houzel (2009, *Frontiers in Neuroanatomy*) demonstrated that neuron number better predicts cognitive performance than brain weight or EQ across vertebrates, and that the relationship between volume and neuron count varies substantially between lineages — cetacean brains, which have undergone massive evolutionary enlargement over approximately 35 million years, have a very different neuron density than primate brains of comparable volume. Cetacean neocortex is notably thin for its surface area, and distinct in having unusually large neurons (von Economo neurons, shared with humans, great apes, and elephants) but lower overall neuron packing density.

What this means is that EQ comparisons between cetaceans and primates are not direct cognitive comparisons. Dolphin brain volume relative to body size is high, but the neural architecture is different from primate neocortex in ways that make the quantitative comparison difficult to interpret.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Charismatic megafauna bias operates at the level of research funding, public communication, and folk taxonomy.** This is the core epistemic mechanism and it merits close examination.

The concept of charismatic megafauna — large, visually appealing, and behaviourally relatable animals that attract disproportionate attention and resources relative to their ecological or scientific significance — was developed in conservation biology (Brugiere et al., 2010, among others) to describe funding distortions in wildlife conservation. Dolphins exemplify this category: they are large enough to be visible and impressive, they are mammals with expressive faces and behaviours that resemble human social dynamics, they are associated with positive human encounters, and they are featured heavily in entertainment and tourism.

The comparative cognition research field is not immune to these biases. The literature on dolphin cognition is vastly larger than the literature on octopus cognition, even though the octopus case is arguably more scientifically compelling because it represents a completely independent evolutionary origin of complex cognition. The research infrastructure — captive facilities, trained animals, established behavioural paradigms — that has been built around dolphins, great apes, and corvids over decades creates a gravitational pull toward research continuations that produces more positive findings and more detailed characterisations than exist for less-studied taxa.

The epistemic novelty here is a specific form of taxonomic anthropocentrism that survives the surface-level acknowledgement that other animals are intelligent. Researchers and popular communicators who would never openly claim that intelligence is a uniquely human property often retain, implicitly, a preference for cognitive similarities between target animals and humans as the criterion for intelligence attribution. Dolphins are interesting to us partly because they seem socially similar — they live in groups, they cooperate, they appear to play, they produce something that sounds like expressive communication. This social similarity works as a heuristic for intelligence attribution (Nelson and Shankman, 2010, *American Anthropologist* — on the mirror test) but it is not a neutral scientific criterion. It is an anthropocentric filter.

This is why crows, octopuses, and bees are perennially surprising to popular audiences: their demonstrated cognitive capacities systematically exceed the expectations generated by their appearance and evolutionary distance from humans. Every few years a corvid or cephalopod study generates the surprised headline "turns out X is smarter than we thought" — and then public belief reverts to its baseline, because the animal does not activate the same anthropomorphic signals that maintain the dolphin case in working memory.

**The second-best framing creates a false comparative structure.** Ranking intelligence "after humans" presupposes that the human case establishes a benchmark and that measuring distance from that benchmark in other species is meaningful. But human intelligence is itself a cluster of domain-specific capacities that emerged under particular evolutionary pressures — language, symbolic reasoning, cumulative culture, theory of mind, extended planning — none of which is a universal maximum that other species are approximate versions of. Crows' episodic memory, octopus distributed information processing, and bat echolocation are not weaker versions of human cognition; they are different solutions to different problems.

The framing systematically biases the evaluation toward species that evolved under social conditions similar to us (primate-like social intelligence) and toward tasks that resemble human cognitive tasks (symbol learning, mirror recognition). Dolphins score well on these tasks partly because they live in complex social groups under conditions that favoured social intelligence, making them a convergent case. But this does not establish their intelligence as generically superior to all other non-human species; it establishes their superiority at a particular set of human-relevant metrics.


## 4. Verdict

**Classification: FALSE (as stated) / MISLEADING (in narrower forms)**

The claim that dolphins are definitively the second-most intelligent species after humans is false in the specific, ranked form in which it typically circulates. No rigorous comparative cognition research supports a claim that dolphins outperform all other non-human animals on all or most intelligence metrics; the claim requires ignoring large bodies of evidence for great apes, corvids, cephalopods, and elephants, and it requires imposing a single linear scale on a multidimensional set of domain-specific cognitive traits.

In narrower forms — "dolphins are among the most cognitively sophisticated non-human animals with respect to social intelligence and acoustic communication" — the claim is defensible. But this careful formulation is not what the popular version asserts or implies.

The underlying science about dolphin cognition is strong and interesting. The error is not in the evidence but in the comparative inference drawn from it — specifically, the inference that impressive performance on the metrics we have tested justifies a species-level superiority claim over all other tested and untested animals.

**What should replace the claim:** Dolphins display remarkable cognitive abilities across social, communicative, and problem-solving domains. They are among a small number of non-human animal groups to have demonstrated mirror self-recognition, cultural transmission, learned vocalisation, and syntactic sensitivity to artificial symbol sequences. Great apes, corvids, and cephalopods show comparable sophistication in different cognitive domains. The comparative cognition field does not support ranking these groups — the available evidence establishes multiple independent centres of cognitive complexity, not a single hierarchy with a clear second place.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The dolphin intelligence claim connects to a larger failure mode in popular science communication: the persistent use of anthropocentric intelligence rankings as a primary way of conveying the significance of animal cognition research. This failure mode is not uniquely harmful to dolphins — it also misrepresents the cognitive sophistication of every other species that deserves recognition for its own domain-specific capabilities — but it has particular distorting effects in species conservation and research resource allocation.

The history of cetacean cognition research shows how a compelling popular narrative can sustain a research literature even as the foundational assumptions of that literature come under increasing challenge. John Lilly's early claims about dolphin communication, which attracted enormous research funding and public attention in the 1960s, were substantially discredited as methodology improved and the initial enthusiasm was subjected to replication attempts (Doak, 1990, *The Dolphin Lover*; Burnett, 2012, *The Sounding of the Whale*, Chicago). But the associated belief in dolphin superintelligence survived the discrediting of specific claims because the popular narrative had been established with sufficient cultural momentum to absorb scientific revision without updating.

Subsequent rigorous research — the Reiss and Marino mirror work, the Herman lexicon studies, the Krützen cultural transmission work — has supported genuine cognitive sophistication claims and appropriately updated the scientific record. But the popular narrative often treats this subsequent good science as simply confirming Lilly, rather than as a more modest and qualified finding that replaced an overreach.

The sociological dimension of cetacean research involves commercial and educational interests. MarineWorld parks, aquariums, and adventure tourism operators worldwide have substantial economic interests in the maintenance of dolphin cognitive mystique. These operators fund research, host researchers, and communicate findings in ways that emphasise extraordinary intelligence claims. This creates an information environment in which impressive findings are amplified and modest or negative findings are less visible. A study showing that a dolphin failed on a particular cognitive task does not generate the same media coverage as a study showing a dolphin succeeding on an impressive one, and the selective coverage of success over failure systematically overstates the cognitive case.

The conservation stakes are real: dolphin species and their habitats face genuine threats including bycatch, pollution, habitat degradation, and climate change. Public enthusiasm for dolphin intelligence has, historically, generated conservation attention and funding that has been broadly beneficial for cetacean research and protection. The concern is not that the popular narrative is producing net harm — it is that the exaggerated comparative claim draws attention away from other species whose conservation equally demands public support but who benefit from no equivalent popular cognitive narrative.

Research on public perceptions of animal intelligence and conservation priority (Clucas et al., 2008, *Animal Conservation*) has found that charismatic and cognitively impressive species receive disproportionate conservation attention and funding, while ecologically important but cognitively uncharismatic species are chronically under-resourced. The dolphin intelligence narrative participates in this dynamic.

The future of comparative cognition research is methodologically interesting. Next-generation approaches using natural behaviour observation with sophisticated classification tools, including machine learning analysis of vocalisation corpora in sperm whales (Project CETI, ongoing), may soon produce characterisations of cetacean communication that are either more or less impressive than existing data depending on what the analysis finds. These findings will be important regardless of whether they support the existing hierarchy; their significance will not be enhanced or diminished by whether dolphins turn out to be "smarter than chimpanzees."


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The claim that dolphins are the second-smartest animals has not been addressed as a dedicated fact-check article by Snopes, though the platform operates in adjacent territory through its coverage of specific dolphin cognition myths — including the claim that dolphins have been trained to kill enemies by the US Navy (addressed as a distortion of an actual military research programme), and general pieces on animal intelligence that acknowledge dolphin abilities without fully interrogating the comparative ranking.

The comparative intelligence claim occupies a difficult position for traditional fact-checkers: it is not a claim about a specific event, a numerical figure, or a quotation that can be verified against a record. It is a claim about the structure of animal cognition — an empirical claim, but one that requires engaging with an entire scientific field rather than checking a single verifiable fact. Most fact-checking platforms are not equipped to adjudicate comparative cognition debates, and the claim therefore circulates largely un-checked in official debunking venues.

Science journalism outlets and popular science communicators have addressed the claim more directly. Cognitive biologist Jennifer Mather, whose research on octopus cognition has been among the most methodologically rigorous in comparative cognition, has consistently argued in public writing that dolphin intelligence rankings systematically understate cephalopod cognitive capabilities. Evolutionary biologist Frans de Waal (de Waal, 2016, *Are We Smart Enough to Know How Smart Animals Are?*, Norton) devoted substantial attention to the limitations of linear intelligence rankings across species, arguing that each species is optimised for the cognitive demands of its own niche and that attempting to rank them against a human standard is a category error.

The EARC framework designates this claim as epistemologically loaded — a category reserved for claims that cannot be rated simply true or false because they rest on contested definitional assumptions. "Intelligence" is the contested concept here. If intelligence means performance on tasks designed by humans to test human-like cognitive capacities, dolphins may plausibly rank high among non-humans. If intelligence means fitness-maximising cognition within the animal's ecological niche, the concept does not support cross-species ranking at all. If intelligence means total neuron count or information-processing capacity, the ranking order would look very different again. The claim thus propagates false certainty about an inherently contested comparative question by packaging it as a settled popular fact.
