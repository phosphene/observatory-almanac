# "Dolphins Are the Second Most Intelligent Animals After Humans"


## 1. The Claim

The assertion that "dolphins are the second most intelligent animals after humans" appears frequently in popular science media, educational materials, and casual discourse about animal cognition. This claim typically rests on several pillars: dolphins' large brain-to-body ratio (encephalization quotient), their demonstrated self-recognition in mirror tests, sophisticated social behaviors, tool use, and complex communication systems. The claim implicitly accepts a hierarchical ranking of intelligence across species, with humans at the apex and dolphins occupying the runner-up position ahead of great apes, elephants, corvids, and other cognitively sophisticated animals.

This framing treats intelligence as a unitary, measurable quantity that can be meaningfully compared across vastly different evolutionary lineages, ecological niches, and neural architectures. The claim gained particular prominence following Lori Marino's influential 2002 research on dolphin brain anatomy and Diana Reiss's work on dolphin mirror self-recognition, which demonstrated that bottlenose dolphins could recognize themselves in mirrors—a capacity previously thought limited to humans, great apes, and elephants.


## 2. What's Actually True

**What the Evidence Actually Supports:**

Dolphins demonstrate genuinely remarkable cognitive abilities that place them among the most sophisticated non-human animals:

- **Mirror Self-Recognition**: Bottlenose dolphins consistently pass the mirror test, demonstrating self-awareness by investigating marks placed on their bodies while viewing themselves in mirrors (Reiss & Marino, 2001).

- **Complex Social Cognition**: Dolphins exhibit sophisticated social behaviors including alliance formation, cooperative hunting, cultural transmission of behaviors, and apparent empathy toward injured conspecifics.

- **Tool Use**: Dolphins in Shark Bay, Australia, use marine sponges as tools to protect their rostra while foraging on the seafloor—a behavior culturally transmitted from mothers to offspring (Krützen et al., 2005).

- **Vocal Learning and Signature Whistles**: Dolphins develop individualized signature whistles that function like names, and can learn to imitate artificial sounds and other dolphins' signatures.

- **Problem-Solving**: Captive dolphins demonstrate flexible problem-solving, including the ability to understand abstract concepts like "sameness" and "difference."

**What the Evidence Does Not Support:**

The evidence does not support the claim that these abilities necessarily place dolphins "second" in any objective ranking of animal intelligence. This conclusion requires several problematic assumptions:

- **Linear Intelligence Ranking**: The assumption that intelligence can be meaningfully ranked on a single linear scale across species with different evolutionary histories, ecological pressures, and neural architectures.

- **Encephalization Quotient Validity**: Heavy reliance on brain-size-to-body-weight ratios as a proxy for intelligence, despite significant criticisms of this approach.

- **Human-Centric Metrics**: The use of cognitive abilities that humans value (self-recognition, tool use) as universal measures of intelligence, potentially overlooking forms of cognition that humans cannot easily recognize or appreciate.


The epistemological foundations of comparative intelligence research reveal why the "second most intelligent" ranking is scientifically problematic:

**Intelligence is Multidimensional**: Contemporary cognitive science recognizes multiple forms of intelligence—spatial, social, linguistic, logical-mathematical, creative. Different species excel in different domains based on their evolutionary pressures. Dolphins show exceptional social intelligence and acoustic processing, corvids demonstrate remarkable causal reasoning and future planning, elephants exhibit extraordinary memory and empathy. Ranking these diverse cognitive profiles on a single scale is like trying to determine whether a violin or a trumpet is the "better" musical instrument.

**Evolutionary Contingency**: Intelligence evolves in response to specific environmental pressures. Dolphin cognition is adapted for a three-dimensional aquatic environment with complex social structures and acoustic communication. Corvid intelligence evolved for omnivorous foraging in varied terrestrial environments requiring flexible problem-solving. These cognitive adaptations cannot be meaningfully ranked against each other—they represent different solutions to different problems.

**Measurement Artifact**: The "second most intelligent" claim often reflects the historical development of comparative psychology research rather than objective cognitive assessment. Dolphins received intensive study partly due to their charismatic appeal and accessibility to researchers. Other cognitively sophisticated species (octopuses, corvids, social insects) received less attention until recently, creating an illusion of dolphin cognitive superiority.

**The Anthropocentric Fallacy**: Ranking animal intelligence with humans at the top assumes human cognition represents the pinnacle of intelligence rather than one particular evolutionary solution among many. This perspective ignores cognitive abilities that other species possess but humans lack (echolocation, magnetic navigation, infrared detection) and privileges cognitive domains where humans excel.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The "dolphins as second most intelligent" claim emerged from legitimate scientific research but became oversimplified in popular transmission. Key epistemological issues include:

**The Encephalization Quotient Problem**: Early support for dolphin cognitive superiority relied heavily on encephalization quotient (EQ)—the ratio of actual brain size to expected brain size for a given body weight. Dolphins score highly on EQ measures, leading to inferences about their cognitive capabilities. However, Paul Manger's controversial 2006 critique argued that large dolphin brains primarily serve thermoregulatory functions in aquatic environments, not enhanced cognition. While Manger's thermogenesis hypothesis remains disputed, his critique highlighted the problematic leap from brain size to cognitive ability.

**Anthropocentric Bias**: Much research on animal intelligence focuses on capacities that humans possess and value: self-recognition, tool use, language-like communication. This creates a "cognitive chauvinism" that may miss forms of intelligence adapted to different ecological niches. For instance, echolocation represents a sophisticated cognitive ability that humans lack entirely—should this "count" less than mirror self-recognition simply because we cannot directly experience it?

**Cherry-Picking Comparative Data**: The "second most intelligent" ranking often ignores inconvenient counterexamples. New Caledonian crows fashion complex tools and demonstrate causal reasoning that rivals that of young children, despite having brains roughly 1/1000th the size of dolphin brains. Octopuses solve multi-step problems and show apparent play behavior with neural architectures completely different from vertebrates. Great apes demonstrate theory of mind and learn human sign language systems.

**Cultural vs. Biological Intelligence**: Some dolphin behaviors attributed to "intelligence" may reflect cultural transmission rather than individual cognitive ability. The sponge-tool use in Shark Bay appears to be culturally transmitted, but the underlying capacity for tool use is shared with many other species that score lower on traditional intelligence metrics.


## 4. Verdict

**AUGMENTED**: While dolphins demonstrate remarkable cognitive abilities that place them among the most sophisticated non-human animals, the claim that they are definitively the "second most intelligent" species reflects scientifically problematic assumptions about the nature and measurement of intelligence across species.

The claim is not factually incorrect about dolphin cognitive abilities—dolphins do exhibit self-recognition, complex social behaviors, cultural transmission, tool use, and sophisticated communication. However, the assertion of a clear hierarchical ranking is not supported by current understanding of comparative cognition.

The evidence suggests that intelligence manifests differently across species based on evolutionary pressures and ecological niches. Corvids demonstrate remarkable causal reasoning and future planning with brains 1/1000th the size of dolphins. Octopuses solve complex problems with different neural architectures. Great apes show sophisticated theory of mind and language-like abilities. Elephants demonstrate empathy and death awareness that may not exist in dolphins.

Rather than asking "which animal is second most intelligent," more productive questions include: "In what cognitive domains do different species excel?" and "How do different forms of intelligence represent adaptive solutions to environmental challenges?"

The persistence of the "second most intelligent" ranking reflects several cognitive biases: the appeal of simple hierarchies, anthropocentric definitions of intelligence, historical research focus on charismatic megafauna, and the tendency to conflate brain size with cognitive ability.

A more scientifically accurate statement would be: "Dolphins possess a sophisticated form of intelligence adapted to their aquatic, social environment, demonstrating abilities in self-recognition, social cognition, and acoustic communication that place them among the most cognitively complex non-human animals, alongside corvids, great apes, elephants, and cephalopods—each of which excels in different cognitive domains shaped by their respective evolutionary histories."

The claim's partial truth combined with its categorical oversimplification makes it an excellent example of how scientific findings can be distorted through oversimplified popularization, even when the underlying research is sound. Understanding dolphin intelligence requires appreciating both their remarkable abilities and the complexity of comparative cognition, rather than accepting simple ranking systems that may tell us more about human psychological preferences than about the nature of intelligence itself.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Understanding dolphin cognition requires situating it within the broader landscape of comparative cognition research:

**Corvid Cognition**: New Caledonian crows and ravens demonstrate remarkable cognitive abilities including multi-step tool manufacture, future planning, and causal reasoning. Betty the crow's spontaneous wire-bending to retrieve food showed cognitive flexibility that rivals primate performance. Corvids plan for future needs, show episodic-like memory, and demonstrate social intelligence including alliance formation and tactical deception.

**Cephalopod Intelligence**: Octopuses represent convergent evolution of complex cognition in invertebrates. They demonstrate observational learning, play behavior, individual personality differences, and sophisticated problem-solving despite having a distributed nervous system completely different from vertebrate brains. Their cognitive abilities challenge vertebrate-centric assumptions about the neural requirements for intelligence.

**Primate Cognition**: Great apes show theory of mind, cultural transmission, tool use, and language-like communication abilities. Chimpanzees demonstrate altruism, future planning, and numerical concepts. Bonobos show remarkable social intelligence and conflict resolution abilities. Some researchers argue that ape cognitive abilities more closely parallel human cognition than dolphin abilities do.

**Elephant Intelligence**: Elephants demonstrate self-recognition, empathy, death rituals, complex social structures, and sophisticated memory abilities. Their cognitive profile includes abilities (like death awareness) that may not be present in dolphins.

**Social Insect Collective Intelligence**: Ant and bee colonies demonstrate collective decision-making, efficient problem-solving, and adaptive responses to environmental challenges that could be considered forms of distributed intelligence.

**The Domestication Effect**: Domestic animals (dogs, horses, pigs) show sophisticated social cognition adapted to human environments, raising questions about whether human-oriented intelligence should be weighted differently in comparative assessments.

Recent neuroscience research has also revealed the importance of neural organization over brain size. Bird brains pack neurons more densely than mammalian brains, allowing for sophisticated cognition in smaller packages. This finding further undermines simple brain-size-based rankings of intelligence.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed this specific claim in their fact-checking database. The absence of coverage likely reflects the claim's complex nature—it straddles the boundary between empirical fact and categorical assumption. While Snopes typically focuses on discrete, verifiable claims with clear true/false determinations, the dolphin intelligence ranking involves deeper questions about how we define, measure, and compare cognitive abilities across species.

The claim's persistence in popular culture and educational contexts, combined with its scientific complexity, makes it an ideal candidate for the contested-consensus category. Unlike straightforward factual claims about dolphin behaviors (which can be verified through observation), the "second most intelligent" assertion requires accepting particular frameworks for defining and measuring intelligence across species.
