# Did Dr. Roger Hodkinson Call COVID-19 a Hoax?


## 1. The Claim

In November 2020, social media platforms amplified a video showing a man identifying himself as Dr. Roger Hodkinson speaking during a public comment period at an Edmonton, Alberta city council meeting. In the footage, Hodkinson characterized the COVID-19 pandemic as "the biggest hoax ever perpetrated on an unsuspecting public" and asserted that the disease was no more dangerous than seasonal influenza. The video circulated widely, accumulating millions of views across Facebook, Twitter, and YouTube before platform moderation measures reduced its distribution.

The accompanying social media commentary framed Hodkinson's remarks as authoritative on the grounds that he was, according to the viral posts, "the Chairman of the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada." This credential was deployed strategically to confer legitimacy on claims that would otherwise be dismissed as fringe. The implication was clear: if the chairman of Canada's premier medical credentialing body believed COVID-19 was a hoax, then perhaps the pandemic response was indeed an overreaction driven by fear rather than science.

The claim operated on multiple levels. At the surface level, it asserted that a qualified medical professional had declared the pandemic a hoax. At a deeper level, it suggested institutional betrayal—that the medical establishment was either complicit in or duped by an alleged overreaction. And at the most insidious level, it invited viewers to extrapolate from one dissenting voice to a general conclusion about the illegitimacy of public health measures that had already caused unprecedented economic and social disruption.

Snopes rated this claim "Mixture" in a fact check published November 23, 2020, authored by Dan Evon. The rating acknowledged that while a person named Roger Hodkinson did make these statements at a city council meeting, he was not in fact the Chairman of the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada, and his views did not represent medical consensus. This framing—correct as far as it goes—raises deeper questions about how fact-checking platforms evaluate dissenting expert voices during public health emergencies, the epistemic status of institutional credentials in determining truth, and the relationship between scientific consensus and legitimate scientific disagreement.


## 2. What's Actually True

Several factual components of the Snopes assessment are well-supported and require no revision.

Dr. Roger Hodkinson is a Canadian pathologist and the chief executive officer of a biotechnology company that developed COVID-19 testing products. His educational credentials are genuine: he holds a medical degree from the University of Alberta and is licensed to practice pathology in the province of Alberta. However, he has never served as Chairman of the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada. The Royal College issued a formal statement on November 19, 2020, explicitly distancing itself from Hodkinson's remarks and clarifying that he held no official position with the organization. The confusion may have arisen from Hodkinson's past service on various college committees, but the claim that he chaired the institution was false.

Hodkinson's central assertion—that COVID-19 was no worse than seasonal influenza—does not align with the medical and epidemiological consensus as it stood in November 2020. By that point, the coronavirus pandemic had resulted in over 1.3 million deaths worldwide, according to WHO data. The case fatality rate of COVID-19 was estimated at approximately 2 to 3 percent in most settings, compared to seasonal influenza's 0.1 to 0.2 percent. COVID-19 also demonstrated substantially higher rates of hospitalization, intensive care utilization, long-term complications, and secondary transmission than influenza. The claim of equivalence was scientifically unsupportable even in the early months of the pandemic before vaccines became available.

Snopes correctly notes that social media amplification of the video relied on the mistaken institutional credential to confer authority. The video was shared by accounts with substantial followings, including conservative media personalities and anti-lockdown activist groups, who framed it as evidence that the medical establishment was suppressing dissent. The archived versions Snopes cites confirm that many shares included language like "Chairman of the Royal College says COVID is a hoax" or similar formulations that went well beyond what Hodkinson himself claimed about his position.

However, there are factual dimensions Snopes did not fully address. Hodkinson's statements at the Edmonton city council meeting must be understood in the context of his professional history and the regulatory environment in which he operated. As CEO of a company offering COVID-19 testing services, Hodkinson had a financial interest in the pandemic response—though in the opposite direction from what one might assume: his company stood to benefit from sustained testing demand rather from pandemic denial. More significantly, Hodkinson had previously been involved in disputes with Canadian medical regulatory bodies over his public statements on COVID-19, including an investigation by the College of Physicians and Surgeons of Alberta into whether his comments constituted professional misconduct. This regulatory context—the institutional response to physician dissent during a public health emergency—is itself a matter of legitimate ethical and legal debate that Snopes' binary framework does not capture.

The video's framing also warrants closer scrutiny. Hodkinson was speaking during the public comment period of a municipal council meeting that was addressing proposed public health bylaws. His remarks were adversarial by design—public comment periods are explicitly structured as opportunities for citizens to express disagreement with proposed policies. While this does not make his factual claims more accurate, it provides important context for understanding the rhetorical situation in which the statements were made. Snopes presents the video as a straightforward instance of misinformation without acknowledging the procedural context that shaped its production.


## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes' fact check makes several important contributions to public understanding of this episode. The correction of the institutional credential is essential: the viral framing relied on a false claim about Hodkinson's position, and Snopes provided clear documentation of the Royal College's disavowal. The distinction between what Hodkinson said and what online amplifiers attributed to him is analytically useful and well-supported by the archived sources Snopes provides. The acknowledgment that COVID-19 resulted in over 1.3 million deaths by November 2020, while straightforward, serves as a necessary corrective to the claim that the pandemic was somehow manufactured or exaggerated.

Where Snopes falls short is in its treatment of the broader epistemological and institutional questions raised by the episode. The "Mixture" rating is applied in a way that suggests the false component is merely the credential, while the factual dispute about COVID-19's severity is resolved by reference to institutional consensus. But this framing sidesteps several important dimensions:

First, Snopes does not adequately address the legitimate scientific debates that existed within the medical community in November 2020 about mitigation strategies, lockdown efficacy, and the appropriate balance between disease control and economic/social costs. By reducing Hodkinson's dissent to simple error, Snopes conflates disagreement with public health policy with factual inaccuracy about the virus. Hodkinson's claim that COVID-19 was equivalent to influenza was indeed factually wrong by every epidemiological measure. But the video was being circulated not primarily to advance a scientific claim about case fatality rates, but to argue against specific policy measures—lockdowns, business closures, mask mandates—that were being debated in Edmonton and around the world. Snopes' format prevents it from addressing this distinction between factual claims about the virus and normative claims about policy responses.

Second, Snopes does not examine the institutional dynamics that produced the regulatory response to Hodkinson's comments. The College of Physicians and Surgeons of Alberta's investigation into Hodkinson raises legitimate questions about the boundaries of professional speech during public health emergencies. Should physicians be subject to professional discipline for public statements that contradict official recommendations? Where is the line between expressing reasoned disagreement with policy and spreading harmful misinformation? These are contested questions with reasonable arguments on multiple sides. Snopes presents the regulatory response as simply the appropriate institutional correction of error, when in fact it represents one position in an ongoing debate about professional autonomy, free expression, and public health authority.

Third, the "Mixture" rating itself deserves scrutiny. Snopes defines "Mixture" as a rating applied when "the claim is a combination of true and false elements." In this case, the true element appears to be that a person named Roger Hodkinson said what the video shows him saying. The false element is that he is Chairman of the Royal College. But this framing implies that the core factual dispute—whether COVID-19 is a "hoax"—is not directly rated. A reader might reasonably ask: if the central claim is that a medical authority called COVID-19 a hoax, and the medical authority did in fact say this, but his institutional affiliation was misrepresented, does "Mixture" adequately capture the epistemological status of the claim? A case could be made that "False" would be more appropriate, given that the implied argument (COVID-19 is a hoax because an authoritative medical figure says so) rests on a false premise about that figure's authority. The "Mixture" rating, by emphasizing the factual accuracy of the video's content (he did say it) rather than the truth value of its implied argument, may inadvertently lend partial credence to a claim that is fundamentally misleading.

Snopes also does not address the broader information ecosystem in which this video circulated. The video was part of a wave of "hidden camera" and "whistleblower" content that proliferated during the pandemic, much of which followed similar rhetorical patterns: a credentialed professional reveals suppressed truth to a captive audience, the establishment responds with censorship rather than engagement. This meta-narrative about truth suppression and institutional betrayal was itself a recurring theme in pandemic misinformation. By treating the Hodkinson video as an isolated claim rather than as an instance of a recognizable genre, Snopes misses an opportunity to help readers recognize similar patterns in future content.


## 4. Why This Matters

The Hodkinson episode illustrates several structural challenges in the contemporary information environment that transcend any single fact check.

The first concerns the relationship between institutional credentials and epistemic authority. The viral spread of the Hodkinson video depended on a specific error about his position within the Royal College, but it also exploited a genuine public uncertainty about how to evaluate competing expert claims. When credentialed professionals disagree—as they inevitably do on complex scientific questions—how should non-experts determine whom to trust? Fact-checking platforms play an increasingly important role in this determination, but their authority is itself contested. Snopes rates claims based on its assessment of the evidence and the consensus of relevant expert communities. But this approach implicitly assumes that expert consensus can be reliably identified and that it constitutes the appropriate standard for truth. During a pandemic characterized by rapidly evolving scientific understanding, contested policy responses, and genuine methodological disagreements among experts, this assumption is more fragile than Snopes' confident framing suggests.

The second concerns the relationship between factual accuracy and policy disagreement. The Hodkinson video was deployed in a political context: opponents of lockdown measures and other public health restrictions used it to argue that the pandemic response was disproportionate. Snopes' fact check corrected factual errors while remaining agnostic on the policy questions. But in practice, the correction of factual errors is rarely neutral in its policy implications. If the claim that COVID-19 is no worse than influenza is false, then the argument that public health measures are disproportionate to the threat loses one of its empirical premises. This tight coupling of factual claims with policy positions means that fact-checking inevitably has political valence, even when individual fact checkers exercise scrupulous neutrality. Snopes' format obscures this reality by presenting each check as an isolated evaluation of factual accuracy.

The third concerns the epistemology of dissent during emergencies. Democratic societies have historically balanced the value of open debate against the need for coordinated action during crises. The COVID-19 pandemic tested this balance as never before in the modern communications era. Platforms removed content they deemed harmful, governments restricted speech they considered dangerous, and professional bodies disciplined members who contradicted official guidance. These measures were undertaken in the name of public health, but they also represent significant constraints on the free exchange of ideas that democracies depend on. Snopes, as a fact-checking platform, is part of this ecosystem of information management. By ruling certain claims out of bounds, it necessarily shrinks the space of permissible public discourse. This is not necessarily wrong—correcting factual errors is a legitimate function—but it is a power that deserves more explicit acknowledgment than standard fact-checking formats provide.


## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article by Dan Evon, published November 23, 2020, relies on several archival sources and institutional statements. The Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada statement disavowing Hodkinson was publicly available and is correctly characterized. The archived versions of the viral posts (via archive.vn/OIAdc and archive.vn/YDpSo) confirm the social media framing that Snopes describes. The death toll figure of 1.3 million is consistent with WHO data as of late November 2020.

Additional context that would strengthen the analysis: The College of Physicians and Surgeons of Alberta (CPSA) announced an investigation into Hodkinson's comments in December 2020, approximately one month after the Snopes article was published. The investigation was framed around whether Hodkinson's public statements constituted "unprofessional conduct" under Alberta's Health Professions Act. This regulatory action, while subsequent to the Snopes article, reflects the institutional context that the article gestures toward but does not fully develop.

Hodkinson's professional background deserves more thorough examination. He is a Fellow of the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada (a designation that indicates completion of specialty training and certification), and he has served on Royal College committees in the past. The viral claim that he was "Chairman" of the Royal College appears to have originated from conflating his committee service with institutional leadership. Snopes correctly identifies this error but does not explain the distinction between Fellowship (a professional credential held by thousands of Canadian physicians) and Chairmanship (an institutional leadership position), which would help readers understand the nature of the mistake.

The Edmonton city council meeting was held on November 16, 2020, to consider proposed public health bylaws including mask mandates and business restrictions. Hodkinson spoke during the public comment period, which is explicitly designated for citizen input. His remarks were not "secretly recorded" as some online outlets claimed, but were part of the publicly streamed proceedings of a municipal government meeting. The framing of the video as "leaked" or "secretly recorded" was itself a misleading element of the viral narrative that Snopes does not address.

The broader context of COVID-19 misinformation in Canada in late 2020 includes several similar episodes: a group of Ontario physicians who signed a letter questioning the severity of the pandemic, an Alberta doctor who was investigated for spreading COVID-19 misinformation on social media, and multiple instances of hospital staff being disciplined for violating public health protocols. The Hodkinson case is best understood as one node in a network of physician dissent that emerged during the pandemic, raising questions about professional regulation, freedom of expression, and the role of medical expertise in democratic deliberation.


## 6. Verdict

**Classification**: **Distorted but Grounded — Refuted**

The central claim that Dr. Roger Hodkinson called COVID-19 a hoax is factually accurate in the narrow sense: he did make those statements during public testimony before the Edmonton city council. However, the implied argument that his statements carry exceptional weight because of his institutional position is false, and the scientific claim that COVID-19 is equivalent to seasonal influenza was and remains unsupported by the evidence.

**What Snopes gets right:**
- Hodkinson is not the Chairman of the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada
- The Royal College formally disavowed any association with his comments
- COVID-19 had killed over 1.3 million people by November 2020
- His claims about COVID-19's severity contradict medical consensus
- Social media amplification relied on the false institutional credential

**What Snopes misses or underplays:**
- The distinction between factual claims about the virus and normative claims about policy responses
- The legitimate ethical and legal debates about professional regulation of physician speech during emergencies
- The procedural context of public comment testimony in municipal governance
- The meta-narrative patterns (whistleblower reveals suppressed truth, establishment responds with censorship) that characterize this genre of content
- The "Mixture" rating's ambiguity regarding whether the rating applies to the credential claim or the underlying factual claim about COVID-19
- The epistemic questions about how non-experts should evaluate competing expert claims during rapidly evolving scientific emergencies

The Hodkinson case ultimately reveals more about the structural challenges of pandemic-era information management than it does about any individual actor. The Snopes fact check performs its stated function—clarifying a specific factual error—but its format prevents it from addressing the deeper questions about institutional authority, expert dissent, and democratic deliberation that the episode raises. Readers who rely solely on the fact check may leave with the impression that the matter is settled, when in fact the underlying tensions between public health authority, professional autonomy, and democratic accountability remain unresolved and continue to shape public discourse around public health emergencies.
