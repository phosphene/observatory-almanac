# Drinking or transfusing young blood has anti-aging health benefits

## 1. The Claim

Few ideas at the boundary of biomedical science and cultural mythology have generated quite as much internet fascination, tabloid coverage, and Silicon Valley rumor as the proposition that young blood — consumed, injected, or transfused — holds the key to reversing aging. The claim appears in multiple forms, ranging from cautious extrapolations of legitimate scientific research to baroque extensions into full-scale longevity biohacking ideology.

In its most extreme popular form: drinking or regularly receiving transfusions of blood from young donors will meaningfully reverse biological aging, restore youthful vitality, and extend healthy lifespan. The basis cited for this belief varies. Some proponents point to historical legends — Elizabeth Báthory, the 16th-century Hungarian countess reputedly accused of bathing in the blood of young women to preserve her youth; vampire mythology more broadly; ancient accounts of rulers consuming the blood of young soldiers to maintain strength. Others point to genuine scientific research, particularly the striking results of parabiosis experiments in mice, in which older animals surgically joined to share circulation with younger animals showed measurable signs of biological rejuvenation.

A commercial dimension emerged in the late 2010s, when several US-based startups began offering infusions of plasma from young donors to wealthy older customers, sometimes framed as clinical trials, at prices ranging from several thousand to tens of thousands of dollars per infusion. This commercial activity attracted regulatory attention and media coverage, creating a news cycle in which legitimate aging science, Silicon Valley transhumanism, and medical fraud concern became entwined in public perception.

The claim is not a single, simple assertion. It encompasses a spectrum from the clearly false (drinking blood is an anti-aging intervention) through the scientifically interesting but unproven (young plasma transfusion reverses human aging) to the actively researched (specific circulating factors in young blood modulate aging-related biological processes). Evaluating it requires separating these levels clearly.

## 2. What's Actually True

The scientific foundation for young-blood interest is genuine and has produced some of the most compelling results in biogerontology research over the past two decades. Parabiosis experiments — particularly heterochronic parabiosis, in which the circulatory systems of a young mouse and an old mouse are surgically linked — have consistently demonstrated that exposure to young circulating factors can produce measurable biological rejuvenation in older subjects, while exposure to old circulating factors can accelerate biological aging in young subjects.

The landmark work of Amy Wagers, Saul Villeda, and their respective collaborators established through controlled mouse studies that certain circulating proteins — including growth differentiation factor 11 (GDF11) and factors influencing neurogenesis — behaved differently in young versus old blood, and that restoring young-blood factor concentrations in older animals produced functional improvements: increased neurogenesis in the hippocampus, improved cardiac muscle function, enhanced skeletal muscle regeneration, and altered gene expression patterns associated with more youthful tissue states. These results were published in high-quality peer-reviewed journals (Cell, Nature Medicine, Science) and represent genuinely productive lines of scientific inquiry.

Several specific proteins have been identified as candidate mediators of young blood's rejuvenating effects in animal models. Pigment epithelium-derived factor (PEDF) has been implicated in muscle stem cell regulation. GDF11, though its role has been contested and partially re-evaluated in subsequent studies, remains a subject of ongoing investigation. Klotho, a protein that declines with age, shows protective effects against age-related pathology in animal models. These findings have motivated significant scientific effort to identify precisely which circulating factors produce observed effects, with the aim of eventually developing pharmaceutical interventions that deliver those factors without requiring actual blood transfusion.

However, the gap between these animal-model results and the popular claim about young blood as a human anti-aging intervention is very large, and crosses several critical thresholds.

First, mouse parabiosis involves surgical conjoining of circulatory systems so that blood — including cells, proteins, and the full range of signaling molecules — circulates continuously between the animals for extended periods. This shares very little with administering discrete plasma infusions to a human subject over a clinic visit. The continuous systemic exposure in parabiosis, combined with the dramatic biological differences between young and old animals in accelerated mouse aging models, does not translate straightforwardly to a clinical protocol for humans aging on a decades-long timescale.

Second, human clinical trials of young plasma infusion and related interventions have produced results that are, at best, modest and inconsistent. Early clinical work by Alkahest and other research groups tested infusions of young donor plasma in Alzheimer's patients and elderly individuals with various biomarker endpoints. A pilot study of young plasma infusion in Alzheimer's disease found the procedure safe but detected no significant improvement in cognitive outcomes. Other small studies have shown improvements in specific biomarkers — reduced inflammatory markers, altered DNA methylation age, modest changes in kidney function metrics — but these biomarker shifts have not been translated into demonstrated clinical benefits in randomized controlled trials.

In 2025, a study from the Buck Institute for Research on Aging reported that therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) — a procedure in which a patient's plasma is removed and replaced with albumin solution, removing aged plasma factors rather than adding young ones — reduced biological age as measured by epigenetic clocks by an average of 1.32 years in older adults, with greater reduction when combined with intravenous immunoglobulin therapy. This is a scientifically interesting result for an established medical procedure (TPE has long been used for specific autoimmune and hematological conditions), but it is not evidence that young blood transfusion works as popularly claimed. The mechanism — diluting or removing harmful aged plasma factors — is conceptually different from the popular claim that young blood's properties are transferred to older recipients.

Third, and most important for evaluating the popular mythology: drinking blood is not and cannot be an anti-aging intervention through any known biological mechanism. Blood proteins are large macromolecules that are denatured by gastric acid and enzymatically broken down during normal digestion, just like any other protein source. The circulating proteins and factors that show biological effects in parabiosis experiments (and potentially in transfusion research) work through direct interaction with blood vessels, receptors, and tissues when in the bloodstream — they cannot survive oral ingestion to reach the bloodstream intact in functionally meaningful concentrations. Drinking blood is, from a biomedical standpoint, simply eating a protein-rich food. Any historical or mythological association between consuming blood and health benefits is not supported by any coherent biological mechanism.

The US Food and Drug Administration addressed the commercial young blood infusion market directly in February 2019, issuing a safety communication stating: "Young donor plasma infusions are not FDA-approved for any use, including anti-aging, memory and muscle function, or any other conditions linked to aging. We strongly discourage consumers from pursuing this therapy outside of clinical trials under appropriate institutional review board oversight and regulatory supervision." This was a notable statement from a regulatory agency, explicitly warning consumers against paying for a service being marketed as anti-aging therapy without adequate clinical evidence.

The scientific picture is therefore: parabiosis in mice demonstrates compelling rejuvenating effects from young circulating factors; the specific mediating factors remain partially identified; human clinical trials have produced modest and inconsistent biomarker results; therapeutic plasma exchange (removing old factors) shows some promise; and no demonstrated anti-aging benefit from young blood transfusion has been established in humans through well-controlled clinical trials. The popular claim extrapolates far beyond available evidence.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The young blood belief draws on one of humanity's oldest narrative templates. Every major culture has vampiric or blood-drinking mythologies featuring some version of the idea that vital life force — strength, youth, magical power — can be transferred through blood. These narratives exist independently of one another across geographically isolated cultures, suggesting they tap into deep human intuitions about blood as a locus of life, vitality, and identity.

Modern belief in young blood's anti-aging properties has the unusual advantage of a genuine scientific substrate: the parabiosis research is real, published in top journals, and has been widely reported by science journalists. The problem is a translation error — between the controlled surgical conditions of mouse parabiosis and the discrete commercial plasma infusion; between continuous systemic exposure and periodic transfusion; between mouse aging timescales (months for a full lifespan) and human aging timescales (decades); between biomarker changes and demonstrated clinical benefit.

Popular science journalism about longevity research has a structural incentive to emphasize dramatic findings over careful qualification. A headline reading "Young blood reverses aging in mice" is more engaging than "Certain circulating protein fractions modulate age-related gene expression in heterochronic parabiosis models, with uncertain translation to human aging therapies." The dramatic version travels farther and gets compressed further in retelling, stripping away the methodological context that would allow readers to calibrate their expectations appropriately.

The Silicon Valley biohacking culture has also contributed significantly to mainstreaming young blood beliefs. A set of prominent technology investors and longevity researchers have been publicly associated with exploration of young plasma as part of aggressive personal aging-intervention protocols. This cultural visibility gave young blood claims an air of rational, systematic investigation — because smart, successful people were apparently pursuing it — that insulated the belief from some of the skepticism that obvious pseudoscience receives.

Commercial platforms that offered young plasma infusions for substantial fees benefited from telling clients they were receiving cutting-edge longevity science rather than paying for an experimental and inadequately-tested procedure. When wealthy clients reported feeling better after treatments — which some did — the placebo effect, general wellness effects of elective medical procedures, and expectation effects were attributed to the young blood itself.

The fact that legitimate scientists were asking genuine questions about young circulating factors also created a halo effect, in which the credibility of animal-model research was extended to human applications that had not received the same experimental support. When someone criticizing young blood claims could be countered with "but top journals have published this research," the distinction between mouse parabiosis and human plasma infusion was effectively obscured.

## 4. Verdict

**FALSE for the popular claim, PREMATURE-EXTRAPOLATION for the scientific claim**: Drinking blood as an anti-aging intervention is biologically inert as a health strategy — blood proteins are digested like any other food, and no credible mechanism exists for oral blood consumption to deliver anti-aging benefits. The claim that commercial young plasma transfusions reverse human aging is not supported by adequate clinical evidence and was explicitly warned against by the FDA in 2019.

The underlying scientific question — whether specific young circulating factors can modulate aging-related biological processes in humans — is a legitimate area of active research with genuinely interesting animal-model results. Clinical investigation of therapeutic plasma exchange and specific identified factors (rather than whole young plasma) represents a more targeted direction that current evidence supports investigating further. As of 2026, that research has not produced the demonstrated human clinical benefits required to validate the popular claim.

The category most accurate for this entry is premature extrapolation: real science has been stretched well beyond its demonstrated scope by popular media, commercial interests, and cultural mythology, producing a belief in young blood anti-aging benefits that is substantially in advance of available evidence.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The young blood story illuminates a recurring pattern in how legitimate scientific discoveries become distorted in translation from research context to popular belief. Parabiosis research represents one of the genuinely exciting frontiers in biogerontology, raising fundamental questions about whether aging is driven by cell-intrinsic processes or by systemic signaling environments — questions with profound implications for how we understand and potentially intervene in aging. But the excitement is for basic science findings in animal models, not for commercial applications in humans, and the gap between those two things is very large.

The commercial dimension of the young blood phenomenon is worth examining carefully as an example of how entrepreneurial incentives can distort scientific communication. Several companies, operating in the grey zone between research and clinical practice, charged customers substantial sums for young plasma infusions framed as innovative anti-aging treatments. The companies were sometimes structured as research studies to create an appearance of scientific legitimacy without the rigorous controls, randomized assignment, blinding, or statistical power that would allow claims of efficacy to be evaluated. The FDA's 2019 warning represented a relatively unusual direct consumer advisory about this specific commercial practice, reflecting regulatory concern that commercial hype had moved significantly ahead of evidence.

The phenomenon connects to the broader longevity industry, which has grown substantially in the 2020s as wealthy individuals have become increasingly interested in aggressive personal aging interventions. The market now includes a range of practices with varying evidence bases — from well-established interventions (exercise, caloric restriction, sleep hygiene, blood glucose management) through plausible but insufficiently tested approaches (metformin, rapamycin, NAD precursor supplementation) to speculative or insufficiently supported claims (young plasma infusions, various IV drip protocols). Young blood sits toward the speculative end of this spectrum.

The scientific progress that has emerged from taking young circulating factors seriously is real and has generated productive research trajectories. The identification of specific proteins (PEDF, GDF11, Klotho, and others) as potential anti-aging mediators has motivated genuine molecular biology work aimed at developing targeted pharmaceutical interventions. Rather than requiring transfer of whole young blood, this approach aims to identify which specific factors produce measured effects and develop them as precision medicines — a fundamentally different approach from the popular claim about young blood, and one where the evidence base is more tractable.

Therapeutic plasma exchange (TPE) has emerged as a parallel line of investigation that may ultimately prove more clinically productive than young blood infusion. The hypothesis here — that removing accumulated harmful factors from aged plasma may be more important than adding young-blood factors — reframes the question in ways that are better suited to clinical testing, since TPE is an established medical procedure with known safety profiles, and the intervention is more mechanistically specific than whole young blood transfer.

The historical and mythological dimensions of young blood belief are worth noting as a case study in how ancient cultural templates can be reactivated by partial scientific validation. The vampire's anti-aging feeding is an ancient narrative. The notion that old rulers could restore youth through contact with young life energy appears in sources spanning multiple centuries and continents. Modern parabiosis research, by demonstrating that there genuinely are demonstrable biological effects of young versus old blood circulation, gave this ancient template a patina of scientific respectability. The result is a recursive amplification in which pseudoscientific belief borrows credibility from adjacent legitimate science without inheriting its methodological constraints.

Public health communicators face a genuine challenge in this space: how to communicate the genuine scientific interest of parabiosis and circulating-factor research without validating the premature human applications and commercial exploitation that have followed. Too dismissive a response risks appearing to deny real scientific findings; too permissive a framing risks appearing to endorse commercial services that lack adequate evidence and may cause direct harm (blood transfusions carry real medical risks including transfusion reactions, infection transmission, and immune sensitization).

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not published a dedicated fact-check on the specific claim that young blood transfusion or consumption provides anti-aging health benefits. Given the prominence of the topic in technology culture reporting, the FDA warning about commercial young plasma infusion services, and the ongoing popular fascination with longevity hacking, this represents a substantive gap in the fact-checking record.

The absence of comprehensive Snopes coverage has meant that many people encountering young blood claims — in technology journalism, longevity discussion forums, or social media — have difficulty accessing a single authoritative synthesis of what has and has not been demonstrated. The story has been covered extensively in science journalism (The Atlantic, MIT Technology Review, STAT News, Science) and in regulatory contexts (the FDA 2019 consumer advisory), but these sources are fragmented across different contexts and do not provide the integrated claim-evidence-verdict structure that a dedicated fact-check would offer.

Existing fact-checking coverage has generally focused on specific peripheral stories — whether a particular celebrity or billionaire has received young blood transfusions, or whether specific commercial services have been shut down — rather than the core scientific question of whether young blood provides measurable anti-aging benefits.

A thorough fact-check would benefit from distinguishing the multiple levels of the claim: the animal model science (real and interesting), the human clinical trial evidence (modest and inconsistent as of current reporting), the commercial young plasma infusion services (FDA-warned, inadequately evidenced), and the popular mythology of drinking blood (biologically implausible). Collapsing these into a single true/false verdict risks either overstating the scientific support for the idea or unfairly dismissing genuine research progress. The most accurate framing — credible science is being distorted by premature commercial application and popular exaggeration — requires space for nuance that headline verdicts often cannot accommodate.

The story will likely generate additional fact-checking attention as human clinical trials proceed and as the longevity industry continues to grow. The combination of real scientific interest, wealthy consumer demand, active commercial activity, and ancient mythological resonance makes this one of the more durable and multi-layered health claims in the contemporary media environment.
