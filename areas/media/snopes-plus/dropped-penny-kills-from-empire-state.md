# A Penny Dropped from the Empire State Building Can Kill Someone


## 1. The Claim

The claim states that if a penny were dropped from the top of the Empire State Building in New York City, it would fall with sufficient velocity to kill a person standing on the street below. In its strongest form, the myth asserts that the penny would hit with the force of a bullet, penetrating the skull or causing fatal blunt trauma. Softer variants suggest the penny would at minimum cause serious injury — cracking a skull or driving itself into flesh. The claim circulates widely in casual conversation, is repeated in school settings, and has appeared in popular media, serving as a stock illustration of how dramatic height translates into lethal force.

The building itself provides the myth with its rhetorical power. Completed in 1931, the Empire State Building stands 1,250 feet to roof (1,454 feet to the tip of its antenna mast), and its 86th-floor observation deck — the most famous vantage point — sits at approximately 1,050 feet above street level. These are genuinely imposing heights, and the intuitive extrapolation that falling objects from such heights would acquire devastating force is psychologically compelling even if it misrepresents the relevant physics.

The penny is also important to the myth's internal logic. Its small size and low mass make it seem like the most innocuous possible object to drop — the reasoning proceeds that if even a humble penny would be lethal from this height, then the Empire State Building is a particularly dangerous place, and height in general is a more powerful force multiplier than people appreciate. The contrast between the mundane object and the supposedly dramatic outcome gives the myth its edge.

The claim has enough surface plausibility that it recurs in safety discussions about observation decks, in physics classrooms as an example of harmful misconceptions, and in popular culture as a cautionary assertion. It has been repeated by figures who should know better and is frequently offered as a piece of everyday physics that "most people get wrong." Its persistence makes it a useful case study in how incomplete physical intuitions generate durable false beliefs about projectile motion.


## 2. What Is Actually True

**Terminal velocity is the governing variable.** The central physical error in the myth is ignoring air resistance as a terminal constraint on falling velocity. In a vacuum, a penny dropped from 1,050 feet would indeed reach a dramatic velocity at impact — applying the kinematic equation $v = \sqrt{2gh}$, with $g \approx 9.8 \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $h \approx 320 \mathrm{m}$, yields a theoretical final velocity of approximately 79 m/s (~177 mph). This would indeed be potentially lethal. But this calculation applies only in the absence of an atmosphere.

In reality, as any object falls through air, it experiences a drag force that increases with the square of its velocity. When the drag force equals gravitational force, acceleration ceases and the object falls at a constant terminal velocity regardless of how much further it falls. The terminal velocity for any given object depends on its mass, its cross-sectional area perpendicular to the direction of fall, and its drag coefficient — a dimensionless number that captures the aerodynamic "slipperiness" of its shape.

A US penny weighs approximately 2.5 grams (post-1982 zinc-core composition) and has a diameter of 19.05 mm. Crucially, a dropped penny does not fall edge-on like a streamlined projectile. It tumbles as it falls, which means it presents its full face — a flat disc — to the airflow for much of its trajectory. This dramatically increases its effective drag. The drag coefficient for a flat disc perpendicular to airflow is approximately 1.1, compared to approximately 0.05 for a streamlined bullet-shaped object. This more than twenty-fold difference in aerodynamic drag is decisive.

**Experimental and theoretical estimates of penny terminal velocity.** Louis Bloomfield, a physicist at the University of Virginia, has extensively studied this question and concluded that a penny reaches a terminal velocity of approximately 25–40 mph, depending on its tumbling orientation during fall (Bloomfield, L.A., *How Things Work: The Physics of Everyday Life*, 2016, John Wiley & Sons). The MythBusters television program conducted empirical experiments with penny drops from heights sufficient to achieve terminal velocity, measured impact forces, and concluded the penny was painful — one host described being hit as a sting similar to a bee sting — but clearly non-lethal and insufficient to break skin under normal circumstances.

At 25–40 mph, the kinetic energy delivered by a 2.5g penny is approximately 0.09–0.23 joules. For comparison, a typical golf ball struck by a club delivers several hundred joules of kinetic energy, and even a low-powered air rifle pellet delivers around 5–10 joules. The penny's impact energy at terminal velocity is roughly comparable to being hit by a thrown grape — uncomfortable, capable of causing a bruise, but not capable of penetrating the skull or causing serious blunt-force trauma.

**Why height becomes irrelevant above a threshold.** A key physical insight is that once an object has fallen far enough to reach terminal velocity, additional height adds nothing to impact speed or force. The Empire State Building is 1,050 feet tall, but the critical height for a penny is the distance required to reach terminal velocity — estimated at approximately 50–100 feet. Above this height, each additional foot of fall adds zero velocity at impact. The penny achieves its maximum speed well before it passes the 20th floor, and the remaining thousand-plus feet of fall simply maintain but do not increase that speed. The building's imposing height, which is the source of the myth's rhetorical power, is physically irrelevant to the impact velocity for an object with the penny's aerodynamic properties.

**Contrast with genuinely dangerous dropped objects.** The penny case stands in instructive contrast to objects that are genuinely dangerous when dropped from height. A golf-ball-sized stone has a much higher mass-to-drag ratio and reaches a terminal velocity of roughly 150 mph — capable of causing serious injury. A bolt or washer falling from a construction scaffold has sufficient mass and a more favorable aerodynamic profile that its terminal velocity can enter genuinely dangerous ranges, particularly if falling edge-first. Workers in industries from construction to oil and gas are required to use "tool lanyards" precisely because high-mass metal tools dropped from elevated work platforms can be lethal. The distinction is not height but mass-to-drag ratio — the physical quantity that determines terminal velocity.

A bullet fired horizontally also illustrates the principle from the other direction: a bullet's lethality derives not from gravity but from the chemical propulsion that imparts a high initial velocity, and horizontal bullets lose velocity and energy over distance due to drag in the same way that vertical dropped objects gain and then cap velocity.

**What actually happens if a penny is dropped from the Empire State Building.** For practical purposes, the scenario also has to contend with the fact that the observation deck of the Empire State Building is enclosed by a fence and mesh that prevents objects from being casually dropped. Wind conditions at 1,050 feet are also substantial — horizontal wind speeds can easily deflect a light, tumbling penny significantly from vertical, such that predicting where it would land is impossible. Pennies dropped from nearby large buildings in archival accounts have been reported to drift blocks away, land in gutters, or simply become untrackable. The probability of a dropped penny reaching the specific location of a pedestrian's head — even if that head were unprotected — involves multiple coincidences of trajectory, deflection, and timing that further reduce the practical risk.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The gravitational potential energy intuition.** Human intuition about falling objects is shaped primarily by the experience of falling solid objects at human scale — a rock dropped from a wall, a tool dropped from a ladder, a glass falling from a table. In all of these cases, greater heights produce more damaging impacts, and the relationship appears roughly proportional to height. This experiential learning produces a reliable heuristic for the heights we ordinarily encounter. Extrapolating this heuristic to a 1,000-foot building feels natural: if a rock dropped from 10 feet can break a toe, and from 100 feet could injure someone severely, then from 1,000 feet it must be lethal. The extrapolation is numerically coherent but physically wrong because it ignores the nonlinear role of drag that terminates velocity at heights well below 1,000 feet.

**The mass-velocity conflation.** A related cognitive error involves conflating the potential consequences of high-velocity impacts with the assumption that height produces high velocity without limit. Bullets kill because they travel at very high velocities, and the association between high-velocity impacts and lethality is empirically grounded. The myth transfers this association to dropped objects without accounting for the terminal velocity constraint. A penny falling at 35 mph is not comparable to a bullet traveling at 900 mph, but the verbal characterization "falling from 1,000 feet" activates associations with speed that are not warranted.

**The absence of counterexperience.** Most people have no personal experience of being hit by a dropped object from extreme height, which means experiential correction of the myth is unavailable. The claim is essentially untestable in daily life, which allows it to persist as a plausible hypothesis that never encounters disconfirming evidence. Unlike some health myths that are corrected when predicted outcomes fail to materialize, the penny-from-the-Empire-State myth is unlikely to be falsified by anyone's personal experience.

**Epistemic novelty — the mass-to-drag ratio as a hidden variable in falling objects.** The key insight that popular accounts of this myth consistently underemphasize is the concept of the mass-to-drag ratio as the decisive physical variable determining terminal velocity. This ratio — sometimes expressed in related terms as the ballistic coefficient — explains why a feather, a penny, a baseball, a cannonball, and a raindrop all fall to Earth at completely different terminal velocities despite experiencing the same gravitational acceleration. The ratio captures in a single quantity the competition between gravitational force (proportional to mass) and drag force (proportional to cross-sectional area, drag coefficient, and velocity squared).

For the penny, the relevant comparison is between its mass (2.5 g) and its aerodynamic drag when tumbling (high, due to its flat-disc geometry). The penny has an unfavorable mass-to-drag ratio: it is light relative to its area and has a high drag coefficient due to its shape. Contrast this with a lead musket ball of similar diameter: much higher mass, similar area, similar drag coefficient — terminal velocity of roughly 200 mph, which is genuinely dangerous. Shape matters even more: a penny falling edge-first (which it doesn't reliably do) would have a much lower drag coefficient, reach a higher terminal velocity, and deliver substantially more kinetic energy. The mythology implicitly assumes the penny falls like a bullet — streamlined and stable — when in reality it tumbles through the air like a very poor frisbee.

This dimensional analysis insight also illuminates why raindrops do not damage skin despite falling from thousands of feet of cloud: they too reach terminal velocity well before impact (roughly 9 mph for average droplet size), and their mass is far too low to deliver meaningful kinetic energy even at that velocity. The atmosphere's resistance function operates as a universal terminal-velocity cap that is shape-dependent and mass-dependent in ways that the naive free-fall intuition does not capture.

**The authority of the specific example.** The Empire State Building is arguably the most famous skyscraper in the world, and its observation deck is a cultural landmark. Claims attached to famous locations gain a kind of authority by association: because the location is real and dramatic, the physical claim attached to it feels more credible than a generic assertion about "a tall building." The mythic status of the building lends verisimilitude to the physical claim even when the physics is identical to any sufficiently tall structure.


## 4. Verdict

**False.** A penny dropped from the Empire State Building observation deck would not kill a person on the street below. The penny's terminal velocity is approximately 25–40 mph, governed by its large aerodynamic drag relative to its small mass. This terminal velocity is reached well before the penny descends past the lower floors of the building, meaning the building's exceptional height contributes nothing to impact velocity beyond what would be achieved by a drop of 50–100 feet. At terminal velocity, the kinetic energy delivered by a 2.5g penny is approximately 0.09–0.23 joules — sufficient to cause a sting or superficial bruise but entirely incapable of fracturing bone or causing lethal blunt trauma.

The myth's persistence derives from the gravitational potential energy intuition common in everyday experience at human-scale heights, extrapolated — incorrectly — to heights where terminal velocity becomes the binding constraint. The physical mechanism of terminal velocity, and specifically the penny's unfavorable mass-to-drag ratio due to its flat tumbling geometry, is the decisive factor that the myth ignores.

Dropped pennies are not safe in the sense that no one should be cavalier about dropping objects from height — there are more dangerous objects, and even a penny sting at 35 mph is unpleasant. But the specific claim that a penny "would kill someone" is false, and the broader intuition that the building's height makes any dropped object automatically lethal is physically incorrect.

**Propositional status:** False
**Confidence:** High
**Epistemic basis:** Physical calculation confirmed by experimental measurement
**Folk concern:** Partially grounded (dropping objects from height is genuinely hazardous for heavy/dense objects)
**Mechanistic error:** Terminal velocity constraint ignored; mass-to-drag ratio not applied


## 5. The Wider Picture

The penny myth belongs to a family of urban legends about extreme height and falling objects that conflate gravitational potential energy with uncontrained terminal velocity. The same underlying error generates myths about icicles falling from skyscrapers ("deadly falling daggers"), about golf balls hit off aircraft ("supersonic projectiles"), and, in a different register, about swimming after eating ("digestion causes cramps and drowning"). In each case, a real physical or physiological mechanism is identified but incompletely understood, and extrapolation beyond experience produces a claim that sounds plausible but fails when the underlying constraints are examined.

The physics classroom value of this myth is substantial: it illustrates the concept of terminal velocity, drag as a velocity-dependent force, the difference between potential and kinetic energy under air-resistance conditions, and the dimensionless drag coefficient as a measure of aerodynamic efficiency. Indeed, several introductory physics textbooks and curricula use the penny-from-the-Empire-State scenario precisely as a worked example to teach these concepts, which means the myth paradoxically serves as a teaching vehicle for the physics that refutes it — a self-undermining piece of misconception pedagogy.

From a science communication standpoint, the myth is instructive because its debunking cannot be achieved by simple contradiction ("a penny wouldn't kill you") without providing the physical mechanism (terminal velocity, drag, mass-to-drag ratio). A simple contradiction leaves the listener's naive physical model intact and likely to regenerate the same conclusion later. Effective correction requires building the physical model of drag-limited fall from the ground up: demonstrating that air resistance is real and significant, that it produces a maximum falling speed that depends on the object's shape and mass, and that for a tumbling penny this maximum is far below lethal. Without this model construction, the debunking feels counter-intuitive and may not be retained.

The Empire State Building adds a layer of cultural resonance that makes this an enduring urban legend rather than just a casual misconception. The building occupies a particular niche in American mythology as a symbol of ambition and danger — King Kong scales it; suicides have been attempted from it; "falling off the Empire State Building" is a cultural image for maximum danger. The physical claim about pennies plugs into this existing cultural representation of the building as a place of extreme risk. The correction of the myth, to be fully effective, may need to engage with this cultural layer rather than addressing only the physics — acknowledging that the building is genuinely dangerous in various ways (suicides, construction accidents during its record-speed construction) while clarifying that aerodynamics places strict constraints on what small tumbling objects can do when dropped.

Studies of risk perception (Slovic, 1987, *Science*; Sunstein, 2002, *Risk and Reason*) demonstrate that people systematically overestimate dramatic, visible risks involving height and falling, associating them with severity because of their vividness, even when statistical expectation is low. The penny myth fits perfectly into this pattern: a dramatic scenario (penny from the Empire State Building kills a pedestrian) is intuitively compelling precisely because it is vivid and specific, and the cognitive ease of picturing the scenario reinforces belief in its possibility. The less-vivid physical reality — that air is quite thick to a tumbling coin — requires effortful model construction rather than easy visualization.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim as False, correctly identifying the terminal velocity argument as the central refutation. Their fact-check notes that a penny dropped from the Empire State Building would reach only a moderate terminal velocity due to air resistance and would be incapable of killing a pedestrian, consistent with the physics outlined above. The verdict aligns with the scientific consensus as expressed through physicist Louis Bloomfield's analysis and the MythBusters empirical test.

The Snopes treatment correctly identifies the decisive physical mechanism and applies it clearly. The primary gap in standard fact-check treatments — including Snopes — is the failure to convey the broader physical concept that makes the answer non-obvious and that would allow readers to apply the same logic to other scenarios. Without a clear explanation of mass-to-drag ratios and terminal velocity as a universal cap (not just an Empire State Building feature), readers who encounter the myth again in a different context (roof tiles, quarters, golf balls) cannot apply the corrective logic independently.

A more complete treatment would also quantify the impact forces involved — providing the comparison with bee stings, golf balls, or air rifle pellets — to give readers an intuitive grip on the actual energy delivered. Abstract assurances that "a penny won't kill you" are less convincing than a concrete comparison that grounds the claim in familiar experience.

For educators using this myth as a physics teaching tool, the most productive approach is to have students calculate the terminal velocity themselves using the drag equation, then compute the kinetic energy at impact, and compare it with known impact-energy thresholds for human injury — rather than simply presenting the answer. The myth's remarkable instructional value lies precisely in the gap between intuition and calculation, and that gap is best closed by working through the numbers rather than receiving a verdict.
