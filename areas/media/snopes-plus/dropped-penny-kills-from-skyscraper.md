# A penny dropped from a skyscraper can kill someone on the ground

## 1. The Claim

One of the most durable pieces of urban physics folklore holds that a penny dropped from the top of the Empire State Building - or any sufficiently tall skyscraper - would hit a pedestrian below with lethal force. In some versions of the story, the coin would move so fast it would embed itself in concrete, pierce a skull, or cut through flesh like a bullet. The claim is widespread enough to have been treated as fact in school classrooms, passed along at tourist sites, and invoked repeatedly as a cautionary reason not to drop things from tall buildings.

The logic seems sound on the surface: the Empire State Building is 1,454 feet tall to the tip of its antenna. Objects fall faster as they drop. By the time a penny has fallen 1,454 feet, it must be moving at tremendous speed. Tremendous speed plus an object striking a person equals serious injury or death. QED.

The problem is that this chain of reasoning, while seductive, omits a crucial physical process that completely changes the outcome.

## 2. What's Actually True

The penny cannot kill you, and it probably cannot seriously injure you. The physics are clear and have been confirmed both theoretically and experimentally.

**Terminal velocity:** Objects falling through air do not accelerate indefinitely. As an object falls faster, air resistance increases quadratically - double the speed, quadruple the drag. At some speed, drag equals the gravitational force pulling the object down, and acceleration stops. The object then falls at a constant speed called terminal velocity. For a penny, terminal velocity is approximately 25 to 50 miles per hour (roughly 40 to 80 km/h), depending on its orientation and degree of tumbling.

**The penny's poor aerodynamics:** A penny is a thin, flat disc weighing 2.5 grams. Unlike a bullet, which is designed to be aerodynamically stable and maintain orientation, a penny tumbles and flutters as it falls. This tumbling dramatically increases drag relative to the coin's weight, lowering terminal velocity significantly. A penny falling edge-on would behave differently than one falling flat, but neither orientation is stable - the coin continually shifts as it descends.

**Louis Bloomfield's calculations:** Louis Bloomfield, a professor of physics at the University of Virginia, has addressed this question extensively and calculated the penny's terminal velocity at roughly 25 mph when it's tumbling (the realistic case). At that speed, a penny striking your head would cause pain - roughly comparable to being hit by a thrown penny - but would not penetrate skin, fracture skull, or produce lethal injury. The penny simply doesn't have enough kinetic energy to do serious damage. Kinetic energy scales with the square of velocity (KE = 1⁄2mv2), but the low mass (2.5 grams) keeps the absolute energy figure small regardless of velocity.

**MythBusters experimental confirmation:** In Season 1, Episode 7 of *MythBusters* (2003), the team tested this myth directly. They constructed a device to fire pennies at human skin at velocities consistent with terminal velocity from a tall building, and they also dropped pennies from a 35-foot tower while a human volunteer stood below. The results were unambiguous: the penny stung but caused no injury. A penny dropped from any realistic height would not even break skin, let alone kill anyone. The episode rated the myth "busted."

**Why height above terminal velocity doesn't matter:** Once an object reaches terminal velocity, additional height adds zero speed to the final impact. Whether the penny falls from 500 feet or 1,500 feet, if it reaches terminal velocity before hitting the ground, the impact velocity is the same. The Empire State Building's height is irrelevant to the lethal-impact calculation once you recognize that the coin reaches equilibrium long before the ground.

**What actually would be dangerous:** A dense, aerodynamically stable, heavy object dropped from great height is a different matter. A large wrench, a heavy bolt, or a crowbar, depending on its weight, shape, and orientation, might maintain substantially higher velocity due to a much more favorable mass-to-drag ratio and might cause serious injury on impact. Construction sites, for this reason, have strict protocols about objects at elevation. But a penny shares almost nothing with these objects in terms of its aerodynamic profile.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The myth persists because it exploits a systematic failure in intuitive human reasoning about physics - one that is deep, well-documented, and almost universal in people without formal physics training.

**Intuitive physics ignores air resistance:** When most people think about a falling object, they apply something like basic kinematic equations: the object starts at rest, falls under gravity, and its speed when it hits the ground depends on the height of the fall. This reasoning is correct in a vacuum. But we don't live in a vacuum. Human intuition was calibrated in an environment where thrown rocks, spears, and heavy objects had trajectories broadly consistent with simplified physics because their mass-to-drag ratios are high enough that air resistance is a second-order effect over short distances. Air resistance is invisible and unfamiliar as a force, so it gets ignored.

**The "obviously scary" effect:** Tall buildings produce a powerful intuition of danger. Most people have an immediate visceral reaction to looking down from a skyscraper, and the emotional intensity of this response contaminates reasoning about specific physical scenarios. The combination of great height + falling object triggers a danger signal that overrides careful analysis of what actually happens aerodynamically.

**Missing the terminal velocity concept:** Terminal velocity is a concept that is taught in physics classes but rarely internalized by people who didn't take physics. The notion that a falling object has a speed ceiling - and that this ceiling is determined by drag, not by height - is genuinely counterintuitive. It seems to violate the basic expectation that taller = faster at impact.

**Vivid narrative payoff:** The claim makes for a compelling story, particularly in the context of a famous landmark like the Empire State Building. "Did you know a penny dropped from the Empire State Building could kill you?" is a far more striking thing to tell a tourist than "Did you know a penny reaches terminal velocity pretty quickly and would just sting?" Stories compete for attention on the basis of surprise and consequence, and the myth wins that competition easily.

**Secondhand transmission and authority laundering:** Once a myth is established enough that schoolteachers, tour guides, and parents repeat it, the claim acquires the patina of received wisdom. People who encounter it as children from authority figures are unlikely to subject it to critical scrutiny as adults, and the source-laundering that occurs in human transmission chains - "I heard it somewhere credible" - insulates it from challenge.

**The plausibility of the underlying physics analogy:** The myth gains further traction because it is *almost* physically coherent. Things dropped from greater heights *do* hit harder in normal circumstances (below terminal velocity). A heavier coin *would* do more damage than a lighter one. These partial truths make the myth plausible to anyone who hasn't worked through the full aerodynamic analysis.

## 4. Verdict

**REFUTED** - this myth is definitively false.

A penny dropped from the Empire State Building or any other skyscraper cannot kill a pedestrian below and is unlikely to cause serious injury. The coin reaches terminal velocity well before hitting the ground, at a speed of approximately 25-50 mph that produces impact energy insufficient to penetrate skin or cause lethal trauma. The physics have been calculated theoretically by professional physicists and confirmed experimentally.

The error in the myth is a classic case of naive physics: applying simplified kinematic analysis without accounting for air resistance, in a regime where air resistance is the dominant limiting factor. The myth is wrong in a way that is completely correctable with first-semester college physics, and its persistence is a product of intuitive reasoning rather than any genuine ambiguity in the evidence.

This does not mean dropping things from buildings is safe - heavy, aerodynamically efficient objects are a genuine hazard - but the penny specifically poses minimal threat beyond annoyance.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The penny myth is a window into the nature of intuitive physics and the conditions under which folk understanding of physical phenomena systematically diverges from reality.

**Intuitive vs. formal physics:** Research in cognitive science and science education has documented the systematic nature of naive physics errors. Aristotelian physics - the intuitive framework most people operate with - assumes that heavier objects fall faster, that objects in motion naturally decelerate to rest, and that forces are required for continued motion. These intuitions are broadly useful in everyday life but systematically wrong when applied outside the limited domain in which they were calibrated. Air resistance is particularly invisible to intuitive reasoning because it is invisible to the eye.

**The aerodynamics of dangerous falls:** While the penny is safe, the aerodynamics of falling objects is not merely academic. Construction site accidents involving falling tools and materials injure and kill workers regularly. The safety calculus depends on mass, shape, velocity, and aerodynamic stability in ways that naive physics cannot predict. A penny and a cinderblock don't just differ in size - they differ in the relationship between gravitational force and aerodynamic drag in ways that produce completely different impact profiles.

**Skyscraper physics more broadly:** The physics of very tall buildings involves many counterintuitive effects. Wind effects on supertall buildings require complex engineering. The Coriolis effect on very long falls (though negligible for the Empire State Building's height) becomes relevant for objects dropped from aircraft or spacecraft. Temperature and pressure changes over several hundred meters affect aerodynamics. None of this is captured by simple mental models.

**Why this myth category matters:** Myths about physics that generate fear - the penny, the "you can drown hours after leaving a pool," various electrical hazards - have a distinctive social function. They are often told by adults to children as cautionary tales, and they serve a genuine safety-signaling function even when the specific mechanism is false. The Empire State Building is a tourist attraction; warning visitors away from careless behavior near the observation deck has real social value. When the warning mechanism is a false physics story rather than accurate information, this creates a kind of epistemic pollution: people learn that heights are dangerous via a mechanism that doesn't work, and this makes them poorly calibrated about what *actually* makes heights dangerous.

**Mythbusters as a cultural institution:** The fact that MythBusters addressed this myth - and the myth is widely cited in fact-checking contexts in relation to that episode - reflects the role that popular science programming has played in recent decades in testing folk beliefs empirically. MythBusters at its best modeled something valuable: taking intuitive claims seriously enough to test them rather than dismissing them, and being willing to be surprised by results. The penny episode is a pedagogically clean case because the result is unambiguous and the physical mechanism is fully explicable.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The falling penny myth has been addressed more thoroughly by fact-checkers than many myths of comparable age and penetration, largely because it has a clean answer.

**Snopes:** Snopes has addressed the Empire State Building penny myth and rated it false, explaining terminal velocity and the coin's low mass. The entry is among the cleaner Snopes analyses because the physics permits a definitive answer with no ambiguity.

**MythBusters as authoritative reference:** The 2003 MythBusters episode has become a standard reference point in popular fact-checking of this claim. Its experimental methodology - actually firing pennies at human skin at terminal velocity and having someone stand below a drop - is appropriately empirical and produced a clear result. Many fact-checkers cite it alongside physicist analysis.

**The physics education context:** Unlike many myths that live in contested social or political territory, the penny myth lives in the domain of physics, where expert consensus is accessible, unambiguous, and not subject to motivated reasoning on either side. This makes it a useful case study in fact-checking pedagogy: here is a claim, here is how to find the relevant expertise, here is what that expertise says, and here is why the popular belief was wrong.

**Failure mode:** The main failure mode for coverage of this myth is uncritical transmission - travel writers, tour guides, and casual media repeating the claim as interesting trivia without verifying it. The myth has appeared in legitimate publications as a fun fact, demonstrating that editorial fact-checking does not always catch claims that aren't politically contentious. Physical trivia gets less scrutiny than political claims, which creates a channel through which physics myths circulate even in otherwise well-edited contexts.

**What the myth teaches about verification:** The penny case illustrates an important fact-checking principle: claims that seem to follow from first principles (“tall building + falling object = fast impact + death”) are not self-verifying just because the reasoning seems coherent. The chain of reasoning is missing a step — air resistance and terminal velocity — that completely changes the conclusion. Fact-checking requires not just evaluating the stated reasoning but asking what the reasoning omits, which is a harder and more demanding cognitive operation than checking whether the stated premises are true.

**The tourist context and its asymmetry:** The Empire State Building observation deck is one of the most visited tourist attractions in the United States. The penny myth is regularly invoked by tour guides and circulates among visitors as interesting trivia. There is something instructive about the asymmetry between how the myth is transmitted (casually, as fun trivia, by people who believe it) and what it would take to displace it (a functional understanding of terminal velocity, drag coefficients, and why a coin tumbling through air behaves so differently from a dense projectile). The barriers to correction are not primarily access to information — the physics is freely available and clearly explained in many places — but the cognitive investment required to understand why the intuitive account is wrong. Most people never have occasion to invest in that understanding, so the myth persists.

**Physics education and intuition calibration:** One of the most consistent findings in physics education research is that students arrive in introductory courses with robust intuitive physics models that are systematically wrong in predictable ways, and that correcting these models requires more than presenting correct information. Students who memorize terminal velocity formulas for an exam often continue to apply naive Aristotelian physics in everyday reasoning after the exam. The penny myth is a useful diagnostic: a student who believes it has not internalized the significance of air resistance even if they can calculate drag force in a test. This is why physics educators who study conceptual understanding treat intuitive physics myths not as curiosities but as evidence of genuine learning targets. Displacing the penny myth requires building an accurate model of air resistance as a real, limiting force — not just a correction factor to be added to equations.
