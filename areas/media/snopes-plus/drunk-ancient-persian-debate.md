# Ancient Persians Made Important Decisions While Drunk and Sober to Ensure Wisdom


## 1. The Claim

The story circulates regularly on social media, in pop-psychology listicles, and in corporate decision-making workshops: the ancient Persians had a practice of deliberating on any important matter twice—once while drunk and once while sober—on the grounds that alcohol would liberate intuitions suppressed by sober caution, while sobriety would prevent premature commitment to foolish emotion-driven conclusions. The implication, frequently made explicit in modern retellings, is that the Persians had stumbled upon a sophisticated cognitive insight: that inebriation and sobriety activate different but complementary reasoning modes, and that combining both yielded decisions superior to either state alone.

This claim appears in history podcasts, management consulting blogs, Reddit threads discussing "ancient wisdom," and motivational content from productivity influencers. It is frequently cited to argue that loosening inhibitions through intoxicants, informal environments, or "thinking outside the box" exercises should be paired with rigorous sober review to balance creativity and rationality. In the most expansive versions of the claim, the Persians are treated as empirical proto-psychologists who anticipated findings from modern creativity and decision-making research.

The story is almost always presented with a knowing, slightly surprised tone: "Can you believe these ancient people had figured this out?" The implication is that a pre-scientific civilization had independently discovered something modern cognitive science now confirms. This makes it ideal content for audiences who enjoy finding functional rationality in exotic historical practices.

The story has a well-known source: Herodotus, the fifth-century BCE Greek historian. But whether Herodotus's account says what modern retellings claim, whether Herodotus himself was reliable on this point, and what the practice actually looked like in Persian court culture are questions that most popular retellings ignore entirely.


## 2. What's Actually True

Herodotus does record the practice. In the *Histories* (Book I, Chapter 133), writing roughly 440 BCE, he states: "It is also their general practice to deliberate upon affairs of weight when they are drunk; and then on the morrow, when they are sober, the decision to which they came the night before is put before them by the master of the house in which it was made; if it is then approved of, they act on it; if not, they set it aside. Sometimes, however, they are sober at their first deliberation, but in this case they always reconsider the matter under the influence of wine." This passage is genuine. The Loeb Classical Library translation (Herodotus, *The Histories*, trans. Godley, 1920) renders the Greek substantially intact.

The claim is not fabricated. It has a credible primary source. However, several important qualifications apply.

First, Herodotus was writing ethnographic descriptions of a foreign culture from an outsider's perspective, often relying on secondhand reports, travelers' accounts, and court diplomacy. His book on Persian customs (Book I, Chapters 131-140) is a mixed inventory of accurate observations, plausible generalizations, and probable exaggerations designed partly to frame Persia as exotic and Other for a Greek audience. Scholars including Hartog (1988, in *The Mirror of Herodotus*, University of California Press) have analyzed how Herodotus systematically constructed Persian customs as inverted mirror-images of Greek norms—the Persians, in Herodotus's ethnographic framing, often did things Greeks considered irrational but which turned out to have paradoxical wisdom. This rhetorical device of the "wise barbarian" was a common Greek literary convention that Herodotus deployed consistently.

Second, the practice, if it existed, was almost certainly confined to Persian aristocratic and court deliberation—the royal council and satrapal advisors, not a broad cultural norm for ordinary Persians. The context in Herodotus is explicitly elite: decisions "of weight," involving political and military counsel. The idea that Persian farmers, merchants, or craftspeople regularly convened drunk-and-sober councils before planting crops or negotiating prices is not supported by any ancient source. Modern retellings frequently strip the elite political context entirely.

Third, corresponding Persian-language sources do not describe or confirm this practice. The Persepolis Fortification Tablets (Hallock, 1969, Oriental Institute Publications), the most extensive surviving administrative archive from the Achaemenid Empire, record allotments of beer, wine, and grain for workers, officials, and travelers across the empire but contain no reference to deliberative protocols involving intoxication. Court administration texts from Persepolis, Susa, and Pasargadae that survive concern logistics, not political philosophy.

Fourth, Greek sources other than Herodotus mention Persian court drinking in different terms. Athenaeus's *Deipnosophistae* (late second/early third century CE) records symposium customs attributed to Persian royalty but does not corroborate the specific double-deliberation protocol. Xenophon's *Cyropaedia* portrays Cyrus the Great enforcing sobriety in military command contexts, which sits in some tension with a universal drunk-then-sober deliberative norm.

Fifth, the Zoroastrian religious context—which was the dominant spiritual framework for Achaemenid Persian elites—does not unambiguously endorse ritual intoxication in deliberative settings. The Avestan texts and later Pahlavi literature have complex and sometimes contradictory positions on haoma (the sacred ritual intoxicant), which was used in religious ceremony but does not map straightforwardly onto secular political deliberation.

What is true: elite Persian political culture, like nearly all ancient Near Eastern and Mediterranean court cultures, involved banquets at which wine was consumed and political matters were discussed. Symposia in early Greek political culture, as documented by Murray (1990, in *Sympotica: A Symposium on the Symposion*, Oxford University Press), served similar mixed deliberative and social functions. The Persian double-deliberation may reflect a court norm in which initial wine-lubricated discussions at banquets were formalized the following morning—a practice that would be logistically plausible and consistent with Near Eastern administrative patterns.

Hartog's analysis suggests the practice as Herodotus framed it served a rhetorical purpose: to portray the Persians as paradoxically wise in ways that Greeks would find counterintuitive, reinforcing the literary construction of Persia as an inverted but functional civilization. Whether the practice existed as described, existed in a more limited form, or was a projection of Greek symposium culture onto observed Persian banqueting customs is genuinely uncertain.

The claim is therefore: historically attested in a single credible primary source, plausible in broad outline, almost certainly more episodic and elite than universal, lacking corroboration from Persian-language archives, and embedded in Greek ethnographic rhetoric that complicates straightforward acceptance.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The claim persists through a mechanism best described as **retrospective functional rationalism**: the tendency to find sophisticated, near-scientific justifications in ancient practices when those practices can be mapped onto frameworks modern readers already accept. Modern audiences are primed by popular psychology to believe that inhibition-loosening and systematic verification are both valuable cognitive operations. The Persian story appears to validate both simultaneously, providing ancient authorization for an already-believed principle.

This mechanism is distinct from general confirmation bias. It is specifically the projection of contemporary cognitive models—System 1 vs. System 2 thinking, as popularized by Kahneman (2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Farrar, Straus and Giroux); dual-process models of creativity; convergent vs. divergent ideation—backward onto historical practices that may have had entirely different rationales. In ancient Persian court culture, the deliberative value of banquet discussion may have been social rather than cognitive: bonding, loyalty-testing, and reading the unguarded opinions of subordinates, rather than activating "creative" cognition.

The Herodotus origin makes the claim more persuasive than purely anonymous folklore. Attribution to a recognized classical historian gives the story a patina of scholarly grounding that social media content about ancient wisdom typically lacks. Readers familiar with Herodotus as a legitimate historical source transfer that credibility to the specific claim without critical examination of Herodotus's ethnographic methods or the potential rhetorical purposes of his Persian descriptions.

Social sharing dynamics favor the story's clean, counterintuitive structure: "the ancients figured out that being drunk helps, actually." Counterintuitive claims circulate more widely than confirmatory ones, as demonstrated by Heath et al. (2001, "Emotional selection in memes," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 81:6). The story's structural surprise—intoxication as epistemic tool—makes it inherently shareable regardless of its accuracy.

Finally, the story appears frequently in contexts where credibility cues are high but verification is low: TED Talk-style presentations, business books citing "ancient wisdom," and history-interest social media accounts that traffic in attractive historical anecdotes without systematic fact-checking. The mismatch between these high-credibility presentation contexts and the relatively thin evidentiary foundation allows the story to accrete authority through repetition.


## 4. Verdict

**Mostly true with significant context lost.** Herodotus reports the practice genuinely and specifically. The claim is not a fabrication or a misattribution. However: (1) Herodotus's reliability on Persian social customs is disputed by modern historians; (2) the practice, if real, was elite and political, not a general Persian cultural norm; (3) no Persian-language administrative sources confirm it; (4) the modern cognitive rationale applied to it is a retrospective projection absent from any ancient source; and (5) the Greek framing surrounding the story reflects ethnographic rhetoric that complicates its face-value acceptance. The claim is historically attested but popularly overread.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Herodotus passage sits within a long intellectual history of debates about alcohol and cognition in Western thought. The Stoic and Platonic traditions were generally hostile to intoxication in deliberative contexts, treating drunkenness as antithetical to reason. Symposium culture in the Greek world, however, involved a complex negotiation between wine as social lubricant and wine as threat to rational discourse—Plato's *Symposium* itself is organized around the question of how to drink wisely at intellectual gatherings.

The ancient Near East more broadly associated wine with royal banquets and divine hospitality rather than cognitive science. The Epic of Gilgamesh includes Enkidu's transformation through civilized eating and drinking (Tablet II), which modern readers sometimes analogize to the Persian deliberation story—but in both cases the cultural meaning of intoxication was primarily social-transformative, not epistemically calibrated.

In alcohol research, the relationship between moderate intoxication and creative or lateral thinking has attracted genuine scientific attention. Jarosz et al. (2012, "Uncorking the muse: Alcohol intoxication facilitates creative problem solving," *Consciousness and Cognition*, 21:1) found that participants at a blood alcohol content of 0.075 solved remote associate problems faster than sober controls, suggesting alcohol may reduce working memory capacity in ways that benefit certain types of divergent thinking. However, alcohol reliably impairs executive function, working memory, and inhibitory control—capabilities essential to complex political decision-making. The Jarosz et al. effect applies to specific puzzle-solving tasks, not to the type of political and military deliberation described in Herodotus.

The Persian case should be understood in the context of how all ancient administrative cultures managed collective deliberation under conditions of information scarcity, interpersonal power dynamics, and loyalty uncertainty. Drunk advisors are harder to dissemble; sober review imposes procedural commitment. The practice, if real, may have functioned as much as a social-political check on courtly deception as a cognitive-epistemological optimization strategy.

The claim also illustrates how "ancient wisdom" framings frequently strip cultural specificity from historical practices to produce universalizable self-help content. The Persian double-deliberation, if it existed, was embedded in a specific court culture, a specific relationship between Persian elites and wine as a status marker, and a specific political problem of managing powerful subordinates. Abstracting it into "drunk for creativity, sober for verification" loses everything historically interesting about the practice.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Standard fact-checking outlets have not systematically addressed this claim, which explains its "not-addressed" Snopes status. The claim is technically true at its core (Herodotus does say this), which makes it resistant to the simple true/false verdict structure that generates fact-check content. Claims with genuine primary-source grounding but significant scope distortion in popular retelling—the category this belongs to—fall through fact-checking filters calibrated for straightforward misinformation.

When the claim has been examined by historians, as in Briant (2002, *From Cyrus to Alexander: A History of the Persian Empire*, Eisenbrauns), the assessment tends to accept the Herodotean report as plausible for court contexts while noting the absence of corroborating sources and the rhetorical purposes of Herodotus's Persian ethnography. Academic evaluation does not translate readily into shareable verdicts.

The appropriate approach for sophisticated fact-checking of this claim would be to distinguish between three questions: (1) Does Herodotus say it? Yes. (2) Was it a real Persian practice? Plausible for elite settings, unverified. (3) Does it mean what modern retellings say it means? No—the deliberate cognitive optimization narrative is a modern retrospective projection with no ancient basis.

For readers encountering this claim in corporate training materials, TED-style content, or "ancient wisdom" social media, the healthy response is not dismissal—the story has a genuine classical source—but skepticism about the cognitive interpretation layered onto it. The Persians, if they deliberated under both conditions, were almost certainly doing something politically and socially meaningful within their own cultural logic, not running an ancient version of a randomized controlled trial on creativity and decision-making.
