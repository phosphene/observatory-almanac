# Shaking out a duvet or making your bed vigorously can trigger a heart attack

## 1. The Claim

The claim has existed in assorted forms for generations, passing through word of mouth, advice columns, and eventually the internet: that shaking out a duvet, vigorously fluffing pillows, or engaging in the physical activity associated with making a bed could be dangerous to the heart. In some versions, the claim is attributed to a doctor's warning recalled from decades ago. In others, it arrives as an anecdote: someone's neighbour, someone's relative, had a heart attack while making the bed. In more recent circulatings, it appears as a social media post or health-tip thread warning older people in particular to take care with vigorous household chores.

The claim is occasionally attached to broader folk wisdom about cold mornings being dangerous for heart attack risk, or to admonitions about sudden movements after waking. It gets grouped with advice about avoiding hot baths after exercise, not shovelling snow too aggressively, and being cautious about vigorous activity before an adequate warm-up. Some of these related claims have genuine clinical foundations; the duvet-shaking claim borrows ambient credibility from them.

It is worth being specific about what the claim actually asserts. In its strongest form, it suggests that the specific act of shaking out a duvet — the arm movements, the brief burst of physical effort, the possible postural change — is itself a cardiac risk. In its weaker form, it simply cautions that any vigorous physical exertion in the early morning hours could be problematic for people with underlying heart conditions. These two versions require different analyses, and conflating them is itself a source of confusion.

## 2. What's Actually True

There is no clinical evidence that the specific act of shaking out a duvet or making a bed poses a meaningful risk of triggering a cardiac event in a healthy or normally functioning cardiovascular system. The activity level involved — even in vigorous bedmaking — is minor compared to efforts that have been studied in the context of exercise-induced cardiac events. It does not approach the threshold at which sudden strenuous exertion has been associated with increased cardiac risk.

To understand why the claim has any traction at all, it is necessary to understand what actually does elevate cardiac risk, and why morning exertion is sometimes discussed in that context.

**The morning vulnerability window.** There is well-established epidemiological evidence that the incidence of acute myocardial infarction (heart attack) follows a circadian pattern, with a peak in the early morning hours — typically between 6 and 12 — and a trough in the overnight period. Several physiological mechanisms contribute to this. The transition from sleep to waking involves a rapid increase in sympathetic nervous system activity, a surge in cortisol, rises in heart rate and blood pressure, increased platelet aggregability (the tendency of blood platelets to clump together), and, in some individuals, reduced fibrinolytic activity (the process by which the body dissolves blood clots). These changes collectively create a background environment in which an existing vulnerable plaque in a coronary artery is somewhat more likely to rupture, and in which a clot forming as a result of that rupture is harder to clear.

This morning vulnerability is real and has been demonstrated in multiple large-scale epidemiological studies. But it is important to characterise it correctly. The morning peak in cardiac events is driven by the underlying physiological transition from sleep to waking — not by specific activities performed after waking. A person who wakes at 7 a.m. and lies in bed for an hour faces a similar cardiac risk profile to one who wakes at 7 a.m. and immediately gets up to make the bed. The trigger for most morning infarcts is the physiological transition itself, sometimes combined with a pre-existing vulnerable atherosclerotic plaque, not any particular activity superimposed on it.

**Exertion and cardiac events.** There is a separate, genuine literature on exercise-triggered cardiac events. Sudden, strenuous physical exertion — particularly in sedentary individuals, and particularly involving activities like vigorous competitive sports, heavy lifting, or snow shovelling — does appear to transiently increase the risk of a cardiac event in people with underlying but undiagnosed or poorly managed coronary artery disease. The mechanism involves a sudden spike in heart rate, blood pressure, and myocardial oxygen demand that exceeds the capacity of partially occluded coronary arteries to deliver blood. The classic example often cited in public health contexts is snow shovelling; multiple studies have found an association between heavy snowfall and increased acute myocardial infarction rates in populations engaged in manual snow clearance.

Snow shovelling, however, involves sustained, heavy, repetitive effort that can reach the metabolic equivalent of moderate to vigorous aerobic exercise — roughly 6 to 7 METs (Metabolic Equivalents of Task). Making a bed, including shaking out a duvet, involves activities in the range of 2 to 3 METs — equivalent to slow walking or light housework. The two activities are not comparable in terms of cardiovascular demand. The threshold at which exertion has been associated with increased cardiac risk is well above bedmaking.

The relevant clinical literature would not support an assertion that making a bed poses a specific cardiac risk even for individuals with existing heart disease, absent other compounding factors (a simultaneous acute infection, for instance, or marked psychological stress). Guidelines for cardiac rehabilitation and post-event activity resumption typically permit light household activities including bed-making within days to weeks of an event, well before vigorous exercise is cleared.

**Cold exposure.** Some versions of the claim invoke cold exposure — specifically, the idea that the combination of morning cold and physical exertion is dangerous. Cold exposure does cause peripheral vasoconstriction, which raises blood pressure and increases cardiac workload; the evidence for a cold-weather effect on myocardial infarction is reasonably robust. But the temperatures involved in shaking out a duvet indoors are not the temperatures that produce clinically significant cold-induced cardiovascular responses. Those responses have been studied primarily in the context of outdoor cold exposure at low temperatures.

In summary: the specific claim that bedmaking can trigger a heart attack is not supported by clinical evidence. The biological mechanisms that could theoretically connect exertion to cardiac events are real, but they operate at levels of effort substantially higher than those involved in making a bed.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The duvet-shaking claim persists for reasons that have more to do with cognitive pattern-matching and the structure of folk medical wisdom than with any systematic evidence.

**The coincidence problem.** Heart attacks occur in the morning more frequently than at other times of day. People make beds in the morning. Some people who have heart attacks in the morning are in the act of making the bed or doing similar light household tasks when the event begins. These coincidences are then reported — by the patient, by family members, by clinicians — and accumulated into a pattern that feels causally meaningful. The fact that the underlying pathology was triggered by the physiological transition from sleep, not by the specific activity, is not visible at the bedside. The temporal sequence *A happened before B* is misread as *A caused B*. This is a classic instance of post hoc ergo propter hoc reasoning, and it is structurally difficult to resist when the coincidence is reported repeatedly and the alternative explanation is abstract.

**The plausibility gradient.** The claim lives near a genuine truth: vigorous cardiorespiratory exertion can be dangerous for people with severely compromised cardiac function. That genuine truth is so medically well-established — shovelling snow, sprinting, heavy lifting — that the claim about bedmaking benefits from ambient plausibility. It sits at one end of a gradient whose other end is unambiguously real. Most people are not trained to think precisely about thresholds and magnitudes, and the claim does not require precision to feel credible.

**The authority signal.** Many versions of the duvet-shaking claim are attributed to a doctor, or are presented in the format of helpful health advice. In an information environment where health tips circulate without attribution or verification, anything that looks like advice from a medical professional carries substantially more weight than its evidential basis deserves. The claim has acquired a patina of medical authority simply by being old enough and widely enough repeated.

**Catastrophising and protective attention.** There is a particular psychology to health claims that frame mundane activities as dangerous. They are compelling partly because they invoke the possibility of sudden death in an ordinary domestic context — a context that feels safe, that is not supposed to be dangerous. This produces a distinctive emotional register: a kind of vigilance that feels like care. Passing on the claim becomes an act of concern for others. Believing it feels like prudence. The social function of the claim (expressing care, demonstrating health awareness) sustains it independently of its factual status.

**Older adults as a focal audience.** The claim is most often directed at older people, framed as advice about managing the physical limitations of age. This framing has a patronising but affectionate quality — it implies that the listener is in a category of vulnerability that requires special consideration. Older adults who have had cardiac scares, or who know others who have, are predisposed to receive this kind of caution sympathetically. The claim also circulates through informal carer networks — people looking after elderly relatives — where the fear of an avoidable cardiac event is realistic and present.

## 4. Verdict

**Unsubstantiated.** The specific claim that shaking out a duvet or making your bed vigorously can trigger a heart attack is not substantiated by clinical evidence. While there are real physiological phenomena in the vicinity — morning cardiovascular vulnerability, exertion-triggered cardiac events in people with severe coronary artery disease — they do not apply to the level of physical effort involved in making a bed. The claim is best understood as a piece of folk health wisdom that has drifted away from the more limited real truths adjacent to it. It does little direct harm, but it may contribute to an unfounded anxiety about light domestic activity, which in people with heart conditions can actually be counterproductive — excessive sedentariness and fear of movement are themselves risk factors for cardiac mortality.

Snopes has **not addressed** this specific claim as of publication.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The duvet-shaking claim is representative of a broad category of what we might call **benevolent health folklore** — misinformation that is not malicious in intent, carries no obvious commercial or political motivation, and often circulates through channels of genuine concern (older relatives, community health messaging, GP waiting rooms). This category presents distinct challenges for fact-checkers, because the social response to a debunking can feel like an attack on the well-meaning people who spread the claim.

Several structural features of the health information ecosystem produce and sustain claims of this kind.

**The plausibility-adjacent inflation problem.** Many genuine medical phenomena have simplified versions circulating in popular understanding. "Avoid strenuous activity in the morning if you have heart disease" is real clinical advice, grounded in evidence. "Making your bed can give you a heart attack" is a distorted amplification of it that removes the conditional, removes the severity threshold, and applies the caution to the entire population rather than a specific at-risk subset. This process of inflation — claim to simplified claim to distorted claim — is continuous and hard to observe because each step in the chain feels like a reasonable paraphrase of the preceding step.

**The measurement gap.** Many folk health claims persist partly because they sit in spaces where controlled research has not been done. No randomised controlled trial has studied the cardiac effects of bedmaking; no large-scale epidemiological study has examined whether vigorous duvet-shaking is associated with adverse cardiac outcomes. The absence of direct evidence creates an asymmetry: the claim can circulate freely because there is no specific study that directly contradicts it, even though the claim is inconsistent with everything we know about cardiac physiology and exertion thresholds.

**Cautionary overcorrection.** In clinical practice, there is a genuine tension between giving maximally protective advice and giving proportionate advice. A cardiologist who knows their patient is prone to anxiety about heart health, and who knows that light activity is generally safe, may still hedge their advice toward caution to avoid any appearance of endorsing risk. That caution can then be misrepresented or amplified as it moves through informal communication chains. The clinician who said "don't overdo housework in the first week after discharge" becomes the source for "a doctor said bedmaking can kill you."

**The comparative risk communication problem.** One of the most consistent findings in health communication research is that people are very poor at comparing risks of different magnitudes. The risk gradient between "making the bed" and "shovelling snow in a blizzard" is enormous, but both involve physical activity in a morning context and both can be loosely framed as exertion. For many people, the specific claim that bedmaking is dangerous sits on a continuum with the genuine claim about snow shovelling and feels like a minor variation on it rather than a claim with a completely different evidentiary status.

Looking at the claim from a positive framing: what is actually true and useful about the kernel of truth within it? The morning vulnerability window is real, and people with diagnosed coronary artery disease or heart failure should understand that the transition from sleep is a higher-risk period and should discuss with their cardiologist what this means for their morning routine. The counterintuitive clinical message is that regular, moderate physical activity — including regular light household activity — is *protective* for cardiac health, and that the benefits of activity vastly outweigh the risks for all but the most severely compromised patients. Excessive sedentariness in elderly or cardiac-compromised individuals is itself a significant contributor to morbidity and mortality, and health messaging that amplifies fear of light activity can do real harm by pushing people toward inactivity.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim has not, as of the publication of this entry, been directly addressed by Snopes or other major fact-checking organisations. Its absence from the major fact-checking record is itself informative: claims of this kind — domestic health folklore with no clear political or commercial dimension, no identified origin point, and no viral spike triggering coverage — often fall outside the triage systems of major fact-checking operations. Those systems are broadly responsive to volume (how widely is a claim circulating?) and to harm potential (what are the consequences of belief?). The duvet-shaking claim does not circulate at a volume that would typically trip a major fact-checker's threshold, and its direct harm potential is low.

This does not mean it is beyond the scope of useful fact-checking work. Organisations focused specifically on health misinformation — including the Health Feedback network, NHS Choices, MedlinePlus, and various national health service digital media programmes — address folklore claims of this type more systematically than general fact-checking outlets. Their approach tends to focus on explanatory journalism: explaining the real phenomenon (morning cardiovascular vulnerability, exertion thresholds) in enough detail to allow readers to contextualise the claim themselves, rather than simply rating it true or false.

The most effective responses to folk health claims of this type tend to have several features: they acknowledge the kernel of truth that makes the claim plausible; they explain the mechanism or evidence clearly without requiring professional training to follow; they avoid the condescending register that can make corrections feel like attacks on the people who shared the claim; and they provide a positive, actionable message to replace the anxiety produced by the claim — in this case, something like: moderate daily activity, including making your bed, is good for your heart, and the fear of light activity is itself a health risk worth addressing.

For media literacy purposes, this case is a useful illustration of how health misinformation can emerge and persist without any deliberate disinformation campaign. No one invented the duvet claim cynically; it grew from a genuine medical neighbourhood, distorted slightly with each retelling, and found a stable ecological niche in informal health communication networks. Addressing it requires not the identification and discrediting of a bad actor, but the patient cultivation of better health literacy — an understanding of thresholds, mechanisms, and the difference between individual anecdote and population-level evidence — across the general public.
