## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

On the night of February 4, 2018, the Philadelphia Eagles defeated the New England Patriots 41–33 in Super Bowl LII in Minneapolis—the franchise's first Super Bowl victory in its 85-year history. In the hours that followed, large portions of Philadelphia devolved into a chaotic street celebration involving overturned cars, shattered windows, fires, pulled-down traffic light poles, and thousands of exuberant and often intoxicated fans flooding the streets. During this chaos, a rumor propagated rapidly across social media: that a Philadelphia Eagles fan had stolen an ostrich from the Philadelphia Zoo and ridden it through the streets in celebration.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2018) rated this claim **False**. The claim was entirely fabricated. No ostrich was stolen from the Philadelphia Zoo. The Philadelphia Zoo does not and did not house any ostriches. The rumor originated as a joke on social media—primarily via the viral #PhiladelphiaPoliceScanner hashtag—and spread far enough to be briefly referenced in at least one news outlet as an "unconfirmed rumor," before the Philadelphia Zoo itself explicitly debunked it on Twitter.

## §2 The Philadelphia Police Scanner Phenomenon and the Night of February 4, 2018

To understand how the ostrich rumor propagated, it is necessary to understand the specific media phenomenon from which it emerged: the viral amplification of the Philadelphia police scanner feed on the night of the Eagles' Super Bowl victory.

The Philadelphia Police Department radio scanner—a public broadcast of law enforcement communications—became itself a viral media event as thousands of listeners tuned in and began live-tweeting and quoting the dispatch communications. Real dispatch calls were reported covering scenes that were both dramatic and darkly comic: a car overturned in front of a hotel; fans climbing atop a trash truck; a Christmas tree fire requiring extinguisher deployment; various reports of bottles thrown, assaults, and fireworks deployments. Sports Illustrated (Bullock, 2018) collected and published a selection of genuine scanner communications, which read as a running absurdist comedy of minor civic chaos layered atop genuine disorder.

Into this environment, multiple social media users began inserting fabricated "scanner reports"—jokes pretending to be real dispatch messages—under the #PhiladelphiaPoliceScanner hashtag. The ostrich claim appears to have originated from one of these fabrications (Evon, 2018). Two tweets referencing the ostrich story were identified by Evon, from accounts with obvious satirical intent, but both received significant engagement that carried them into a broader information stream where their satirical origin was stripped away.

The mechanics of this amplification followed a pattern well-documented in misinformation research: the inherent plausibility of the general context (real disorder was being reported via the scanner) transferred credibility to the fabricated specific claim (ostrich theft). Within the chaotic information environment of a viral event, it becomes cognitively difficult to maintain track of which specific claims have been verified and which are jokes. Evon (2018) noted that the rumor "made it to some news reports," including a reference by Syracuse.com to "an unconfirmed rumor that an ostrich had been stolen from the local zoo"—illustrating how even a single degree of amplification toward a professionally maintained outlet gives a fabrication partial legitimacy.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Satirical Injections and the Credibility Transfer Problem

The ostrich rumor is a near-perfect case study of what might be called *contextual credibility transfer*: the mechanism by which a fabricated claim acquires apparent plausibility not from any evidence in its favor, but from the documented credibility of the general informational context in which it appears.

This mechanism is structurally distinct from standard claim fabrication. In conventional misinformation, a false claim is presented as a direct factual assertion—"X happened." The audience evaluates the claim on its face. In contextual credibility transfer, a false claim is embedded in a stream of verified accurate information; the audience is not evaluating the claim in isolation but against a background of contemporaneous accurate reports that have established a general credibility baseline for the stream.

The #PhiladelphiaPoliceScanner hashtag was, on the night in question, a legitimate conduit for real, newsworthy scanner communications alongside the fabricated material. The scanner itself—an unmediated public record—carries institutional authority that the hashtag partially inherited. When a tweet purporting to quote scanner traffic inserted the ostrich theft into this stream, it benefited from not merely the claim's own face plausibility but from the established plausibility of the stream as a whole.

Pennycook et al. (2021, *Psychological Science*) found that prior exposure to accurate claims in a shared information context increases acceptance of subsequent false claims from the same apparent source—an effect they termed "illusory truth" extended to source-level credibility. Specifically, when participants repeatedly encountered accurate claims attributed to a source before encountering a false claim from the same source, the false claim received significantly higher credibility ratings than when presented in isolation. The #PhiladelphiaPoliceScanner viral phenomenon created exactly this dynamic at population scale.

The further irony is that the authenticity of the scanner itself—genuine law enforcement radio—made some real reports sound barely believable ("there should be no one on top of the trash truck"), which may have made the ostrich story appear to fit the genre of the real reports rather than standing out as an obvious absurdity. Real chaos made fabricated chaos more credible rather than less.

The epistemic novelty for fact-checking purposes: fabricated claims inserted into legitimate high-traffic information streams during breaking news events are epistemically different from stand-alone fabrications. They require not just assessment of the specific claim but assessment of the information stream's integrity as a whole—a substantially harder inference task, particularly in real time. The appropriate epistemological response is heightened skepticism toward any claim circulating in a high-noise breaking-news environment, regardless of how well-sourced adjacent claims in the same stream appear to be.

## §4 The Philadelphia Eagles Celebrations: Real Chaos and Its Civic Dimensions

The underlying event—the disorder following the Eagles' Super Bowl victory—was real and was documented through multiple independent channels. Porter (2018, *Newsweek*) described large-scale street celebrations that included property damage across downtown Philadelphia. Police reports confirmed several significant incidents: a car overturned in front of a hotel, multiple fires in trash receptacles, pulled-down light poles, smashed windows at several establishments including a Ritz-Carlton entrance, and crowds climbing atop and in some cases collapsing awnings and scaffolding.

The contrast between this real disorder and the fabricated ostrich story is itself informative. The real reports were less narratively satisfying than the fabricated one. Overturned cars and smashed windows are depressing; a fan riding a stolen zoo ostrich through the streets of Philadelphia is exhilarating as narrative. The fabrication's greater narrative appeal relative to the real reporting helps explain its viral success—it was the story people wished were true as a fitting capstone to a night of extraordinary excess and joy.

This connects to broader research on the relationship between narrative appeal and perceived truth. Fazio et al. (2015, *Journal of Experimental Psychology*) found that claims rated as more interesting or entertaining were more likely to be judged as true, an integration level of the "truthiness" literature (separate from the Pennycook illusory truth line of research). A fan riding an ostrich through the streets of Philadelphia after a Super Bowl victory is simply a better story than a fan smashing a window—and the empirical research predicts that better stories are systematically at risk of being believed more readily.

The Philadelphia Zoo's Twitter response deserves specific attention: the Zoo's verification of the ostrich's absence from their inventory was an unusually direct and effective form of institutional fact-checking. By tweeting that they didn't have ostriches and no animals were missing, the Zoo delivered a single decisive refutation that required no extended analysis. This type of institutional response—brief, authoritative, from the specific entity with definitional authority over the claim—is what makes the Philadelphia case a cleaner resolution than many viral rumors.

## §5 The Dynamics of Super Bowl and Sports Championship Celebration Disorder

Sports championship celebrations have historically been associated with civil disorder across a range of cities and events. The academic literature on this phenomenon treats it as a well-characterized, if poorly controlled, social phenomenon.

Bernburg et al. (2009, *Deviant Behavior*) analyzed the sociology of collective violence in post-sporting-event celebrations, distinguishing between "rioting" proper and the more typical "revelry disorder" that characterizes most sports championships, in which most participants are not engaged in systematic violence but in an aggregate of individually lower-stakes acts (climbing structures, shouting, jaywalking en masse) that cumulatively overwhelm normal public order. Philadelphia in February 2018 was primarily the latter: a massive and poorly coordinated expression of collective euphoria that occasionally crossed into property damage without the targeted violence and looting typical of civil unrest.

Stott et al. (2017, *Policing: A Journal of Policy and Practice*) developed an elaborated social identity model of crowd psychology that challenges traditional "mob mentality" frameworks, arguing that participant behavior during crowd events is more rationally tied to perceived identity threat and legitimacy than earlier models suggested. By this analysis, the celebratory disorder following the Eagles' Super Bowl victory was a collective expression of longstanding identity investment: Philadelphia fans had endured decades of sports heartbreak, and the release was socially meaningful in ways orthogonal to the individual rationality of any specific act of property damage.

The ostrich rumor's appeal within this context becomes legible as wish-fulfillment: Philadelphia fans wanted to celebrate in a way that rose to the level of the historical significance they felt. An ostrich ride was mythologically appropriate to that level of joy. The fabrication, in a meaningful sense, expressed an emotional truth even though it was factually false—a feature of some viral misinformation that makes simple debunking feel inadequate as a response. Correcting the claim without acknowledging the emotional context that made it appealing risks the condescension that renders corrections counterproductive (Nyhan & Reifler, 2010, *Political Behavior*).

## §6 Contemporary Relevance and Knowledge Gaps

The EARC gap assigned here is **A (Attribution)**. The claim is thoroughly false, and the institutional authority of the Philadelphia Zoo's denial is definitive. However, the precise attribution of the fabrication's original source—which specific user or users first injected the ostrich claim into the #PhiladelphiaPoliceScanner stream as a joke—was not established in Snopes' investigation and has not been definitively resolved in subsequent reporting. Whether the fabrication originated independently in multiple places (parallel invention, consistent with the general chaos of the moment) or was carried forward from a single source is unclear.

This attribution gap is of theoretical more than practical importance: the falseness of the claim is fully established regardless of who first made it. But from a misinformation-studies perspective, understanding whether viral sports-celebration fabrications originate from coordinated networks of satirical accounts or from isolated organic jokes that happen to achieve scale is relevant to intervention design. The Eagles ostrich case involved no apparent coordinated manipulation, which is consistent with organic viral spread of low-stakes entertainment fabrications rather than the targeted disinformation campaigns that have received more policy attention.

Knowledge gaps: What fraction of claims propagating under crisis-event hashtags during breaking news events are fabrications, and how does this fraction change as events age? Are institutions like the Philadelphia Zoo routinely monitoring social media for factual claims about themselves during high-salience events, and how much of the fast, effective denial was contingent on the Zoo noticing the rumor quickly? How do public corrections from institutional sources spread relative to the original fabrications, and does institutional authority accelerate correction uptake in low-stakes satirical fabrication cases of this type more than in politically motivated misinformation cases?

For consumers of breaking-news social media: the ostrich case illustrates that entertaining specificity (a fan riding an ostrich) is not evidence of accuracy—in fact, within a chaotic news environment, extreme narrative appeal is a signal for heightened rather than reduced skepticism.
