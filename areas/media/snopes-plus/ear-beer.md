## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In May 2013, a video began circulating online depicting what appeared to be a woman at a Czech beer festival placing a straw in her ear and, apparently, consuming beer through it. Viewers who shared and discussed the video debated whether such a feat was biologically possible — whether a person could actually absorb or "drink" a liquid through their ear canal in any physiologically meaningful sense — and whether the video documented a genuine phenomenon or a staged performance. The video's origins and context were, when the Snopes fact-check was published, uncertain; the Czech beer festival attribution was unverified.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2013) assigned this claim the verdict **Undetermined**. The indeterminate rating reflects the evidential situation of many novelty viral claims: the video exists and shows something visually striking, but the underlying biological plausibility of the action depicted, and whether the video was staged or authentic, cannot be resolved from the available information. The broad question — can a person drink through their ear? — has a determinate anatomical answer that is largely negative under ordinary conditions, but includes a genuinely interesting exception involving the Eustachian tube that gives the claim a small residue of physiological plausibility.

This entry examines the anatomical structures that bear on the claim, the specific physiological conditions under which any fluid transfer from ear canal to throat might theoretically occur, the epistemics of novelty-stunt video dissemination, and the historical context of ear-to-throat claims in folk biology and popular science.

## §2 Anatomy of the Ear Canal and its Communication with the Upper Respiratory Tract

Understanding whether the claim in this video is biologically possible requires a basic account of the anatomy of the outer, middle, and inner ear and the structures connecting them to the rest of the body. The ear canal (external auditory meatus) runs from the external ear (pinna) inward approximately 2.5 centimeters to the tympanic membrane (eardrum). The eardrum is a thin, flexible membrane that serves as the boundary between the outer ear and the middle ear cavity (tympanic cavity). Under normal circumstances, the eardrum is intact and creates a sealed barrier: liquid introduced into the ear canal cannot pass through the eardrum into the middle ear, much less into the throat, without the eardrum being perforated.

The middle ear is connected to the nasopharynx (the upper part of the throat behind the nose) by the Eustachian tube (auditory tube), a muscular-cartilaginous channel approximately 3.5 centimeters long in adults. The Eustachian tube's primary function is to equalize pressure between the middle ear cavity and the outside atmosphere by periodically opening — briefly and incompletely — during swallowing and yawning. In most adults, the Eustachian tube is collapsed and closed at rest and opens only transiently during these maneuvers (Gray, 1918, *Gray's Anatomy*, 20th ed.). The tube's lumen is narrow, its mucosal lining is designed to drain middle ear secretions downward toward the nasopharynx, and the muscle-controlled opening mechanism is not designed to admit large volumes of fluid.

In individuals with intact eardrums, no direct fluid pathway exists between the outer ear canal and the nasopharynx. A person with intact eardrums placing a straw in their ear canal and attempting to drink through it would find the eardrum as an impenetrable barrier. No voluntary "sucking" from the external ear canal is biologically achievable without puncturing the eardrum, because the middle ear cavity is not continuous with the outer canal.

The exception — which is the source of whatever residual physiological plausibility the claim possesses — concerns individuals with **patent (open) Eustachian tubes** or, more critically, individuals with **perforated tympanic membranes** (ruptured eardrums). In persons whose eardrum has been perforated through trauma, surgery, or chronic ear infection, the outer ear canal connects directly to the middle ear, which in turn is continuous with the nasopharynx via the Eustachian tube. Under these conditions, a sufficiently pressurized fluid column in the outer ear canal could theoretically enter the middle ear through the perforation and, under favorable pressure gradients, be drained into the throat through the Eustachian tube.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Eustachian Tube Physiology and the Rare-Anatomy Loophole in Implausibility Arguments

The epistemic novelty of this case — beyond the straightforward anatomical analysis — lies in what it reveals about how implausibility arguments interact with rare anatomical or physiological variation. A claim can be false for the vast majority of individuals while being physiologically possible, even if unusual, for a specific subset. The challenge for fact-checkers and debunkers is that categorical "this is anatomically impossible" statements are vulnerable to rare-anatomy counterexamples in a way that more carefully hedged statements are not.

Persistent Eustachian tube dysfunction — either patulous (chronically open) Eustachian tube or severely dilated tube following chronic middle-ear disease — creates anatomical conditions where the separation between outer ear, middle ear, and nasopharynx is compromised in ways that increase fluid communication pathways. Patulous Eustachian tube (PET) is a condition affecting approximately 1–6% of the general population (Poe et al., 2001, *Laryngoscope*, Vol. 111, No. 9) in which the tube remains anomalously open rather than closing at rest. While PET creates autophony (hearing one's own breathing) and other symptoms, it does not straightforwardly enable beer consumption via ear canal — the outer ear's eardrum would still need to be perforated or absent for fluid to reach the middle ear cavity.

More directly relevant is the situation of Eustachian tube-competent fluid drainage observed in post-tympanostomy tube patients (individuals with surgically implanted pressure equalization tubes, commonly called "ear tubes"), where the tube creates a continuous channel between outer ear and middle ear. Browning & Gatehouse (1992, *British Medical Journal*, Vol. 304, No. 6840) documented that fluid introduced into the ear canal in tympanostomy tube patients could, under experimental conditions, enter the middle ear and that some patients reported tasting materials in the nasopharynx consistent with middle ear drainage. This is not "drinking" through the ear in any nutritionally or behaviorally meaningful sense, but it documents a physiological pathway — in a surgically altered population — for fluid to travel from the outer ear to the throat.

The broader epistemological lesson is that **claims about human physiological impossibility require explicit quantification of the anatomical population over which the impossibility is asserted**. A statement like "it is impossible to drink through your ear" is false in a narrow technical sense for individuals with both perforated eardrums and patent Eustachian tubes — a small but nonzero population. This does not mean such individuals are "drinking beer through their ears" in any meaningful sense; the volume that could transit through this pathway under normal conditions (without surgical intervention or pathological anatomy) would be minimal and the mechanism bears no resemblance to voluntary consumption.

The EARC gap classification here is **A (anatomical)**: the primary knowledge gap is at the level of basic human anatomy and the specific structural conditions required for fluid transfer from outer ear to throat. Resolution of this gap — for purposes of this specific claim — requires understanding the three-compartment model of the ear (outer/middle/inner), the role of the eardrum as a barrier, and the conditions under which that barrier is absent or compromised.

## §4 The Video Evidence and Staged-Performance Considerations

Mikkelson (2013) was brief in his treatment of the video itself, noting the claim's undetermined status without extended analysis of the video's content. Based on the accounts provided, the video shows a woman inserting a straw into her ear and apparently performing a sucking or suction action. The visual cues suggesting fluid consumption — straw in ear, apparent negative pressure, possible beer level change in container — do not in themselves confirm any actual fluid transfer through the ear canal and into the body.

Performance artists and stage magicians have a well-documented tradition of creating apparent physiological impossibilities through hidden tubes, prosthetics, or misdirection. The straw in ear, from a stage-performance standpoint, could be attached to a hidden secondary tube routed under the performer's hair or clothing, with any liquid "consumed" being drawn through the secondary tube rather than through the ear canal. This category of possibility — which is extremely difficult to rule out from external video observation alone — substantially reduces the evidential weight of any video purporting to document a physiologically improbable feat.

Verifying that an ear-canal drinking video is genuine would require: (a) prior documentation of the performer's ear anatomy including eardrum integrity status; (b) close-range observation under controlled conditions that rule out concealed tubes; (c) post-performance verification that a liquid volume traceable to the container consumed appeared in the performer's stomach (via imaging or tracer methods). None of these verification conditions were met in the viral video context of 2013, rendering any verdict other than Undetermined unsupportable on the available evidence.

## §5 Folk Biology of Ear-to-Throat Connections and Popular Science Narratives

The claim that the ear and the throat are directly connected, and that objects or substances introduced into the ear can therefore be experienced or consumed through the throat, has a persistent presence in folk biology and popular anatomy discourse. The Eustachian tube's existence is relatively well-known at a lay level — most adults have experienced the pressure equalization sensation of Eustachian tube opening during altitude changes or descending in airplanes — and this lay knowledge creates a plausible (if incomplete) framework for imagining that swallowing and ear sensations are mechanically linked.

This folk understanding is accurate about the pressure-equalization function but incorrect about the size, permeability, and directionality of the Eustachian tube under normal conditions. The tube is not a free-flowing pipe; it is a collapsed channel that opens transiently under specifically muscular control and whose mucosal lining actively resists fluid ingress. The directionality of the tube's function — draining middle ear secretions outward rather than admitting external fluids inward — is opposite to what the "drinking through the ear" narrative requires for an intact ear.

The persistent appeal of ear-related physiological stunts in popular media is consistent with general features of "body oddity" viral content: claims that subvert normal assumptions about what human bodies can and cannot do. Dijksterhuis et al. (2005, *Science*, Vol. 311, No. 5755) documented that autonomy-surprising information — information that violates expected agency or body-schema norms — receives heightened attention and encoding compared to expected content. Ear-drinking, if real, would represent a striking body-schema violation accessible to almost any viewer's intuitions.

## §6 Verdict Stability and the Role of Undetermined Verdicts in Fact-Checking

The Undetermined verdict assigned in 2013 has neither been upgraded to True nor downgraded to False in subsequent updates, reflecting the enduring inaccessibility of verification for the specific video in question. This outcome is epistemologically appropriate: the anatomical analysis establishes that the claim is physiologically impossible under standard conditions but possible under specific rare anatomical conditions, and the video evidence does not permit ruling out staged performance.

The existence of an Undetermined category in fact-checking practice is epistemically important. The binary True/False structure of many folk-reasoning contexts creates pressure to assign determinate verdicts even where genuine uncertainty exists. Verdicts like Undetermined, Mixture, or Unproven serve important functions in accurately representing the epistemic state of a community of inquiry with respect to specific claims; assigning False to the ear-beer claim, for instance, would flatly misrepresent the biological situation (it is not categorically impossible for all humans under all conditions) without improving the user's understanding.

Lewandowsky et al. (2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, Vol. 13, No. 3) documented that individuals who receive epistemically hedged but accurate information ("this claim is possible but unverified") update their beliefs more durably than those who receive categorical corrections that are later shown to be oversimplified. The Undetermined verdict for ear-beer preserves the real biological nuance — intact-eardrum humans cannot drink through their ears; perforated-eardrum humans in unusual configurations might transit tiny fluid volumes — without making a strong false categorical claim in either direction.

The residual question motivating the Undetermined status — whether the specific woman in the video had unusual ear anatomy and was performing a genuine physiological feat, or whether the performance was staged — is unlikely to ever be definitively resolved for this specific 2013 video. The broader biological question has a clear and determinate answer grounded in human anatomy, even as the specific evidentiary claim remains open.
