# Earth Captured a Second Moon

## 1. The Claim

Periodic headlines announce that Earth has "captured a second moon" or "gained a new moon." These headlines have appeared multiple times in the past two decades, each time a small near-Earth object enters an Earth-bound orbit. Notable examples include:

- The 2006 discovery and characterization of the asteroid **2006 RH120**, which orbited Earth for roughly a year before departing.
- The 2020 discovery of **2020 CD3**, announced by the Minor Planet Center as a second confirmed temporary natural satellite of Earth.
- Recurring speculation about **Cruithne** (3753 Cruithne), a kilometer-scale asteroid in a peculiar co-orbital resonance with Earth, sometimes described in popular media as a "quasi-moon" or "second moon."
- The 2022–2025 tracking of **2023 FW13**, described as a "quasi-satellite" of Earth.
- The January 2025 discovery of **2024 PT5**, a small object (~11 meters) that briefly entered Earth's gravitational capture in late 2024.

In each cycle, headlines typically use the unqualified phrase "second moon" or "new moon," implying a discovery comparable to the existing Moon in some meaningful cartographic or astronomical sense. Social media amplification typically strips out qualifications that appear in the original scientific reporting.

The implicit claim structure is: *Earth now has a second large, permanent companion in orbit comparable to the Moon's relationship with Earth.*

## 2. What's Actually True

The astronomical reality is both genuinely fascinating and substantially different from typical headline framing.

**Earth's Orbital Neighborhood Is More Complex Than Most People Know**

The region of space in Earth's vicinity contains several categories of objects that interact gravitationally with our planet in ways that are not captured by the simple "Earth has one Moon" model that most people carry:

1. **Permanent natural satellite:** The Moon — roughly 3,475 km diameter, orbiting at ~384,400 km average distance, stable on billion-year timescales.

2. **Temporary captured satellites ("minimoons"):** Small near-Earth asteroids that are briefly captured into Earth's gravitational sphere of influence, typically remaining for months to a few years before escaping. **Granvik et al. (2012)** in *Icarus* performed Monte Carlo simulations of near-Earth asteroid trajectories and estimated that at any given time, there is approximately a 0.5% probability that Earth hosts at least one temporarily captured rotating object (TCRO) larger than 10 cm in diameter. The study suggested that Earth maintains a steady-state population of minimoons, with roughly one sub-meter object captured at any given moment.

3. **Quasi-satellites:** Objects in heliocentric (Sun-orbiting) orbits that are resonant with Earth's orbit and remain in close proximity to Earth for extended periods — decades to centuries — without being gravitationally bound to Earth in the strict sense. Cruithne (3753) and 2023 FW13 fall into this category.

4. **Co-orbital asteroids (Trojans):** Objects at Earth's gravitational Lagrange points (L4 and L5), 60 degrees ahead and behind Earth in its orbit. **Connors et al. (2011)** in *Nature* confirmed the first Earth Trojan asteroid, 2010 TK7, approximately 400 meters in diameter, librating around Earth's L4 point.

**The Documented Temporary Captured Satellites**

The closest match to the "second moon" claim are the confirmed temporary captured satellites:

*2006 RH120:* Discovered in September 2006, this ~3-meter object was first detected by the Catalina Sky Survey. **Kwiatkowski et al. (2009)** in *Astronomy & Astrophysics* confirmed that 2006 RH120 was a natural object (initially suspected to be a spent rocket stage) that had been captured into Earth orbit around mid-2006. It completed approximately four orbits around Earth before escaping back to heliocentric orbit in mid-2007. This was the first confirmed temporary natural satellite of Earth beyond the Moon.

*2020 CD3:* Announced by the International Astronomical Union's Minor Planet Center in February 2020, this ~1-3 meter diameter object was confirmed to have been captured approximately 2.7 years earlier. **Fedorets et al. (2020)** in *The Astronomical Journal* provided the orbital analysis, confirming that 2020 CD3 was a second confirmed temporary captured satellite of Earth. It escaped Earth's gravitational influence in May 2020. Crucially, Fedorets et al. noted that the object showed no non-gravitational forces consistent with a spacecraft, confirming its natural origin.

*2024 PT5:* Described in **Marcos & Marcos de la Fuente (2024)** in *Research Notes of the AAS*, this approximately 11-meter diameter object briefly entered a "horseshoe" orbit that temporarily made it a co-orbital companion of Earth for roughly two months in late 2024. This case was widely reported as Earth gaining a "mini-moon" in late 2024 and early 2025.

**Why These Captures Occur**

The dynamics of temporary capture are explained by the three-body problem — the gravitational interplay between the Sun, Earth, and small asteroid. Near-Earth asteroids sometimes enter Earth's Hill sphere (the region where Earth's gravity dominates over the Sun's tidal force, extending to roughly 1.5 million km) with low enough relative velocity to be temporarily bound. **Astakhov et al. (2003)** in *Nature* documented the chaotic "stickiness" of these capture trajectories, showing that small objects can enter and exit the Hill sphere multiple times in irregular patterns.

The captures are typically short-lived because the Sun's gravitational perturbations accumulate and eventually disrupt the Earth-bound orbit. The temporary chaotic nature of these captures — unlike the stable resonant orbit of the real Moon — is what distinguishes them from true natural satellites.

**Cruithne and the Quasi-Satellite Concept**

3753 Cruithne, a 5-km asteroid discovered in 1986, is perhaps the most persistent source of "second moon" claims. **Wiegert et al. (1997)** in *Nature* described Cruithne's complex orbit: it completes one heliocentric orbit in almost exactly one year, locked in a 1:1 orbital resonance with Earth. From Earth's reference frame, Cruithne traces a horseshoe-shaped path that brings it periodically into the inner solar system. It will remain in this resonance for approximately 5,000 years.

However, Cruithne is emphatically not in Earth orbit. It orbits the Sun, not Earth. Its minimum distance from Earth is approximately 15 million km — roughly 40 times the Earth-Moon distance. Calling it a "second moon" requires such a loose definition of moonhood that virtually any near-Earth asteroid would qualify.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**Mechanism: Orbital Category Conflation and the Scalar Moon Assumption**

The primary claim-specific epistemic mechanism driving belief is **orbital category conflation** — the tendency to treat any gravitational relationship between Earth and another object as equivalent to the Moon-Earth relationship, ignoring the categorical distinctions between permanent satellite, temporary captured object, quasi-satellite, and co-orbital resonance.

This conflation is facilitated by the **scalar moon assumption**: the intuitive belief that moon-ness is a binary property determined by the existence of a gravitational relationship, rather than a spectrum defined by orbital stability, binding energy, object size, and relationship duration. In the public mental model, if something orbits Earth (in any sense), it "is a moon."

The scientific literature does not share this framing. The International Astronomical Union's definitions distinguish carefully between natural satellites (moons), quasi-satellites, and co-orbital objects. In IAU classification, a natural satellite must be gravitationally bound to the primary body in a stable or long-lived orbit. Temporary captured objects in chaotic orbits lasting months to years are not classified as natural satellites in the same sense as the Moon.

**Why Headlines Converge on "Moon"**

Several structural factors drive the "second moon" framing in media:

*Headlines require brevity:* "Earth temporarily captures a 3-meter near-Earth asteroid into a chaotic, weakly-bound heliocentric orbit with Earth-proximity characteristics spanning approximately 9 months" is accurate but not publishable as a headline. "Earth Has a Second Moon" achieves compression at the cost of accuracy.

*Scale-independent noun usage:* English uses "moon" both as a proper noun (the Moon) and a common noun (a moon of Jupiter). This linguistic ambiguity makes the headline technically defensible while practically misleading — most readers process "second moon" against the template of the Moon, not against the template of a 3-meter boulder.

*The discovery hook:* Astronomy reporting disproportionately uses discovery frames. A temporary object that has been in Earth's vicinity for months becomes a story when detected. This discovery-framing discourages discussion of the object's temporary and unstable character — the news is the finding, not the caveat.

*Viral optimization pressure:* On social media platforms, simplified celestial superlatives ("Earth gains second moon") outperform hedged accurate descriptions. Platform-level selection pressure rewards compression and certainty.

**The Asymmetric Correction Problem**

Corrections to "second moon" headlines are structurally disadvantaged. The initial headline is bold and singular; corrections are proportionally qualified ("well, actually it depends on your definition of moon, and it's only temporary, and smaller than a house"). Studies of correction effectiveness in science communication — including **Chan et al. (2017)** in *Science Communication* — find that corrections must be simple and emotionally engaging to effectively compete with erroneous originals, and nuanced orbital mechanics do not meet this bar.

## 4. Verdict

**TRUE BUT MISFRAMED** — Earth does periodically capture small objects into temporary orbital relationships, and these events are genuine astronomical occurrences documented in peer-reviewed literature. The claim that these objects are "moons" in the same sense as the Moon, or that Earth has a new permanent companion, is a misrepresentation driven by headline compression and scale-insensitive terminology.

**What's accurate:**
- Earth's Hill sphere does temporarily capture small near-Earth objects, and these events have been confirmed by orbital analysis
- The objects designated 2006 RH120 and 2020 CD3 are confirmed temporary natural satellites of Earth — the only two such objects definitively characterized as of the time of writing
- At any given time, Earth may possess one or more undetected minimoons, according to statistical modeling
- Earth Trojan asteroids (e.g., 2010 TK7) and quasi-satellites (e.g., Cruithne, 2023 FW13) represent additional categories of co-orbital objects in Earth's gravitational neighborhood
- These phenomena are scientifically interesting and represent active areas of research, including for their potential as accessible asteroid sample-return targets

**What's misframed:**
- Temporary captured objects are not "moons" in the sense that the Moon is a moon — they are weakly bound, transient, and often meter-scale objects
- Quasi-satellites like Cruithne orbit the Sun, not Earth, and their minimum Earth distance is tens of millions of kilometers
- The word "captured" implies deliberate or permanent acquisition; astronomically, these captures are temporary outcomes of chaotic three-body dynamics
- No newly discovered object constitutes a second permanent natural satellite of Earth comparable to the Moon

**Overall guidance:** Earth's relationship with small near-Earth objects is genuinely more complex than the single-Moon picture, and periodic captures do occur. The science is interesting enough to be reported accurately without requiring misleading lunar analogies.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**Minimoon Science and the Catalina Sky Survey Legacy**

The systematic discovery of near-Earth objects — including potential minimoons — has accelerated substantially since the inception of wide-field sky survey programs in the late 1990s. The Catalina Sky Survey, Pan-STARRS, and the Zwicky Transient Facility now survey the sky with sufficient cadence to detect meter-scale objects in near-Earth space.

**Granvik et al. (2012)** noted that before these surveys, minimoon captures were essentially invisible to astronomy — the objects are too small and too close for traditional asteroid detection programs optimized for fainter, more distant targets. The very existence of minimoon captures as a phenomenon was inferred from dynamics simulations before any confirmed detection.

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory (formerly the LSST), now operational in Chile, is expected to dramatically increase the cadence and sensitivity of near-Earth object surveys. **Bolin et al. (2014)** in *Icarus* modeled the expected detection rates for the LSST, predicting that minimoon discoveries should increase substantially — potentially to several confirmed temporary captures per year. This means "Earth gains a second moon" headlines are likely to proliferate over the next decade, making clear public understanding of what these events actually mean increasingly important.

**The Reclassification Problem in Planetary Science**

The "second moon" claim touches on a broader epistemological challenge in planetary science: the instability of classification categories when new objects are discovered. The same conceptual stress that produced the Pluto reclassification debate has created ongoing ambiguity around the edges of "moon," "minor planet," "dwarf planet," and "asteroid."

**Margot et al. (2015)** in *The Astronomical Journal* proposed a formal quantitative criterion for distinguishing planets from dwarf planets based on orbital clearing efficiency, but no equivalent formal criterion distinguishes "moon" from "quasi-satellite" or "temporary captured satellite" in IAU nomenclature. This definitional softness is partly responsible for the category-conflation problem in popular coverage.

**Mission Planning Implications**

One reason minimoon science matters beyond headlines is spacecraft mission planning. Temporarily captured near-Earth asteroids represent the lowest delta-v (energy) targets in the solar system for potential sample-return missions. **Granvik et al. (2013)** in *Meteoritics & Planetary Science* analyzed the energetics of minimoon rendezvous and return, finding that confirmed captures could, in principle, be reached with lower fuel requirements than the Moon itself.

NASA's Asteroid Redirect Mission (cancelled) and various international proposals for asteroid sample return have all considered near-Earth asteroids as targets partly for their accessible delta-v profiles. If a minimoon were detected far enough in advance — which the forthcoming Rubin Observatory should enable — it could represent an accessible primitive material sample, potentially from the asteroid belt.

**Lunar Genesis and the Protoplanetary Context**

The Moon itself formed through a giant impact approximately 4.5 billion years ago, when a Mars-sized body (Theia, in the dominant hypothesis) struck the proto-Earth and ejected material that coalesced into the Moon. **Canup & Asphaug (2001)** in *Nature* modeled this giant impact scenario and found that it produces a debris disc consistent with the Moon's composition.

That original capture event was not reversible — the impact energy was sufficient to generate debris that remained gravitationally bound. Modern minimoon captures are comparably ephemeral precisely because the objects are so small: their binding energies are insufficient to overcome the Sun's tidal perturbations over long timescales. The Moon is the product of a high-energy, irreversible event; minimoons are products of low-energy, reversible gravitational conditions.

**Cultural Dimensions of Moon Narratives**

The Moon occupies an extraordinary place in human cultural imagination. It is the first extraterrestrial body visited by humans, the object around which most ancient lunar calendars were organized, and a persistent symbol across multiple cultural traditions. **Kelley & Milone (2011)** in *Exploring Ancient Skies* document the centrality of lunar observation to virtually every ancient astronomical tradition.

This cultural weight means that any claim involving "a moon" immediately acquires significance disproportionate to the astronomical facts. A 3-meter boulder temporarily captured into a chaotic orbit for nine months is astronomically interesting and practically unimportant; its designation as "a second moon" transforms it, in the popular imagination, into something that competes conceptually with the object that has dominated human night-sky observation since our species emerged.

This linguistic borrowing of cultural resonance — attaching astronomical trivia to a culturally freighted term — is a recognizable pattern in science communication that not only distorts individual claims but gradually erodes the linguistic precision with which scientists discuss the solar system.

## 6. Observatory Assessment

Earth's temporary capture of small near-Earth asteroids is a real phenomenon, confirmed by peer-reviewed orbital analysis of multiple objects. The science is interesting, and the prospect of more systematic detection by next-generation surveys makes it increasingly worth public understanding.

But "second moon" is a category error. The objects involved are orders of magnitude smaller than the Moon, occupy chaotic temporary orbits rather than stable bound trajectories, and typically remain in Earth's vicinity for months rather than eons. Calling them moons borrows the cultural and astronomical significance of a word that belongs to a structurally different class of object.

The accurate story — that Earth periodically hosts temporary small natural satellites, that a suite of co-orbital asteroid types exist in Earth's gravitational neighborhood, and that detection rates will increase substantially with new survey capabilities — is genuinely interesting and does not require misleading framing to generate public engagement.
