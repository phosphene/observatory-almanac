# Common Earthquake Myths — Animals Predict Them, Doorways Are Safest, California Will Fall Into the Ocean

## 1. The Claim

Earthquake mythology is distinctive among scientific misconceptions because it clusters: multiple false beliefs about the same phenomenon reinforce each other, and all three major myths addressed here carry public safety consequences that make accurate understanding practically important rather than merely academically interesting.

The three claims evaluated in this entry are:

**Claim A — Animals reliably predict earthquakes**: Animals — particularly dogs, cats, fish, reptiles, and farm animals — exhibit distinctive abnormal behaviour in the hours or days before a major earthquake strikes, and this behaviour can be used as a reliable early warning system. The claim appears as folk knowledge in Chinese, Japanese, Italian, and American popular earthquake culture and has been endorsed at various times by government bodies undertaking earthquake prediction research.

**Claim B — Doorways are the safest place during an earthquake**: The correct response to feeling an earthquake is to stand in a doorframe or doorway, which will maintain structural integrity and protect occupants if the building collapses. This advice has been standard emergency guidance in the United States for much of the 20th century, and many people over forty received it explicitly in school earthquake drills.

**Claim C — California will fall into the ocean**: The continued tectonic activity along the San Andreas Fault will eventually cause California, or at least its coastal regions, to separate from the rest of North America and slide into the Pacific Ocean. This claim appears in casual conversation, popular culture, and is sometimes cited as a motive for avoiding California real estate.

All three claims require nuanced evaluation. The animals-predict claim contains a genuine scientific question, still under active research, that remains unresolved rather than definitively refuted. The doorway myth is unambiguously false and dangerously so, representing a case where obsolete safety advice has persisted past its expiry date. The California-into-ocean claim is geologically impossible under any standard model but points toward real features of California's tectonic future that are themselves dramatic and worth understanding accurately.

## 2. What's Actually True

**Claim A — Animals and Earthquake Prediction: What Research Shows**

The scientific literature on animal earthquake precursors is substantial, heterogeneous, and unresolved. The question is not, as casual debunking sometimes implies, simply that animals don't respond to earthquakes — they do, in documented ways. The question is whether these responses constitute a reliable, prospective predictive signal or are a retrospective pattern-matching artefact.

The best-documented animal responses to earthquakes involve detectable physical precursors that animals may plausibly sense before human instruments register them. These include: P-wave ground motion (primary seismic waves travel faster than the damaging S-waves and surface waves; animals with sensitive vibration detection may sense P-waves seconds before humans feel the shaking); ionospheric and electrical field changes that some researchers believe precede large earthquakes; infrasound; and possibly changes in groundwater chemistry. Several of these mechanisms are physically plausible, though the evidence for their reliable occurrence before major quakes is contested (Ikeya, M., 2004, *Earthquakes and Animals: From Folk Legends to Science*, World Scientific, Singapore).

The cited evidence for animal precursors comes primarily from retrospective accounts and a small number of prospective studies. The most scientifically significant prospective study was conducted at Yanachaga National Park in Peru, where a motion-sensor-equipped camera network designed to study wildlife also captured an unusual anomaly: five days before the magnitude 7.0 Mw Haiti earthquake in 2010, animal activity near a watercourse fell dramatically — species that normally appeared nightly essentially stopped appearing (Wikelski, M. et al., 2020, "Potential short-term earthquake forecasting by farm animal monitoring," *Ethology*, 126(9), 931–941). The authors were cautious about interpretation, noting the study was observational and that the proposed mechanism (increased radon release from ground stress activating soil and air charge changes that discomfited animals near water) was speculative.

The Max Planck Institute's ICARUS project (International Cooperation for Animal Research Using Space) has deployed satellite tags on farm animals in earthquake-prone regions of Italy to test whether coordinated abnormal behaviour patterns precede earthquakes. Preliminary results published in *Ethology* (Wikelski et al., 2020) showed that goats, sheep, and dogs exhibited statistically elevated activity levels hours to days before several seismic events in the Apennines, with the advancement of the signal over time correlating with epicentral distance (animals closer to the epicentre showed earlier signals). These results are intriguing but the sample sizes are small in seismological terms and have not been independently replicated at scale.

Against these intriguing signals stands a substantial body of negative evidence. A systematic review by U.S. Geological Survey scientists found that for every rigorously documented instance of unusual animal behaviour before an earthquake, there were multiple instances of similar behaviour that did not precede any seismic event (Kirschvink, J.L., 2000, "Earthquake prediction by animals: Evolution and sensory perception," *Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America*, 90(2), 312–323). The fundamental problem is base rate: unusual animal behaviour is common; earthquakes large enough to matter are relatively rare; the combinations that appear to be predictive are subject to massive confirmation bias — people remember the times the animals acted strangely before a quake but do not log the many times strangeness preceded nothing.

The Chinese experience provides a cautionary case. After the successful (by some accounts) use of animal precursors to evacuate Haicheng before a magnitude 7.3 earthquake in February 1975 — saving an estimated 150,000 lives — Chinese authorities expanded their earthquake prediction network using animal monitors, among many other methods. The system failed catastrophically at the Tangshan earthquake of July 28, 1976 (magnitude 7.8), which struck without warning and killed approximately 250,000 people despite the prediction network being operational. Investigation of the Haicheng case itself has raised questions about how much of the evacuation was based on animal observations versus foreshock sequences and other indicators (Wang, K. et al., 2006, "Predicting the 1975 Haicheng earthquake," *Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America*, 96(3), 757–795).

**Summary on Claim A**: Animals show responses consistent with sensing specific physical precursors to earthquakes. There is preliminary evidence, not yet replicated at scale, that coordinated animal behaviour changes might provide some advance warning in specific experimental conditions. There is no validated, operationally reliable animal-based early warning system, and the existing literature is dominated by retrospective confirmation bias rather than prospective predictive success. The claim that animals "predict" earthquakes reliably is false as stated; the claim that they may respond to physical precursors is plausible but unproven.

**Claim B — The Doorway Myth: Definitively False**

The doorway safety advice was originally generated in the context of unreinforced adobe construction. In traditional adobe buildings — mud-brick construction without structural steel or reinforced concrete — the door frame sometimes incorporated wooden structural members that could maintain their shape and provide an air pocket when the surrounding adobe walls collapsed. Photographs of 19th-century California earthquake damage do show doorframes standing amid collapsed adobe walls, and this visual evidence became the source of advice that was originally context-specific.

Modern building construction is completely different. In standard Western residential and commercial construction since the mid-20th century, doorways are not structurally stronger than adjacent wall sections. They are typically hollow-core or solid-core doors hung on standard wooden or metal frames that are part of, not independent of, the wall structure. There is nothing inherently load-bearing about a doorframe in a wood-frame, steel-frame, or reinforced concrete building. Worse, doorways are locations of swing hazards — doors can slam violently during shaking — and exit locations that are often near other occupants, creating collision and trampling risks during a sudden earthquake response.

The U.S. Geological Survey, the American Red Cross, and the California Governor's Office of Emergency Services all abandoned the doorway recommendation decades ago. Current consensus guidance, widely known as the "Drop, Cover, and Hold On" protocol, directs people to: drop to hands and knees immediately; take cover under a sturdy table or desk if one is within a step or two, or against an interior wall away from windows; cover the back of the head and neck with the arms; and hold on until shaking stops (U.S. Geological Survey, 1999, "Earthquake myths," *USGS Fact Sheet*, 2003-3045; updated subsequently on usgs.gov).

Research by the Earthquake Country Alliance and FEMA on the distribution of injuries during earthquakes confirms that most earthquake injuries are caused by falling objects — unsecured shelving, ceiling tiles, light fixtures, window glass, and brick/masonry façades — rather than structural collapse. Seeking cover under a sturdy surface addresses the dominant injury mechanism. Doorways provide no protection against any of these hazards and create additional risks (Wood, M.M. et al., 2007, "Improving earthquake preparedness: Comparison of the effectiveness of an earthquake preparedness campaign," *Natural Hazards Review*, 8(3), 79–88; Shoaf, K.I. et al., 1998, "Injuries as a result of California earthquakes in the past decade," *Disasters*, 22(3), 218–235).

The persistence of the doorway advice despite its official obsolescence is a well-documented case of safety-guidance lag. A 2004 survey by Wood and colleagues found that a majority of Americans still believed the doorway was safest during an earthquake, despite the U.S. Geological Survey having published corrective information. Shoaf et al. (1998) documented injury patterns from the 1994 Northridge earthquake confirming that doorway-seekers had no meaningful injury advantage compared to occupants who performed other behaviours.

**Claim C — California Into the Ocean: Geologically Impossible**

California will not fall into the ocean. The geological mechanism through which this could occur does not exist. Understanding why requires explaining what the San Andreas Fault actually is and how it works.

The San Andreas Fault is a transform fault: a boundary between the Pacific Plate (which carries Los Angeles and much of coastal southern California) and the North American Plate (which carries the rest of North America). At a transform fault, tectonic plates slide horizontally past each other rather than one plate subducting beneath the other; there is no process at a transform fault that causes either plate to descend or to detach from continental crust. The San Andreas Fault generates lateral displacement — southern California is moving northwest relative to the rest of North America at approximately 4.6 centimetres per year, the approximate rate at which fingernails grow (United States Geological Survey, *San Andreas Fault System*, fact sheets, multiple years).

The long-run trajectory of this movement is that Los Angeles, currently about 600 kilometres south-southeast of San Francisco, will be adjacent to San Francisco in approximately 13 million years, and will continue moving northwestward. There is no point in this trajectory at which California "falls into the ocean" or separates from the continent. The Pacific Plate will carry its portion of California northward along the coast; there is no mechanism for coastal separation into a floating island, submergence, or oceanic exile.

The Cascadia Subduction Zone in northern California, Oregon, and Washington is a different fault system with a different structure — here the Juan de Fuca Plate does subduct beneath North America, and the region is subject to the highest magnitude earthquake risk in the contiguous United States. But the Cascadia system does not threaten to detach California; it creates the conditions for large subduction-zone megathrust earthquakes of the type that struck Japan in 2011. The two fault systems are often confused in popular discussion.

What is true and genuinely dramatic about California's tectonic future: the state will experience multiple magnitude 7.0+ earthquakes within the lifespans of people currently alive; portions of both the San Andreas and Hayward Fault systems are considered by USGN to be in late stages of their earthquake cycle, meaning significant earthquakes are expected; and the long-term northward movement of the Pacific Plate will transform California's geography over geological timescales, though not through anything resembling submergence (Field, E.H. et al., 2015, "Long-term time-dependent probabilities for the Third Uniform California Earthquake Rupture Forecast (UCERF3)," *Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America*, 105(2A), 511–543).

## 3. Why People Believe This

Each myth has a specific epistemic mechanism in addition to sharing the general features of vivid imagery, cultural transmission, and lack of counter-exposure.

**Claim A — Animals: Retrospective Confirmation Bias and the Human-Animal Communication Fantasy**

The specific mechanism maintaining animal-prediction beliefs is **retrospective pattern completion**: after an earthquake, people search their memories (and social networks) for anything unusual that preceded it. Unusual animal behaviour, precisely because animals cannot explain themselves and because humans attribute meaning to their behaviour anxiously, provides an irresistible candidate for a pattern. Since animals exhibit unusual behaviour regularly for reasons entirely unrelated to seismology — seasonal changes, predator presence, health conditions, weather, mating cycles — there is virtually always something that can be recruited as a "sign" in retrospect. The confirmatory memory is highly salient (tied to a dramatic event); the disconfirmatory evidence (all the times unusual animal behaviour preceded nothing) is never logged or remembered.

This mechanism is reinforced by a cultural archetype: the idea that animals possess sensory or spiritual access to natural processes beyond human perception. This archetype appears in Pacific Rim, South and Southeast Asian, indigenous American, and European folk traditions, meaning that animal-prediction beliefs arrive accompanied by cultural prestige and ancestral authority, not merely as individual observations. Animal precursor claims can invoke both scientific plausibility (animals might sense P-waves) and traditional wisdom simultaneously, which makes them resistant to single-vector refutation.

**Claim B — Doorways: Safety Advice Fossilisation**

The doorway myth's specific persistence mechanism is what might be called **authoritative source inertia**: the advice came from official emergency guidance transmitted through school earthquake-drill programmes. Information received in childhood from authoritative institutional sources is particularly resistant to later revision, because its original encoding is associated with trust and official finality. Many people carry the doorway instruction as something "I was officially taught" rather than something they reasoned toward — and official childhood instruction is not typically experienced as provisional or repealable.

The mechanism is amplified by the absence of personal earthquake experience in most people's lives. Unlike, say, dietary advice that can be tested against immediate feedback, earthquake safety behaviour is not tested until a major earthquake strikes. The years-long gap between instruction and potential test opportunity means that updated guidance rarely reaches the people who received initial guidance, and there is no experiential feedback loop to generate natural updating. A person who "knows" doorways are safest and never experiences a major earthquake will carry that knowledge unchallenged indefinitely.

**Claim C — California Into the Ocean: Literal Cataclysm Imaging of Metaphorical Drift**

The California-into-ocean claim's specific mechanism is **metaphorical-literal confusion applied to scientific language**. Geologists and journalists describing the San Andreas Fault routinely use spatial language: California is "sliding toward" the Pacific; plates "drift"; the coast is "moving away." For an audience without tectonic background, "sliding," "drifting," and "moving away" activate mental imagery of coastal separation — an island drifting free, like an ice floe breaking from a shelf. The imagery is intuitive for metaphorical motion in a way that "horizontal strike-slip displacement at 4.6 cm/year" simply is not.

This mechanism is specific to earth science communication: geological and tectonic language uses directional motion verbs to describe slow horizontal displacement at the scale of continents, but those verbs map onto human-scale kinematic experience in ways that generate false physical imagery. The "California drifting into the ocean" mental model is a reasonable naive physics inference from the language used to describe the San Andreas, even though it is completely wrong about the direction and nature of movement. The claim is not the result of bad information; it is the result of correct information being processed through an inappropriate physical intuition.

## 4. Verdict

**Claim A — Animals Predict Earthquakes**
**Propositional Status:** Unresolved (not false, not validated)
**Confidence:** Moderate
**Epistemic Basis:** Preliminary prospective evidence; large retrospective confirmation bias literature
**Phenomenological Status:** Real animal responses to some precursors; unreliable as prediction system
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Moderate (identifies real phenomenon; overstates reliability)

The claim is best evaluated as "not yet supported by sufficient prospective evidence to be operationally useful" rather than as definitively false. Animals may respond to P-waves and/or geophysical precursors; the ICARUS data and the Yanachaga findings are genuinely interesting signals. But the confirmation bias problem is severe enough that all existing evidence must be treated cautiously, and no reliable animal-based early warning system exists or has been demonstrated to work in prospective conditions at scale.

**Claim B — Doorways Are the Safest Place**
**Propositional Status:** False. Dangerously so.
**Confidence:** Very High
**Epistemic Basis:** Official USGS/Red Cross guideline revision; injury data from real earthquakes
**Phenomenological Status:** Not applicable
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Negative (may increase injury risk by directing people away from proper cover)

This is the most important correction in this entry from a public safety perspective. The doorway advice is wrong, the correct advice (Drop, Cover, Hold On) is well-established and publicly available, and the gap between the false belief and the correct guidance continues to have material consequences when earthquakes strike. School curricula, public emergency preparedness campaigns, and workplace safety training should standardise on Drop, Cover, Hold On and explicitly revise the doorway assumption for cohorts who received older guidance.

**Claim C — California Will Fall Into the Ocean**
**Propositional Status:** False (geological impossibility under any known tectonic model)
**Confidence:** Very High
**Epistemic Basis:** Standard plate tectonic science; USGS San Andreas Fault scientific literature
**Phenomenological Status:** Not applicable
**Folk Wisdom Value:** None (though attention to tectonic reality is warranted and valuable)

The claim is geologically impossible. Its persistence causes low-level harm by distorting public understanding of actual earthquake risk in California, occasionally influencing real estate decisions and public policy arguments, and distracting from the genuine and serious seismic hazards the state faces.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**The History of Earthquake Prediction**: The earthquake prediction problem is one of the most intellectually contested issues in geophysics. Unlike weather forecasting, where the governing physics is well-understood and runs deterministically forward from initial conditions, earthquakes involve processes at the scale of geological faults that are inherently stochastic at the human observational timescale. Fault stress accumulation follows physical laws, but the triggering of rupture depends on conditions at the fault interface — asperity geometry, pore fluid pressure, small-scale heterogeneity — that cannot be fully characterised by surface observation.

The history of earthquake prediction is littered with claims of breakthrough that failed validation. The VAN method (Varotsos, P., Alexopoulos, K. & Nomikos, K., 1986, "Seismic electric signals, a new method of detecting impending earthquakes," *Tectonophysics*, 126(3–4), 321–341), which claimed that specific electric signals in the earth preceded major earthquakes, attracted significant attention in the 1980s and 1990s but failed independent validation studies (Wyss, M. & Booth, D.C., 1997, "The short-term prediction of the 1975 Haicheng earthquake," *Geophysical Journal International*, 131(1–15), 149–160). Radon gas anomalies in groundwater prior to earthquakes have been studied extensively; some anomalies exist, but their predictive reliability is similarly unestablished.

The reigning consensus in seismology, as expressed through the International Association for Seismology and Physics of the Earth's Interior and the USGS Earthquake Hazards Program, is that short-term deterministic earthquake prediction — specifying a magnitude, location, and time window narrow enough to be operationally actionable — is not currently achievable and may be inherently limited by the physics of fault systems. Probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA) — estimating the probability that an area will experience shaking above a threshold over a given time period — is well-validated and forms the basis of building codes and land-use planning (Cornell, C.A., 1968, "Engineering seismic risk analysis," *Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America*, 58(5), 1583–1606).

This context matters for interpreting the animal precursor literature: even if animals respond to some physical precursors, those precursors would need to be reliably present before large earthquakes (not present without subsequent earthquakes), detectable with sufficient advance notice, and translatable into actionable warning to contribute meaningfully to earthquake early warning systems. The existing evidence does not approach any of these thresholds. Modern earthquake early warning systems (such as ShakeAlert in the western United States and the nationwide system in Japan) work on P-wave detection by geophysical instrument networks, which can provide 10–60 seconds of warning before damaging S-wave arrival — a very different and far more reliable mechanism (Allen, R.M. & Melgar, D., 2019, "Earthquake early warning: Advances, scientific challenges, and societal needs," *Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences*, 47, 361–388).

**The Drop, Cover, Hold On Evidence Base**: The transition from doorway advice to Drop, Cover, Hold On was driven by systematic analysis of earthquake injury data. Research by Shoaf, Bourque and colleagues using the 1994 Northridge earthquake as a natural experiment found that injury patterns were dominated by falling objects (furniture, decorative objects, shelving), structural element failures (ceiling tiles, plaster), and window glass, with structural collapse causing a minority of injuries even in a damaging moderate-to-large earthquake (Shoaf, K.I. et al., 1998, "Injuries as a result of California earthquakes in the past decade," *Disasters*, 22(3), 218–235).

The "triangle of life" claim — an internet-distributed alternative to Drop, Cover, Hold On that advises people to adopt foetal positions next to large furniture rather than under it, based on the geometry of void spaces in collapsed buildings — has been specifically evaluated and rejected by earthquake safety authorities. The USGS and FEMA have published explicit refutations noting that the triangle-of-life theory is based on collapsed-building scenarios that represent a small minority of earthquake situations in modern construction, and that the recommended position (next to rather than under furniture) provides less protection from falling objects than the under-table position (USGS FAQS, 2013, "Triangle of life," usgs.gov).

**California's Real Tectonic Future**: The San Andreas Fault system generates the most well-documented seismic hazard in North America. The Third Uniform California Earthquake Rupture Forecast (UCERF3), published in 2015, estimated a 60 percent probability of a magnitude 6.7 or greater earthquake in the greater San Francisco Bay Area within the following 30 years, and a 60 percent probability of the same threshold being met in the greater Los Angeles metropolitan area (Field et al., 2015). The Hayward Fault — which runs through the densely populated East Bay region of the San Francisco Bay Area — is considered one of the most dangerous urban faults in the United States because of its proximity to high-density infrastructure.

The threat is not California "falling into the ocean" but something both more mundane and more consequential for the millions of people who live on or near these fault systems: the near-certain occurrence of severe ground shaking in major urban areas within the lifetimes of current residents, with cascading consequences for buildings, infrastructure, water supply, and emergency response capacity. The USGS ShakeOut scenario modelling estimates that a magnitude 7.8 earthquake on the San Andreas Fault would cause approximately 1,800 deaths, 50,000 injuries, and $200 billion in damage in the southern California scenario alone (Jones, L.M. et al., 2008, *The ShakeOut Scenario*, U.S. Geological Survey Open-File Report 2008-1150).

The policy implication of accurate tectonic understanding is therefore not California-avoidance but preparedness investment: in building code enforcement, infrastructure retrofit, emergency supply storage, and community resilience. The "California into the ocean" myth, by exaggerating and displacing the actual threat onto an impossible scenario, paradoxically reduces preparation for the real hazard by making it seem science-fictional rather than probabilistically certain.

**Cross-Cultural Earthquake Mythology**: Earthquake myths are global because earthquakes are global. Japanese mythology attributes earthquakes to the movement of a giant catfish (*namazu*) restrained beneath the earth — a myth that was deployed not only as cosmological explanation but as political commentary after the Ansei Edo earthquake of 1855 (Ouwehand, C., 1964, *Namazu-e and Their Themes: An Interpretive Approach to Some Aspects of Japanese Folk Religion*, Brill, Leiden). Mesoamerican traditions held that successive ages of the world end in earthquakes; Irish mythology includes earthquake spirits; Norse mythology attributed earthquakes to the bound god Loki's struggles beneath the earth.

These mythological systems served different functions than empirical disaster preparedness, but they share with more recent earthquake myths the characteristic of personalising and agentifying a process that is in actuality mechanical, impersonal, and probabilistic. The cultural tendency to attribute earthquake activity to personal agents — spirits, animals exercising intention, the earth "punishing" human behaviour — reflects the broader human preference for intentional over mechanical explanations of significant events, a cognitive tendency described as intentionality bias (Kelemen, D. & Rosset, E., 2009, "The human function compunction: Teleological explanation in adults," *Cognition*, 111(1), 138–143). Modern earthquake myths like the California-into-ocean claim are secular descendants of these older traditions: they personalise and dramatise tectonic process into narratives of consequence and destination rather than mechanical displacement.

**Earthquake Communication and Public Preparedness**: The earthquake myths discussed in this entry have practical consequences for public safety. Incorrect behaviour during an earthquake (seeking a doorway rather than cover) can contribute to injury. Overreliance on animal behaviour as a warning system can create false confidence and, more acutely, false alarms — in one documented case in Peru in 1981, a government official's announcement of an earthquake prediction based partly on animal observations caused mass evacuation, economic disruption, and, when no earthquake followed, significant erosion of public trust in official warnings (Geller, R.J., 1997, "Earthquake prediction: A critical review," *Geophysical Journal International*, 131, 425–450).

The communication challenge is nuanced: the corrections to the doorway myth and the California-into-ocean myth are clear, but the animal precursor question is genuinely scientifically unresolved, and communicating "we don't know yet" without feeding further mythology requires precision. The gap between "animals may sense some physical precursors" and "animals reliably predict earthquakes" is precisely the gap between ongoing scientific inquiry and operational disaster warning, and collapsing it — in either direction — causes different kinds of harm.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes does not maintain a unified entry on earthquake myths, and its individual coverage of the three claims addressed here is absent or incomplete as of this entry's publication date. The USGS does maintain authoritative factsheet corrections to the doorway myth and the California-into-ocean claim on usgs.gov, and these represent the most authoritative and current source for both topics. The American Red Cross website and Ready.gov provide current Drop, Cover, Hold On guidance with explicit comparison to the outdated doorway advice.

Fact-checking the animal precursor claim is complicated by the genuine scientific unresolved nature of the question. A flat "false" verdict would misrepresent the current state of research; the ICARUS/Wikelski findings are peer-reviewed published work in a reputable journal, not pseudoscience. The honest characterisation is "unvalidated and not operationally reliable, with some preliminary signals worth scientific investigation." This is a more complex verdict than binary fact-checking platforms typically accommodate, which may partially explain the absence of major fact-check entries on the topic.

The earthquake domain illustrates a structural limitation of fact-checking as a genre: it works best for claims that are either clearly true or clearly false, but many important scientific communication failures involve claims at the frontier of scientific knowledge — claims that are not yet validated but not yet definitively refuted, where the appropriate public communication is probabilistic uncertainty rather than binary verdict. The animal precursor question is exactly this type, and the fact-checking ecosystem has not developed robust conventions for engaging with it responsibly.

The most effective corrections come not from fact-check articles but from embedded public-safety campaigns: school earthquake drills that explicitly teach Drop, Cover, Hold On and explain why the doorway advice is obsolete; USGS public outreach materials on tectonic reality; and science journalism that engages with the animal precursor literature at its actual level of evidence rather than either credulous acceptance or dismissive debunking. The long-term goal is earthquake literacy — an educated public that understands probabilistic seismic hazard, knows correct protective behaviours, and can evaluate speculative prediction claims with appropriate scepticism. No single fact-check article achieves this; it requires sustained educational investment across multiple channels.
