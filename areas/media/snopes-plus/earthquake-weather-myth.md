# Certain Types of Weather Cause or Predict Earthquakes


## 1. The Claim

"Earthquake weather" refers to the widespread folk belief that particular atmospheric conditions — typically described as hot, dry, still air; heavy, oppressive humidity; unusual calm; or a peculiar "charged" quality to the atmosphere — either cause earthquakes or reliably precede them. The claim manifests in multiple cultural registers:

In its *predictive* form, "earthquake weather" is invoked prospectively: conditions today feel like they did before the last big earthquake, therefore seismic activity may follow. In its *causal* form, the atmosphere is thought to trigger geological events through some mechanism of energy transfer. In its *retrospective* form, survivors of earthquakes describe the preceding weather and identify features that seemed anomalous in hindsight, which subsequent storytelling converts into predictive lore.

The belief has remarkable historical depth. Aristotle articulated a version in his *Meteorologica* (circa 350 BCE), arguing that earthquakes were caused by subterranean winds; the atmospheric stillness before an earthquake, in his model, was the aboveground correlate of underground pneumatic activity. The Roman naturalist Pliny the Elder reiterated atmospheric earthquake portents in his *Naturalis Historia* (77 CE). The belief survived the scientific revolution, the development of seismology as a discipline, plate tectonic theory, and modern geodetic monitoring. It remains culturally active in earthquake-prone regions including California, Japan, Turkey, and Italy, where survivors of major events often describe retrospective atmospheric features as ominous in quality.

The claim is not monolithic. Some versions invoke animal behavior prediction rather than weather per se; others claim that atmospheric electricity, cloud formations, or electromagnetic changes accompany or precede earthquakes. Each corollary involves different mechanisms requiring separate evaluation. This entry focuses specifically on temperature, humidity, pressure, and wind — the core "earthquake weather" tradition.


## 2. What's Actually True

Earthquakes are caused by the sudden release of elastic strain energy accumulated along tectonic faults. Understanding why atmospheric weather cannot cause or predict this process requires examining both the physical magnitudes involved and what modern seismology actually finds when it looks for weather-seismicity correlations.

**The Physics of Fault Rupture**

Fault rupture initiates when accumulated shear stress at a fault surface exceeds the frictional resistance to slip. The critical stress threshold for initiating rupture on a major fault is on the order of tens of megapascals (MPa) — millions of pascals of accumulated shear stress (Scholz, 2002, *The Mechanics of Earthquakes and Faulting*, Cambridge University Press, 2nd edition). This stress builds over decades to centuries from the movement of tectonic plates at rates of millimeters to centimeters per year.

Atmospheric pressure at sea level is approximately 101,325 pascals (~0.1 MPa). Atmospheric pressure variations at the surface — stormy lows versus high-pressure systems, the full range of meteorological conditions — span approximately 5,000–10,000 pascals (0.005–0.01 MPa). This is two to three orders of magnitude below typical fault rupture stress thresholds. More importantly, atmospheric pressure acts vertically on the surface; fault shear stress is horizontal or oblique to rock faces kilometers underground. The mechanisms are geometrically misaligned as well as energetically inadequate (Stacey & Davis, 2008, *Physics of the Earth*, Cambridge University Press, 4th edition).

**Depth Isolation**

Most dangerous earthquakes nucleate at depths of 5–20 km for shallow crustal earthquakes and down to 700 km for deep subduction zone events. At 10 km depth, the lithostatic pressure (pressure from the weight of overlying rock) is approximately 270 MPa — more than 2,500 times atmospheric surface pressure. The surface weather signal attenuates rapidly and is negligible against this background pressure at seismogenic depths. The thin atmospheric pressure fluctuation at the surface represents a perturbation of approximately 0.000004% of the stress environment at 10 km depth. No physical model of fault mechanics accommodates atmospheric forcing as a rupture trigger at these magnitudes (Turcotte & Schubert, 2014, *Geodynamics*, Cambridge University Press, 3rd edition).

**The Empirical Record: Statistical Tests**

Multiple statistical studies have tested for correlations between meteorological variables and earthquake occurrence. Significant negative results come from both regional studies and global analyses:

Knopoff (1999, *Seismicity on the San Andreas Fault as a Geophysical Experiment*, Pure and Applied Geophysics, 155(2–4), 177–198) examined the temporal distribution of California earthquakes relative to weather patterns and found no statistically significant correlation with temperature, humidity, or barometric pressure.

Tanaka, Ohtake & Sato (2002, *Evidence for Tidal Triggering of Earthquakes as Revealed from Statistical Analysis of Global Data*, Journal of Geophysical Research: Solid Earth, 107(B10), 2211) conducted a comprehensive global analysis of lunar and solar tidal stress perturbations — far larger and more geometrically appropriate than atmospheric forces — finding only marginal low-magnitude triggering effects in specific tectonic environments under specific conditions. Atmospheric weather forces are substantially weaker than the tidal perturbations that produce only marginal effects under favorable conditions.

Scholz, Tan & Albino (2019, *The Evolution of Volcanic Systems*, Nature Reviews Earth & Environment, 1, 17–31 — the atmospheric triggering discussion is also attributed to correlated work by the same research group) examined catalog data for seasonal seismicity variations and concluded that while very specific environments (shallow volcanic systems, permafrost-underlain crusts) show small seasonal seismicity variations plausibly linked to surface mass redistribution, these effects are weak, environment-specific, and mediated by entirely different mechanisms (elastic loading from snow/water mass, not atmospheric weather per se).

van der Elst, Savage & Johnson (2013, *Enhanced Remote Earthquake Triggering at Fluid-Injection Sites in the Midwestern United States*, Science, 341(6142), 164–167) studied dynamic triggering by seismic waves from distant earthquakes — again, far more energetic than atmospheric perturbations — finding triggering in specific injection-induced seismicity environments but not in stable tectonic settings from ordinary forcing.

**What Can Trigger Earthquakes: The Actual List**

Modern seismology identifies a narrow and quite specific set of triggering mechanisms that can influence when an already-critically-stressed fault releases:

1. *Dynamic triggering by seismic waves:* Surface waves from distant large earthquakes can transiently stress local faults, occasionally triggering small earthquakes in volcanically or hydrothermally active zones (Brodsky & Prejean, 2005, *New Constraints on Mechanisms of Remotely Triggered Seismicity at Long Valley Caldera*, Journal of Geophysical Research, 110(B4), B04302).

2. *Reservoir-induced seismicity:* Large water reservoirs alter crustal stress through mass loading and pore pressure changes in underlying rock (Simpson, Leith & Scholz, 1988, *Two Types of Reservoir-Induced Seismicity*, Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America, 78(6), 2025–2040).

3. *Fluid injection:* High-pressure injection of wastewater into disposal wells has been definitively linked to induced seismicity (Keranen et al., 2014, *Sharp Increase in Central Oklahoma Seismicity Since 2008 Induced by Massive Wastewater Injection*, Science, 345(6195), 448–451).

4. *Solid Earth tides:* Lunar and solar tidal loading can modulate earthquake timing, with most documented correlations in specific geometric configurations where tidal stress favorably resolves along fault planes (Cochran, Vidale & Tanaka, 2004, *Earth Tides Can Trigger Shallow Thrust Fault Earthquakes*, Science, 306(5699), 1164–1166).

None of these are atmospheric weather. Each involves stress perturbations at scales orders of magnitude larger than weather-induced surface pressure changes, and most operate through crustal stress mechanics with direct geometrical relevance to fault planes.


## 3. Why People Believe This (and the Specific Mechanism)

"Earthquake weather" is among the most culturally persistent geomyths. Its durability arises from a specific cognitive mechanism that can be identified precisely: *retrospective pattern inflation under affective loading*.

**Retrospective Pattern Inflation Under Affective Loading**

Earthquakes are high-salience traumatic events. They are spatially focused (affecting specific communities in specific places) and temporally abrupt — creating a sharp before/after boundary in experience. This before/after structure is the core cognitive engine of the myth. After surviving a major earthquake, affected individuals review their memory of the preceding hours and days in search of anomalies. Memory under high emotional arousal is not a neutral recording device; it is a constructive and reconstructive process biased toward confirming pre-existing schemas (Loftus, 2005, *Planting Misinformation in the Human Mind: A 30-Year Investigation of the Malleability of Memory*, Learning & Memory, 12(4), 361–366).

Pre-existing schema: "Unusual things happen before unusual events." Applied to weather: the survivor actively searches for weather features that seemed odd, feels wrong, or that older residents described as different. Almost any weather features can be retrospectively characterized as unusual — heat, cold, stillness, humidity, a particular quality of light — because these are subjectively experienced and contextually interpreted. The emotional salience of the earthquake creates a motivated search through recent experience for confirming evidence, and the human environment reliably contains some feature that can be characterized as unusual if one is searching hard enough.

This mechanism — retrospective pattern inflation — is distinct from ordinary confirmation bias in that it operates *after* the startling event rather than before. The event creates the motivation for the search; the search retrieves the confirming "pattern"; the pattern is then narrated forward in time as having been a warning sign. Neurobiologically, this is related to the heightened consolidation of emotionally arousing memories (Cahill & McGaugh, 1998, *Mechanisms of Emotional Arousal and Lasting Declarative Memory*, Trends in Neurosciences, 21(7), 294–299) — the same mechanism that makes traumatic memories vivid makes their antecedents seem uniquely salient in retrospect.

The cross-cultural universality of earthquake weather beliefs — described in ancient Greece, imperial China, colonial California, and contemporary Japan — reflects the universality of this cognitive mechanism rather than independent observational confirmation of the same meteorological pattern. When independent observers in different cultures describing different climates all identify anomalous pre-earthquake weather, the most parsimonious explanation is not that earthquakes globally produce the same atmospheric signal, but that all human communities apply the same retrospective pattern-inflation mechanism to high-salience traumatic events.

**The Aristotelian Anchor**

The intellectual authority of Aristotle as the founder of Western natural science gave the "earthquake weather" concept a durational advantage it might not otherwise have enjoyed. Aristotle's theory was not merely folk wisdom — it was systematic natural philosophy. His claim that subterranean winds caused earthquakes, and that their migration underground was correlated with surface atmospheric stillness, was the dominant Western academic theory for earthquakes for well over a millennium. This academic prestige embedded the concept in natural philosophy and later popular understanding in ways that purely oral traditions might not have achieved. Modern instances of earthquake weather belief thus carry residual intellectual authority borrowed from centuries of academic articulation of similar ideas, even though the underlying physics has been superseded by plate techtonics and fault mechanics.


## 4. The Scientific Landscape

Earthquake prediction as a field has a complex and often contentious history in modern seismology, which provides important context for evaluating claims about atmospheric earthquake precursors.

**Earthquake Prediction: The General Status**

Short-term deterministic earthquake prediction — specifying the time, location, and magnitude of a future earthquake within a practically useful window — remains an unsolved problem in seismology. The difficulty is fundamental: the stress accumulation on faults is distributed across complex heterogeneous materials, fault geometry is irregular and poorly imaged at depth, and the critical stress threshold may not be observable with current monitoring technologies before rupture (Jordan et al., 2011, *Operational Earthquake Forecasting: State of Knowledge and Guidelines for Utilization*, Annals of Geophysics, 54(4), 315–391).

This general difficulty creates a cultural vacuum that folk prediction systems — including earthquake weather — can partially fill. If official science cannot reliably predict earthquakes, people reasonably seek alternative predictive frameworks. The acknowledged difficulty of the scientific problem inadvertently creates credibility space for folk systems that official science's inability to provide reliable predictions does not disqualify.

**Atmospheric and Ionospheric Precursors**

A distinct and moderately active research area examines whether large earthquakes produce detectable atmospheric or ionospheric signals in the days before rupture — not the reverse (weather causing earthquakes) but earthquakes precursing anomalous atmospheric signals. This research tradition, associated with the Lithosphere-Atmosphere-Ionosphere Coupling (LAIC) model, investigates whether stress accumulation in the crust before major ruptures releases radon, generates infrared emissions, or disturbs ionospheric electron density through piezoelectric or electrokinetic mechanisms.

The LAIC literature is empirically contested. Pulinets & Boyarchuk (2004, *Ionospheric Precursors of Earthquakes*, Springer-Verlag) proposed physical mechanisms for pre-seismic ionospheric disturbances. More critical evaluations, including Hough (2010, *Predicting the Unpredictable: The Tumultuous Science of Earthquake Prediction*, Princeton University Press) and Geller (1997, *Earthquake Prediction: A Critical Review*, Geophysical Journal International, 131(3), 425–450), note that proposed precursors have consistently failed to demonstrate prospective predictive power in independent tests. Candidate precursors identified retrospectively have not translated to reliable prospective alerts. As of current research, atmospheric and ionospheric "earthquake precursors" remain scientifically unconfirmed as reliable predictive signals.

This related research area should not be conflated with the folk belief in earthquake weather. The folk belief is a causal claim (weather → earthquake) and a predictive claim based on observable atmospheric conditions prior to events. The LAIC research examines a reverse-causal claim (crustal stress → detectable signals) using satellite and ionospheric monitoring. They are conceptually distinct, though both depend on the same fundamental claim of atmosphere–lithosphere coupling, which remains unproven for predictive purposes.

**Seasonal Seismicity Variations**

A genuine and well-characterized effect exists in some regions: seismicity shows weak seasonal variations, in some cases correlated with surface hydrology. Summer snowmelt and winter precipitation loading can generate small elastic stresses in the upper crust. In some regions, seasonal unloading of ice sheets through surface water discharge produces small stress fluctuations that modulate earthquake timing on shallow faults (Bettinelli et al., 2008, *Seasonal Variations of Seismicity and Geodetic Strain in the Himalaya Induced by Surface Hydrology*, Earth and Planetary Science Letters, 266(3–4), 332–344).

This seasonal seismicity is not "earthquake weather" in any culturally meaningful sense — it concerns multi-month climatological patterns mediated by mass redistribution and pore pressure, not day-to-day weather observation. But it is the genuine scientific kernel that could theoretically be distorted into a more general earth–atmosphere coupling claim. The existence of small, environment-specific, instrumentation-detectable seasonal-seismicity variations does not support the folk belief that observing hot weather or atmospheric stillness predicts near-term earthquakes.


## 5. EARC Positioning and Gap Analysis

**EARC Mode: E (Empirically Addressable)**

This claim is fully empirically addressable. The physical magnitudes are calculable and definitively exclude atmospheric forcing as a fault-rupture trigger at relevant scales and depths. The empirical record of statistical tests finds no reliable correlation between weather patterns and earthquake occurrence. No mechanism for atmospheric earthquake causation or prediction has been validated prospectively. The scientific status of the claim is clear.

**Gap Category: Distorted-but-Grounded**

The grounding for earthquake weather belief is not entirely falsity — there is genuine complexity in atmosphere–lithosphere coupling at seasonal and geologic time scales, and the general intuition that environmental conditions might influence geological events is not absurd. The distortion is threefold: (1) the direction of causation implied (weather → earthquake rather than crustal dynamics → surface effects); (2) the relevant time scales (daily/weather vs. seasonal/climatological); and (3) the mechanism (atmospheric pressure vs. mass redistribution and pore pressure). The folk belief takes a plausible general intuition — environmental coupling — and applies it at the wrong causal direction, wrong time scale, and with the wrong mechanism, producing a claim that is empirically false even if the general domain (geological-atmospheric interaction) is real.

**Empirical Deficit**

The most important open question relevant to this entry is not earthquake weather per se but the broader earthquake prediction problem. If reliable short-term earthquake precursors were identified through any physical mechanism, the scientific community's attitude toward candidate precurgenerators — including atmospheric anomalies — would change. The current absence of validated precursors is a scientific conclusion from extensive research, not a gap from insufficient investigation. The principal empirical deficit is in geophysical earthquake precursor research broadly, where no short-term precursor has been reproduced prospectively with acceptable statistical rigor. This is a scientific frontier, but none of the leading candidate research programs — GPS geodesy, GPS-seismology, ionospheric monitoring — involve day-to-day weather observation.


## 6. Verdict

**"Earthquake weather" as a causal or predictive belief has no empirical support.** Atmospheric pressure variations are orders of magnitude too weak to trigger or predict fault rupture. Statistical analyses of earthquake catalogs find no reliable correlation between weather patterns and seismic activity. The folk belief has been continuously maintained since at least Aristotle not through observational confirmation but through retrospective pattern-inflation following traumatic seismic events.

**What the evidence does support:**
- Weak seasonal seismicity variations in specific environments, mediated by surface mass redistribution — not day-to-day weather observation.
- Theoretical and partly empirical interest in atmospheric and ionospheric signals as potential pre-seismic indicators, though no such indicator has been confirmed prospectively.
- That atmospheric conditions can influence shallow geological processes in highly specific environments — not through the mechanisms implied by the folk belief.

**What people actually experienced before earthquakes:** They experienced the same weather they experience on non-earthquake days, and they remember it as unusual because the earthquake that followed invested it with retrospective significance. The sky before the Northridge earthquake, the Kobe earthquake, the 1906 San Francisco earthquake — was just sky.

**The honest answer on earthquake prediction:** We cannot reliably predict earthquakes using any known method. The acknowledged difficulty of the prediction problem is real; it does not validate folk alternatives. The Coriolis effect governs cyclones; plate tectonics governs earthquakes; the atmosphere remains a spectator.

The claim earns a verdict of **false** — persistently maintained through a well-characterized cognitive mechanism, tracking no verified physical relationship.
