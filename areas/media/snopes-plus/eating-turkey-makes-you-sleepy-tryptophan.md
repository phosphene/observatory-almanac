# Turkey Doesn't Make You Sleepy — Your Dinner Does

## 1. The Claim

Eating turkey makes you sleepy. The cause is tryptophan — an amino acid found in turkey that the body converts to serotonin and then to melatonin, the sleep hormone. This is why Thanksgiving gatherings in the United States reliably end in napping relatives on couches. Turkey is uniquely rich in tryptophan. Eating it sends you to sleep.

The claim has achieved enough cultural saturation in the United States to be referenced in sitcoms (*Seinfeld*, *Friends*, *How I Met Your Mother*), cited in Thanksgiving segments on morning news programs, and repeated as established fact by millions of people who consider it a useful piece of applied neuroscience. It is one of the few pieces of biochemistry that a significant portion of the non-scientific public believes they know.

## 2. What's Actually True

Turkey contains tryptophan. This is true. Tryptophan is an essential amino acid — the body cannot synthesize it and must obtain it from food. It is a precursor in the biosynthetic pathway to serotonin, which is itself a precursor to melatonin. The biochemical chain from tryptophan to sleepiness is real.

The problem is that turkey is not a particularly rich source of tryptophan compared to other common foods. Per gram of protein, turkey's tryptophan content is roughly equivalent to chicken, beef, pork, and cheese — in some cases lower. Cheddar cheese actually contains more tryptophan per gram than turkey. So does chicken breast. Yet no comparable cultural narrative attributes post-cheese-platter drowsiness to tryptophan.

**Why turkey doesn't make you sleepy via tryptophan specifically:**

Tryptophan's conversion to serotonin and melatonin depends critically on conditions in the brain. To cross the blood-brain barrier, tryptophan competes with other large neutral amino acids (LNAAs) — including leucine, isoleucine, valine, tyrosine, and phenylalanine — for the same transport proteins. These amino acids are all present in protein-rich foods. When you eat a protein-heavy meal, the ratio of tryptophan to competing LNAAs in the bloodstream is not favorable for tryptophan — the competition reduces the proportion of tryptophan crossing into the brain.

Tryptophan crosses the blood-brain barrier most efficiently under a specific condition: a high-carbohydrate, low-protein meal. In this case, carbohydrates stimulate insulin secretion, which drives competing LNAAs into muscle tissue — removing the competition and allowing tryptophan relatively easier passage into the brain. This is counterintuitive: tryptophan's sleep effect is potentiated by carbohydrates, not by protein. Eating turkey alone would be one of the less effective ways to raise brain tryptophan.

Thanksgiving dinner, however, is not turkey alone. It is a very large meal containing:

- **Multiple large protein servings** (turkey, often ham or other meats)
- **Large carbohydrate loads** (stuffing, mashed potatoes, sweet potatoes, rolls, pie)
- **High fat content** (gravy, butter-rich dishes, pie crusts)
- **Often alcohol** (wine, beer, cocktails before and during)
- **Total caloric volume** substantially higher than a typical meal

The post-Thanksgiving sleepiness is attributable to this combination. A large meal diverts blood flow to the gastrointestinal tract to support digestion, reducing circulation to other areas and inducing a state of relative fatigue. Carbohydrate loading causes blood sugar to rise and then drop, producing a characteristic energy dip. Alcohol is a central nervous system depressant with well-established sedative effects. The combination of these factors reliably produces drowsiness.

Turkey is the named centerpiece of the meal and receives the explanatory credit. But it would be more accurate to say that Thanksgiving dinner makes you sleepy — and that any meal of comparable size, carbohydrate content, fat, and alcohol would produce similar effects.

### The Tryptophan Supplement Evidence

The confusion is partly understandable because tryptophan does have genuine effects on sleep when administered in the right way. Tryptophan supplements taken on an empty stomach — removing the LNAA competition problem — have been shown in clinical studies to reduce sleep latency and improve sleep quality. This effect is real. L-tryptophan was sold as a dietary sleep aid in the 1980s before being withdrawn from the market after a contaminated batch caused an eosinophilia-myalgia syndrome outbreak.

The clinical evidence for tryptophan's sleep effects refers to supplemental tryptophan under controlled conditions, not to tryptophan consumed as part of a protein-rich meal. The conditions are entirely different.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The tryptophan-turkey story has features that make it extremely sticky: it has a scientific-sounding mechanism, it attaches to a specific, widely shared experience, and it was propagated by media that gave it a patina of authority. Understanding why the claim persists despite repeated debunking requires examining each of these factors in some depth.

**The experience is phenomenologically real and demands explanation.** People genuinely feel sleepy after Thanksgiving dinner. The phenomenon being explained is not imaginary — it is one of the most universally reported features of the American Thanksgiving experience. When a plausible biochemical mechanism is available for a real and widely shared experience, it tends to be adopted without further scrutiny. The experience validates the explanation; the explanation validates the experience. This mutual reinforcement is a powerful epistemic trap: the person who knows the tryptophan story experiences Thanksgiving drowsiness and feels confirmed. The person who doesn't know the story encounters the tryptophan explanation and immediately recognizes it as fitting their experience. No additional evidence is sought on either end.

No one feels the need to check whether other foods have comparable tryptophan levels, or whether the other features of the meal might be sufficient causes, because the tryptophan explanation is *already working* — it fits the phenomenon. The absence of a control condition (a Thanksgiving-sized meal without turkey; a turkey-only meal without carbohydrates or alcohol) is never felt as a gap, because real-world belief formation doesn't run controlled experiments.

**It sounds like science and invites people into a gratifying expertise.** Tryptophan, serotonin, melatonin — these are real biochemical terms describing a real metabolic pathway. The claim does not ask people to believe in anything implausible. It invites them into a simple causal chain (turkey → tryptophan → serotonin → melatonin → sleep) that is internally coherent and sounds knowledgeable to recite. This is important: the tryptophan story functions socially as a piece of "informed" conversation. Saying it at the Thanksgiving table signals that you know something about nutrition science. The people listening don't know the comparison baseline — how much tryptophan is in chicken, or cheese — and the speaker typically doesn't either. The social function of the claim reinforces its transmission independently of its accuracy.

The fact that several key conditions for this biochemical chain are absent in the actual Thanksgiving meal scenario — the LNAA competition problem, the requirement for fasting or carbohydrate-only conditions — is not visible in the simple version of the story and requires enough biochemical detail to explain that the social context of Thanksgiving conversation rarely supports.

**The cultural transmission has institutional support.** The Thanksgiving-tryptophan connection has been presented on morning news programs as seasonal science content, included in Thanksgiving-themed newspaper articles, referenced in sitcoms (*Seinfeld*, *Friends*, *How I Met Your Mother*), and repeated so often in American popular culture that it has achieved the status of trivia-fact — the kind of thing people say when they want to sound knowledgeable without being pedantic. When mainstream media presents a claim as a useful piece of applied science, the claim acquires authority that is not easily challenged by informal correction. The seasonal recurrence of the story means it is reinforced every November regardless of how many times it has been debunked in the preceding eleven months.

Media incentives also play a role. Thanksgiving science content is reliably produced every year because editors know it will be read. The tryptophan story is easy to explain, connects to a universal experience, and doesn't require difficult expertise to cover. The debunking of the story is also produced every year, for similar reasons. But the original claim and its debunking do not compete on equal terms: the claim has decades of prior installation, social function, and experiential reinforcement; the debunking is a one-time encounter with a negative claim about something people already believe.

**The alternatives are less glamorous and attribute causation to less prestigious elements.** "You're sleepy because you ate too much" is true but uninteresting. "You're sleepy because of the carbohydrate crash and the alcohol" is more accurate but credits wine and stuffing rather than the star of the meal. The tryptophan explanation elevates turkey to a special neurochemical status and provides a more satisfying scientific story than "you overate and drank too much." There is a social and narrative logic to centering turkey: it is, after all, the organizing symbol of the occasion. Attributing the post-meal torpor to the centerpiece of the feast makes the story feel unified. Attributing it to a combination of carbohydrate overload, fat, and ethanol is more accurate but disperses the causal story across elements that feel less thematically central.

**Correction requires knowing the comparison, which is almost never volunteered.** To understand that turkey is not a special source of tryptophan, you need to know that chicken, beef, pork, and cheddar cheese contain comparable or greater amounts per gram of protein — information that is not usually volunteered in the context of the claim. The claim is easily accepted without knowing what the comparison baseline would be. If someone told you that turkey contains 250-300 mg of tryptophan per 100g of protein, you would have no way of evaluating that figure without knowing that chicken contains roughly the same amount, or that cheddar cheese is actually higher. Most people don't have those comparison figures memorized, which means the claim about turkey's tryptophan content passes unchallenged even when people are specifically told the number.

**The seasonal timing of exposure creates a hostile environment for correction.** The tryptophan story surfaces primarily in November, in the context of Thanksgiving preparation and celebration. This is a context characterized by social bonding, tradition, and emotional warmth — not by critical scrutiny of dietary biochemistry. People who encounter the debunking during Thanksgiving weekend are typically embedded in contexts that prioritize social cohesion over epistemic correction. The debunking — even when it lands — is processed in a state of emotional and metabolic sedation, surrounded by family, and immediately competes with decades of prior installation. The annual rhythm of claim and correction has not resulted in a net movement toward the correct account in popular belief.

## 4. Verdict

**True that:** turkey contains tryptophan; tryptophan is a precursor to melatonin; post-Thanksgiving sleepiness is real.

**False that:** turkey contains more tryptophan than other common proteins; eating turkey causes sleepiness via tryptophan; tryptophan in a protein-rich meal crosses the blood-brain barrier efficiently.

**The accurate account:** Thanksgiving drowsiness is caused by the combined effects of eating a large meal, high carbohydrate and fat intake, and often alcohol. Turkey's tryptophan content is not exceptional, and tryptophan from a protein-rich meal is poor at crossing the blood-brain barrier due to competition from other amino acids.

This is an **A-category claim** (Accepted as True) with a **phenomenologically-real gap**: the sleepiness is real; the biochemical story that explains it is wrong.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The tryptophan story is a textbook example of what might be called **mechanism substitution**: a real phenomenon (post-Thanksgiving drowsiness) is given a real-sounding mechanism (tryptophan → melatonin) that happens to be wrong in context, while the actual mechanisms (overeating, carbohydrate crash, alcohol) are available but ignored. Mechanism substitution is a distinct and underappreciated epistemic failure mode. It differs from simple fabrication (inventing a mechanism where none exists) and from selection bias (picking the evidence that confirms a preferred view). It works by annexing a real mechanism — tryptophan genuinely does feed into the melatonin pathway — and misapplying it to a context where its conditions for operation are not met.

Mechanism substitution tends to occur when:

1. The actual mechanism is mundane and requires no specialized knowledge
2. An alternative mechanism is available that sounds more interesting or scientific
3. The phenomenon being explained is widely shared (everyone has the experience; everyone wants an explanation)
4. There is no obvious route to falsification in daily experience
5. The substituted mechanism contains real components that resist easy dismissal

These conditions are met perfectly in the turkey-tryptophan case. The story could in principle be tested by comparing sleepiness after a large Thanksgiving meal versus a comparably large meal of other foods with similar tryptophan content. Or by comparing sleepiness when the turkey portion is held constant and side dishes and alcohol are removed. These tests are never done in practice because no one experiences the claim as requiring verification — and because running such a study would require a level of experimental rigor that does not fit naturally into the folk epistemology of Thanksgiving science.

**The hierarchy of causes in post-meal drowsiness.** Sleep onset after a large meal is a multi-factor outcome with several established contributing mechanisms, each operating through a different pathway. Understanding these mechanisms collectively is more useful than identifying a single cause:

- **Blood diversion to the gastrointestinal tract:** Digesting a large meal requires substantial blood flow to the gut. This is not a myth — splanchnic blood flow does increase post-meal, though the old claim that this "starves the brain of blood" is an exaggeration. The modest redistribution of blood and the energy cost of digestion do contribute to reduced alertness.

- **Insulin and blood glucose dynamics:** A large carbohydrate load triggers an insulin spike followed by a glucose drop. The glucose drop in the two-to-four hour post-meal window produces the characteristic energy dip that many people experience as fatigue or drowsiness, independent of any amino acid metabolism.

- **Parasympathetic activation:** Eating a large meal activates the parasympathetic nervous system ("rest and digest" mode), reducing sympathetic arousal and promoting a state of reduced alertness and physical relaxation.

- **Alcohol's GABAergic effects:** Ethanol is a central nervous system depressant that enhances GABA-A receptor activity, producing anxiolytic and sedative effects. A moderate amount of alcohol consumed over several hours at a Thanksgiving meal is a substantial sedative load, entirely independent of any food component.

- **Caloric volume and gastric distension:** Simply filling the stomach to capacity sends satiety signals that reduce alertness as part of the body's post-meal regulatory response.

Attributing Thanksgiving drowsiness primarily to a single amino acid in the turkey, while ignoring all these established mechanisms, is a reductive attribution that doesn't survive contact with standard causal reasoning. The actual causal picture is more distributed, less satisfying narratively, but more correct.

**The carbohydrate-tryptophan interaction — the story's ironic reversal.** A more interesting but less discussed version of the science is the actual relationship between carbohydrates and tryptophan uptake. High-carbohydrate, low-protein meals do genuinely raise brain tryptophan and produce drowsiness via the mechanism described in the claim. Carbohydrates stimulate insulin, insulin drives competing large neutral amino acids into muscle, and the result is relatively elevated plasma tryptophan ratio — facilitating its passage across the blood-brain barrier. This effect is real and well-documented.

This means that Thanksgiving dinner's drowsiness is caused partly by tryptophan — but delivered by the stuffing, mashed potatoes, and pie, not the turkey. The turkey, as a protein source, actually makes the tryptophan-to-serotonin pathway *less* efficient by reintroducing competing amino acids. The starring role for tryptophan in Thanksgiving drowsiness, to the extent it exists at all, belongs entirely to the carbohydrate side dishes. This is the ironic reversal embedded in the correct account: the mechanism people invoke is real, but it works through the foods they don't credit.

**Why the correct account is harder to communicate.** There is an asymmetry in cognitive load between the wrong story and the right one. "Turkey has tryptophan, which makes you sleepy" is a single-sentence explanation. The correct account requires explaining the blood-brain barrier, LNAA competition, insulin's role in amino acid uptake, the difference between protein and carbohydrate contexts, and then attributing the phenomenon to a multi-factor combination that includes overeating and alcohol. The correct account is not difficult to understand — but it is longer, less elegantly causal, and requires a sustained attention to mechanism that the social context of Thanksgiving does not typically support.

This is a general asymmetry between simple wrong explanations and complex correct ones. The wrong explanation is an asset in casual transmission; the correct explanation requires more bandwidth. Good science communication about this misconception needs to find a simpler correct frame — not "it's actually about your LNAA plasma ratio" but something closer to "turkey doesn't have special tryptophan — cheese has more; your Thanksgiving nap is really from the stuffing, wine, and how much you ate." That framing is shorter, more concrete, and targets the right comparisons.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim has been addressed by major fact-checking outlets more than many science misconceptions, primarily because it surfaces every November as seasonal Thanksgiving content. Snopes has covered it (rated as a "mixture" — the tryptophan mechanism is real, but turkey is not special), and scientific debunking pieces appear regularly in *Scientific American*, *Time*, *Vox*, and other outlets in the weeks before Thanksgiving.

The "mixture" framing used by some fact-checkers is instructive. It correctly identifies that the tryptophan pathway is real, while noting that turkey is not a special source. Some coverage goes further and identifies the actual causal mechanisms (overeating, carbohydrates, alcohol). The best corrections do all three: acknowledge the real phenomenon, trace the failure of the popular explanation, and substitute the correct multi-factor account.

Despite this, the claim continues to circulate at essentially its historical rate. This is partly because the seasonal nature of the discussion means that most people encounter the original claim and its debunking simultaneously — and the debunking competes with the original for cognitive salience in a context (Thanksgiving preparation or celebration) where people are not in a skeptical mood. The correction is heard, perhaps believed momentarily, and then displaced by the more culturally satisfying original story.

The turkey-tryptophan misconception is effectively unkillable in its cultural form. It will continue to be repeated, continue to be corrected, and continue to be repeated again. What good science communication can do is ensure that a meaningful fraction of people hold the correct belief with some conviction — enough to share it when the annual conversation arises — and that the correction is accessible to anyone motivated to look.
