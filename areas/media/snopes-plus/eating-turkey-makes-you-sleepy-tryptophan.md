# Eating turkey makes you sleepy because of tryptophan


## 1. The Claim

Every November across the United States and Canada, a familiar explanation circulates for why guests fall asleep on the sofa after Thanksgiving dinner: the turkey. Specifically, the claim holds that turkey contains unusually high levels of the amino acid tryptophan, and that tryptophan causes drowsiness by triggering the production of serotonin and melatonin in the brain. The causal chain is presented as well-established nutritional science: you eat turkey → your body absorbs tryptophan → tryptophan converts to serotonin and then to melatonin → melatonin is a sleep hormone → you fall asleep.

The claim is so pervasive it has achieved the status of folk wisdom. It appears in newspaper Thanksgiving features, in sitcom dialogue, in holiday health coverage, and in parenting conversations about why children nap after the holiday meal. Comedians have used it as shorthand for decades. It is invoked not merely as a cultural explanation for a familiar annual phenomenon but as an established physiological fact — something the science supposedly confirms and everybody knows.

The tryptophan-turkey connection has also been used outside the holiday context as a general nutritional claim: that turkey is a food you should eat in the evening if you want to sleep better; that its tryptophan content makes it a natural sleep aid; and, in some wellness content, that turkey-based meal plans can help with insomnia or anxiety through serotonin modulation.

The appeal of the claim is partly its specificity. Unlike a vague appeal to "something in the food" making you sleepy, the tryptophan explanation names a molecule, implies a biochemical mechanism, and sounds like the kind of thing a nutritionist might confirm. This scientific framing gives it credibility that outlasts its accuracy.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Tryptophan is real, and its biochemical role is genuine.** The claim is not baseless invention. L-tryptophan is indeed an essential amino acid — one that the human body cannot synthesise and must obtain from dietary sources. It is a precursor in the two-step metabolic pathway that produces serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine), and serotonin can subsequently be converted to melatonin in the pineal gland. Melatonin is a key regulator of circadian rhythms and the sleep-wake cycle. The pathway from tryptophan to melatonin is biochemically real and well established.

The foundational research on dietary tryptophan and brain neurotransmitter synthesis was conducted by Fernstrom and Wurtman (1972, *Science*), who demonstrated in rat models that the plasma ratio of tryptophan to competing large neutral amino acids (LNAAs) predicted brain tryptophan uptake and serotonin synthesis. This work was seminal and its core findings have been replicated and extended. Subsequent research by Wurtman and Wurtman (1989, *Scientific American*) further developed the model of nutritional effects on central neurotransmitter concentrations, establishing the theoretical framework that the popular turkey story draws upon.

**Turkey does contain tryptophan — but so does almost every protein food.** The critical empirical failure of the turkey-specific claim is straightforward: turkey does not contain especially high levels of tryptophan relative to other common protein sources. The United States Department of Agriculture (USDA) National Nutrient Database records approximately 250–310 mg of tryptophan per 100 g of cooked turkey depending on preparation. Comparable values for other meats are: chicken breast approximately 280–330 mg/100 g; pork loin approximately 230–270 mg/100 g; beef approximately 220–270 mg/100 g; and canned tuna approximately 300–330 mg/100 g. Dairy products including cheddar cheese and milk contain comparable or higher levels per gram. Eggs, soybeans, pumpkin seeds, and many nuts contain tryptophan at similar or higher concentrations. There is nothing uniquely soporific about turkey's tryptophan content relative to the rest of the protein landscape of a normal diet.

**The blood–brain barrier problem: protein meals suppress tryptophan transport.** The deeper biochemical error in the popular claim concerns what happens when you eat a high-protein meal. Tryptophan does not enter the brain freely; it must be actively transported across the blood–brain barrier by a carrier protein that also transports several other large neutral amino acids (LNAAs), including leucine, isoleucine, valine, phenylalanine, and tyrosine. These amino acids compete for the same transporter (Fernstrom & Wurtman, 1972, *Science*; Pardridge, 1998, *Journal of Nutrition*).

When you eat a protein-rich food — like turkey — you substantially increase plasma concentrations of all these competing amino acids simultaneously. The ratio of tryptophan to its competitors, which is what determines brain tryptophan uptake, does not necessarily increase and may actually decrease after a high-protein meal. Eating turkey by itself, in other words, is a poor strategy for raising brain tryptophan and serotonin. This is a counterintuitive but robustly established neurochemical fact.

**Carbohydrates are the actual dietary tryptophan modulator.** The mechanism that actually raises brain tryptophan is carbohydrate consumption, not direct tryptophan ingestion from protein foods. When you eat carbohydrates, the resulting insulin response causes muscle cells to take up branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) — leucine, isoleucine, and valine — from the bloodstream. This selectively reduces the plasma concentration of tryptophan's main competitors while leaving tryptophan relatively unaffected, because insulin does not stimulate muscle uptake of tryptophan at the same rate. The result is an improved tryptophan-to-LNAA ratio in plasma, which increases tryptophan transport across the blood–brain barrier (Fernstrom & Wurtman, 1972, *Science*; Wurtman et al., 2003, *Journal of Psychiatry and Neuroscience*).

This means that the Thanksgiving foods most likely to promote any genuine tryptophan-mediated drowsiness are not the turkey but the stuffing, mashed potatoes, sweet potatoes, cranberry sauce, dinner rolls, and pie — the carbohydrate-heavy accompaniments. The turkey actually works against the mechanism it is credited with activating.

**The real causes of post-Thanksgiving drowsiness.** The physiological torpor that follows a large holiday meal has multiple contributing causes, none of which involve tryptophan uniquely or specifically. First, the sheer caloric volume of a large meal triggers significant diversion of blood flow to the gastrointestinal tract to support digestion, temporarily reducing blood flow and oxygenation available to other systems including the brain (Davenport et al., 2010, *Physiology & Behavior*). This postprandial hemodynamic response produces measurable cognitive slowing and subjective fatigue. Second, the high carbohydrate content of a typical Thanksgiving meal produces a significant insulin spike followed by a relative dip in blood glucose, which is associated with fatigue. Third, alcohol consumption, which accompanies many Thanksgiving meals, is a central nervous system depressant with well-documented sedative effects that are independent of any food component. Fourth, the social context — relaxed family gathering, warm indoor environment, lateral furniture — creates physical and psychological conditions conducive to sleep. Finally, for many people the meal is consumed in the early-to-mid afternoon, a time window that naturally overlaps with a circadian nadir in alertness known as the post-lunch dip (Roenneberg et al., 2007, *Current Biology*).

None of these mechanisms requires turkey-specific tryptophan. The same drowsiness would follow from any sufficiently large, carbohydrate-heavy, socially relaxed meal consumed in the early afternoon, regardless of protein source.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The mechanism sounds just scientific enough.** The tryptophan claim occupies a persuasion sweet spot: it is specific enough to sound like science (it names a molecule, invokes a biochemical pathway) but simple enough to be transmissible without technical education. Most people are not equipped with the knowledge of competitive LNAA transport kinetics required to evaluate it critically. The claim's plausibility rests on a simplified but partially real biochemical story, and the simplification is in the direction of confirmation rather than refutation. This is harder to resist than an obviously implausible claim.

**Post hoc and real correlation.** People really do feel sleepy after Thanksgiving dinner. The drowsiness is genuine and consistent. The tryptophan explanation was available and authoritative-sounding. Once it was widely disseminated, the felt experience of post-meal drowsiness provided constant reinforcing "confirmation" — a textbook case of post hoc reasoning. Every repeated Thanksgiving drowsiness episode feels like re-verification. The claim becomes self-confirming in subjective experience even as it misidentifies the cause.

**Epistemic novelty — what this case reveals about popular neuroscience claims.** The turkey-tryptophan story exemplifies a specific failure mode in popular science communication: the *compound simplification*, in which a biochemically real mechanism is applied to a context where multiple preconditions for that mechanism are absent or inverted. The mechanism from tryptophan to melatonin is real. But the compound claim — turkey raises tryptophan, tryptophan enters the brain, melatonin causes drowsiness — smuggles in at least three empirically false steps (turkey uniquely raises tryptophan; eating protein raises brain tryptophan; the melatonin effect occurs fast enough to explain acute post-meal drowsiness). Each smuggled step is invisible in the simplified narrative. This pattern — real mechanism, wrong context, compounded oversimplification — is extremely common in popular neuroscience and nutrition claims, and the turkey story is a textbook teachable case. The corrective requires understanding not just the molecule but the rate-limiting step in its transport and the dietary context required for its activation. This is the kind of mechanistic nuance that popular health communication almost never carries, yet which is often the entire point.

**Reinforcement by official-seeming channels.** By the time the tryptophan story was being routinely questioned by biochemists, it had already been embedded in mainstream media Thanksgiving coverage and popular culture. Cultural feedback loops — it appears in a sitcom, therefore it must be true; it is repeated by a news anchor, therefore it must be confirmed — insulate the claim from correction. Corrections are also less shareable: "Turkey makes you sleepy because of a complex interplay of carbohydrate-mediated LNAA displacement, postprandial hemodynamics, and alcohol" is not a sentence that travels well on social platforms.


## 4. Verdict

**Mixture — the biochemistry is real but the specific claim about turkey is not supported.** Turkey contains tryptophan, and tryptophan is a precursor to serotonin and melatonin. These facts are correct. But turkey does not contain more tryptophan than comparable protein foods; eating a protein-rich meal actually reduces rather than increases the brain's tryptophan access relative to competing amino acids; and the post-meal drowsiness associated with Thanksgiving is adequately explained by overeating, carbohydrate load, alcohol, and postprandial hemodynamics — none of which are specific to turkey. The tryptophan-turkey connection assigns causal credit to the wrong component of the meal for physiological reasons that are well-established in the biochemical literature. The Snopes verdict of "mixture" is apt: the chemistry exists but the specific causal story is wrong.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The turkey-tryptophan story is one of a family of popular nutritional claims that take a genuine biochemical finding and misapply it to a dietary context. Related examples include the claim that warm milk causes drowsiness through tryptophan (same objection: protein meal competition), that chocolate causes serotonin boosts (the serotonin from food does not cross the blood–brain barrier), and that sugar causes hyperactivity in children (a claim that double-blind trials have repeatedly been unable to confirm; see Wolraich et al., 1994, *New England Journal of Medicine*).

What these claims share is the use of an authentic molecular pathway as a rhetorical anchor. The molecule is real. The pathway is real. But the dietary quantity, the bioavailability, the pharmacokinetic preconditions, and the competing variables are ignored in the popular version. Nutritional biochemistry is genuinely complex: the effect of any given dietary component on neurochemistry depends on the entire meal composition, the individual's metabolic state, gut microbiome, enzyme expression, and timing. Popular claims that isolate a single compound and a single downstream effect from this complexity will almost always be oversimplifications.

The turkey story also illustrates a temporal problem in science communication: claims become cultural fixtures faster than corrections travel. The tryptophan-turkey connection was in wide popular circulation by the 1980s. Biochemists, nutritionists, and sleep researchers were questioning it seriously by the 1990s. The popular correction — even after thorough debunking by outlets including Snopes, Scientific American, and academic nutrition publications — has still not displaced the original claim in general cultural knowledge. The claim persists because it is woven into a beloved cultural ritual (Thanksgiving), and the emotional and social significance of that ritual makes its associated myths particularly resistant to revision.

For nutrition educators and health communicators, the tryptophan story offers a teaching example of what happens when biochemical specificity is stripped from a claim for the sake of public accessibility. The complete story — tryptophan is real, but competitive transport kinetics mean dietary protein timing matters, and carbohydrates actually help, while the meal's caloric volume and alcohol are the main acute sleep drivers — is accurate and not beyond public comprehension. But it requires more than one sentence, which is one sentence more than most pop-health communication allocates.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has addressed this claim directly, assigning a "mixture" rating: turkey contains tryptophan, and tryptophan can cause drowsiness under certain conditions, but turkey's tryptophan content is not unusual, and the meal context explains the drowsiness better than any turkey-specific compound. This is an accurate and well-calibrated verdict.

Major fact-checking platforms generally find this claim straightforward to address because the biochemical literature is clear, expert consensus is available from nutritionists and sleep researchers, and the claim is falsifiable against USDA food composition data alone (tryptophan levels in turkey vs. comparable meats). The main fact-checking challenge is conveying the competitive transport mechanism in accessible language without creating a more confusing story than the original myth.

Science communication outlets including Scientific American, *Live Science*, and the nutrition literature outlet *The Journal of Nutrition* have published accessible but scientifically accurate accounts of why the turkey-tryptophan story does not hold up. These resources are available to fact-checkers who need backup sourcing beyond the primary biochemistry literature.

The media literacy lesson applicable beyond this specific claim is: when a folk-science claim names a real molecule and a real pathway, the instinct to verify the mechanism rather than just the premise is essential. The molecule being real does not mean the causal story is right. Checking requires asking: does this food uniquely or strongly increase this compound? Does eating it in this context actually activate this pathway? Are there confounders that better explain the observed effect? For tryptophan and turkey, the answers to all three are: no, no, and yes. That structure — real molecule, wrong context — is a reliable signature of a claim that will pass casual plausibility tests and fail rigorous biochemical ones.
