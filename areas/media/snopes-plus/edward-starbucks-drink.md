## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In early May 2021, a photograph posted to Twitter by user @ProjectJosiee—apparently a Starbucks barista—went massively viral. The image showed a Starbucks cup holding what the label indicated was a Caramel Ribbon Crunch Frappuccino belonging to a customer identified by name as "Edward." The beverage's label was covered in an exceptionally long list of customizations and additional ingredients, extending the drink far beyond the typical modifier count for a Starbucks order. The tweet, framed from the perspective of the barista who prepared the drink, conveyed a tone of exhausted disbelief and touched a nerve with hundreds of thousands of social media users—resulting in extensive sharing, liking, and a cascade of humorous response memes and parody tweets.

Snopes journalist Bethania Palma (2021) investigated the claim and explicitly declined to issue a standard fact-check verdict, noting that while this was a "trending topic," it had not been rated by Snopes for specific reasons: neither the original Twitter user nor Starbucks corporate had provided the confirmatory documentation needed to establish with certainty whether the photograph depicted a genuine customer order at a real Starbucks location or had been staged, exaggerated, or otherwise constructed for viral purposes. The Snopes investigation was still pending responses from both the Twitter poster and Starbucks corporate at time of publication, with a public commitment to update the article upon receiving further information.

Starbucks did, however, respond to a query from Dayton, Ohio television station WDTN, issuing a statement affirming the company's commitment to beverage customization as "the heart of the Starbucks Experience" while noting that baristas are instructed to "do their best" in cases where drink customizations physically exceed what a cup can contain. This partial corporate response neither confirmed the specific order in the photograph as genuine nor denied it—it addressed the customization policy as a general matter without engaging the virality of the specific image.

The absence of a Snopes rating is itself analytically significant and will be addressed in detail in §3 below.

## §2 The "Edward Order" and the Archaeology of the Viral Photograph

The specific drink order attributed to "Edward" in the viral photograph was described in the original tweet as a Caramel Ribbon Crunch Frappuccino base with an extraordinarily extensive list of modifications. While the precise modification list was visible in the viral photograph and was extensively discussed and mocked in the social media response, Palma's (2021) Snopes article does not reproduce the full list, describing it primarily through the characterization of a very long and complicated order. The *Today* show, which covered the viral moment, characterized the drink as including modifications that stretched across the entire label on the cup, far exceeding normative Starbucks customization behavior.

The specific construction of the order—whatever its exact ingredients—was understood by Starbucks employees, food journalists, and social media commentators as an extreme manifestation of what has been dubbed Starbucks "secret menu" culture. Starbucks has long maintained an official commitment to customer beverage customization as a core corporate identity value; the phrase "have it your way" (a Burger King trademark phrase that, notably, also went viral in fast food contexts) maps onto a structurally identical consumer empowerment logic. Starbucks's app-based ordering system, in particular, facilitates and normalizes granular modification requests by allowing customers to specify add-ons, substitutions, temperature changes, and preparation instructions through an interface that presents these options as a menu rather than as exceptions.

The "secret menu" phenomenon—documented by journalists including at *Food & Wine*, *Cosmopolitan*, and on dedicated social media accounts—describes beverages that are not listed on the official Starbucks menu but are described on social media and fan blogs as achievable through combinations of available customization options. The "TikTok Pink Drink" and similar viral recipes represent a large and growing class of these items. For baristas, secret menu orders represent a particular form of occupational stressor: the customer who orders from the secret menu expects the barista to either already know the recipe or translate a social media screenshot into a workable set of instructions in real time while managing a queue of other customers.

The Edward order is plausible within this ecosystem. A customer who had assembled an elaborate customization list—whether from a secret menu source, from personal taste experimentation, or from deliberate trolling of the barista labor experience—and ordered it at a Starbucks counter would receive a cup with that order printed on the label in exactly the format visible in the viral photograph. The visual evidence of the photograph is, in this sense, consistent with a real order at a real Starbucks. What cannot be determined from the image alone is whether the name "Edward" was genuine (customers provide their names at the counter; Starbucks staff enter them into the point-of-sale system), whether the customization list was assembled by a real customer or by someone with access to the POS system as a test or joke, or whether the photograph was of a real drink or a staged set-up designed for viral sharing.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Limits of Fact-Checking Viral Moments Without a Falsifiable Claim

The Edward Starbucks case exposes a fundamental tension in the fact-checking enterprise: what is the appropriate epistemic response when a viral photograph is real (no digital manipulation), entertaining, emotionally resonant, and consistent with the known parameters of the claimed context, but lacks confirmatory documentation that would allow a confident verdict on the specific claim's truth?

The Snopes decision not to rate this article is unusual enough to warrant analytical attention. Palma (2021) explicitly states that the trend was documented but not rated "for reasons we'll outline below"—and the outlined reason is the absence of confirmatory response from the barista who originally posted the photograph or from Starbucks corporate about the specific image. This is a responsibly conservative epistemic stance, but it creates a paradox: the article exists, has been indexed, and generates traffic, meaning that the Snopes brand is associated with coverage of the claim, but the Snopes rating infrastructure—designed to communicate a verdict to readers scanning the site—does not deliver a verdict.

This case is representative of a category of viral social media content that Wardle and Derakhshan (2017), in their foundational report on information disorders for the Council of Europe, described as content that is "true or likely true but whose significance or context is distorted in sharing"—a category distinct from fabricated content, manipulated content, or imposter content, and one for which standard true/false fact-checking logic is imperfectly suited.

The Edward Starbucks photograph presents a specific epistemic challenge because its viral significance is not primarily about whether it is a "real" photograph of a "real" order. The photograph's emotional power derives from what it presents itself as representing: the labor experience of a Starbucks barista overwhelmed by an excessive and inconsiderate customer order. This is less a factual claim than a narrative framing—a claim about the character of the customer (inconsiderate, entitled) and the experience of the worker (burdened, underpaid, exploited). Even if the order were confirmed as 100% genuine, the "claim" embedded in the viral photograph's framing involves sociological assertions about Starbucks labor culture that are not verifiable through standard fact-checking methodology.

Conversely, if the photograph were confirmed as staged, the sociological assertion would not necessarily be falsified: Starbucks baristas genuinely face complicated orders, the customization culture genuinely places labor burdens on workers, and the emotional content of the tweet would remain sociologically accurate even if the specific cup in the photograph were a prop. This structural quality—where the falsification of the evidentiary artifact does not falsify the sociological claim the artifact is used to express—represents a class of viral content that fact-checking frameworks have difficulty processing without more nuanced analytical tools.

Soroka et al. (2019), writing in *Political Communication* (Taylor & Francis), documented that emotionally resonant content expressing labor grievance or consumer power asymmetries achieves significantly higher sharing metrics than equivalent content without emotional valence, independent of the factual accuracy of the specific claim. This accounts for the explosive virality of the Edward photograph: it was not primarily verified as factual but experienced as emotionally true—consistent with what many viewers believed about Starbucks workers and the customers who make their lives difficult.

## §4 Starbucks Customization Culture: Labor, Policy, and Consumer Identity

Starbucks's corporate commitment to beverage customization is not merely a marketing positioning statement; it is operationalized at the point-of-sale level through a technology architecture specifically designed to accommodate, record, and transmit complex modification instructions to baristas. The mobile app ordering system, introduced in 2015 and significantly expanded in subsequent years, allows customers to add unlimited modifications to beverages through a cascading menu of options—additional pumps of syrup, alternative milk bases, temperature specifications, specific blend times, and extra inclusions like powder or sauce toppings that can be requested in non-standard quantities.

The labor implications of this architecture have been extensively documented. Dickey and Stanton (2020), reporting in *Business Insider*, found that Starbucks baristas identified mobile order complexity as one of the primary drivers of occupational stress, with particular concern about the inability to push back on orders that were physically difficult or impossible to execute as specified—a policy constraint that the WDTN Starbucks statement obliquely confirmed when noting that baristas are instructed to "do their best" in cases where customizations defy physical cup capacity.

The labor relations dimension is not incidental to the virality of the Edward photograph. In 2021, Starbucks was in the early stages of what would become an unprecedented labor organizing wave; the first successful union vote at a Starbucks location (the Elmwood Avenue store in Buffalo, New York) occurred in December 2021, with hundreds of subsequent stores voting to unionize over the following two years. The viral resonance of content depicting the labor burden of complicated customer orders was, in this context, part of a broader cultural shift in public consciousness about service-sector working conditions that also manifested in the "Great Resignation" discourse, the "essential workers" recognition during COVID-19, and growing public interest in unionization at Chipotle, Amazon, and other consumer-facing employers.

Statista data on Starbucks customer satisfaction (2021 surveys) indicated that beverage customization options were among the highest-rated sources of consumer satisfaction for the brand, suggesting that the very feature creating labor stress for baristas was simultaneously a core driver of consumer loyalty. This tension—between the consumer value of customization and the labor cost it imposes—is one of the defining structural contradictions of the service-experience model in contemporary retail coffee and fast food.

The Edward photograph, whether staged or real, activated this tension in concentrated form: a single cup of coffee became a visual synecdoche for a structural conflict between consumer empowerment and worker exploitation, legible to vast audiences who had never worked in a Starbucks but understood, or intuited, both sides of the relationship.

## §5 Source Analysis and Verification Chain

Palma's (2021) source methodology for the Edward Starbucks article was constrained by the specific evidentiary requirements of the claim. The primary source was the tweet by @ProjectJosiee, which provided the viral photograph and its framing context. Palma pursued two secondary source inquiries: a direct message to the Twitter user (who had not responded at time of publication) and an email to Starbucks corporate communications (also pending at time of publication).

The partial response that was obtained came through a third channel—Starbucks's statement to WDTN television station, which addressed the general customization policy rather than the specific image. This statement was substantively useful for contextualizing the claim within Starbucks's known operational policy but did not resolve the crucial factual question: was this specific order real?

The absence of a direct response from @ProjectJosiee is particularly significant. The barista's original post was what created the viral moment; their testimony about whether the order was genuine would have been the most epistemically valuable single piece of evidence available. The failure to obtain this response—despite the immense visibility of the viral moment—may reflect the barista's reasonable concern about corporate retaliation, the volume of media inquiries they were likely receiving simultaneously, or simple non-responsiveness. All three are understandable; none resolves the epistemic impasse.

The decision to publish the investigation in a state of pending verification reflects Snopes's practice during the peak of the COVID-19 social media era of documenting trending claims in near-real-time even when verification was incomplete. This practice served the function of providing readers with a qualified, contextualized account of what was known and unknown about a viral claim, even in the absence of the definitive verdict that standard fact-checking methodology produces. The trade-off—appearing to legitimize a claim through coverage even without rating it—was accepted in exchange for the value of contemporaneous documentation.

## §6 Synthesis: Verdict Architecture and the Sociology of Viral Labor Content

The Edward Starbucks case is analytically rich precisely because Palma's (2021) investigation produced no verdict. The no-verdict outcome is itself epistemically informative: it signals that the claim was evaluated against verification standards that it could not meet, not that it was dismissed as obviously false or obviously true. The absence of digital manipulation evidence means the photograph is authentic in the sense of being unaltered; the absence of corporate or barista confirmation means the specific order's genuine or staged character remains unresolved.

What the case reveals most clearly is the gap between the epistemic requirements of standard fact-checking practice and the nature of viral labor-grievance content. The Edward photograph was not primarily a factual claim in the propositional sense; it was a communicative act using imagery to express something felt to be true about the relationship between Starbucks workers and customers, whether or not the specific cup in the image had been ordered by an actual customer named Edward. Fact-checking frameworks optimized for assessing concrete empirical claims ("Did X say Y?" "Is Z statistically true?") face irreducible difficulty with content whose viral power derives from its emotional and sociological legibility rather than its specific factual content.

From a communications perspective, Starbucks's response—defending customization policy as "the heart of the Starbucks Experience" in the same statement that acknowledged baristas should "do their best" with physically impossible orders—is a corporate rhetorical performance that affirms the consumer-facing value while diplomatically eliding the labor-cost dimension. This response pattern is consistent with Starbucks's pre-unionization communications posture documented by Royle (2010) in *Work, Employment and Society* (SAGE): consistently framing the employment relationship in terms of "partner" status and consumer experience while avoiding direct engagement with structural critiques of working conditions.

The cultural legacy of the Edward Starbucks photograph in the history of viral labor content is independent of whether the cup was real: it became a meme template, a reference point in discourse about service-worker exploitation, and one of the most shared examples of what might be called "barista solidarity content" in the early 2020s—a category of social media output that prefigured and contributed to the cultural environment in which the Starbucks unionization wave later emerged.
