# Did Einstein say, 'Insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results'?


## 1. The Claim

"Insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results." This sentence, frequently attributed to Albert Einstein, has become one of the most widely shared quotations in modern popular culture. It appears on motivational posters, in self-help books, across social media platforms, and in the speeches of politicians, CEOs, and wellness influencers. The quote is so ubiquitous that many people assume its attribution to Einstein is settled fact—a profound observation from one of history's greatest scientific minds.

The claim takes several forms. The most common is the direct assertion that Einstein said or wrote these exact words. Some versions add context, claiming the quote came from Einstein's definition of insanity. Others present it as something Einstein supposedly said in response to a question about his methods. A variation appearing in self-help literature expands the quote: "The definition of insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results." Still others claim Einstein used the phrase in his correspondence or in one of his published essays.

The claim has been reinforced by its appearance on quote-collecting websites like AZquotes and BrainyQuote, in printed collections of quotations, and in countless social media posts featuring images of Einstein's famously disheveled face alongside the offending text. The quote's simplicity and apparent profundity make it a natural fit for the Einstein brand—the image of a genius who saw through conventional wisdom to fundamental truths about reality.

The cultural entrenchment of this misattribution is extraordinary. A Google search for the phrase returns millions of results, the vast majority of which attribute it to Einstein without qualification. The quote has been used in contexts ranging from addiction recovery programs to corporate strategy meetings to political commentary. Its perceived authority derives almost entirely from Einstein's name—without that attribution, it is a reasonably clever observation; with it, it becomes a pearl of scientific wisdom.

## 2. What's Actually True

The quote did not originate with Albert Einstein. Snopes, following the trail blazed by quotation researcher Garson O'Toole (the pseudonymous author of Quote Investigator), traced the earliest known appearance of the exact phrase to a surprising source: a 1981 article in the Knoxville News Sentinel.

Staff writer Betsy Pickle's article, published on October 11, 1981, covered an Al-Anon support group meeting. In the article, an unnamed Al-Anon member reportedly shared the following reflection: "Insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results." The context was the recovery community's discussion of how people struggling with addiction or codependency often repeat self-destructive behaviors while hoping for different outcomes. The phrase was not attributed to anyone famous—it appears to have been an anonymous share within a support group context.

Two years after the Knoxville article, the quote appeared in a published novel. Rita Mae Brown, the bestselling author known for works including "Rubyfruit Jungle," included the line in her 1983 mystery novel "Sudden Death." The novel, which follows a professional tennis player turned amateur detective, contains on page 68 the line: "Insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results." Brown did not attribute the line to Einstein or anyone else; it appears as dialogue from one of her characters.

The first documented connection between the quote and Einstein's name appeared in 1990—35 years after Einstein's death. In a November 19, 1990, article in the Austin American-Statesman, staff writer Mike Ward quoted Travis County District Attorney Ronnie Earle as saying, "Einstein once said that insanity is doing the same thing over and over and expecting a different result." This appears to be the earliest record of anyone attributing the quote to Einstein, and it demonstrates the chain of transmission: an anonymous Al-Anon share in 1981, a novelist's dialogue in 1983, a prosecutor's rhetorical flourish in 1990, and eventually the full-blown attribution to Einstein that became standard on the internet.

The official Albert Einstein Facebook page, managed by the brand licensing firm CMG Worldwide, has explicitly stated that the quote is not Einstein's. In a 2014 post, the page wrote in all caps: "THIS IS NOT AN EINSTEIN QUOTE. 'Insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results.'" The post attributed the quote to Rita Mae Brown, though it did not mention the earlier 1981 newspaper article.

Even the Princeton University Press volume "The Ultimate Quotable Einstein," which AZquotes cited as the source for the quote, does not actually attribute it to Einstein. The quote appears on page 474 of that book—in a section labeled "Misattributed to Einstein."

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's fact-check, published November 7, 2025, by senior reporter Jordan Liles, is thorough and well-sourced. The article correctly identifies the rating as "Incorrect Attribution" and provides a detailed chronological account of the quote's actual origins. The article draws on the foundational research of Garson O'Toole's Quote Investigator, Google Books searches, and Newspapers.com archives—all appropriate and authoritative sources for this kind of investigation.

The Snopes article does several things particularly well. It documents the range of platforms where the misattribution has appeared, from TikTok to LinkedIn to X (formerly Twitter), providing a comprehensive picture of the quote's viral spread. It correctly notes the irony that the quote itself is about doing the same thing repeatedly and expecting different results—a perfect description of the internet's tendency to recirculate the same misattribution year after year. The article also highlights the AZquotes error in citing "The Ultimate Quotable Einstein" without noting that the relevant entry is in the misattribution section.

Where Snopes's treatment could be deepened is in examining why this particular misattribution has proved so resilient. The article documents the "what" and "when" of the misattribution but does not fully explore the "why." Einstein is among the most misattributed figures of the 20th century—alongside Mark Twain, Winston Churchill, and Abraham Lincoln—and understanding the psychology of misattribution would add valuable context.

The Snopes article also does not address the broader phenomenon of misattributed quotations as a genre of misinformation. Unlike false claims about factual events, misattributed quotes are often true in their content—they just weren't said by the person credited. This creates a different debunking challenge, because the correction does not involve replacing a falsehood with a truth but rather detaching a truth from an erroneous source. People resist this kind of correction more strongly because it feels pedantic; the quote is correct, so who cares who said it? This resistance is itself worth examining.

The article could also have explored the quote's actual provenance in more depth. The 1981 Al-Anon connection is mentioned but not contextualized within the history of 12-step recovery programs, where the concept of "insanity" as repeating the same behavior has specific therapeutic meaning. Similarly, Rita Mae Brown's use of the quote in a mystery novel is noted but not explored as a literary device.

Finally, the Snopes article focuses heavily on the American context of the quote's spread. Given that Einstein is a global figure and the quote has been shared in numerous languages, some attention to its international circulation would have been valuable.

## 4. Why This Matters

The Einstein insanity quote is one of the most broadly consequential misattributions in modern culture. It affects how people think about both mental health and scientific genius, and its persistence reveals important patterns in how misinformation spreads.

First, the quote's ubiquity has consequences for mental health discourse. By attributing a recovery-community insight to a physicist, the quote strips the concept of insanity from its clinical and therapeutic context and relocates it in the realm of armchair philosophy. The 12-step understanding of "insanity" as the cycle of addiction is specific and practical; Einstein's supposed definition is abstract and universal. The misattribution implicitly legitimizes a pop-psychological concept of mental illness that may not align with clinical reality.

Second, the misattribution distorts public understanding of Einstein. By associating him with folksy, aphoristic wisdom, the quote contributes to a sanitized, poster-friendly image of the great physicist—the kindly old genius dispensing platitudes rather than the complex, politically engaged intellectual who actually existed. Einstein's actual writings on war, socialism, racism, and nuclear weapons are far more interesting and challenging than the sanitized version of him that appears on inspirational posters. The misattributed quote replaces substantive engagement with Einstein's thought with a comfortable illusion of wisdom.

Third, the quote's persistence is a case study in the mechanics of misinformation. It spread through well-understood channels: quote-collecting websites that prioritize traffic over accuracy, social media algorithms that favor shareable content over verified information, and a cultural preference for attributing insightful statements to famous names. The fact that the official Einstein Facebook page has stated the quote is not his has not slowed its spread—a demonstration of how difficult it is to correct deeply entrenched misinformation.

Fourth, the quote reveals something about how authority and credibility function in the internet age. The quote is attributed to Einstein not because anyone has evidence he said it but because his name adds weight to the observation. This is the opposite of how evidence-based reasoning should work: the authority of the source should derive from the evidence, not the other way around.

For the Truth Vault, this entry is part of a broader project of documenting the genealogy of widely misattributed quotations. The same research methods that uncovered the true origin of this quote—searching newspaper archives, examining bibliographic records, tracing claims to their earliest known sources—can be applied to countless other misattributed lines.

Fifth, the quote's persistence reveals something about the sociology of quotation attribution. People attribute quotes to famous figures for a variety of reasons: to add weight to an argument, to borrow reflected authority, to make a personal observation seem universal, or simply because they encountered the attribution in an authoritative-seeming source. The Einstein insanity quote is a particularly clear example because the quote's content—about repeating the same behavior—is self-referentially ironic in its endless recirculation. But the pattern extends to countless other quotations attributed to Einstein, Mark Twain, Winston Churchill, Abraham Lincoln, and Marie Curie, among others.

Sixth, the quote reveals how the economics of online content creation incentivize misattribution. Quote-collecting websites like AZquotes and BrainyQuote generate revenue through traffic and advertising. Attributing a quote to a famous name increases its search visibility and shareability, which in turn generates more traffic and revenue. The accuracy of the attribution is a secondary consideration at best. The AZquotes page that cited "The Ultimate Quotable Einstein" did not need to be deliberately deceptive to cause harm; the structural incentives of the platform were sufficient to produce the error. The editors who compiled the page likely found the quote associated with Einstein in another source and reproduced it without independent verification. This is how the vast majority of quote misattributions propagate: not through malice but through a cascade of unverified reproductions, each of which adds a layer of apparent authority.

## 5. Research Notes

The foundational research on this quote was conducted by Garson O'Toole (pseudonym) of the Quote Investigator website. O'Toole's 2017 article on the quote, updated from his original 2011 investigation, is the definitive treatment. His method involves systematic searching of digitized newspaper archives, Google Books, and periodical databases to establish the earliest known occurrence of a phrase.

Snopes's verification independently corroborates O'Toole's findings. The 1981 Knoxville News Sentinel article is on file at Newspapers.com and was examined directly by both O'Toole and the Snopes researcher. The Rita Mae Brown connection is confirmed through Google Books, where "Sudden Death" (1983) shows the quote in context.

The 1990 Austin American-Statesman article is significant because it is the first known instance of anyone attributing the quote to Einstein. The source was a district attorney's rhetorical flourish, not a scholarly citation. This demonstrates a common pattern in quote misattribution: once the quote is connected to a famous name in a public forum, the association becomes self-reinforcing.

Notable additional context: there is a 1924 newspaper column by Betty Brainerd that mentions Einstein and discusses the idea of doing the same thing repeatedly, but it does not include the word "insanity" or the exact quoted phrase. Some defenders of the Einstein attribution have pointed to this column as evidence of early association, but it is not—it is a red herring.

The official Einstein Facebook page's 2014 correction is available in the Wayback Machine. The page, managed by CMG Worldwide on behalf of the Einstein estate, has been definitive in rejecting the attribution. This is noteworthy because CMG would have a financial interest in maintaining the Einstein brand's association with quotable wisdom—their rejection of the quote therefore carries particular weight.

For those interested in the broader phenomenon of misattributed quotations, O'Toole's Quote Investigator website is the single best resource. His book "Hemingway Didn't Say That" collects many of his investigations and is recommended for general readers.

An instructive comparison can be drawn with another famous misattribution: the quote "The definition of insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results" has also been misattributed to Benjamin Franklin, Mark Twain, and the novelist Rita Mae Brown herself (in reverse). This pattern—a popular quote being attributed to multiple famous figures before settling on the most prominent one—is a common feature of quote evolution. The Einstein version won out because his name carries the greatest cultural authority and because his public image as a deep thinker makes the attribution feel plausible.

The Al-Anon connection deserves additional exploration. The 12-step recovery movement has generated a rich body of aphoristic wisdom—"one day at a time," "let go and let God," "progress not perfection"—that has permeated popular culture. The "insanity" quote fits naturally within this tradition. Its attribution first to an anonymous Al-Anon member, then to Rita Mae Brown, then to Einstein, represents a gradual detachment from its original context and elevation to universal truth. The irony is that the quote lost its specific therapeutic meaning—the cycle of addiction—and gained a vague, universal meaning that is actually less useful.

## 6. Verdict

The claim that Albert Einstein said "Insanity is doing the same thing over and over again and expecting different results" is **incorrectly attributed**. No evidence exists that Einstein ever said or wrote this phrase, and the authoritative "The Ultimate Quotable Einstein" explicitly places it in its "Misattributed to Einstein" section.

The earliest known appearance of the exact quote dates to 1981, when an anonymous Al-Anon member used it during a support group meeting in Knoxville, Tennessee. Novelist Rita Mae Brown included the line in her 1983 book "Sudden Death." The first documented connection to Einstein occurred in 1990, when a Texas district attorney used the quote in a speech.

The quote itself may be a useful observation about human behavior—one that resonates with the recovery community that appears to have generated it—but it has no connection to Albert Einstein. His name was attached later, through a process of misattribution that is well documented and entirely typical of how such errors spread. The quote's popularity is in fact ironic proof of its own content: the internet has been doing the same thing over and over again (attributing the quote to Einstein) while expecting different results (hoping it will eventually be true).
