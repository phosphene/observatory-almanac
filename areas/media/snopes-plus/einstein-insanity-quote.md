# "The definition of insanity is doing the same thing over and over and expecting different results" was said by Einstein


## 1. The Claim

Few aphorisms have achieved wider digital saturation than the one popularly presented as Einstein's pithy definition of insanity: "The definition of insanity is doing the same thing over and over and expecting different results." The formulation appears in thousands of books, on millions of social media posts, in corporate strategy presentations, political speeches, self-help programmes, therapy group materials, and sports coaching manuals. It is delivered as motivational advice, as sardonic commentary on repeated political failure, as a critique of outdated business models, and as a cornerstone of twelve-step recovery philosophy.

The Einstein attribution is central to the quote's rhetorical power. Albert Einstein occupies a unique cultural position as the archetype of pure intellectual capability — the person for whom the word "genius" was long synonymous in popular usage. Framing a statement about rational decision-making and the psychology of persistence under his name gives it an automatic credibility that would be difficult to manufacture any other way. The implication is that if the greatest mind of the twentieth century identified this behavioural pattern as the hallmark of irrationality, readers had better take note.

The claim thus does double work: it provides strategic or psychological advice, and it provides the authority of Einstein's verified intellectual stature as a guarantee of that advice's validity. That double function explains why the attribution has been so stickily maintained even by people who might in other contexts be sceptical of unverified quotations — resisting the Einstein connection feels like resisting the wisdom, because the authority and the content have been fused in cultural circulation.

What is actually being claimed here? The claim is that Albert Einstein — the theoretical physicist, Nobel laureate, and author of special and general relativity — uttered or wrote this specific definition as part of his documented intellectual output, and that the definition appropriately carries his professional and intellectual authority. That is a strong, specific, and falsifiable historical claim. And extensive investigation has found it to be unsupported.


## 2. What's Actually True

**No Einstein source exists.** The comprehensive Albert Einstein Archives at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, which holds Einstein's papers, correspondence, and notebooks, contains no document in which this definition appears. The *Collected Papers of Albert Einstein* project, a major ongoing editorial enterprise that has published critically edited volumes of Einstein's writings in German with English translation since 1987, does not include this aphorism. Scholars and archivists who have been directly asked about the attribution have consistently been unable to identify a source.

Einstein's authentic writings are extensive and well-documented. His scientific papers, popular science articles, political essays, correspondence with other physicists, and personal letters have all been collected and are available for research. He was a prolific and eloquent writer in German and had a documented talent for expressing complex ideas accessibly. His style is recognisable and well-studied. The insanity definition does not appear in any of this material, and its rhetorical structure — a formulaic definitional frame used to make a pop-psychology point — is inconsistent with his documented writing habits.

**The earliest traceable source.** The most thorough documented investigation of this attribution, conducted in stages by the Quote Investigator project, traced the earliest known print appearances of the "do the same thing, expect different results" formulation to Narcotics Anonymous literature from the early 1980s. A version of the phrase appeared in NA literature by approximately 1981, framed not as Einstein's definition but as a recovery-community insight about the self-defeating patterns of continued substance use. The formulation resonated because it precisely captured a failure mode that many in recovery programmes recognised from their own experience.

From NA, the phrase migrated into Alcoholics Anonymous adjacent materials and the broader self-help ecosystem. By the mid-1980s it was appearing in various motivational and self-improvement contexts without specific attribution. The Einstein attachment appears to have developed during the decade-long period in which the quote was already widely circulating — a retroactive glamorisation that made the wisdom seem more universal and less tied to a specific recovery context.

**Alternative attributions.** In addition to Einstein, the aphorism has been attributed over the years to Benjamin Franklin, Mark Twain, and various unnamed political figures. The Franklin and Twain attributions share the same evidentiary problem as the Einstein one: no verified primary source exists, and they appear to be secondary attributions that accumulated after the quote was already in wide circulation. The multiplicity of famous names attached to the same phrase is itself a strong indicator of misattribution — when a quote has several famous hosts in circulation, this typically means none of them is the genuine original source and the phrase has simply been appropriated into multiple celebrity attribution streams.

**What the underlying claim actually asserts.** The statement itself, separately from its authorship, makes a specific claim about human psychology: that continuing a course of action that has not produced the desired result while expecting it to eventually do so is definitionally irrational. This is philosophically questionable on multiple grounds. Probabilistic reasoning, scientific method, and everyday learning all involve repeated action with adjusted expectations — trying the same experiment multiple times under controlled conditions, practising the same skill across many repetitions, persisting in a failed relationship strategy while seeking new insight. The sharpness of the aphorism depends on context suppression: it is only definitively true when the conditions are absolutely unchanged and the outcome genuinely invariant. Real situations are rarely that clean. Even as practical advice, the quote is less universally applicable than its viral success suggests.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The Einstein gravity well.** Einstein's name has become a cultural attractor for quotes about intelligence, curiosity, persistence, and unconventional thinking. The same structural process that makes Napoleon a magnet for military wisdom makes Einstein a magnet for intellectual and scientific wisdom: name recognition, documented brilliance, a romantic biographical narrative, and a historical era that predates easy verification. The more quotes circulate under a famous name, the more plausible any new addition seems — familiarity with Einstein-attributed wisdom makes each new addition easier to accept.

**Pre-existing exposure to the content.** By the time most people first encounter the Einstein attribution, they have often already encountered the underlying idea — in recovery literature, in self-help contexts, in casual conversation. The idea feels familiar, and familiarity triggers credibility. When a recognisable and seemingly useful insight is then attributed to a prestigious source, readers process the attribution as confirmation of something they already intuitively believed, rather than as a new claim requiring independent verification.

**Appropriate context fit.** The quote actually fits Einstein rhetorically. Physics frequently involves running the same experimental conditions over and over, and a scientist arguing for conceptual revision over procedural repetition resonates with the history of paradigm shifts in physics. Einstein himself was associated with challenging established orthodoxy — he resisted quantum mechanics' probabilistic character, as it happens, and famously said "God does not play dice." There is enough biographical texture around the idea of Einstein challenging ineffective thinking that the attribution feels plausible to people with general cultural knowledge of his persona.

**Recovery community circulation patterns.** The NA and AA communities in which the quote genuinely circulated have a strong oral tradition and deliberate anonymity culture, both of which are poorly suited to attribution tracking. Recovery literature traditionally foregrounds the message rather than the messenger. When this material moves out of recovery contexts and into mainstream self-help, the lack of a named source creates an attribution vacuum that gets filled by whatever authoritative name is culturally proximate. For a quote about the failure of repetitive behaviour in the late twentieth century, Einstein was the natural candidate.

**Motivational poster industrial complex.** The quote is compact, typographically convenient, and attaches easily to portrait photographs. Einstein's widely circulated images — the wild hair, the rumpled sweater, the concentrated or playful expression — provide instantly recognisable visual contexts that pair naturally with pithy wisdom. This has created a feedback loop: Einstein-quote images are shared because they are visually recognisable, and their wide sharing trains cultural expectation that this kind of content comes from Einstein, making each subsequent appearance more credible.


## 4. Verdict

**False attribution — not traceable to Einstein.** The aphorism "The definition of insanity is doing the same thing over and over and expecting different results" does not appear in any verified Einstein source. The comprehensive Einstein archives, the Collected Papers project, and direct consultation with Einstein scholars all fail to produce this statement. The earliest documented appearances trace to Narcotics Anonymous literature from the early 1980s — more than twenty-five years after Einstein's death in 1955. The Einstein attribution is a misattribution that developed during the quote's period of wide circulation, not a credible transmission from a primary source.

The underlying idea is independently useful in certain limited contexts and belongs to no single author. The Einstein link is a cultural embellishment, not a historical fact.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Einstein insanity quote sits at the intersection of two important phenomena in contemporary information culture: the misattribution industrial complex and the flattening of complex thinkers into quotable brand mascots.

On misattribution: several researchers have documented that Einstein, Mark Twain, Winston Churchill, Abraham Lincoln, and Yogi Berra (in sports contexts) collectively absorb an extraordinary proportion of unverifiable internet quotations. This concentration is not random. These figures are chosen because their names are recognisable to people with minimal historical education, their verified views are interesting enough to make extensions plausible, their historical eras predate reliable recording, and their documented biographies contain enough breadth to make almost any topical extension seem like a possible lost remark. The misattribution dynamic is a feature of how authority operates in low-attention-cost information environments: attribution to a famous name converts a statement from an anonymous opinion into certified wisdom with a single noun.

On brand mascot flattening: the authentic Einstein was a complex figure whose intellectual range extended beyond physics into pacifism, Zionism, socialism, and philosophy of science. His actual views on psychology, irrationality, and human behaviour were considerably more qualified and contextual than any aphoristic definition allows. His famously stubborn resistance to quantum mechanics' probabilistic foundations — a position the physics community regards as having been ultimately mistaken — is itself an example of a brilliant mind repeating an approach across decades without producing the expected results. The real Einstein, in other words, is a more interesting and more complicated figure than the quote mascot, and the misattribution process actively works against understanding him.

The recovery community context in which the quote likely originated is worth recovering, because it actually adds useful specificity. In that context, "doing the same thing and expecting different results" referred to the specific pattern of continued substance use — a case where the conditions genuinely are unchanged and the expected result is genuinely invariant. The irrationality diagnosis is actually sharper in that specific context than it is as a general motivational principle. Stripping the provenance decontextualises the idea and makes it less useful.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Major fact-checking organisations have not formally adjudicated this specific Einstein attribution, in part because it does not directly attach to a current political claim or policy debate of the kind that drives conventional fact-check production. The attribution has been addressed extensively by quotation-specific research resources, most comprehensively by the Quote Investigator project run by Garson O'Toole, which represents the most detailed publicly available documentation of the attribution's history and the absence of any Einstein source.

For fact-checkers encountering Einstein attribution claims generally, the most efficient verification path involves: (1) the Albert Einstein Archives online portal at the Hebrew University, which provides searching access to the collection catalogue; (2) the Collected Papers of Albert Einstein through the California Institute of Technology's editorial project; (3) direct consultation with archivists at the Einstein Papers Project; and (4) the Quote Investigator database, which has documented dozens of Einstein misattributions in systematic detail.

The challenge for mainstream fact-checking is that quotation misattribution rarely meets the threshold of "harmful misinformation" in policy terms — it does not cause direct electoral harm, spread medical danger, or produce immediate legal consequences. This means it is systematically under-investigated by organisations that prioritise political and health misinformation. The result is that millions of daily social media interactions propagate false historical claims that go un-corrected because the correction infrastructure is not aligned with the scale of the problem.

Media literacy educators have increasingly identified quotation misattribution as a tractable entry point for teaching source evaluation skills. The Einstein insanity quote, specifically, appears in many digital literacy curricula as a worked example precisely because the attribution failure is clean, well-documented, and illustrates the basic principle that prestigious attribution requires positive primary source evidence rather than the mere absence of obvious counter-evidence. The fact that even sophisticated and generally sceptical readers often accept this attribution without checking reflects the depth of the challenge that information literacy programmes are working to address.
