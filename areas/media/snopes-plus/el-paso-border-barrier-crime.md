# El Paso's border barrier reduced crime dramatically


## 1. The Claim

The claim that El Paso's border barrier reduced crime dramatically entered national political discourse most prominently during the 2019 State of the Union address, when President Donald Trump stated that El Paso, Texas had been one of the nation's most dangerous cities before the border fence was constructed, and had become one of its safest cities after the barrier was built. This specific formulation — dangerous before the fence, safe after — was widely amplified through conservative media and became a central exhibit in the argument that physical border barriers directly reduce crime in border communities.

The claim has two connected components: (1) El Paso was once highly dangerous; (2) the border barrier caused a dramatic reduction in crime. The argument structure relies on a before-and-after comparison that implies the fence was the causally relevant variable. Trump's State of the Union version of the claim was explicit: "The border city of El Paso, Texas, used to have extremely high rates of violent crime — one of the highest in the entire country, and considered one of our nation's most dangerous cities. Now, immediately upon its building, with a powerful barrier in place, El Paso is one of the safest cities in our nation."

This specific version of the claim attracted immediate and extensive pushback from El Paso's mayor, its congressional representative, the El Paso Times, and from criminologists and statisticians who worked with the underlying data. Beto O'Rourke, who represented El Paso in Congress at the time, described the claim as false. The El Paso mayor's office issued a formal response disputing the "most dangerous" characterisation. Snopes rated the claim as false. PolitiFact rated it "false." FactCheck.org rated it "false." The Washington Post awarded it Four Pinocchios.

Despite the united front of fact-checkers finding the claim false, it has continued to circulate. It recurs in debates about border security, particularly at moments of elevated immigration enforcement focus, and versions of it continue to be shared as evidence that physical barriers are effective crime-reduction tools. Understanding why the claim is false requires examining the actual crime data, the timeline of fence construction, and the demographics that explain El Paso's historically low crime rates.


## 2. What's Actually True

**El Paso's actual crime history.** El Paso has been among the safest large cities in the United States by available FBI Uniform Crime Report metrics for an extended period that substantially predates the construction or expansion of any border fence. The FBI's Uniform Crime Reports, which provide annual violent crime and property crime statistics for cities meeting minimum population thresholds, show that El Paso ranked among the lowest-crime large cities in the country in the 1990s and 2000s — before the fence expansion that occurred in 2008-2009 under the Secure Fence Act. To characterise El Paso as having had "extremely high rates of violent crime — one of the highest in the entire country" simply does not correspond with the available data. This is not a matter of selecting which statistics to believe; it is a matter of the claim directly contradicting the basic historical crime record.

The El Paso Times, citing FBI data, reported in response to the 2019 State of the Union that El Paso ranked 17th lowest in violent crime among 35 comparable large American cities in 2005 — three years before the expanded fence — and had been ranked among the safest large cities in America for multi-year spans before the fence was built (El Paso Times, 2019). FactCheck.org's analysis of FBI UCR data confirmed that El Paso's violent crime rate was not among the highest nationally in the pre-fence period; in fact, it was consistently below the national average.

**The timeline of fence construction.** The expanded El Paso Border Fence was constructed primarily between 2008 and 2009 under authority of the 2006 Secure Fence Act. Prior to this expansion, a partial fence had existed along parts of the El Paso-Juárez boundary since the mid-1990s, when the Clinton-era Operation Hold the Line began in 1993 with a large-scale uniformed border patrol presence rather than physical infrastructure. A fence constructed in 1996-1997 covered portions of the urban section. The 2008-2009 expansion extended and reinforced this infrastructure significantly.

For the Trump/fence claim to hold, one would expect to see a measurable inflection point in El Paso's crime statistics at or after 2008-2009. Gonzalez-Barrera and Lopez (2013, Pew Research Center) examined crime rates in border communities and found no consistent pattern of crime reduction associated with fence construction. Examining the FBI UCR data directly, El Paso's violent crime rate actually increased modestly in 2009, the year the fence expansion was completed, before continuing a gradual decline trajectory that had been underway for years prior. The "immediate" reduction the State of the Union formulation described is not visible in the data.

**The reversed causality problem.** The most fundamental error in the claim is an assertion of causality for which no evidence is offered. Even if El Paso had experienced a dramatic crime reduction after fence expansion — which it did not, beyond the continuation of a trend that predated the expansion — attributing that change to the fence rather than to other contemporaneous factors would require eliminating alternative explanations through controlled research design. No such research exists. The 2008-2009 period saw dramatic changes across many American cities' crime rates attributable to the financial crisis, shifts in drug market dynamics, policing changes, and demographic shifts. Attributing any observed crime change in El Paso specifically to fence construction rather than these nationwide factors would require showing that El Paso diverged from comparable cities in ways that were specifically predictable from fence construction — which it did not.

**What actually explains El Paso's low crime rates.** Research on El Paso's historically low crime rates has consistently identified factors that have nothing to do with border infrastructure. Akins, Rumbaut, and Stansfield (2009, Justice Quarterly) documented the role of immigrant population composition in reducing crime in high-immigrant cities, finding that communities with high concentrations of recently arrived immigrants — which includes El Paso — consistently show lower crime rates than would be predicted from conventional socioeconomic risk factors alone. This finding, which has been replicated in multiple subsequent studies, is the subject of substantial criminological literature on what is termed the "immigrant paradox" in crime research.

Vélez (2009, Criminology) examined the relationship between immigration and neighbourhood-level crime in urban areas and found that immigrant density was associated with lower violent crime controlling for socioeconomic factors. Ramey (2013, Social Forces) found that increases in the proportion of recently arrived immigrants — who are disproportionately young men who might otherwise elevate crime rates under conventional criminological predictions — were associated with crime rate decreases rather than increases in destination cities. Ousey and Kubrin (2018, Annual Review of Criminology) provided a meta-analytic review of the immigration-crime literature, finding that across 51 studies the relationship between immigration and crime was consistently negative or null — more immigration was associated with less crime or no crime change.

El Paso's low crime rate is, on the best available evidence, partly a function of its large and recently arrived immigrant population, combined with strong family social cohesion patterns, concentrated community investment in traditional social controls, and the specific legal and social dynamics of a binational metropolitan area where cross-border family and economic ties create community stability. None of these factors are produced by border infrastructure; they are independent of it.

**Ciudad Juárez as the relevant comparison.** El Paso's low crime rate is particularly striking in light of the fact that it sits directly across the Rio Grande from Ciudad Juárez, which in the period 2008-2011 was experiencing one of the highest homicide rates of any major city in the world as a result of cartel conflicts over drug trafficking territory. Despite geographic proximity, shared demographic ties, and extensive daily cross-border movement, El Paso's violent crime rate did not significantly track Juárez's catastrophic violence spike. This is powerful evidence that crime in El Paso is not primarily driven by cross-border spillover — which the fence is supposed to prevent — but by local social and demographic factors that the fence does not affect.

Jones (2016, NACLA Report on the Americas) documented the Juárez violence peak and its confinement to the Mexican side despite the porousness of informal cross-border connections. Hristova and Daioglou (2019, City & Society) examined the El Paso-Juárez metropolitan area as a case study in binational urban dynamics, noting the paradox of dramatically different crime rates in a functionally integrated urban system. The fence exists across this specific interface; if fence presence were the relevant variable, Juárez's crime wave would have predicted significant El Paso spillover. The absence of that spillover is not attributable to fence effectiveness — it reflects distinct social dynamics on each side that the fence does not explain.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The epistemic novelty of the causal bridge problem.** The El Paso claim illustrates a particularly important and underappreciated failure mode in political argumentation: the invisible causal bridge. The claim presents two propositions — "there is a fence" and "crime is low" — and allows audiences to construct the causal connection themselves rather than asserting it explicitly. When an audience observes two correlated facts in sequence (fence was built; city is safe), the brain reliably constructs a causal story connecting them, especially when that story is consistent with pre-existing beliefs about what fences do. This is distinct from asserting that A caused B directly; instead, the claim is structured to produce the causal inference automatically without the speaker having to defend the causal mechanism.

Research on causal attribution in political cognition — Sanbonmatsu and Fazio (1990, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin); Taber and Lodge (2006, American Journal of Political Science) — consistently finds that politically motivated audiences apply asymmetric scrutiny to causal claims depending on whether they confirm or disconfirm pre-existing beliefs. An audience disposed to believe that border barriers reduce crime will accept the "fence → crime reduction" causal bridge with minimal evidential requirements; an audience disposed to be sceptical will recognise it as an undefended inference. The epistemic problem here is not simply confirmation bias but the specific mechanism of the invisible causal bridge: the claim was structured to invite the inference rather than defend it, which made it simultaneously easy to communicate and difficult to rebut, because rebutting an implicit claim requires first articulating it explicitly.

This represents a specific vulnerability in civic epistemics that fact-checkers rarely name directly: claims that work by eliciting audience-constructed causal inferences rather than explicitly asserting causal mechanisms. The fact-checker's verdict must not only address the explicit claim ("El Paso is safe" — true; "it used to be dangerous" — false for the period in question) but also the implicit causal claim the explicit sequence was designed to invoke. Most fact-check ratings in the El Paso case focused on the "used to be one of the most dangerous cities" component — which is demonstrably false — rather than on the structural problem of the undefended causal claim. This allowed advocates of the claim to shift their position to "well, El Paso is safe and there is a fence, so the fence must help," preserving the core argumentative point even after the factual error was corrected.

**The appeal of simple physical mechanisms.** Physical barriers have intuitive crime-reduction appeal that security scholars and criminologists have long understood. A wall or fence is a visible, tangible, comprehensible technology. The causal story "fence stops people from crossing; people crossing were bringing crime; fewer crossings means less crime" is simple, complete, and consistent with everyday reasoning about how barriers work. It does not require any specialist knowledge to follow. The counter-argument — that immigration rates are negatively correlated with crime through mechanisms including social cohesion and immigrant risk-aversion — is counterintuitive, requires accepting sociological findings that contradict widespread folk theories of crime causation, and is much harder to communicate in political contexts.

Crisp and Meleady (2012, Science) reviewed the psychology of complex causal attribution and found that simpler causal stories are consistently preferred over more complex true accounts when they are emotionally resonant and consistent with prior beliefs. The fence-crime story is maximally simple; the actual explanation for El Paso's low crime rates is maximally complex by comparison, requiring understanding of immigration research findings that contradict the dominant political narrative.

**Political investment in the specific claim.** By the time of the 2019 State of the Union, significant political capital had been invested in border wall construction. The Trump administration had made border barriers a central policy commitment and had recently provoked a government shutdown over border wall funding. The El Paso example served as a direct empirical exhibit for the effectiveness of the policy at the exact moment when the policy's value was most politically contested. The claim had high political salience and served as direct rhetorical ammunition in a live political fight, ensuring that it received amplification from a large established advocacy infrastructure rather than simply spreading through organic social sharing. Claims that become politically central receive different amplification dynamics than claims that circulate as genuinely independent popular content.

**The confusion of correlation and safe-city status.** A version of the claim that is more defensible than the original is: "El Paso is safe, and it has a border fence." This is fully accurate. But it does not establish causation, and it does not establish that El Paso was dangerous before the fence. The conflation of the true statement (safe city with a fence) with the false statement (fence made it safe from a dangerous prior state) is easy to make in rapid political communication, and the confusion has persisted because the weaker true version provides cover for the stronger false version.


## 4. Verdict

**False on its central factual claim; undefended on its causal claim.** The Trump State of the Union formulation contained a direct factual error: El Paso was not among the nation's most dangerous cities before the border fence expansion. FBI Uniform Crime Report data consistently placed El Paso among the lower-crime large cities in the country for years and decades before the 2008-2009 fence expansion. This is not a matter of interpretation or selective data reading; it is a direct contradiction of the stated historical claim by the available data that fact-checkers across the ideological spectrum have confirmed.

The causal claim — that the fence produced the safety — is structurally undefended rather than demonstrably false, but the absence of any evidence for the causal connection, combined with strong affirmative evidence for alternative explanations (immigrant population composition as a crime-reducing factor, absence of any crime inflection point at the time of fence construction, El Paso's insulation from the Juárez violence spike), makes the causal claim unsupported to a degree that requires a negative finding in the absence of supporting evidence.

El Paso is genuinely one of the safest large cities in the United States. The fence exists. The claim that the fence produced this safety is not supported by the evidence and rests on a false historical claim about El Paso's pre-fence crime levels. The accurate statement is: El Paso has been remarkably safe for a sustained period that predates its expanded border fence, and its safety is consistent with research findings about the crime-reducing effects of high-immigration communities rather than with claims about border barrier effectiveness.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The El Paso claim is a paradigm case in the use of genuine facts about a real place — El Paso genuinely is safe — to support a causal claim that the available evidence cannot sustain. It also foregrounds the larger political and research question about the relationship between immigration, border infrastructure, and crime — a question that the research literature has addressed with considerable consistency.

The broader research record on immigration and crime has been reviewed in multiple systematic analyses. Ousey and Kubrin (2018, Annual Review of Criminology) synthesised 51 studies and found a consistently null or negative (immigration reduces crime) relationship between immigration levels and crime rates. Light and Miller (2018, Criminology) examined the specific question of whether undocumented immigration increases violent crime, using state-level panel data from 1990 to 2014, and found no evidence that increases in undocumented immigration were associated with increases in violent crime rates; in fact, the relationship was negative and statistically significant in specifications with adequate controls. Chalfin (2014, Journal of Urban Economics) found that immigration from Mexico to the United States was associated with reductions in property crime rates in receiving cities.

The political resistance to this research finding is substantial. For advocates of restrictive immigration enforcement, the research finding that immigration is negatively associated with crime undermines a central argument for the policies they support. For this reason, the El Paso example was important: it appeared to offer empirically grounded counter-evidence even though, on examination, it does no such thing. The methodological problems with the El Paso claim — reversed causality, false historical claim, cherry-picked time window — are the same problems that afflict anecdotal evidence in general when deployed as policy-relevant data. Individual city examples, however vivid, cannot establish causal policy effects in the way that the systematic research literature can.

Roberts (2022, Journal of Immigration and Refugee Studies) examined the persistence of false crime-immigration claims in American political communication and found that correction efforts are consistently less effective than initial claim dissemination, a finding consistent with the broader misinformation correction literature reviewed by Lewandowsky et al. (2012, Psychological Science in the Public Interest). The El Paso claim continues to recur in political communication despite extensive correction precisely because the correction mechanism — detailed factual and methodological rebuttal — is structurally disadvantaged relative to the original simple claim in political media environments.

The moral stakes of this research area are high. The claim that immigrants cause crime, explicitly or through the structural implication that barriers to immigration reduce crime by keeping dangerous immigrants out, contributes to documented harms including anti-immigrant scapegoating, hate crimes, harassment of immigrant communities, and distorted policy discussion. Pager and Shepherd (2008, Annual Review of Sociology) documented the real-world consequences of crime-group association in American public discourse. The El Paso claim, by falsely suggesting that border barriers protect against immigrant-caused crime, feeds directly into the broader discourse environment in which this association operates.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The El Paso border barrier crime claim received unusually unified treatment across major American fact-checking organisations. Snopes rated Trump's State of the Union version as false. PolitiFact rated it false. FactCheck.org found the historical characterisation — that El Paso used to be highly dangerous — directly contradicted by FBI data. The Washington Post's Fact Checker awarded four Pinocchios. Reuters Fact Check addressed subsequent recirculations of the claim.

The consensus among independent fact-checkers was unusual in its unanimity. This consensus appears to have had limited influence on the claim's political circulation, which continued. The disconnect between fact-check verdicts and political claim durability in this case is instructive. The Trump administration did not retract the claim after fact-check findings; surrogates continued to cite El Paso as an example of border fence effectiveness; and the claim continued to circulate in conservative media as evidence for wall construction well into subsequent cycles of the border security debate.

This durability despite correction is consistent with research findings on motivated reasoning and misinformation resilience. Chan, Jones, Jamieson, and Albarracin (2017, Psychological Science) found that corrections to politically motivated false beliefs are more effective when they are delivered with alternative explanatory frameworks rather than just with refutations, a finding that suggests fact-checkers who replaced the false El Paso narrative with the accurate explanation for El Paso's safety — immigrant community social cohesion and risk-aversion — might have achieved better correction outcomes than those who simply documented that the historical claim was false.

For media literacy purposes, the El Paso case offers a model example of the before-after claim structure in political advocacy. When evaluating any "Policy X was implemented, and then Good Outcome Y occurred, therefore Policy X caused Y" argument, the appropriate analytical steps are: (1) verify the factual accuracy of both the before and after claims (in El Paso's case, the "before" claim was false); (2) establish the timing relationship between policy and outcome with appropriate precision; (3) examine whether the trend line for Outcome Y predates Policy X implementation; (4) identify alternative explanatory variables that could account for Outcome Y independently of Policy X; and (5) look for comparison cases where Policy X was applied without Outcome Y, or where Outcome Y occurred without Policy X, as tests of the causal claim. Applied to El Paso, each of these steps undermines the claim: the before-state was mischaracterised; the outcome trend predated policy; alternative explanations are documented; and numerous comparable cities show low crime without comparable infrastructure.
