# "Electric Cars Are Worse for the Environment When You Account for Manufacturing"

*Observatory Editorial Team | July 20, 2026*

---


## 1. The Claim

**Core Claim:** "Electric vehicles are actually worse for the environment than gasoline-powered cars once you account for the carbon cost of manufacturing the battery."

**Variants & Context:**

The electric vehicle manufacturing argument circulates in multiple registers across a broad ideological spectrum, from sincere environmental concern to strategic delay tactics deployed by fossil fuel interests. Its most common formulations include: "The carbon debt of EV battery manufacturing takes decades to pay off," "Mining lithium and cobalt for batteries causes more environmental destruction than ordinary oil extraction," "EV batteries will create a catastrophic waste problem," "The electricity powering EVs just comes from coal anyway," and "Building an EV produces more CO₂ than building a gasoline car, erasing the environmental benefit."

These variants share a structural logic: they acknowledge that EVs produce lower tailpipe emissions during operation, but argue that when the full supply chain is considered — particularly battery manufacturing — the net environmental balance tips against EVs. This reasoning appears persuasive to general audiences because it invokes lifecycle thinking, a genuinely sophisticated analytical framework, while applying it selectively to EV production costs and not to the equivalent supply chain of oil extraction, refining, and transportation.

The claim acquired significant mainstream traction in the 2010s as EV sales began to grow. A frequently cited early version emerged from reporting on a 2011 study commissioned by the Low Carbon Vehicle Partnership in the UK, which found that battery production added approximately six tonnes of CO₂ to an EV's manufacturing footprint compared to a comparable gasoline car. Media coverage frequently omitted the study's conclusion that EVs still produced lower lifetime emissions, transforming a nuanced lifecycle analysis into a simple anti-EV talking point. The truncated version spread widely, disconnected from its original context. By the 2020s, the claim had become a reliable fixture of online arguments about climate policy, transportation, and renewable energy adoption.

The argument functions simultaneously as a sincere technical concern about methodological thoroughness and as a rhetorical tool for resisting EV adoption without explicitly defending gasoline vehicles. Its dual nature makes it particularly effective: it can be deployed by those who genuinely want rigorous lifecycle analysis AND by those who want to slow EV adoption without appearing to be climate deniers. This dual-use character has made it one of the most durable claims in the climate debate's second tier — the tier of arguments about the costs and limitations of clean energy solutions, rather than about whether climate change is real.


## 2. What's Actually True

### The Manufacturing Reality

The claim contains a kernel of legitimate science. Producing a lithium-ion battery for an electric vehicle does require significantly more energy and generates significantly more greenhouse gas emissions than manufacturing an equivalent gasoline engine and fuel system. This is not disputed in the scientific literature.

Romare and Dahllöf published the most frequently cited systematic review of battery manufacturing emissions for the IVL Swedish Environmental Research Institute in 2017. Their analysis of available production data found that manufacturing a lithium-ion battery generates between 61 and 106 kilograms of CO₂-equivalent per kilowatt-hour of battery capacity, depending on the energy source used in manufacturing and the specific battery chemistry. For a 60 kWh battery pack — the approximate capacity of a mid-range passenger EV in 2020 — this translates to roughly 3.7 to 6.4 additional tonnes of CO₂ during manufacturing compared to an equivalent gasoline vehicle. This is a real and significant upfront carbon cost. A more recent analysis by Emilsson and Dahllöf, published by IVL in 2019, extended the data range to 61–146 kg CO₂-equivalent per kWh, reflecting the wide variation in manufacturing energy sources across different countries.

Ellingsen and colleagues, writing in *Environmental Research Letters* in 2016, found that larger battery packs carried disproportionate manufacturing emission burdens, and that the battery would represent the single largest component of an EV's lifecycle emissions from manufacturing. Their study underscored the importance of where batteries are manufactured and what energy source powers those factories — findings that have driven subsequent investment in European and North American battery manufacturing to take advantage of cleaner grid electricity than is available in some Asian manufacturing hubs.

### The Lifecycle Analysis

The critical question is not whether EV manufacturing costs more in emissions terms — it does — but whether this manufacturing premium is outweighed by lower operational emissions over the vehicle's lifetime. This is the question that lifecycle analysis (LCA) studies are designed to answer, and the answer from the mainstream research literature is unambiguous.

The landmark comparative study is Hawkins, Singh, Majeau-Bettez, and Strømman's 2013 analysis published in the *Journal of Industrial Ecology*. The authors compared the full lifecycle environmental impacts of conventional and electric vehicles across multiple impact categories using the European average electricity mix of the time, which included a substantial proportion of coal and natural gas generation. Even under this relatively carbon-intensive electricity scenario, the study found that EVs produced 10–24% lower global warming potential over their full lifecycle than comparable gasoline vehicles. Under low-carbon electricity scenarios, the advantage reached 40% or greater. Critically, the study applied equivalent methodological rigor to both vehicle types, including the full fuel supply chain for gasoline vehicles — a symmetric accounting rarely reflected in popular presentations of the manufacturing argument.

Knobloch and colleagues published a comprehensive global analysis in *Nature Sustainability* in 2020, examining whether EVs and heat pumps achieve net emission reductions in 59 world regions. Their finding was decisive: EVs already produced net emission reductions compared to gasoline vehicles in 53 of the 59 regions studied, including countries like China and India where coal still dominates electricity generation. The six exceptions — all characterized by extremely coal-heavy grids — accounted for a small fraction of global vehicle markets. This analysis used detailed country-level electricity data and projected decarbonization trajectories, finding that the EV advantage would grow substantially as power sectors evolved.

The Union of Concerned Scientists published multiple iterations of their "Cleaner Cars from Cradle to Grave" analysis, most recently updated in 2020. They consistently found that EVs charged on the average US electricity grid produce lifetime emissions equivalent to a gasoline car achieving 88–100 miles per gallon — roughly two to three times better than the average new gasoline vehicle sold in the United States. Even in US regions with the dirtiest electricity grids at publication date, EVs attained lifetime emission equivalence with a 35–40 mpg gasoline vehicle, better than most vehicles then on American roads.

### The Carbon Payback Period

One of the most persistent misconceptions concerns the length of the "carbon payback period" — the time required for an EV's operational emission savings to offset its manufacturing premium. The claim that this period spans "twenty years" or "decades" is not supported by the mainstream research literature.

A systematic review of EV lifecycle analyses conducted by various researchers consistently places the payback period at one to four years of average driving under European or North American conditions. Romare and Dahllöf (2017, IVL) noted that manufacturing studies showing large upfront emission burdens must be contextualized within operational performance — a vehicle driven 15,000 km per year on the European average electricity mix would repay even a worst-case manufacturing premium within approximately three years. Under low-carbon electricity conditions — Scandinavian hydroelectric, French nuclear, or US Pacific Northwest hydro — payback periods measured in months rather than years are plausible. Even under the most conservative assumptions in the literature — maximum manufacturing emissions, coal-dominated regional grids, and minimally efficient driving patterns — payback periods rarely exceeded six years for standard passenger vehicles. A vehicle operated for a normal fifteen-year lifespan easily generates net savings of ten or more tonnes of CO₂ equivalent.

### The Selective Accounting Problem

The manufacturing argument as commonly deployed engages in a fundamental accounting asymmetry: it applies rigorous full-supply-chain thinking to EV manufacturing while treating gasoline vehicle operation as though the fuel arrives costlessly and cleanly from nowhere.

Oil extraction, refining, and distribution carry substantial embedded carbon and environmental costs. The US Department of Energy's GREET (Greenhouse Gas, Regulated Emissions, and Energy Use in Technologies) model — the most comprehensive lifecycle assessment tool available for US transportation, developed by Argonne National Laboratory — estimates that well-to-wheel emissions from extracting oil, refining it into gasoline, and delivering it add roughly 25–30% to what comes out of a gasoline car's tailpipe. The carbon embedded in oil infrastructure, the methane emitted during drilling and refining, and the energy consumed across thousands of miles of pipeline and tanker transport are every bit as real as battery manufacturing emissions, but they are structurally invisible in the typical framing of the manufacturing argument. A symmetric accounting — full supply chain for both sides — consistently widens the EV advantage over gasoline vehicles beyond what vehicle-only comparisons suggest.

Beyond operational supply chains, oil refinery infrastructure represents an enormous amount of embedded industrial carbon cost that is rarely attributed to individual gasoline vehicles but that has no equivalent for EVs charged on existing electrical infrastructure.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The electric vehicle manufacturing argument has proven exceptionally durable despite substantial scientific evidence against it. Understanding why requires examining several intersecting epistemic mechanisms that enable factually incorrect claims to persist in technically literate populations.

**The temporal framing asymmetry.** Manufacturing emissions are concentrated, immediate, and easily visualized: a factory burning energy to smelt aluminum and process lithium creates emissions that are happening *now*, before the car is ever driven. Operational emission savings are diffuse, cumulative, and distributed across years or decades of future driving. Human cognition is systematically poorly calibrated for comparing concentrated immediate costs against distributed future benefits — a pattern well established in behavioral economics literature. Slovic and colleagues, in research on risk perception published in *Risk Analysis* in 2004, documented this asymmetry across both lay and technically sophisticated populations. The manufacturing argument exploits this temporal mismatch by making upfront costs vivid and concrete while rendering lifetime savings abstract and hypothetical. The asymmetry is not a product of intellectual failing — it is a feature of normal human temporal discounting that can be exploited regardless of educational background.

**The asymmetric application of rigorous skepticism.** The manufacturing argument deploys lifecycle thinking selectively and strategically. When EV advocates cite lower operational emissions, critics invoke the manufacturing burden. But when critics make this objection, they almost never apply equal supply-chain rigor to their implicit baseline case — the gasoline-powered vehicle. What are the full-supply-chain emissions of petrol, including extraction site energy, methane venting, pipeline losses, shipping, and refinery operations? What are the lifecycle environmental impacts of a catalytic converter, which requires platinum-group metals mined in operations with their own documented environmental impacts? These questions are structurally identical to questions about battery mining, but they are essentially never raised by the same critics who invoke manufacturing complexity to challenge EVs. This selective rigor — demanding comprehensive accounting for the challenger technology while exempting the incumbent — is an epistemological pattern, not merely an empirical oversight, and recognizing it as such fundamentally changes how the argument should be evaluated.

**The "responsible environmentalism" capture.** The manufacturing argument's most sophisticated form positions itself not against environmentalism but as a corrective to naive environmentalism. By using the language of lifecycle analysis, supply chain accounting, and mining impact, critics of EVs can occupy the rhetorical position of the more rigorous, more honest environmentalist. This positioning is epistemologically powerful because it disarms rebuttals on the grounds of environmental concern — if you support EVs, you are portrayed as an uncritical booster ignoring genuine impacts; if you question EVs, you are portrayed as a thoughtful analyst demanding higher standards. Internal documents from organizations including the American Petroleum Institute, obtained through litigation and investigative journalism, have revealed coordinated messaging strategies that use exactly this framing: emphasizing the environmental limitations of clean energy alternatives as a tool for slowing adoption without explicitly defending fossil fuels. The claim is operationally a fossil fuel industry talking point dressed in the language of environmental rigor — but that dressing makes it resistant to correction in a way that a straightforward defense of gasoline could not be.

**The counterfactual paralysis trap.** A structural feature of the manufacturing argument that is rarely remarked upon: it is almost never followed by a specific, actionable alternative recommendation. Critics who argue that EVs are "actually worse for the environment" do not typically propose a specific alternative technology that passes their implied lifecycle assessment threshold, nor do they specify what manufacturing emission intensity would be acceptable, nor at what grid carbon intensity EVs would qualify as beneficial. When pressed, the goalposts shift: the battery is too large, the mining is too destructive, the electricity grid is too dirty, the recycling infrastructure is inadequate. Each genuine improvement in EV technology or battery manufacturing — and improvements have been substantial, with manufacturing emission intensity per kWh declining by roughly 60% between 2010 and 2023 — reveals a new inadequacy. This pattern reveals that the manufacturing argument functions not as a sincere metric for EV acceptability but as a structural barrier to approval — a deployment of the classic "perfect solution fallacy" in which any remaining imperfection of a demonstrably better alternative justifies continued use of a far-worse incumbent.


## 4. Verdict

**Verdict: FALSE as a general claim. NARROW EDGE CASES only in extreme electricity grid and manufacturing scenarios.**

The statement that electric vehicles are "worse for the environment when you account for manufacturing" is false as a general, globally applicable assertion. Lifecycle analyses conducted across multiple independent research institutions, using varied methodological approaches, consistently find that EVs produce substantially lower total greenhouse gas emissions than equivalent gasoline vehicles when the full vehicle lifetime is considered — including battery manufacturing, materials extraction, operation, and end of life. This finding holds across the vast majority of electricity grids globally, including relatively carbon-intensive ones. It is the finding of Hawkins et al. (2013, *Journal of Industrial Ecology*), Knobloch et al. (2020, *Nature Sustainability*), the Union of Concerned Scientists "Cleaner Cars" series, and the IVL analyses that the manufacturing-burden argument itself relies upon — all of which show a manufacturing premium that is real but outweighed by lifetime operational benefits.

The claim is technically contestable only under narrow, specific conditions: regions where electricity generation is overwhelmingly coal-powered, where battery manufacturing occurs in high-emission factory contexts, and where vehicles are driven significantly fewer miles than average. These conditions apply to very few vehicle markets globally, are declining as both electricity grids and manufacturing processes decarbonize, and do not support a blanket conclusion that EVs are worse for the environment.

**EARC Mode: C (Contested)** — This verdict requires engagement with lifecycle analysis methodology and regional electricity data. The manufacturing concern is scientifically legitimate and deserves serious treatment; the error is in its generalization into a blanket claim about global EV environmental performance.

**Gap Category: Contested-Consensus.** Strong scientific consensus supports the net lifetime environmental advantage of EVs. The public debate systematically misrepresents this consensus by elevating manufacturing concerns — real, discussed, and addressed within the consensus literature — into a blanket indictment of EV environmental credentials.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The EV manufacturing debate sits within a broader, well-documented pattern of how incumbent fossil fuel industries respond to disruptive environmental alternatives — not with direct climate denial, which has become increasingly untenable, but with a second-generation argument strategy that accepts the existence of environmental problems while disputing the adequacy of proposed solutions.

**Grid decarbonization compounds and compounds the EV advantage.** EVs charged on a given electricity grid today will be charged on an increasingly clean grid over their lifetime. A vehicle manufactured in 2026 and operated for fifteen years will spend most of those years on a substantially cleaner grid than the one that exists at purchase. Analyses by Cazzola and colleagues at the International Energy Agency (2021, IEA Global EV Outlook) found that accounting for projected grid decarbonization trajectories over ten-year vehicle lifetimes further widens the lifetime emission advantage of EVs over gasoline vehicles in most global markets, and that the "break-even" carbon intensity — the grid emissions factor above which an EV stops being better than gasoline — is higher than most current real-world grids already.

**Battery recycling and second-life applications.** The claim that EV batteries will create an environmental waste catastrophe does not reflect the trajectory of the battery recycling industry. Commercial-scale recycling processes — including hydrometallurgical processes that recover 90–95% of lithium, cobalt, nickel, and manganese — have been developed and deployed by companies including Redwood Materials, Li-Cycle, and Umicore. The European Union's Battery Regulation, passed in 2023, established legally binding recycled content minimums that will normalize battery recycling across the world's second-largest EV market. The "second life battery" market — repurposing retired EV batteries for stationary grid energy storage before eventual recycling — extends the effective material lifetime and further reduces lifecycle impact per unit of service delivered. The claim that EV batteries represent an unmanageable waste stream treats a maturing recycling industry as though its 2015 state were its permanent state.

**The fossil fuel supply chain comparison.** Every critique applied to EV battery supply chains — mining impacts, processing energy, transportation emissions, labor conditions — applies with equal or greater force to the oil supply chain that the EV is replacing. Agusdinata and colleagues, writing in *Environmental Research Letters* in 2018, documented the water and ecosystem impacts of lithium brine extraction in the Atacama. These are real and serious. But the Deepwater Horizon blowout released 4.9 million barrels of oil into the Gulf of Mexico. The Alberta oil sands represent the world's third-largest oil reserve, extracted through surface mining and in-situ processes that have disturbed hundreds of thousands of hectares of boreal forest. Global oil spills, routine methane venting and flaring at drilling sites, and the carbon locked into proven fossil fuel reserves — which cannot be burned without catastrophic climate outcomes — constitute an environmental cost of the incumbent system that dwarfs the supply chain challenges of battery technology.

**The Norway and Iceland natural experiments.** Norway, where EVs accounted for over 80% of new vehicle sales by 2023 and where most electricity is generated from hydroelectric power, provides the strongest real-world evidence for the long-term potential of EVs charged on clean grids. Lifetime emission analyses for Norwegian EVs show reductions exceeding 85% compared to European average gasoline vehicles — not a projection but a measured outcome from a large, sustained deployment in a real economy. Iceland presents a near-zero case. These are not outliers to be explained away; they are proof-of-concept demonstrations that the combination of EV adoption and grid decarbonization eliminates the vast majority of personal vehicle emissions.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Multiple major fact-checking organizations have examined versions of the EV manufacturing claim, generally reaching conclusions that characterize it as false or misleading when stated as a blanket assertion.

AP Fact Check has addressed specific viral claims about EV lifetime emissions on multiple occasions, consistently noting that lifecycle analyses show EVs outperforming gasoline vehicles in total emissions even when manufacturing is fully included, and that the manufacturing finding is routinely stripped of the lifecycle context that gives it meaning. The specific figure of a "twenty-year carbon payback period" — a commonly circulated formulation — has been examined and found to lack basis in the lifecycle analysis literature, which consistently places payback periods at one to four years for typical driving conditions.

Reuters Fact Check has addressed versions of the claim asserting that EVs are categorically worse for the environment, finding them to be false when evaluated against the lifecycle analysis literature. Their analyses have noted, in particular, that the claim frequently cites manufacturing studies without citing those same studies' conclusions about net lifetime performance.

Carbon Brief, the UK-based climate science journalism and fact-checking publication, has published extensive analyses of EV lifecycle emissions concluding that the manufacturing argument significantly understates EV lifetime benefits and often relies on outdated battery manufacturing energy intensity data, which has declined substantially — by roughly 60% — since the early 2010s as manufacturing processes have improved and energy sources in manufacturing regions have shifted toward lower-carbon electricity.

Snopes has addressed adjacent EV environmental claims, examining specific viral assertions about the carbon emissions of EVs relative to gasoline vehicles. The general pattern of Snopes' findings on EV environmental claims aligns with the broader fact-checking consensus: claims that EVs are categorically "worse for the environment" than gasoline vehicles, when evaluated against the lifecycle analysis literature, are rated false or misleading.

The consistency of findings across organizationally and politically diverse fact-checking bodies reflects the strength of the underlying scientific consensus. The manufacturing argument's continued circulation despite this consensus illustrates a durable pattern in applied misinformation: claims that require technical engagement to refute, that weaponize the language of rigor, and that allow advocates to pose as more responsible analysts have exceptional staying power, and that staying power can be mistaken for evidentiary legitimacy by audiences who encounter them without the relevant scientific context.
