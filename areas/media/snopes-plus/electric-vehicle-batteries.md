# "Electric Vehicle Batteries Are Worse for the Environment Than Gasoline Cars"


## 1. The Claim

A claim circulating widely in social media commentary, opinion journalism, and political discourse holds that electric vehicles (EVs), when properly accounting for battery manufacturing impacts, are not environmentally superior to—and may in fact be worse for the environment than—conventional gasoline-powered automobiles. The argument takes several related forms, often appearing in combination.

The strongest version asserts that the carbon dioxide and other greenhouse gas emissions generated during the mining of lithium, cobalt, nickel, and manganese and the manufacturing of high-capacity battery packs exceed the lifetime emissions savings from operating an EV versus a gasoline car, making EVs a net negative from a climate perspective. A related version limits the comparison to battery manufacturing alone, suggesting that the "carbon debt" incurred in building a battery is so large that an EV never repays it through cleaner operation.

A further variant focuses on specific materials: cobalt mining in the Democratic Republic of Congo (often involving criticized labor conditions), lithium extraction in the Atacama Desert (involving freshwater use in arid ecosystems), and nickel mining in Russia and Indonesia (with associated sulfur dioxide emissions). These concrete, geographically specific harms are presented as making the full environmental accounting of EV batteries more damaging than the distributed, invisible harm of gasoline combustion tailpipe emissions.

A grid-dependent version argues that EVs powered by electricity from coal-heavy grids emit more greenhouse gases than efficient gasoline vehicles, making them environmentally harmful in large portions of the developing world and in regions with old coal capacity. This version is sometimes extended to argue that EVs merely "move the smokestack" from the vehicle tailpipe to the power plant.

Political valence is significant in how this claim circulates. It is most commonly deployed in arguments against government subsidies for EVs, against fuel economy standards, against phase-out timelines for internal combustion engine vehicles, and against the generality of "clean technology" branding in climate policy discussions. The claim is occasionally deployed by legacy automotive interests and fossil fuel industry-aligned communications, though it also circulates independently and is sometimes raised by environmentalists concerned about supply chain justice rather than overall climate impact.

What distinguishes this claim from simple misinformation is that several of its component observations have genuine empirical basis. Battery manufacturing does generate significant emissions. Cobalt mining does involve documented human rights concerns. Electricity from coal-heavy grids is dirtier than electricity from renewable sources. The question is not whether any of these observations are false but whether they aggregate to the conclusion that EVs are worse for the environment than gasoline cars on a life-cycle basis—and on that question, the evidence is clear and consistent.


## 2. What's Actually True

The preponderance of life-cycle analysis (LCA) literature—the field that attempts to account for total environmental impacts from raw material extraction through vehicle manufacture, operation, and end-of-life disposal—consistently finds that battery electric vehicles produce substantially lower greenhouse gas emissions than comparable gasoline vehicles over their full operational lifetimes, even under conservative assumptions about battery manufacturing emissions and electricity grid carbon intensity.

The most comprehensive recent synthesis is provided by Emilsson and Dahllöf (2019, *Energies*), which reviewed and meta-analyzed over 100 LCA publications comparing EVs to conventional vehicles. The review found that across the range of electricity grid carbon intensities and battery manufacturing scenarios surveyed in the literature, EVs produced lifecycle greenhouse gas emissions between 17% and 79% lower than comparable internal combustion engine vehicles, with the wide range reflecting variation in grid carbon intensity, vehicle class, usage patterns, and battery size assumptions. The midpoint of the distribution consistently favored EVs by a margin that grows as electricity grids decarbonize.

A landmark analysis specifically addressing the manufacturing phase was conducted by Peters et al. (2017, *Nature Energy*). The study found that battery manufacturing for a typical medium-sized EV generates approximately 70–75 kg CO₂ equivalent per kWh of battery capacity under current global average manufacturing energy mix assumptions. A 60 kWh battery—representative of a mid-range EV—therefore incurs a manufacturing carbon debt of roughly 4–5 tonnes CO₂ equivalent relative to manufacturing a comparable gasoline vehicle's drivetrain. Against the operational emissions savings of an EV on a typical European electricity grid (approximately 1–1.5 tonnes CO₂ equivalent per year versus a comparable gasoline car), this manufacturing debt is repaid in three to five years of operation—well within the vehicle's expected 10–15-year service life.

Volkswagen, which had significant institutional reasons to accurately account for this analysis given the reputational and regulatory stakes of its electrification strategy, conducted an independent LCA of its ID.3 electric hatchback compared to its Golf gasoline equivalent (Volkswagen Group, 2021). The study found that even with production of the ID.3 battery in Poland using electricity from a grid with above-average carbon intensity, and even accounting for the full manufacturing supply chain, the ID.3 had lower lifecycle CO₂ emissions in every European country studied. By year five of operation, the ID.3 had lower cumulative emissions than the Golf in every scenario.

The grid dependency argument has more purchase in genuinely coal-heavy electricity systems. Hawkins et al. (2013, *Journal of Industrial Ecology*) modeled EV performance across electricity production scenarios and found that in grids powered primarily by coal (intensities above approximately 600–700 g CO₂ equivalent per kWh), the lifetime emissions advantages of EVs over gasoline vehicles substantially narrow and can in some scenarios approach parity. This is a real finding. However, it applies to a shrinking portion of electricity production globally: the International Energy Agency (2023, *Electricity Market Report*) documented that coal's share of global electricity generation has been declining and that new renewable capacity additions now substantially exceed new fossil fuel capacity across all major economies. The average carbon intensity of global electricity grids is falling, systematically improving the environmental case for electrification over time.

The United States Environmental Protection Agency's own analysis (EPA, 2023, *Greenhouse Gas Emissions from a Typical Passenger Vehicle*) finds that even accounting for electricity generation emissions, the average American EV produces roughly 3,267 lbs of CO₂ equivalent per year versus 11,435 lbs for the average gasoline car—a factor-of-3.5 difference that persists across grid regions including those with significant coal capacity.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemic novelty in how this claim persists and spreads lies in what might be called the *manufacturing asymmetry of environmental salience*. The environmental costs of battery production are temporally concentrated, spatially visible, and narratively specific—they happen in named mines in named countries, they have identifiable physical footprints, and they occur before the vehicle ever moves. The environmental costs of gasoline combustion are temporally distributed across years of operation, spatially diffuse (distributed across all tailpipes in all locations where the vehicle is driven), and narratively invisible (internal combustion is so normalized as to be background noise). This asymmetry means that the manufacturing phase of EV environmental impact is systematically more cognitively available and more visually documentable than the operational-phase costs of the status quo alternative, creating a systematic distortion in comparative perception.

This is a specific instance of a broader cognitive pattern identified in environmental decision-making research. Sunstein and Reisch (2014, *European Journal of Risk Regulation*) documented how novel risks—those introduced by a new technology—are systematically overweighted relative to baseline risks, which are treated as the natural order of things. The environmental cost of a lithium mine is novel and attributable to EV technology; the environmental cost of a Saudi Arabian oil field or a Texas refinery is baseline and not perceived as attributable to gasoline vehicles in the same psychologically immediate way. The comparison is structurally tilted toward finding the new technology problematic.

Social media and partisan media ecosystems amplify this effect through selective evidence presentation. Images of Atacama salt flat lithium operations or DRC cobalt mines generate high engagement because they are visually striking, geographically exotic, and consistent with a "clean cars aren't really clean" narrative that has appeal across different political communities—environmentalists concerned about supply chain justice, conservatives skeptical of climate technology policy, and anyone who distrusts corporate "green" branding. This convergent interest in the narrative from different political directions gives it unusual cross-partisan reach.

The claim also feeds into what Moser and Dilling (2007, *Creating a Climate for Change*) identified as "solution aversion" in climate communication: when proposed solutions threaten existing behavioral or economic patterns, people selectively attend to evidence that the proposed solutions are ineffective or counterproductive. For people oriented toward preserving the status quo of personal vehicle transportation powered by fossil fuels—or for political actors with economic interests in the fossil fuel or legacy automotive sector—the "EVs are actually worse" claim provides valued permission to discount or oppose electrification policy.

An important complication is that the claim was more empirically grounded in earlier years of EV technology. Circa 2010, when battery production was more energy-intensive per kWh, manufacturing emissions were higher, and the vehicles being manufactured were compared against a mature gasoline technology, the lifecycle comparison was closer than it is today. Many of the most pessimistic LCA studies circulating on social media are outdated, reflecting battery manufacturing conditions from 10–15 years ago. The technological and energy transition trajectory has been systematically positive for EVs and negative for the "actually worse" claim's empirical basis.


## 4. Verdict

**MOSTLY REFUTED** — The claim that EV batteries make electric vehicles worse for the environment than gasoline cars fails to survive rigorous life-cycle accounting. Manufacturing a large battery pack does generate substantially more emissions than manufacturing a gasoline drivetrain, but this manufacturing carbon debt is consistently repaid within three to five years of vehicle operation in most electricity grid contexts, and often sooner. EVs operated over a typical vehicle lifetime produce total greenhouse gas emissions between 30% and 70% lower than comparable gasoline vehicles in most current grid conditions, with the improvement growing as electricity grids decarbonize. The claim has a partial empirical basis in coal-heavy grid scenarios, where lifecycle advantages narrow significantly, and in the genuine environmental costs of specific battery material supply chains. These costs are real and warrant attention. But they do not aggregate to the conclusion that EVs are worse for the environment than gasoline cars—they aggregate to the conclusion that the electrification transition has real costs that should be managed well, while being clearly preferable in climate terms to the status quo.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Battery chemistry and manufacturing have been among the fastest-improving areas of industrial technology over the past decade. The cost per kWh of lithium-ion battery storage has fallen approximately 89% since 2010, from over $1,200/kWh to approximately $130/kWh in 2023, according to BloombergNEF (2023, *EV Battery Prices Report*). This cost decline has been accompanied by improvements in energy density (more storage with less material), manufacturing energy efficiency, and supply chain decarbonization as battery giga-factories increasingly run on renewable electricity.

Cobalt content in batteries has been deliberately reduced by major manufacturers in response to supply chain concerns. The shift from NMC 111 chemistry (one-third cobalt by cathode metal content) toward NMC 811 (approximately 10% cobalt) and toward lithium iron phosphate (LFP) chemistry (no cobalt) has meaningfully reduced dependency on DRC cobalt extraction. Tesla's standard-range vehicles shifted entirely to LFP chemistry in 2021. CATL, the world's largest battery manufacturer, has promoted LFP extensively in the Chinese market. The cobalt supply chain concern, while legitimate and ongoing, is describing a rapidly diminishing feature of the battery technology landscape rather than a fixed constraint.

Lithium extraction methods are themselves diversifying. Conventional evaporation-pond brine processing in the Atacama (Chile and Argentina) is associated with freshwater use concerns in water-scarce ecosystems. Direct lithium extraction (DLE) technologies, which can process brine more efficiently and with substantially lower water consumption, are reaching commercial deployment in multiple locations. Hard-rock spodumene mining in Australia involves different environmental footprints. Geothermal brine extraction in the Salton Sea region of California has been piloted as a combined energy-and-lithium-production system with significantly lower land and water impacts. The extractive pathway for battery minerals is not fixed at its current most-criticized state.

End-of-life battery recycling is the third major area where current state differs from the near-future trajectory being locked in. Battery recycling currently recovers cobalt, nickel, lithium, and manganese at varying efficiencies depending on chemistry and process. The European Union's Battery Regulation (2023) mandates that batteries sold in the EU market meet minimum recycled content thresholds for cobalt (12% by 2031, 20% by 2036), lithium (6% by 2031, 12% by 2036), and nickel (6% by 2031, 12% by 2036), creating economic incentives that will drive investment in recycling infrastructure. Second-life battery applications—using batteries that have degraded below vehicle performance thresholds (typically around 80% of original capacity) for stationary energy storage—extend useful service life before recycling.

The social justice dimensions of battery material supply chains are real and distinct from the climate accounting question. Child labor in artisanal cobalt mining in the DRC, community displacement in lithium extraction areas, and environmental contamination around nickel smelters are genuine harms that deserve sustained attention in corporate supply chain governance, trade policy, and international development frameworks. These harms are not minimized by acknowledging that EVs are better for the climate than gasoline vehicles—they represent a separate set of obligations that the electrification transition needs to address if it is to be genuinely just rather than simply shifting environmental and social costs from wealthy consumers to low-income mining communities in other countries.

Comparative framing matters in both directions. The argument that EV supply chains involve harms does not become less true when we also observe that fossil fuel supply chains involve comparable or greater harms—oil extraction in the Niger Delta, coal mining health impacts, natural gas infrastructure emissions—that are routinely treated as acceptable costs in the existing energy system. A rigorous comparative analysis of supply chain social impacts would need to account for both, and available evidence does not suggest gasoline supply chains are socially benign compared to battery mineral supply chains.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addresses versions of this claim as **Mostly False**, acknowledging that battery manufacturing does generate significant environmental costs while finding that the claim as typically stated—that EVs are *worse* for the environment—overstates those costs and misrepresents the lifecycle accounting. Snopes specifically notes that the claim often fails to account for operational-phase emissions comparisons and frequently relies on outdated figures from earlier generations of battery technology.

Climate Feedback reviewers have evaluated multiple iterations of this claim and consistently noted that the scientific literature supports a clear lifecycle advantage for EVs over gasoline vehicles. Reviewers have flagged that many widely-shared articles making "EVs are worse" arguments rely on cherry-picked scenarios, use outdated manufacturing emissions figures, or fail to account for the full operational period of the comparison.

The Union of Concerned Scientists has produced publicly accessible, independently reviewed lifecycle analyses specific to the United States, broken down by electricity grid region, finding that EVs have lower lifetime emissions than gasoline vehicles in every region of the continental US including those with the highest coal dependence. Carbon Brief, a specialist climate science media outlet, has published and updated a landmark analysis of EV emissions by country under current and projected grid carbon intensities, finding that EVs are lower-emission than gasoline equivalents in 95% of the world's population geography under current grid conditions.

The political economy of this claim bears noting in any complete fact-checking treatment. The Americans for Prosperity Foundation, which is substantially funded by fossil fuel interests, and the Institute for Energy Research have been among the more active institutional promoters of EV battery environmental skepticism. Neither organization's involvement proves their arguments are wrong, but the funding environment appropriately contextualizes their selective emphasis on manufacturing-phase emissions over lifecycle comparisons. Independent academic LCA research—which has no analogous funding interest in the direction of its conclusions—has produced a clear and consistent body of evidence supporting the lifecycle environmental advantage of EVs.
