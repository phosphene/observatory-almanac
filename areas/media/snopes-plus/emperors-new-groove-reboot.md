# Disney is rebooting The Emperor's New Groove as a live-action film


## 1. The Claim

A claim that recurs in entertainment fan communities, social media discussions about Disney's live-action remake strategy, and clickbait entertainment aggregation holds that Disney is developing or has confirmed a live-action remake of *The Emperor's New Groove*, the 2000 animated feature film directed by Mark Dindal. The claim typically asserts that the studio has a production in development, that casting speculation is warranted, and sometimes that specific named actors are being sought for the roles of Kuzco, Yzma, Pacha, or Kronk. In more elaborate versions, the claim includes an asserted release date, a production studio credit, or a statement that original voice cast members David Spade, Eartha Kitt (deceased), John Goodman, and Patrick Warburton have been approached about involvement.

The claim appears against a specific and genuine background: Disney has, across the 2010s and 2020s, remade a substantial number of its animated features as live-action films. *Alice in Wonderland* (2010), *Maleficent* (2014), *Cinderella* (2015), *The Jungle Book* (2016), *Beauty and the Beast* (2017), *Dumbo* (2019), *Aladdin* (2019), *The Lion King* (2019), *Mulan* (2020), *Cruella* (2021), *Pinocchio* (2022), *Peter Pan & Wendy* (2023), *The Little Mermaid* (2023), *Snow White* (2024), and *Lilo & Stitch* (2024) have all been produced and released, with multiple additional projects — including *Bambi*, *Aristocats*, *Moana*, *Hercules*, *Tangled*, and *The Hunchback of Notre Dame* — in various stages of development or announced. The live-action remake pipeline is real, large, and ongoing.

Against that real background, the assertion that *The Emperor's New Groove* is next (or is somewhere in the pipeline) has surface plausibility: Disney is clearly doing this with its catalogue, and the question of which films are next is commercially and culturally interesting. The claim exploits that plausibility to assert as confirmed what is at most speculative.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Emperor's New Groove: what it is and what makes it unusual.** *The Emperor's New Groove* was released by Walt Disney Pictures on December 15, 2000. It was directed by Mark Dindal and produced by Randy Fullmer, with a story by Chris Williams and Mark Dindal and a screenplay by David Reynolds. The film stars David Spade as the voice of Emperor Kuzco, a self-absorbed young Incan emperor who is transformed into a llama by the scheming royal advisor Yzma (voiced by Eartha Kitt) and her dim-witted henchman Kronk (Patrick Warburton), and must rely on the honest peasant Pacha (John Goodman) to help him find his way back to human form and reclaim his throne.

The film was produced in difficult circumstances. Its predecessor at the Disney animation studio during what critics now call the "Disney Renaissance" had included artistically ambitious and commercially successful films; *The Emperor's New Groove* emerged from a substantially revised version of a much more ambitious project called *Kingdom of the Sun*, which was to be a musical inspired by Inca mythology with music by Sting. The transition from that project — documented in the 2002 documentary *The Sweatbox*, made by Trudie Styler (Sting's wife) and largely not publicly released until bootleg circulation on the internet, showing the creative conflict between director Roger Allers and Disney executives — to the comedic, irreverent film that was ultimately produced is one of the more documented creative about-faces in Disney history (Ghez, D., 2018, *Walt's People*, Volume 22, Theme Park Press).

The final film is tonally distinctive from most of the Disney animated canon: it is self-aware, anachronistic, comedically aggressive, and departs from the musical and emotional structure of the Renaissance films. It performed modestly at the box office — earning approximately $169.3 million worldwide against a production budget of $100 million, considered a disappointing commercial result for a Disney theatrical release at the time (Box Office Mojo, 2023) — but has since gained a substantial cult following, particularly among audiences who encountered it in childhood via home video and who have developed affection for its unusual comic sensibility. The film generated a direct-to-video sequel, *Kronk's New Groove* (2005), and an animated television series, *The Emperor's New School* (2006–2008), set in a reimagined continuity.

**Disney's live-action remake programme: scope and logic.** Disney's systematic live-action adaptation of its animated back catalogue began in earnest with Tim Burton's *Alice in Wonderland* (2010), which grossed over $1 billion worldwide and demonstrated the commercial viability of the approach. The strategic logic was clearly articulated in subsequent industry commentary: the animated originals provide a pre-sold concept and built-in audience nostalgia, making the live-action productions lower marketing risk than wholly original properties; live-action allows for updated visual aesthetics, expanded runtime and story, and the commercial exploitation of streaming via Disney+; and the remakes extend the commercial lifecycle of IP that would otherwise be in a long tail of passive catalogue revenue (Grainge, P., 2021, *Journal of Popular Television*, 9(2), 125–143).

By the early 2020s, Disney had developed a public list of announced or confirmed live-action adaptations. Projects in late development or with confirmed production starts are typically disclosed via press releases, presentation slides at the annual shareholder meeting (D23 Expo when held biannually), or sourced reporting in trade publications. The announced and confirmed live-action pipeline does not, as of mid-2026, include *The Emperor's New Groove*.

**What has been reported versus confirmed for Emperor's New Groove.** Unlike some Disney properties for which specific development conversations have been confirmed by named trade sources — a *Hercules* live-action adaptation, for example, was reported as in development with the Russo Brothers attached (though that too remains in an uncertain state as of mid-2026) — no credible trade report with named industry sources has confirmed a greenlit or even formally optioned *Emperor's New Groove* live-action production. Reports of fan petitions, social media campaigns advocating for such a production, and speculative casting discussions have been covered by entertainment aggregation sites, but this coverage describes audience desire rather than studio activity.

The absence of confirmed development is not evidence that the project will never happen — development conversations occur outside the public record. But it is evidence that the claim of an active production is false.

**Challenges specific to an Emperor's New Groove live-action adaptation.** The particular characteristics of *The Emperor's New Groove* that make it a beloved cult property also create genuine challenges for a live-action adaptation that may explain the absence of a confirmed production despite the film's cult status and the broader remake pipeline.

The most significant challenge is tonal: the film's particular warmth operates through a specific comedic register — rapid-fire anachronistic jokes, fourth-wall awareness, Looney Tunes-influenced physical comedy, and a cast of voice actors working in a highly stylised mode — that does not straightforwardly translate to live action. The live-action remakes that have worked most smoothly (*The Jungle Book*, 2016; *Cruella*, 2021) either leaned into spectacle and realism (*Jungle Book*) or committed to a new genre and tonal register (*Cruella* as a stylised character study). The live-action remakes that generated the most critical disappointment (*Mulan*, 2020; the Jon Favreau *Lion King*, 2019 for critics if not audiences) were perceived as losing something essential in translation. *The Emperor's New Groove*'s comedy depends heavily on the unreality of animation — characters can survive things that live actors cannot, timing works differently, the film's visual jokes are structured for cartoon physics. A live-action version would need to make substantial creative decisions about what to retain, what to replace, and how to preserve the frenetic energy.

The death of Eartha Kitt in 2008 also removes the possibility of an audio cameo or involvement from the voice performer most indelibly associated with the film's most iconic character. Yzma's combination of imperious camp and comic malevolence was inseparably connected to Kitt's performance, and recasting the character invites the same unfair but real comparison dynamic that makes any beloved property's recasting challenging.

**What is actually confirmed: status as of mid-2026.** No *Emperor's New Groove* live-action remake has been announced by Disney. No production team, director, or cast has been attached. No release date has been set. No trade publication has reported the project as greenlit or in confirmed development with specific attached personnel. The claim that Disney is rebooting the film is false.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Disney's real and documented remake factory makes any individual title plausible.** The single most powerful driver of false confidence in Disney remake claims for any given title is the simple inductive inference from the company's behaviour: Disney has remade (or is remaking) the great majority of its animated library. The list of confirmed or completed live-action remakes is long; the titles not yet remade grows shorter. A curious observer looking at the Disney animated catalogue and asking "which ones haven't been done yet?" will find *The Emperor's New Groove* on the list, along with *Bolt*, *Treasure Planet*, *Atlantis*, *Fantasia*, *Bambi* (announced), *Aristocats* (announced), and others. The observation that the film hasn't been remade yet is accurate; the inference that it must therefore be in development is the logical leap that the evidence does not support.

This is a classic inductive fallacy applied to a cultural phenomenon: because a series (live-action Disney remakes) has continued with high regularity, and because a given member of the relevant category (beloved Disney animated films) has not yet been covered, observers conclude the series is about to cover it. The inference has a plausible premise but is invalidated by the fact that Disney makes deliberate selections about which properties to develop, the selections are constrained by creative and commercial feasibility, and the company has not indicated that a particular title is next merely because it is logically available.

**Fan community momentum around the film creates continuous low-level viral activity.** *The Emperor's New Groove* has a distinctive and active fan community, disproportionate to its original commercial performance. The film's cult status, particularly among millennials who grew up with it in the home video era, means that social media activity about the film — nostalgia posts, fan art, discussion of a potential remake, casting speculation — is continuous and generates periodic surges. When an entertainment blog or YouTube channel publishes speculative content about who should play Kuzco in a hypothetical live-action remake, it generates substantial engagement from the fan community, which in turn generates algorithmic amplification, which results in the speculative content reaching audiences who may mistake enthusiasm and discussion for reportorial confirmation.

The specific comedic sensibility of the film — its self-aware, referential humour — also means that content about the film tends to be itself referential and humorous in tone, which blurs the line between genuine reporting and humorous speculation in ways that can mislead casual readers about what is actually being claimed.

**Epistemic novelty: the "active imagination property" and why some fan communities generate more false production claims than others.** An underexamined structural phenomenon in entertainment rumour ecology is the correlation between a property's *fan community activity level* and the frequency of false production claims about that property. This is counterintuitive: one might expect that a more active fan community would be better at information quality, with fans rapidly correcting false claims. In practice, active fan communities generate higher volumes of speculative and aspirational content, which creates more opportunities for the speculation-to-news pipeline to activate.

The Emperor's New Groove fan community is a specific example of what might be called an "active imagination property" community: a fan base whose primary cultural activity is constructive imagination about the property — fan fiction, casting speculation, fan art, unofficial sequels — rather than documentary tracking of the property's actual commercial and production history. These communities have a different epistemic relationship to claims about production status than, say, communities primarily interested in box office analysis or production history. For an "active imagination property" fan community, a vivid and emotionally resonant speculative narrative — "imagine Oscar Isaac as Kuzco, Emma Stone as Yzma" — can propagate within the community and outward from it without the community as a whole particularly caring whether it is confirmed. The imaginative exercise has value independent of its accuracy, but it enters the broader information environment as a claim that can mislead observers who are not aware of its speculative origins (Jenkins, H., Shresthova, S., et al., 2016, *By Any Media Necessary: The New Youth Activism*, New York University Press, applying fan engagement frameworks to information production).

**The general mechanism of "streaming pipeline" rumour generation.** The Disney+ era created a specific variant of the production rumour: the assertion that a beloved property is "coming to Disney+" as a series, film, or feature-length special. The platform's appetite for content, particularly content leveraging existing IP, is genuine and documented. The list of Disney+ originals produced or announced is long. The gap between the platform's known appetite and the list of known confirmed titles is the space in which "X is coming to Disney+" speculation proliferates. For *The Emperor's New Groove*, the claim has appeared in both theatrical remake form and Disney+ special/series form, with both variants drawing on the same mechanism: the platform needs content, the property is beloved, therefore the production exists or is imminent.

The commercial logic is real; the specific production is not confirmed.


## 4. Verdict

**False as stated; unverified as a production future.** No Disney live-action remake of *The Emperor's New Groove* has been announced, greenlit, cast, or confirmed in any credible industry source as of mid-2026. The claim that Disney "is rebooting" the film in any currently active sense is false.

The EARC classification is C: the claim is *constructed* from the real background of Disney's extensive live-action remake programme, the genuine cult status of the film, and the inferential logic that beloved Disney properties will eventually be remade, assembled into an assertion of current production fact that the evidence does not support.

The gap category is false-but-plausible: unlike "amplified-beyond-evidence" claims, which have a verified real core that is over-stated, this claim is simply false as a production statement, while being grounded in plausible commercial reasoning. It is not implausible that Disney would at some future point develop a live-action *Emperor's New Groove*; the claim is false because that development has not occurred or been confirmed as of the date of publication.

Snopes verdict: false (for the specific current-production claim).


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Emperor's New Groove reboot claim is a specific instance of a broader epistemological challenge in contemporary entertainment media: how to reason accurately about a studio's known intentions and capabilities in relation to its specific unrealised projects, in an environment where the gap between "possible" and "confirmed" is systematically collapsed by a media ecosystem that profits from treating them as equivalent.

**The live-action remake era: what the data actually shows.** Disney's live-action remake programme is one of the most documented strategic patterns in major studio history. Between 2010 and 2026, Disney released approximately fifteen to eighteen live-action adaptations of animated properties, generating collectively tens of billions in worldwide box office revenue. The programme has attracted substantial critical attention, with media scholars examining its relationship to nostalgia capitalism, the commodification of childhood memory, and the aesthetics of remake rather than original production.

Critical surveys have noted a divergence in the programme's outcomes: remakes that found a distinctive creative angle or a significantly updated creative interpretation (*The Jungle Book*, *Maleficent*, *Cruella*) tended to receive better critical reception than remakes perceived as largely faithful reproductions of the original (*The Lion King*, *Beauty and the Beast*, *Dumbo*), though commercial performance did not consistently track with critical response (Proctor, W., & McCulloch, R., 2021, *Transmedia Earth: Global Convergence Cultures*, Routledge; Gray, J., 2022, *Entertainment Values: How Do We Assess Films, Music, and Television and Why Does It Matter?*, Bloomsbury Academic). The question for any specific unmade title is which creative approach a remake would take, whether that approach adds sufficient value to justify the production risk, and whether the specific property's characteristics are amenable to the live-action format.

**The structural question of when and why specific titles are selected.** Disney's selection of titles for the live-action treatment is not random, and its logic can be partially decoded from the announced sequence. Early selections — *Alice in Wonderland*, *Cinderella*, *Jungle Book*, *Beauty and the Beast* — were among the most commercially successful and most universally known animated properties, which minimised the market question of audience awareness. Later selections have tended toward properties with specific audience bases, properties where a particular creative talent expresses interest or is matched to the material, and properties where a contemporary cultural context creates an opening for updated treatment (e.g., the *Mulan* remake's commitment to a Chinese cast and Chinese market ambitions).

*The Emperor's New Groove* fits imperfectly into these selection logics. It is beloved but not universally known — it was a commercial underperformer on original release. Its cult audience is real but demographically narrower than the *Lion King* or *Little Mermaid* audiences. Its creative challenges (tonal, comedic, physicality) are significant. Its most iconic voice performance (Eartha Kitt) cannot be replicated. These are not insuperable barriers but they do explain why the property might rationally remain in the pool of "possible but not yet developed" rather than "actively in production." The animation catalogues of studios always contain more films than any live-action remake programme can ever produce; selectivity is inherent.

**The cult property paradox.** Properties with intense cult followings exist in a peculiar position in the studio development calculus. The cult audience creates commercial evidence that the property has value; the specificity of the cult audience's attachment creates risk that a live-action remake will be judged harshly against the original by the people who care most about it. This is sometimes called the "beloved property trap" in informal industry analysis: the properties most commercially motivated to remake are sometimes the properties where the reputational risk of a failed remake is highest, because the original's admirers will be the loudest critics.

The Emperor's New Groove's cult following is constituted by people whose attachment to the original's specific tone, pacing, and cast is very strong. A live-action remake that replaces David Spade, John Goodman, and Eartha Kitt in the public imagination with new performers risks not satisfying the existing fan base (who will miss the originals) while also not attracting the broader audiences who didn't connect with the original. This calculus may be one reason the project has not — despite two decades of remake activity — moved into confirmed development.

**What would it take for the claim to become true?** A useful exercise for readers navigating entertainment production claims is to specify the evidence that would make the claim true. For the Emperor's New Groove reboot, that evidence would include: (a) a Disney press release or official stakeholder presentation confirming a live-action production in development; (b) a reporting piece in *Variety*, *The Hollywood Reporter*, or *Deadline* with named sources and specific production details — at minimum a director attachment or screenwriter assignment; (c) principal casting announcements through Disney's official channels; or (d) a production start announcement with a projected release date. None of these conditions has been met. Until one of them is, the specific claim of an active remake production remains false.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Emperor's New Groove reboot claim is not addressed in any direct Snopes fact-check entry, which is itself informative. Snopes addresses the claim types that generate enough specific web searches and social concern to justify a dedicated entry. The Emperor's New Groove remake claim is currently at a level of social circulation that generates consistent fan community discussion but has not apparently generated the traffic spike associated with a specific triggering viral post — a social media post by a major account falsely asserting a specific confirmed announcement — that typically prompts Snopes to write a specific verdict.

The appropriate response to entertainment production claims in general, and Disney remake claims in particular, is a workflow that can be summarised as: (1) find the most specific version of the claim (not the social media paraphrase, but the original source it cites); (2) determine whether that source is a primary studio announcement, a confirmed trade report with named sources, or an aggregation of an aggregation; (3) check the most recent reporting from the major entertainment trades for the specific title; and (4) apply the standard production-status vocabulary — "in development" means talks are occurring but nothing is committed; "in production" means principal photography is scheduled or underway; "in pre-production" means a project is greenlit and the production team is being assembled.

For *The Emperor's New Groove* specifically: the most recent major trade reporting does not confirm a production. The most recent credible unverified sourcing suggests the property has not entered the formal development pipeline at Disney. Fan community activity and entertainment blog speculation do not constitute production evidence. The claim is false; the film does not exist in any confirmed form.

One additional consideration for readers: the Disney live-action remake programme has itself become a cultural controversy, with critical discourse about whether it represents creative stagnation, heritage exploitation, or nostalgia capitalism (Proctor, W., & McCulloch, R., 2021, *Transmedia Earth*, Routledge). Some circulation of individual remake claims — including the Emperor's New Groove claim — occurs in the context of this debate, with commentators using the (false) claim of an imminent remake as a prompt for discussing whether such a remake would be desirable or culturally appropriate. In those contexts, the false claim functions as a conversation starter rather than an assertion of fact, further illustrating how the same piece of misinformation can serve multiple social functions simultaneously and how its falsity can be orthogonal to its social utility.
