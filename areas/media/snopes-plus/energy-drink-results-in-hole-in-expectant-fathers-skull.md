## §1 — Claim Anatomy

On 3 October 2017, a post published to the Facebook page of a photography company called Endres Photography described the experience of a client named Brianna, whose husband Austin had undergone emergency brain surgery. The post alleged that physicians concluded Austin's brain hemorrhage was caused by his excessive consumption of energy drinks. The account, written in the first-person voice of the pregnant wife, described a man who had developed a habit of drinking multiple energy drinks per day after beginning longer work hours and extended commutes. A dramatic photograph purportedly showed the aftermath of the surgery — a significant portion of the skull surgically removed to relieve intracranial pressure, a procedure known medically as a decompressive hemicraniectomy.

More than a week after its posting, the content went widely viral. By the time Snopes investigated, the original post had been deleted from the Endres Photography Facebook page, though an archived copy remained accessible. Multiple news outlets, Fox News among them, republished the story with essentially no independent verification, relying entirely on the secondhand social media account.

The core claim is a causal one: energy drink consumption caused the hemorrhage. The secondary claim is diagnostic: Austin's physicians reached this conclusion definitively after ruling out other drug use via a toxicology screen. The tertiary claim, implicit in the viral framing, is that the risk is sufficiently generalizable to serve as a public health warning — that ordinary consumers of energy drinks face similar dangers.

Each layer of the claim presents its own evidentiary problems, but the causal attribution is the most consequential and the most inadequately supported. What was offered as a medical conclusion was, at best, an exclusionary inference — attributing cause to energy drinks because no other substance was found — rather than any affirmative mechanistic finding. That epistemological gap is the fulcrum on which the entire story turns.

The claim's viral traction was amplified by the emotional salience of the context: a young expectant couple, a husband suddenly incapacitated, a wife days away from giving birth. These details were inherently compelling and served to override the normal friction that might otherwise slow the spread of an unverified health scare. The photography company's platform, which existed to document life events, provided an unusual level of apparent authenticity.

## §2 — Verification Record

Snopes investigators Kim LaCapria and Alex Kasprak attempted to verify the underlying facts of the account. Their investigation surfaced multiple categories of missing information. First, the post contained no identifying details that would allow independent verification: no hospital name, no attending physician, no dates of treatment, no city or state, no patient surname beyond the first name Austin. News reports that picked up the story similarly failed to seek this information, publishing under characteristically alarm-driven headlines while drawing exclusively from the photograph and the text of the Facebook post.

Second, the investigators contacted three medical experts to assess the plausibility of the described mechanism but had received no response by press time. This left the medical plausibility component of the investigation incomplete. What the investigators could document was the state of the peer-reviewed literature on energy drinks and hemorrhagic events, which they characterised as suggestive but far from conclusive.

Third, the diagnosis as reported in the social media post rested on a logical structure that is scientifically insufficient for causal attribution: energy drinks were identified as the cause because no other drugs were found in the toxicological screen. This represents a classic exclusionary fallacy — the absence of alternative explanations does not confirm the posited cause, particularly where the posited mechanism (caffeine-provoked hypertension leading to vascular rupture) is unproven in human clinical literature at anything approaching typical energy drink doses.

Fourth, the surgical outcome described — the decompressive hemicraniectomy visible in the photograph — is itself real and well-documented in neurosurgical literature. The surgery is a genuine procedure performed in response to certain types of severe intracranial hemorrhage. This lent the post a veneer of medical authenticity that further facilitated viral spread. Verifying that such a surgery exists and is performed does not, however, verify that energy drinks caused the hemorrhage that necessitated it.

Snopes's final verdict was effectively Unverified: the investigators could neither confirm that events unfolded as described, nor confirm the causal attribution made by the alleged treating physicians. In the absence of corroborating information, they characterised the scenario as medically plausible but extremely rare if true, and noted that the level of evidence provided was wholly insufficient to support the causal claim. The claim remains unverified.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis

This case is epistemically remarkable for what it reveals about the relationship between narrative plausibility and evidentiary burden. The story was structured in a way that mirrored the conventions of genuine medical testimonials: first-person voice, emotionally coherent stakes, apparently corroborating photographic evidence, and an attributed medical authority (the doctors who "concluded" the cause). Each of these features served as a proxy for the kind of verification that was never actually performed.

The epistemic novelty here lies in the mechanism of surrogate verification. Audiences and editors alike appear to have treated the internal consistency of the narrative as a substitute for external confirmation. A consistent, emotionally resonant story told by an identifiable narrator about a real-seeming medical procedure was sufficient to satisfy the informal evidential threshold that determines whether content gets shared. This threshold is almost entirely social and affective, not epistemic. The plausibility heuristic — "this sounds like the kind of thing that could happen" — did the work that evidence was never asked to do.

This represents a specific failure mode distinct from simple fabrication. The Endres Photography post does not appear to have been a deliberate hoax in the mode of a purely invented story. It plausibly described a real event, filtered through Brianna's lay understanding of her husband's medical situation, including the physicians' own speculative causal attribution presented as a confident conclusion. The physicians, if they exist, may themselves have communicated probabilistically in a context that was retransmitted with false precision. The chain of epistemic degradation — from speculative medical inference to confident conclusion to viral health warning — is a documented feature of how health information diffuse in social media environments.

A further epistemically significant feature is the deletion of the original post. Once the content had been archived and was circulating in news reports, the removal of the source eliminated the possibility of the original narrator correcting misinterpretations, adding context, or retracting the account. Deletion paradoxically can authenticate viral content: the disappearance of a post is read by some audiences as evidence of suppression, reinforcing rather than undermining the claim's credibility. The pattern in which original posts are deleted after content goes viral is not rare, and it consistently complicates fact-checking by eliminating access to the primary narrator.

The exclusionary reasoning attributed to the physicians is also worth examining. A toxicological screen negative for controlled substances does not constitute positive evidence for any specific cause of intracranial hemorrhage. Hemorrhagic stroke occurs across a wide population without notable risk factors. Hypertension, arteriovenous malformations, aneurysm rupture, and coagulation disorders can all produce the described outcome with no drug involvement of any kind. The claim that doctors "concluded" energy drinks were the cause, absent imaging evidence of vascular pathology or specific hemodynamic data, represents an inference beyond what the evidence could support.

## §4 — Scientific and Empirical Background

The empirical question of whether caffeine and energy drinks can cause or contribute to cerebrovascular events is genuinely contested in the scientific literature, and the Snopes investigation appropriately characterised the evidence as conflicting rather than settled in either direction.

Caffeine's effect on cardiovascular physiology is well established. Hartley et al. (2004), writing in the *American Journal of Cardiology*, documented caffeine's vasoconstrictive properties and its acute effect of increasing blood pressure. This mechanism is the physiological premise underlying any proposed causal pathway from energy drink consumption to hemorrhagic stroke: elevated blood pressure increases mechanical stress on arterial walls, and acute hypertensive episodes have been associated with rupture of intracranial vessels, particularly in individuals with underlying vascular vulnerability.

Kim et al. (2012) conducted a meta-analysis of epidemiological studies examining coffee consumption and stroke risk, published in the *Korean Journal of Family Medicine*. Their findings were equivocal, reflecting the broader state of the literature: some analyses suggested modest protective effects at moderate consumption levels, while others described increased risk at high intake levels. The inconsistency across studies reflects methodological heterogeneity, confounding variables, and genuine biological variation in caffeine metabolism.

The most directly relevant piece of literature is Venkatraman et al. (2017), who published what the authors described as the first case report linking energy drink consumption to brain hemorrhage in *The American Journal of Emergency Medicine*. Their report concerned a 57-year-old who consumed a high-caffeine energy drink called Redline and subsequently developed a hemorrhagic stroke. Critically, the authors did not claim certainty about the mechanism, noting that the precise component responsible was unclear and speculating that caffeine's hypertensive effects, combined with the drink's other bioactive constituents — yohimbine and 5-hydroxytryptophan — may have interacted in ways not individually predictable.

This single case report is notable for what it is and what it is not. It is the first formal documentation in indexed medical literature of a temporally plausible connection between energy drink use and cerebral hemorrhage. It is not proof of a causal mechanism, nor does it establish a population-level risk. The case report methodology is designed to surface clinically interesting observations that warrant further investigation, not to demonstrate causation. The scenario described in the viral Facebook post — a younger man whose habit involved energy drinks rather than an extreme product like Redline — is not directly analogous.

Alvis-Miranda et al. (2013), in the *Bulletin of Emergency and Trauma*, reviewed decompressive craniectomy practice and outcomes in traumatic brain injury, providing context for the surgical procedure visible in the viral photographs. Hemicraniectomy is performed in cases of severe hemispheric swelling or large hematoma to prevent fatal herniation of brain tissue. The fact that such a surgery was performed, if accurate, is consistent with a serious hemispheric hemorrhage regardless of cause.

From the public health perspective, energy drinks have been increasingly scrutinised by regulatory agencies and researchers. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration has documented adverse event reports involving energy drinks, and the beverage category has been associated in epidemiological data with increased emergency department visits. However, the evidentiary standard for regulatory concern is much lower than the standard required to attribute any individual adverse health event to a single exposure.

## §5 — Misinformation Mechanics

The virality of the Endres Photography post followed a pattern now well documented in health misinformation research: authentic origin, emotional amplification, media amplification without independent verification, and circulation persistence after the original source is removed.

The authentic origin element is particularly important. Unlike content generated by organised disinformation campaigns or commercially motivated hoaxers, the Endres Photography post appears to have begun as a genuine personal testimony. This places it in the category of misinformation rather than disinformation in the strict sense: an account that may accurately reflect the narrator's sincere beliefs but which, as transmitted and received, makes claims that are materially undetermined. The poster's attribution of the cause to energy drinks was plausibly reported in good faith; the amplifying media's failure to seek verification was the point at which the evidentiary failure became consequential.

The photography company context added a layer of social legitimacy. The audience for such a post is people who have engaged with this business in a context of personal trust — families who hire photographers to document births and life milestones. That pre-existing relationship creates a social proof mechanism: if the poster's clients found the story credible enough to share across their networks, editorial judgment by downstream sharers was further suppressed.

Fox News and other outlets that republished the story without verification exemplify a pattern in which the urgency of competition for audience attention overrides the standard practices of source verification. The claim was structured around compelling visual evidence (the photographs), a plausible mechanism (energy drinks as cardiovascular risk), and a sympathetic protagonist (an expectant father). All three elements satisfied the audience-engagement criteria that determine coverage decisions, while none of them constituted actual verification.

The deletion of the original post after viral spread is characteristic of a subset of viral content where the original poster becomes uncomfortable with attention or feels the account has been distorted. Whatever the reason in this case, the deletion did not slow circulation; by that point, the content had migrated into news articles, archived pages, and secondary social media shares, becoming effectively self-sustaining.

## §6 — Verdict and Takeaways

**Verdict: Unverified.** The claim that energy drinks caused an expectant father to develop a brain hemorrhage requiring decompressive hemicraniectomy cannot be confirmed or denied on available evidence. The scenario as described is medically plausible at a low level of probability. The causal attribution made by the alleged physicians, as reported in a single Facebook post that has since been deleted, represents speculative inference rather than diagnostic confirmation. No independent corroboration of the diagnosis, the physician statements, or the causal conclusion has been established.

The case illustrates the gap between plausibility and evidence that is central to health misinformation. Energy drinks are not inert substances, and the scientific literature on their cardiovascular effects is legitimate and worth understanding. Venkatraman et al. (2017) documented one plausible case in a peer-reviewed journal; Hartley et al. (2004) established the relevant cardiovascular mechanism. But a plausible mechanism documented in one case report does not convert an unverified social media testimony into confirmed fact.

The evidentiary gap here is classified as type **E** (Evidence): the claim rests entirely on a single deleted primary source, and all secondary coverage was derivative of that source rather than independently verified. Until independent confirmation of the underlying events and the medical findings is provided, no confident verdict is possible.

For consumers of health information, the case presents several practical lessons. First, the presence of detailed, emotionally credible testimony does not substitute for documentary evidence. Second, a negative toxicological screen does not establish a positive causal attribution. Third, coverage by mainstream news outlets of a viral claim is not the same as independent verification of that claim; many news organisations republish viral content without conducting verification that meets basic evidentiary standards. Fourth, the absence of the original source post does not make the claim more credible; deletion is common for a range of reasons unrelated to truth value.

For researchers and practitioners in health communication, the case underscores the importance of distinguishing between individual anecdote and population-level causal inference. The scientific evidence on caffeine and cardiovascular risk is genuinely worth discussing; the framing provided by the viral post, however, collapsed the distinction between plausible and proven, producing a public health narrative unsupported by the evidence it putatively cited.
