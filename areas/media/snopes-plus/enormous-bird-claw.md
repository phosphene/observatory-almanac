## The Claim

On May 25, 2024, a photograph of what appeared to be an enormous, heavily scaled, claw-tipped bird foot went viral on X, captioned: "A Southern Cassowary claw. Just in case you didn't think that birds are dinosaurs." By the time Snopes investigated the claim, the post — attributed to the account @AMAZlNGNATURE — had accumulated more than 15.9 million views.

The photograph is genuinely startling. The foot is large enough to fill a human handler's two-handed grip; the skin is distinctly reptilian in texture, covered in rigid scales not obviously different from those associated with dinosaurs in popular visual depictions; and the three forward-facing toes terminate in substantial curved claws — with the innermost toe bearing a claw that is notably longer, more blade-like, and more formidable than those of any common bird species. The image triggers immediate recalibration of viewer expectations about what a living bird's anatomy looks like.

The claim is dual: that the photograph shows a real bird foot (not AI-generated, not a prop, not a historical specimen in unusual context), and that the identification as a cassowary is accurate. Both parts of the dual claim are verifiable.

---

## What's Actually True

The photograph is genuine and correctly identified. It was first shared on January 15, 2019, by Sarah Davis, who was at that time a doctoral candidate in the Department of Geological Sciences at the University of Texas at Austin, where she studied both avian and non-avian dinosaur anatomy. Davis published the image with the caption: "Holding the claws of a male southern cassowary… Just in case any of your friends still need convinced that 🦕 = 🦅! #birdsaredinosaurs."

Davis subsequently clarified in follow-up posts that the cassowary from which the foot came had died of natural causes after a long and healthy life, and that the body was being used in multiple graduate research projects studying avian anatomy, feathering, and coloration — a standard practice in vertebrate biology research, where cadavers from natural deaths at zoos, wildlife refuges, and sanctuaries contribute substantially to anatomical and morphological knowledge.

In January 2019, the popular science outlet Live Science published a substantive article on the photograph, "Why This Enormous, Scaly Foot Looks Like It's from a Dinosaur," which included direct quotations from Davis. Davis told the publication that her research involved comparative anatomy of closely related flightless birds — cassowaries, ostriches, and emus — specifically examining leg muscle structure to understand the divergent evolutionary pathways by which each lineage independently lost the ability to fly. She noted that the visual similarity of the cassowary foot to depictions of non-avian dinosaur feet was scientifically meaningful, not merely aesthetic: "Birds are living dinosaurs, and the dinosaurs we see today are equally as interesting as those that went extinct at the end of the Cretaceous."

The southern cassowary (*Casuarius casuarius*) is the largest of the three extant cassowary species. It inhabits lowland tropical rainforest in New Guinea, the Maluku Islands, and northeastern Australia (Queensland). Males are typically smaller than females in this species, a reversal of the size dimorphism pattern common in most bird species. Adult females can reach 1.5–1.8 metres in height and weigh up to 60–70 kilograms, making *Casuarius casuarius* one of the heaviest extant bird species. The characteristic casque — a prominent, keratinous helmet-like structure on the top of the head — and the extremely vivid blue and red neck coloration make cassowaries visually unmistakable.

The cassowary's innermost toe bears a claw that can reach 12 centimetres or more in length, with a comparatively narrow, dagger-like cross-section. This claw is the animal's primary defensive and offensive weapon. The Queensland Government's official wildlife guideline notes that cassowaries can deliver kicks of extreme force and that the inner claw can cause deep puncture wounds in larger animals and humans. Encyclopedia Britannica records that the southern cassowary "has been known to kill human beings with slashing blows of its feet, as the innermost of its three toes bears a long, daggerlike nail." The cassowary is officially classified as the world's most dangerous bird by multiple sources.

## Why People Believe This — and Why They're Right

This claim is true, and the widespread belief in it is, at base, simply an appropriate response to accurate visual information. But the specific mechanism by which the image generates its distinctive impact is worth examining in detail, because it touches on one of the more fascinating chapters in the history of evolutionary biology.

**The dinosaur-bird connection as popular knowledge.** The caption's assertion "birds are dinosaurs" frames the image within scientific evolutionary consensus. The recognition that birds are living theropod dinosaurs — not merely the "descendants" of dinosaurs in a distant genealogical sense, but members of the clade Dinosauria — is among the most consequential revisions to popular and scientific understanding of the Mesozoic era achieved in the late 20th century. The work of John Ostrom at Yale, beginning with the 1964 description of *Deinonychus antirrhopus* (Ostrom, 1969, *Bulletin of the Peabody Museum of Natural History*), initiated a fundamental reassessment of theropod locomotion and behaviour that ultimately established the theropod-bird connection on rigorous morphological grounds. Later, the discovery of feathered theropod fossils from the Yixian and Jiufotang Formations in China — Sinosauropteryx (Ji & Ji, 1996, *Chinese Geology*), Caudipteryx, Microraptor, and dozens of others — provided direct physical evidence of the evolutionary transition in unprecedented detail.

Robert Bakker's popularisation of the "hot-blooded dinosaur" hypothesis and the related argument that birds are living dinosaurs in his 1986 book *The Dinosaur Heresies* brought this scientific reappraisal to popular attention. The contemporary scientific consensus, represented by the comprehensive phylogenetic analyses of the Theropoda (Turner, Makovicky & Norell, 2012, *Bulletin of the American Museum of Natural History*), places birds within Maniraptora, the clade of theropod dinosaurs that includes velociraptorid dromaeosaurs, troodontids, and oviraptorosaurs. The phylogenetic distance between a modern cassowary and *Velociraptor mongoliensis* is, by some measures, smaller than the distance between *Velociraptor* and more basal theropods.

**Why the cassowary looks the way it looks.** The cassowary's foot morphology — the large scaly toes, the dagger claw — is not incidentally dinosaur-like. It reflects genuinely ancient anatomical heritage. The basic tetanuran theropod foot structure, characterised by a tridactyl (three-forward-toed) functional foot with a reduced inner toe and large unguals (claws), was established in the Late Triassic and persisted through the evolution of maniraptoran theropods and into modern birds with relatively little modification in the lineages that retained large body size and terrestrial lifestyle. The cassowary's foot, in other words, preserves an anatomical bauplan — a fundamental body template — that would be recognisable to a palaeontologist examining a cast of theropod trackways from the Jurassic.

Modern bird feet generally show evolutionary modifications away from the ancestral theropod pattern, driven by diverse specialisations: perching, swimming, digging, predatory gripping. But flightless, large-bodied, ground-dwelling birds such as cassowaries, ostriches, and emus have maintained strong selective pressure for powerful, load-bearing legs with effective defensive claws — pressure that keeps their foot morphology closer to the ancestral theropod template. The "dinosaurian feeling" that Davis described experiencing while holding the cassowary's claws is, from an evolutionary biology standpoint, precisely appropriate.

**The epistemic novelty of living evidence.** There is a distinctive epistemological quality to the cassowary photograph that sets it apart from purely documentary or factual viral images. The photograph does not simply verify a claim about the cassowary — it functions as sensory evidence for an evolutionary claim that most people understand abstractly but have never encountered as immediately as direct physical encounter allows. The photograph temporarily collapses the distance between understanding "birds are dinosaurs" as a scientific proposition and experiencing "this foot is a dinosaur foot" as a perceptual reality.

This collapsing of abstract evolutionary claim into perceptual immediacy is an epistemically unusual mode of learning. Most scientific knowledge is mediated through text, diagrams, and the authority of expert report — it is believed rather than seen. The cassowary photograph provides a rare instance of a scientific fact that can be felt, visually, as true. The 15.9 million views the 2024 X post accumulated reflect this unusual epistemic quality: the image carries its own argument, visible to anyone who looks at it.

## Verdict

**The claim is true.** The photograph shows the genuine foot of a male southern cassowary (*Casuarius casuarius*), documented by a doctoral researcher at the University of Texas at Austin in the context of comparative avian anatomy research. The caption's assertion that "birds are dinosaurs" reflects the current scientific consensus in vertebrate palaeontology and evolutionary biology. Neither the photograph's authenticity nor the identification is in doubt.

Snopes rated this claim as True, based on verification through the original poster's identity (Sarah Davis), the corroborating Live Science article with direct quotations from Davis, and the extensive biological documentation of the southern cassowary's distinctive morphology in academic and popular science literature.

## The Wider Picture

The southern cassowary photograph belongs to a specific category of viral content: accurate, verified images that are viral precisely because they are true. Unlike misinformation, which spreads by exploiting false impressions, this content succeads by communicating a genuine reality that most people did not previously know or had not previously experienced as visually immediate.

**The cassowary as an organism.** The southern cassowary is a genuinely remarkable animal beyond its feet. The species is a critical ecosystem engineer in the rainforests it inhabits. Cassowaries are frugivores — they consume large quantities of fruit from dozens of rainforest tree species — and are among the few animals large enough to swallow and disperse the seeds of trees producing large fruits. Research by David Westcott and colleagues demonstrated that cassowaries are the sole effective dispersers for the seeds of some large-fruited rainforest trees in Queensland (Westcott, Bentrupperbäumer & Bradford, 2005, *Ecology*). The loss of cassowaries from a rainforest ecosystem therefore potentially causes the local extinction of the tree species that depend on them for seed dispersal — a trophic cascade effect now well-documented in the ecology of frugivory (Fleming & Kress, 2011, *The Ornaments of Life*).

The Queensland population of southern cassowaries is listed as Endangered under Australian environmental law, threatened by habitat loss, vehicle strikes (cassowaries frequently cross roads through their forest habitat), dog attacks, and human feeding (which causes cassowaries to approach humans and roads). The population is estimated at fewer than 4,000 individuals in Queensland. Conservation programs run by the Queensland government and the Cassowary Coast Regional Council focus on habitat corridor maintenance and road safety measures.

**Allopatric evolution and the Ratite problem.** The cassowary belongs to the ratites — the clade of flightless birds that includes ostriches, emus, kiwis, and the extinct moas and elephant birds. The evolutionary origin of ratite flightlessness has been one of the more contested questions in avian evolutionary biology. For most of the 20th century, the standard explanation was that the ratites descended from a common flightless ancestor and dispersed across the southern continents via continental drift (vicariance). More recent analyses using both molecular phylogenetics and biogeographic modelling have complicated this picture, with some analyses suggesting that flight was lost independently multiple times in different ratite lineages (Harshman et al., 2008, *Science*; Yonezawa et al., 2017, *Current Biology*). This ongoing debate illustrates the active and sometimes surprising nature of evolutionary research even in species as well-studied as the ratites.

**Theropod foot morphology in the fossil record.** The comparison between cassowary feet and theropod feet is well-grounded in the fossil record. Theropod trackways — fossil footprints — are among the most common dinosaur trace fossils, and the three-toed, large-ungual pattern is clearly documented across hundreds of trackway sites worldwide. Research by Lockley, Meyer, and Hunt among others has systematically analysed theropod ichnotaxa — the named forms of fossil footprints — and documented the continuity of functional foot morphology from large-bodied Triassic and Jurassic theropods through maniraptoran theropods and into modern birds (Lockley, 1991, *Tracking Dinosaurs*, Cambridge University Press). The cassowary's foot effectively provides a living reference for interpreting these fossil traces.

**The #birdsaredinosaurs discourse.** Sarah Davis's original caption used the hashtag #birdsaredinosaurs, which functions within science communication circles as both a technical description and an evangelistic claim directed at popular audiences. The scientific community has broadly adopted the position that the phrase "birds are dinosaurs" is more accurate than "birds evolved from dinosaurs" or "birds are descendants of dinosaurs," because the cladistic framework used in modern phylogenetics means that membership in a clade is defined by ancestry, not by grade or superficial resemblance. If birds evolved from theropod dinosaurs and Dinosauria is a clade (a monophyletic group), then birds are dinosaurs in the same sense that humans are mammals.

The popularisation of this framing — through museum exhibitions, science communication, and viral social media moments like the cassowary photograph — represents an ongoing project of updating popular biological intuitions to reflect the phylogenetic revolution in vertebrate palaeontology. The cassowary photograph is, in this sense, doing important science communication work: it is making the abstract evolutionary relationship between living birds and Mesozoic dinosaurs viscerally immediate in a way that text-based explanations struggle to achieve.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check, authored by Aleksandra Wrona and published May 31, 2024, is a clean example of verifying a viral claim through origin tracing and biological expertise.

**Reverse image search as origin-tracing tool.** The primary verification step was a Google reverse image search, which identified the photograph's prior circulation on 9GAG, Instagram, and Reddit, establishing that the image had been in circulation since at least January 2019. This origin-tracing approach is the appropriate first move for viral photographs — establishing a prior circulation history allows fact-checkers to evaluate whether the caption context in a new viral post matches the context in which the image first appeared.

**Connecting the image to its documented source.** The reverse image search led back to Sarah Davis's original January 2019 post and to the Live Science article, which provided direct quotations from Davis establishing her identity (doctoral candidate, UT Austin), her research context (comparative avian anatomy), and the provenance of the cassowary specimen (died of natural causes, used in multiple graduate research projects). These three elements together constitute robust documentary evidence for the claim.

**Biological documentation of the cassowary.** Wrona's fact-check includes the relevant biological context about the cassowary's anatomy and danger classification — Encyclopedia Britannica's description of the inner claw as "daggerlike," the Queensland Government's warning about cassowary kicks — that grounds the image in established natural history documentation. This contextualisation confirms not only that the photograph is authentic but that it accurately represents the animal's notable features.

**The "True" verdict is correct and complete.** This is one of those fact-checks where the claim is accurate and the main value added by the fact-check is restoration of context — the poster's identity, the research purpose, the animal's taxonomy — that viral spread typically strips away. The 15.9 million viewers who saw the image on X in May 2024 benefited from knowing who took the photograph, why, and how the cassowary's extraordinary anatomy connects to the broader story of avian evolution from theropod ancestors. The fact-check provides all of this efficiently.
