# The Truth Vault: What Is the Espionage Act?

## 1. The Claim

Following the August 8, 2022, federal court-authorized search of former President Donald Trump's Mar-a-Lago estate, public attention turned to one of the three potential violations listed in the search warrant: the Espionage Act of 1917. The law, which had been obscure to most Americans despite over a century of existence, suddenly became a subject of intense public interest and debate. Supporters of Trump argued that the law was being weaponized against a political opponent, that it was a relic of an earlier era being applied to circumstances its authors never imagined, and that the former president could have declassified any documents he chose. Critics argued that the law was clear, that its application was appropriate, and that the unprecedented nature of the case lay not in the law's use but in the conduct that prompted it.

Into this heated information environment, Snopes published an explainer on August 15, 2022. The article, "What Is the Espionage Act?" was not a typical Snopes fact-check but a republication of content from The Conversation, a nonprofit media organization that publishes explanatory journalism written by academics. The authors were Joseph Ferguson, Co-Director of the National Security and Civil Rights Program at Loyola University Chicago, and Thomas A. Durkin, Distinguished Practitioner in Residence at the same institution — both attorneys who specialize in national security law.

The Snopes-conversation article aimed to answer a straightforward question: what is the Espionage Act, and how does it apply to the circumstances surrounding the Mar-a-Lago search? The article provided a legal overview of the Act's history, its key provisions, and its application in notable cases. It emphasized that the Espionage Act "seldom pertains to espionage" in the popular sense — that is, the vast majority of cases brought under the Act involve the unauthorized possession or transmission of sensitive government information, not spying for a foreign power. It clarified that a violation does not require an intention to aid a foreign power, and that the Act applies to "national defense information" (NDI) beyond what is formally classified. It also warned that "the public can't judge a case based on classified information," noting that the very nature of the documents at issue prevents outsiders from assessing the merits of the case.

The article was broadly accurate in its legal analysis and provided a useful service to readers seeking to understand the law at the center of a major news story. But as a republication of academic commentary rather than original investigative journalism, it carried limitations that deserve examination.

## 2. What's Actually True

The Espionage Act of 1917 is a real and significant federal statute. It was enacted shortly after the United States entered World War I, at a time of intense anxiety about national security, foreign influence, and domestic dissent. The Act made it a crime to interfere with military operations, support enemies of the United States during wartime, or promote insubordination in the armed forces. It also criminalized the unauthorized possession or transmission of information related to national defense.

The Act has two main sections relevant to modern cases. Section 794 — 18 U.S.C. § 794 — addresses actual spying for foreign governments, carrying a maximum sentence of life imprisonment. This is the section most people think of when they hear "espionage," and it has been used to prosecute individuals like Jonathan Pollard (who spied for Israel in the 1980s), Aldrich Ames (a CIA officer turned double agent for the Soviet Union), and Robert Hanssen (an FBI agent who sold secrets to Moscow). These are genuine spy cases involving intentional betrayal of national secrets to foreign powers.

Section 793 — 18 U.S.C. § 793 — is the more commonly applied provision. It covers the unauthorized gathering, possessing, or transmitting of "information respecting the national defense" with the intent or reason to believe that such information could be used to injure the United States or advantage a foreign nation. This section does not require proof that the accused intended to aid a foreign power. It is enough that the person willfully possessed or transmitted NDI — national defense information — in an unauthorized manner. This is the section that has been applied to leakers, whistleblowers, and, potentially, to former officials who retain classified documents after leaving office.

The Act's application does not depend on whether the information is formally classified. The The Conversation article correctly notes that the Espionage Act applies to NDI, which is a broader category than classified information. Even if documents were declassified — which, the article notes, requires a formal process under Executive Order 13526 and must be done in writing while the declassifying authority is still in office — the Act could still apply if the information falls within the scope of NDI.

The article's warning about the impossibility of judging the case from outside is also accurate. Classified information is, by definition, not available to the public. Observers cannot assess the gravity of the documents at issue because they cannot see them. This creates an information asymmetry that makes it difficult for the public to evaluate the merits of the government's case or the defendant's defense.

The article is also correct that the Espionage Act has a long and controversial history. It was used to target immigrants, labor organizers, and left-leaning radicals in its early years. It was a tool of Cold War anti-communist politicians like Senator Joe McCarthy. The case of Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, executed in 1953 for passing atomic secrets to the Soviet Union, remains the Act's most famous — and most contested — prosecution. In the 1960s and 1970s, the Act was used against peace activists, including Pentagon Papers whistleblower Daniel Ellsberg. Since September 11, 2001, it has been used against whistleblowers like Edward Snowden and Chelsea Manning. This history of politicized use is real and relevant to understanding the Act's current application.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's decision to republish The Conversation article rather than commission original reporting is itself worth examining. The Snopes editorial note at the top of the article states: "This content is shared here because the topic may interest Snopes readers; it does not, however, represent the work of Snopes fact-checkers or editors." This is a transparent and appropriate disclaimer. Snopes is not claiming original research or editorial oversight. It is curating content from a trusted academic source.

The article succeeds as a basic primer on the Espionage Act. It correctly identifies the key sections, their different applications, and the historical context. It provides useful examples of famous cases. It clarifies common misconceptions, particularly the idea that the Act only applies to spying. The fact that the authors are national security law professors gives the article a credibility that a general journalist's explainer might lack.

However, the article has significant limitations that arise from its format as a republished academic commentary rather than a piece of investigative or adversarial journalism.

First, the article presents a fundamentally institutional perspective on the Espionage Act. It describes how the Act works without critically examining whether it should work that way. The Act's breadth — its application to NDI beyond classified information, its lack of a public interest defense, its potential to chill whistleblowing and journalism — is presented as fact rather than controversy. The article does not engage with the substantial body of legal scholarship and advocacy arguing that the Espionage Act is overbroad, that it violates First Amendment protections, and that it is used to punish whistleblowers who expose government misconduct.

Second, the article does not address the most significant criticisms of the Espionage Act as it is currently applied. The Act has no exception for the public interest. An individual who leaks classified information to expose government wrongdoing — the classic whistleblower scenario — can be prosecuted under the same legal framework as someone who sells secrets to a foreign power. This has led to criticism from civil liberties organizations, press freedom advocates, and legal scholars who argue that the Act's lack of a public interest defense makes it incompatible with democratic accountability. The article does not mention this criticism.

Third, the article omits several important cases that would complicate its portrait of the Act's application. The prosecution of Daniel Ellsberg for the Pentagon Papers is mentioned briefly, but the article does not note that the case was dismissed due to government misconduct — specifically, the burglary of Ellsberg's psychiatrist's office by White House operatives. This detail is relevant because it illustrates the lengths to which the government has gone to enforce the Act, and the vulnerability of even successful prosecutions to procedural collapse. The prosecution of Reality Winner, a former Air Force linguist who leaked a document about Russian interference in the 2016 election, is not mentioned. The prosecution of John Kiriakou, a former CIA officer who confirmed the existence of the agency's use of waterboarding, is not mentioned. These cases would provide a more complete picture of how the Act is used in practice.

Fourth, the article does not engage with the specific circumstances of the Trump case in any meaningful depth. It references the Mar-a-Lago search as the occasion for the explainer but does not analyze the legal theories underlying the warrant, the arguments for and against prosecution, or the novel legal questions that the case raises. The article is about the Espionage Act generally; it is not about the Espionage Act and Donald Trump. This is a defensible choice — the article's purpose is explanatory rather than analytical — but it limits the article's usefulness for readers trying to understand the specific controversy that prompted their interest.

Fifth, the article does not examine the political dynamics of the Act's use over time. The article notes that the biggest uptick in Espionage Act prosecutions occurred during the Obama administration, but it does not explore why. It does not examine the role of the Act in the Trump administration's own efforts to prosecute leakers, or the Trump administration's expansion of the Act's scope. The article presents the Act as a law that is applied neutrally across administrations, when in fact its application has been shaped by political priorities and constitutional controversies.

## 4. Why This Matters

The Espionage Act explainer matters for reasons that go beyond the accuracy of any particular claim.

First, the episode illustrates the challenge of providing accessible legal information in a highly polarized information environment. In August 2022, the Mar-a-Lago search was one of the most politically charged stories in the country. Any statement about the Espionage Act was likely to be interpreted through the lens of partisan allegiance. Supporters of Trump would see any acknowledgment of the Act's applicability as evidence of a "deep state" conspiracy. Critics of Trump would see any acknowledgment of the Act's complexity as evidence of a cover-up. An academic explainer could not be neutral in this context, even if it was neutral in content.

Second, the article demonstrates the limitations of "just the facts" explanatory journalism in covering legal controversies. The Espionage Act is not a simple statute with clear application. Its interpretation and application involve contested legal theories, political judgments, and constitutional questions. An explainer that presents the Act as a straightforward legal framework, without engaging with the controversies surrounding it, risks misleading readers into thinking the law is clearer than it actually is.

Third, the republished-content model raises questions about editorial responsibility. Snopes is a fact-checking organization that readers trust for accurate, vetted information. When Snopes republishes content from another source with a disclaimer that it "does not represent the work of Snopes fact-checkers or editors," there is a risk that readers will nevertheless attribute the same level of authority to the republished content as to Snopes's own work. The disclaimer is transparent, but it may not be sufficient to prevent this attribution.

Fourth, the article matters because it highlights the gap between legal accuracy and public understanding. The Espionage Act is a law that most Americans do not understand, and that even many lawyers do not fully understand. An accurate but incomplete explainer can create the impression of understanding while leaving significant gaps in knowledge. Readers who come away from the article thinking they now understand the Espionage Act may be less likely to seek out additional information or to question their own assumptions.

Fifth, the episode raises questions about the role of academic expertise in public discourse. The Conversation's model — having academics write for a general audience — has significant benefits. It brings specialized knowledge to public debates. But it also carries risks. Academics, like all experts, have perspectives and assumptions that shape their analysis. The authors of this article are national security law specialists who work within the framework of the law they describe. They may not share the critical perspective of civil libertarians, whistleblower advocates, or scholars who argue for fundamental reform of the Espionage Act. Presenting only one perspective, even an accurate one, can foreclose the consideration of alternatives.

## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article, published August 15, 2022, is a republication of content from The Conversation. The original article was written by Joseph Ferguson and Thomas A. Durkin of Loyola University Chicago, both attorneys specializing in national security law. The article is attributed to "The Conversation" on Snopes, with a note indicating that it does not represent the work of Snopes fact-checkers or editors.

The article cites the Espionage Act as codified in Title 18 of the U.S. Code, sections 793 and 794. It also references Executive Order 13526, which governs the classification of national security information. The Cornell Legal Information Institute provides the full text of both sections of the Act, and the Obama White House archives contain the text of Executive Order 13526.

The article mentions several key Espionage Act cases: Jonathan Pollard (1987), Aldrich Ames (1994), Robert Hanssen (2002), the WikiLeaks/Julian Assange case (2010), Sandy Berger (2005), David Petraeus (2016), Chelsea Manning (2013), Edward Snowden (post-2013), and Daniel Ellsberg (1971). It also references the prosecutions of Julius and Ethel Rosenberg (1953).

Notable omissions from the article include: the prosecution of Reality Winner (2017), the prosecution of John Kiriakou (2012), the prosecution of Thomas Drake (2010), the prosecution of Jeffrey Sterling (2015), the prosecution of Stephen Kim (2014), and the broader debate about the Espionage Act's application to journalists and media organizations. The article also does not discuss the Shield Act or other legislative efforts to reform the Espionage Act and protect whistleblowers.

The article's claim that the Obama administration saw the biggest uptick in Espionage Act prosecutions is documented in reporting from the Associated Press and other outlets. However, the article does not provide the specific numbers or context for this trend, nor does it examine whether the increase reflects a change in prosecutorial policy, a change in the prevalence of leaking, or other factors.

The article's treatment of the Trump case is limited to a mention that the search warrant listed the Espionage Act as one of three potential violations. It does not discuss the specific facts of the case — the types of documents alleged to have been removed from the White House, the process by which they were requested and returned, or the legal arguments made by Trump's attorneys. This information was available at the time of publication from the unsealed search warrant and affidavit, which the article does not cite.

## 6. Verdict

The Snopes/The Conversation explainer "What Is the Espionage Act?" provides an accurate but limited overview of a complex federal statute. The article correctly identifies the key provisions of the Act, distinguishes between the "spying" section (Section 794) and the more commonly applied "unauthorized possession" section (Section 793), clarifies that a violation does not require intent to aid a foreign power, and notes that the Act applies to national defense information beyond classified materials. The article's warning about the impossibility of judging classified-information cases from the outside is well-taken.

However, the article has significant limitations that arise from its format as a republished academic commentary rather than original investigative journalism. It presents an institutional perspective that does not engage with criticisms of the Act's breadth, its lack of a public interest defense, or its chilling effect on whistleblowing and journalism. It omits important cases that would complicate its portrait of the Act's application. It does not analyze the specific circumstances of the Trump case that prompted its publication. And it does not examine the political dynamics that have shaped the Act's use across administrations.

The most important correction this Truth Vault entry offers is not that the article is wrong — it is not — but that it is incomplete. Understanding the Espionage Act requires more than knowing what it says on paper. It requires understanding how it has been applied, to whom, and with what consequences. It requires engaging with the arguments of critics who contend that the Act is overbroad, unconstitutional in some applications, and used to punish those who expose government wrongdoing in the public interest. It requires recognizing that the Act's interpretation and application are contested, and that the outcome of any particular case will depend on facts that the public — and even most legal experts outside the case — cannot fully know.

The Espionage Act is a serious law with serious consequences. The article provides a starting point for understanding it, but it should not be the endpoint. Readers who want to understand the law and its implications should seek out additional sources, including civil liberties organizations, whistleblower advocacy groups, and legal scholars who offer critical perspectives. The truth about the Espionage Act is not merely what the law says, but how it works in practice — and that truth is more complicated, and more contested, than a single academic explainer can capture.
