# Evolution Doesn't Proceed in a Straight Line — So Why Draw It That Way?


## 1. The Claim

Few images in science are as immediately recognizable as the "march of progress" — a sequence of figures walking left to right, beginning with a hunched, knuckle-walking ape and culminating in an upright, striding modern human. The silhouettes proceed through a handful of intermediate forms, each taller and more erect than the last, each apparently discarding some residue of animality in favor of something more refined. The image has been reproduced on T-shirts, coffee mugs, political cartoons, textbook covers, museum displays, and satirical advertisements so many times that it has achieved a cultural reach far exceeding any other scientific illustration in history.

The claim embedded in this image is not stated in words but is communicated by the structure of the visual itself: evolution is a linear, progressive, directed process. Life moves from simple to complex, from primitive to advanced, from less human to more human. The organisms at the left of the image are "earlier" in a meaningful sense — not merely chronologically but qualitatively. They are inferior. The organism at the right is the destination, the endpoint, the goal toward which the entire process has been striving. The trajectory is as straight as an arrow, and it points unmistakably at us.

This visual logic carries a constellation of implicit propositions. First, that evolution has a direction — that there is a preferred axis along which biological change proceeds. Second, that this direction can be measured by a single metric, typically brain size, tool use, or upright posture. Third, that organisms can be ranked on this axis, with some being more evolved than others. Fourth, that the human species occupies the terminal position in this ranking — the summit, the apex, the purpose. Fifth, and most insidiously, that this ranking is inherent in nature rather than imposed by human observers projecting their own values onto a process that is indifferent to them.

These propositions are not incidental to the image. They are the image. The "march of progress" does not depict evolution; it depicts a philosophical position about the structure of nature that predates Darwin by two millennia and that Darwin's theory explicitly contradicts. To understand why this image is wrong — not merely oversimplified but structurally misleading in every dimension — requires examining both the science of evolutionary biology and the deep history of the visual metaphors through which Western culture has imagined the living world.

The claim is not a single falsifiable statement that can be rated "true" or "false." It is a pervasive visual epistemology — a way of knowing embedded in a way of seeing — that has shaped public understanding of biology more profoundly than any textbook passage or museum placard. Snopes published its treatment of this topic as a "News" feature rather than a "Fact Check" precisely because the distortion resists the standard true-or-false framework. The misconception is not a discrete error; it is a lens.


## 2. What's Actually True

Evolution is a branching process, not a linear one. The fundamental mechanism through which new species arise — speciation, or more technically, cladogenesis — involves the splitting of a single population into two or more reproductively isolated populations that then diverge independently. The result is not a chain but a tree: a vast, tangled, continuously forking structure in which every living species occupies the tip of its own branch, separated from every other living species by millions of years of independent evolutionary history.

Charles Darwin understood this with remarkable clarity. The only illustration in his 1859 masterwork *On the Origin of Species* is a branching tree diagram — not a ladder, not a line, not a progression. The diagram shows lineages splitting and diverging over time, with some branches going extinct and others proliferating. There is no preferred direction. There is no axis running from "primitive" to "advanced." The diagram has a vertical axis representing time and a horizontal axis representing morphological divergence, and neither axis corresponds to "progress." Darwin was explicit about this: he rejected the idea that evolution moves toward any goal, and he specifically warned against the temptation to rank organisms as "higher" or "lower."

In a letter to the botanist Joseph Dalton Hooker written in 1854, Darwin confessed his discomfort with the language of "higher" and "lower" in biology, recognizing how deeply the hierarchical framework was embedded in the thinking of his contemporaries. His theory of evolution by natural selection requires no such hierarchy. Natural selection operates on variation within populations, favoring traits that enhance reproductive success in a given environment at a given time. It has no foresight, no memory, no agenda. A mutation that enhances survival in one environment may be lethal in another. Complexity may increase in some lineages and decrease in others — parasites, for example, often evolve toward stunning simplicity, shedding organs and metabolic pathways their free-living ancestors required. Evolution moves in every direction, and in no direction, simultaneously.

All living species are equally evolved. This is perhaps the single most important — and most counterintuitive — consequence of Darwinian theory. A modern bacterium, a modern oak tree, a modern starfish, and a modern human being have all been evolving for exactly the same amount of time since the last universal common ancestor (LUCA), estimated at approximately 3.5 to 4 billion years ago. The bacterium's lineage has undergone no fewer generations of natural selection than the human lineage. It is not "primitive." It is a contemporary organism, exquisitely adapted to its current niche, carrying the accumulated evolutionary innovations of billions of years of descent with modification. To call it "lower" or "simpler" in any evolutionary sense is to impose a metric (structural complexity, nervous system elaboration, resemblance to humans) that evolution itself does not recognize.

The famous "march of progress" image has a specific and traceable origin. It derives from "The Road to Homo Sapiens," a fold-out illustration created by artist Rudolph Zallinger for the 1965 Time-Life book *Early Man*, part of the Life Nature Library series. The original illustration was a panoramic, continuous mural depicting twenty-five million years of primate evolution, showing fifteen different species walking across a landscape from left to right. Zallinger painted it as a continuous scene, not as a sequence of discrete stages. The figures were spaced across the fold-out to represent actual chronological intervals, and many of the depicted species were not ancestral to modern humans at all — they were evolutionary cousins, species that shared common ancestors with the human lineage but went extinct without contributing to the modern human genome.

The transformation of this nuanced paleontological illustration into the iconic "march" was a process of progressive simplification by subsequent reproduction. As the image was cropped, reprinted, and modified for different contexts, the spatial relationships that conveyed chronological scale were lost. The species that represented evolutionary dead ends — lineages that went extinct — were retained in the visual sequence, giving the false impression that each was ancestral to the next. The fifteen original figures were reduced to five or six. The landscape background disappeared. What remained was a stripped-down visual that communicated precisely the opposite of what evolutionary biology actually describes: a directed, linear, teleological ascent from ape to angel.

The distortion is compounded by the specific morphological features the image emphasizes. The leftmost figure is typically depicted as small, dark, hairy, hunched, and long-armed — carrying connotations of animality, primitiveness, and (in many historical variants) racial otherness. The rightmost figure is tall, pale, smooth-skinned, muscular, and upright — carrying connotations of civilization, progress, and European masculinity. The intermediate figures shed their hair, lighten their skin, straighten their posture, and enlarge their skulls in a smooth gradient. This visual grammar encodes not just biological error but cultural prejudice: it maps a debunked racial hierarchy onto a misunderstood evolutionary one, and it has been used explicitly for this purpose in white supremacist iconography.

Modern phylogenetics — the science of reconstructing evolutionary relationships — uses branching diagrams called cladograms and phylogenetic trees, which represent evolutionary relationships as patterns of shared ancestry and divergence. In these diagrams, the relative positioning of taxa along the tips of the tree is arbitrary; what matters is the branching pattern, which indicates which groups share more recent common ancestors. A phylogenetic tree can be rotated at any node without changing the information it conveys. Placing humans at the far right of a cladogram does not make humans "more evolved" than the taxon at the far left — it is a convention of visual layout, not a statement about biological rank.

Even the tree metaphor has limitations that contemporary biologists are increasingly recognizing. Lateral gene transfer — the movement of genetic material between organisms outside the parent-to-offspring channel — is pervasive in bacteria and archaea and has been documented in eukaryotes as well, including through viral integration, endosymbiosis, and hybridization events. The history of life is better described as a reticulate network, a web of genetic exchange layered onto a branching scaffold, than as a simple bifurcating tree. This observation further undermines the linear imagery: not only is evolution not a ladder; in many domains of life, it is not even a cleanly branching tree.

The concept of "convergent evolution" also illustrates the absence of directionality. Similar structures have evolved independently in unrelated lineages dozens of times: eyes have evolved independently more than forty times across the animal kingdom; flight has evolved independently in insects, pterosaurs, birds, and bats; echolocation has evolved independently in bats and cetaceans. These convergences demonstrate that natural selection can produce similar solutions to similar environmental challenges, but they do not demonstrate a direction, a telos, or a target. They demonstrate that the fitness landscape has recurring topographies — not that evolution is climbing a single peak.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of the linear evolution myth — despite more than 160 years of scientific evidence against it — demands explanation not in terms of ignorance but in terms of cognitive architecture, cultural inheritance, and the sociology of visual communication.

The deepest root is cognitive. Human beings are intuitive teleologists. Developmental psychology research, particularly the work of Deborah Kelemen at Boston University, has demonstrated that children across cultures spontaneously assign purpose to natural objects and processes. Rocks are "for" animals to scratch on. Clouds are "for" raining. Rivers exist "so that" boats can travel on them. This teleological bias extends into adulthood and is suppressed — not eliminated — by scientific education. When asked to evaluate statements quickly under time pressure, even professional scientists with PhDs in biology are more likely to endorse teleological explanations than they are under conditions that allow careful deliberation. The "march of progress" resonates because it aligns with this default cognitive setting: evolution proceeds in a line because it is going somewhere, and it is going somewhere because natural processes have purposes.

The cultural root is equally deep. The *scala naturae* — the "great chain of being" — is a philosophical framework originating with Plato and Aristotle that organized all of nature into a hierarchy from inanimate matter at the bottom through plants, animals, humans, angels, and ultimately God at the top. This framework dominated Western intellectual culture for nearly two millennia. It was codified by medieval Christian theologians, elaborated by Renaissance natural philosophers, and transmitted through educational institutions well into the nineteenth century. The scala naturae is not a naive folk theory; it is a sophisticated philosophical tradition that was endorsed by many of the most brilliant minds in Western intellectual history, from Thomas Aquinas to Gottfried Leibniz.

The scala naturae holds three premises that are directly relevant to the evolution misconception. First, nature is organized hierarchically — not randomly. Second, the hierarchy progresses from simple to perfect and from primitive to modern, along a single axis. Third, the hierarchy has no intermediate stages: each level is a discrete, self-contained degree of perfection. All three premises are contradicted by Darwinian evolution, but all three continue to shape popular intuitions about the natural world. The "march of progress" is, in effect, a Darwinian-looking visualization of an Aristotelian philosophy.

Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, the Jesuit paleontologist and philosopher, provided a twentieth-century bridge between the scala naturae and evolutionary science. In the 1950s and 1960s, his works — particularly *The Phenomenon of Man* (1955) — proposed that evolution, while branching, nonetheless has a direction: it moves toward increasing complexity, increasing consciousness, and ultimately toward an "Omega Point" of spiritual convergence with the divine. Teilhard's ideas were enormously popular in educated religious circles and gave intellectual cover to the intuition that evolution has a direction and that humans are its apotheosis. His framework was explicitly rejected by most evolutionary biologists — the geneticist Peter Medawar famously dismissed *The Phenomenon of Man* as an exercise in "tipsy, euphoric prose-poetry" — but its cultural influence persists in the widespread sense that evolution is "going somewhere."

The visual dimension is crucial. The "march of progress" succeeds as a meme — in the original Richard Dawkins sense of a cultural replicator — because it is simple, memorable, immediately legible, and infinitely adaptable. It can be parodied (ape to human to desk worker), politicized (ape to human to politician), commercialized (ape to human to brand mascot), and inverted (human devolving back to ape) without losing its essential recognizability. Its visual grammar is so powerful that it overwrites textual corrections: a textbook that includes the "march of progress" on its cover and spends an entire chapter explaining why evolution is not linear will leave many students remembering the cover more vividly than the chapter.

Educational practices have compounded the problem. Museum displays that arrange specimens from "primitive" to "advanced" along a wall reinforce the linear narrative even when explanatory text acknowledges the branching pattern. Textbooks that present evolutionary history as a timeline moving from bacteria to fish to amphibians to reptiles to mammals to primates to humans — even with caveats about branching — activate the chain-of-being schema. Teachers who describe organisms as "more evolved" or "less evolved" (a phrase that is meaningless under Darwinian theory) perpetuate the misconception linguistically even as they may understand the branching model intellectually.

The intelligent design movement has actively exploited the linear misconception. By encouraging the belief that evolution is a directed process aimed at producing complexity, ID proponents set up a rhetorical trap: if evolution is directed, it must have a director. The "march of progress" thus becomes an argument for divine teleology smuggled into science education under the guise of a visual aid. The irony is profound: an image that supposedly illustrates Darwin's theory actually illustrates the philosophical framework that Darwin's theory was designed to replace.

Confirmation bias plays a role as well. Because we are the most cognitively complex organisms we know, and because we are the ones doing the classifying, we tend to notice — and valorize — the evolutionary trajectories that produced complexity. We count the "big transitions" (prokaryote to eukaryote, unicellular to multicellular, aquatic to terrestrial, ectotherm to endotherm) and arrange them in a progression that happens to end with us. But this is cherry-picking: for every lineage that increased in complexity over evolutionary time, there are thousands that decreased in complexity, remained stable, or changed in dimensions we do not consider "progressive." The evolutionary "bush" has no preferred direction; we prune it into a "trunk" by ignoring the branches we find uninteresting.


## 4. Verdict

**The "march of progress" is not a simplification of evolutionary theory. It is a contradiction of evolutionary theory.** It encodes a pre-Darwinian philosophy — the scala naturae, the great chain of being — in a Darwinian-looking visual wrapper. Every structural feature of the image communicates a falsehood: the linearity implies direction where none exists; the left-to-right progression implies improvement along a single axis where natural selection optimizes across infinitely many; the termination at a modern human implies that evolution has a goal where it has only consequences; and the absence of branching implies that species transform into one another sequentially where they actually diverge from shared ancestors.

The severity of the distortion is not merely academic. The linear evolution myth feeds directly into hierarchical thinking about human groups, since the intermediate figures in the "march" have historically been drawn to resemble racial caricatures. It supports creationist and intelligent design arguments by implying teleology. It undermines public understanding of critical biological concepts — speciation, adaptation, extinction — by replacing them with a narrative of progress that resembles a fairy tale more than a scientific theory. And it creates a false sense of inevitability that obscures one of evolution's most important lessons: that the existence of any particular species, including our own, is radically contingent. Replay the tape of life from any starting point, and the outcome would be unrecognizably different.

This is one of those cases where a fact-check verdict scale — True, False, Mostly True — is inadequate to the epistemological stakes. The "march of progress" is not a discrete factual claim that can be stamped with a rating. It is a visual paradigm, a way of seeing that corrupts understanding at a level deeper than individual propositions. To correct it requires not a rebuttal but a replacement: a shift from the ladder to the tree, from the chain to the web, from the march to the bush. That replacement has been available since 1859. Its adoption has been — one might say — a long, nonlinear, branching process.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The "march of progress" case illuminates a broader phenomenon in science communication: the power of illustrations to override text. Research in cognitive psychology has demonstrated that when visual and verbal information conflict, the visual information tends to dominate — a phenomenon sometimes called the "pictorial superiority effect." In the context of evolution education, this means that a branching tree diagram buried on page 47 of a textbook will lose the battle for a student's attention against a linear "march" image on the cover, in the margins, on the classroom poster, or embedded in a viral meme. The visual communicates faster, sticks longer, and resists correction more effectively than any paragraph of carefully qualified prose.

This has practical implications for science communication strategy. Evolutionary biologists and science educators have increasingly recognized that replacing the "march" requires not just better text explanations but better images — visual alternatives that are as simple, memorable, and reproducible as the "march" itself. Phylogenetic trees, while accurate, are not intuitive to untrained readers; their branching topology requires understanding of conventions (that the branching pattern, not the tip order, carries the information) that are rarely taught. Some educators have experimented with "bush" diagrams, network visualizations, and interactive digital tools that allow users to explore evolutionary relationships dynamically rather than reading them from a static image.

The political dimensions of the "march" deserve explicit attention. The image has historically been used to justify racial hierarchy by depicting intermediate figures as dark-skinned, stooped, with prominent brow ridges and prognathous faces — visual markers associated with racial stereotypes. This is not a misuse of an innocent image; it is a direct extension of the image's internal logic. If evolution is a progression from "primitive" to "advanced" along a single axis, then any human group that appears "closer" to the primitive end of the axis can be positioned as less evolved. This reasoning is biologically illiterate — all living human populations have been evolving for exactly the same amount of time — but the visual grammar of the "march" makes it intuitively compelling. The image has appeared in explicit white supremacist propaganda, and its echoes persist in subtler forms of cultural bias. Dismantling the linear evolution myth is therefore not only a matter of scientific accuracy but of social justice.

The Zallinger illustration itself was a work of genuine artistry that has been poorly served by its cultural afterlife. Zallinger was a meticulous paleontological illustrator who won the Pulitzer Prize for his "Age of Reptiles" mural at the Yale Peabody Museum. His "Road to Homo Sapiens" was a carefully researched panorama that included species he knew to be evolutionary cousins rather than ancestors, rendered with attention to anatomical detail that reflected the best scientific understanding of its era. The transformation of this panorama into the five-figure "march" was not Zallinger's doing; it was the work of countless subsequent reductions and reproductions that stripped away context, nuance, and scientific accuracy in favor of visual simplicity. The image is a case study in how a complex visual can be degraded into a simple one that communicates the opposite of the original meaning.

The evolution case also connects to larger questions about how societies handle the gap between scientific understanding and public intuition. Evolutionary biology is not unique in facing this challenge — quantum mechanics, climate science, epidemiology, and economics all contend with persistent public misconceptions rooted in cognitive biases and cultural inertia. But the evolution case is distinctive in the degree to which a single image has crystallized the misconception and given it a viral, self-replicating quality. The "march of progress" is perhaps the most successful piece of anti-science propaganda in history — all the more effective because it was never designed as propaganda at all.

The tree of life — Darwin's actual metaphor — carries its own philosophical implications, ones that are more accurate and more humbling than the ladder. In the tree, there is no summit. There is no apex species. There is only an immense, ramifying structure of descent and divergence, each branch tip representing a contemporary solution to the problem of survival, each solution as provisional and as ancient as any other. Humanity is one twig on one branch of one major limb of an enormous tree — a twig of extraordinary cognitive complexity, certainly, but a twig whose existence was neither foreordained by the tree's structure nor necessary for the tree's continued growth. The tree will continue to branch long after our twig has flowered, fruited, and fallen.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Traditional fact-checking is poorly equipped to address the "march of progress" misconception because the distortion operates at the level of visual epistemology rather than propositional truth. A standard fact-check evaluates a discrete claim — "Did X say Y?" "Is statistic Z accurate?" — and assigns a rating on a truth scale. The "march of progress" is not a claim in this sense. It is a mode of representation, a visual grammar, a cognitive frame. It communicates its distortion iconically rather than linguistically, and it does so through cultural omnipresence rather than through any single instance that could be isolated and debunked.

Snopes, recognizing this challenge, published its treatment of the topic as a "News" feature rather than a "Fact Check." This was an appropriate editorial choice: the piece, originally from The Conversation, was structured as an explanatory essay rather than a claim-and-verdict analysis. The piece identified the historical roots of the misconception (the scala naturae, Sambourne's 1882 cartoon, the persistence of linear imagery), described the correct scientific model (branching, non-directional, non-hierarchical), and called for better visual communication. What it could not do — because the format does not support it — was assign a truth value to the image itself or track the image's reproduction across media contexts.

This reveals a genuine gap in the fact-checking toolkit. Fact-checkers have developed robust methods for evaluating textual claims, tracing source chains, and verifying statistical assertions. They have fewer tools for evaluating visual claims — images that communicate propositions without stating them, that shape understanding through repetition rather than argument, and that resist correction because they operate below the threshold of conscious propositional reasoning. The "march of progress" is an extreme case, but the category is broad: misleading maps, deceptive data visualizations, selectively cropped photographs, and digitally altered images all communicate falsehoods visually in ways that resist text-based correction.

The challenge for fact-checkers is further complicated by the image's memetic fertility. Because the "march of progress" is endlessly parodied and adapted — from the evolution of Homer Simpson to the devolution of the desk worker — any attempt to "debunk" the original image is immediately overwhelmed by the proliferation of new variants. Each parody reinforces the underlying linear schema even when the parody's intent is humorous rather than educational. The rate of reproduction far exceeds the rate of correction.

Some promising approaches have emerged. The "Understanding Evolution" project at the University of California, Berkeley, has developed online resources that explicitly contrast the linear myth with the branching reality, using interactive tree-building tools that allow users to construct phylogenies themselves. The "Tree of Life" project has created visual resources that present evolutionary relationships in ways that do not privilege any particular taxon. And some textbook publishers have begun to phase out "march of progress" imagery in favor of branching diagrams, though this process is uneven and ongoing.

The evolution case offers a broader lesson for the fact-checking community: some of the most consequential misinformation is not propositional but paradigmatic. It does not take the form of false statements that can be individually checked and corrected; it takes the form of false frameworks that organize understanding in ways that make entire categories of false statements feel intuitive and entire categories of true statements feel counterintuitive. Addressing this kind of misinformation requires not just debunking individual claims but replacing the cognitive infrastructure that generates them — a task that is less journalism than education, less episodic than systematic, and less about any single article than about the long, slow, branching work of cultural change.
