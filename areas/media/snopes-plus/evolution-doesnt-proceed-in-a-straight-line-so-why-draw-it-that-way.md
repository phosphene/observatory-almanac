# Evolution Doesn't Proceed in a Straight Line, So Why Draw It That Way?

## 1. The Claim

Open any textbook published before the 1990s, scan a museum's natural history wing, or scroll through editorial cartoons from any decade, and you will almost certainly encounter a version of the same image: a row of figures arranged left to right, each one standing slightly more upright than the last, each one less hairy, more angular, more recognisably *us*. The sequence begins with a stooped, knuckle-dragging ancestor and ends with a striding, erect *Homo sapiens*. Sometimes there are four figures. Sometimes fifteen. The march always moves in one direction.

The implicit argument carried by this image is that evolution is a ladder and that humans stand at the top of it. Each species along the sequence is understood as a precursor to the next — a transitional state, necessarily imperfect, destined to be superseded. The logic feels intuitive: organisms do change over time; humans do differ from chimpanzees; surely the process that linked them was a progressive improvement, a series of upgrades toward the present model.

This visual argument is wrong in almost every particular. Evolution does not proceed along a predetermined path. There is no destination. No species is "more evolved" than another in any meaningful sense. The ancestors of modern humans did not line up like relay runners, passing the baton forward in an orderly chain. The popular iconography of evolution misrepresents the actual mechanism of natural selection so thoroughly that it generates specific, falsifiable errors in scientific reasoning — errors that persist in adults who have otherwise received solid scientific education.

Yet the image endures, reproduced in advertising, political satire, memes, and product logos the world over. Understanding why requires tracing a history that stretches back centuries before Darwin and a visual culture that outlasted the scientific revolution that should have ended it.

## 2. What's Actually True

Evolutionary biology is clear on the structure of the process. Life diversifies through branching — lineages split, accumulate differences independently, and radiate into distinct forms. The relationship between any two modern species is not ancestor-to-descendant but rather *cousins* sharing a common ancestor who is, in most cases, long extinct. Humans and chimpanzees did not evolve from each other; they share a common ancestor that no longer exists as a distinct species.

Darwin himself understood this and depicted it. The only diagram in the first edition of *On the Origin of Species* (1859) is a branching tree diagram — lines diverging upward from common trunk points, each divergence representing speciation, each terminal branch a contemporary species. There is no privileged direction in Darwin's illustration, no apex. Every branch tip is equally "finished" and equally subject to ongoing selection.

Modern phylogenetics has deepened this picture dramatically. Molecular clock analyses, comparative genomics, and the fossil record all confirm that the tree of life is not a neat bifurcating structure but a vast, tangled profusion — a bush, as the preferred metaphor goes, with thousands of terminal twigs at any given moment. Horizontal gene transfer among prokaryotes renders the early branches of the tree more like a web than a tree at all. Multiple hominin species coexisted for stretches of hundreds of thousands of years: *Homo sapiens* walked the earth at the same time as *Homo neanderthalensis*, *Homo floresiensis*, *Homo luzonensis*, and the Denisovans, among others. The notion of a single line of succession is flatly contradicted by the evidence.

Two specific scientific principles make the ladder model particularly misleading. First, there is no teleology in natural selection. Selection operates purely on present fitness — the reproductive success of an organism in its current environment. What seems like progress from our vantage point is only improvement relative to a particular environmental context at a particular time. A bacterium living in the anaerobic depths of a sulfurous hot spring is exquisitely well-adapted to its niche; it is not "lower" than a human any more than a claw hammer is "lower" than a smartphone. They solve different problems.

Second, all species alive today have been evolving for exactly the same length of time. The common ancestor of all modern eukaryotes lived roughly two billion years ago. From that ancestor, every lineage — human, flatworm, oak tree, tardigrade — has been accumulating adaptive changes continuously for two billion years. Modern bacteria are not primitive relics; they are superbly optimised organisms that have been fine-tuned by selection for billions of years longer than *Homo sapiens* has existed. To call them "lower life forms" is not science; it is anthropocentric projection.

The hominin fossil record, which does suggest genuine directionality in the features that lead toward modern humans, is often misread as confirmation of the ladder model. In fact the record shows repeated adaptive radiations, dead ends, reversals, and parallel evolution. *Australopithecus afarensis*, *Paranthropus boisei*, *Homo habilis*, and *Homo erectus* were not stages in a single lineage; they were distinct, overlapping species pursuing different adaptive strategies in different environments. Many of the lineages shown in the classic march-of-progress sequence did not lead to modern humans at all and were evolutionary dead ends.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The ladder metaphor is not a modern error. It is an ancient inheritance, refracted through centuries of cultural transmission, that proved too visually and conceptually satisfying to abandon even when science rendered it obsolete.

The original framework is the *scala naturae* — the Great Chain of Being — a philosophical scheme originating with Plato and Aristotle and systematised through the Middle Ages by theologians who mapped a divine hierarchy from inanimate matter through plants, animals, and humans to angels and God. Within this framework, every being occupied a fixed, necessary position in a continuous hierarchy of perfection. The chain was static by design: beings did not move up or down; the hierarchy was eternal and God-given.

When evolutionary thought emerged during the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, it did not displace the hierarchical scaffolding; it animated it. Jean-Baptiste Lamarck's early evolutionary theory, articulated in his *Philosophie Zoologique* (1809), described a process of organisms "striving" toward greater perfection — literally climbing a ladder through inherited effort over generations. Lamarck's mechanism was wrong (acquired traits do not pass to offspring), but his directional framework drew on the same deep intuition as the *scala naturae* and made evolutionary thinking legible to educated audiences steeped in hierarchical natural philosophy.

Even after Darwin replaced Lamarck's mechanism with natural selection, the cultural apparatus of directional progress survived because it solved a communication problem. Darwin's branching tree is accurate but cognitively demanding. It requires that viewers abandon the intuition of progress, accept that cousins rather than ancestors link living species, and relinquish the comfortable position of humans at the apex of the biological order. The ladder offered a simpler story: change happens, it goes somewhere, and it arrives here.

The watershed moment for popular imagery came in 1965, when illustrator Rudolph Zallinger produced "The Road to Homo Sapiens" for the Time-Life book *Early Man* in the Life Nature Library series. The illustration depicted fifteen hominid figures in left-to-right sequence, beginning with a Miocene ape (*Pliopithecus*) and culminating in *Homo sapiens*. It became the most reproduced image in the history of science communication, instantly nicknamed the "March of Progress."

Zallinger's illustration had scientific intentions and drew on the best palaeontological knowledge of 1965. The accompanying text in *Early Man* was careful to note that the sequence was interpretive and schematic rather than literal. But the visual argument overwhelmed the verbal caveats. Stripped of caption and context — which it was, almost immediately, in the thousands of derivative reproductions — the march seemed to say: this is how it happened, this is the direction, and this is the destination.

Palaeontologist Stephen Jay Gould spent portions of his career dismantling the march metaphor, arguing in his 1989 book *Wonderful Life* and in multiple essays that the ladder model encodes a destructive bias — one that positions humans as the inevitable and supreme product of evolution rather than as one improbable twig on an unimaginably complex bush. Gould pointed out that the dominant feature of the history of life is the enormous breadth of bacterial diversity, by comparison with which multicellular life is a recent and minor elaboration.

The persistence of the ladder model also draws on cognitive biases that are otherwise useful. Teleological thinking — perceiving purpose and direction in natural processes — is deeply embedded in human cognition and appears to emerge early in child development. Intuitive biology tends to essentialism: people prefer to think of species as having fixed natures rather than as statistical distributions of varying traits, and prefer to think of change as directed toward a goal rather than as the undirected accumulation of locally adaptive variations. The march-of-progress image flatters these intuitions, while the bushy tree of life demands their suppression.

There is also a cultural dimension that Gould and later scholars have emphasised. Linear evolutionary diagrams frequently encode racial hierarchies, with figures moving from darker to lighter skin tones, from "primitive" to "civilised" facial features. This Eurocentric biasing is not accidental; it reflects the social Darwinist frameworks that were entangled with evolutionary biology during its formative decades and that provided ideological legitimacy for colonial hierarchies. The ladder model made racism feel scientific. Even contemporary parody versions of the march — which are among the most common meme formats on the internet — often unconsciously reproduce this progressive-lightening convention.

## 4. Verdict

**ERRONEOUS**: The visual depiction of evolution as a straight-line march from primitive to advanced is scientifically wrong. It misrepresents the mechanism of natural selection, which has no direction or goal; the structure of evolutionary history, which branches rather than climbs; and the meaning of "modern," which applies equally to every living species. The ladder image traces to pre-Darwinian natural philosophy and was amplified by a 1965 illustration that became culturally dominant despite explicit authorial caveats. Darwin's own preferred visualisation was a branching tree, not a ladder, and contemporary evolutionary biology describes life as a bush so complex that linear sequence diagrams cannot represent it without systematic distortion.

The misconception is not merely an aesthetic simplification. It generates specific scientific errors: the belief that some species are "more evolved" than others; the question "if humans evolved from apes, why are there still apes?"; the expectation that evolutionary change aims toward human-like features; and the underestimation of evolutionary success in "simple" organisms. All of these errors become easier to correct once the underlying visual grammar is replaced with accurate metaphors.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The evolution iconography problem sits within a broader crisis in science communication: the tension between accessibility and accuracy. The march-of-progress image is not an honest simplification that omits certain details while preserving the core truth; it is a fundamental misrepresentation that creates false beliefs. There is a significant difference between a simplified map that omits some roads while correctly relaying the direction of travel, and a map that shows the destination in the wrong place.

Modern evolutionary biologists and science communicators have worked to develop alternative visualisations that are both accurate and accessible. Phylogenetic tree diagrams — showing species as leaves on a branching diagram with the root representing common ancestry — are now standard in biology education and increasingly appear in museums and popular science publications. The Universal Tree of Life, depicting all known life from bacteria to mammals on a single branching diagram, has been published in multiple formats for general audiences and represents a dramatic visual departure from the march metaphor.

The internet has compounded both the problem and the potential solution. March-of-progress memes reproduce the ladder metaphor billions of times daily in contexts that have nothing to do with evolution — marketing campaigns, political satire, technology progressivism narratives. But online science communication has also produced richly interactive phylogenetic tools like OneZoom, which allows users to zoom into any branch of the tree of life, experiencing its density and complexity directly, which no static linear image can convey.

Educational research suggests that exposure to the march-of-progress image in isolation, without explicit correction, strengthens teleological misconceptions about evolution that persist even after formal education. Students who can accurately restate evolutionary mechanisms on examinations nevertheless often revert to ladder thinking when asked to reason about novel evolutionary scenarios. The misconception is not corrected by acquiring correct propositions; it requires replacing the underlying visual schema.

There is a documented downstream consequence: the ladder model makes evolutionary biology more susceptible to creationist misrepresentation. "If evolution is a direction, why isn't it still happening?" and "Why are there still monkeys?" are rhetorical questions that draw their apparent force from the ladder model; they are nonsensical questions under the branching tree model, where the ancestral population that gave rise to the human lineage split rather than transformed wholesale, leaving descendants in both branches. The ladder image is not merely a harmless cognitive shorthand; it leaves people vulnerable to specific bad-faith arguments.

Beyond evolution, the case illustrates a general principle: when simplified visual representations become sufficiently widespread and culturally entrenched, they develop their own epistemic gravity. The image becomes the primary evidence that the claim is true, because the image is so familiar. Seeing a thousand versions of the march tells the naive viewer that evolution must proceed linearly, because why would that image recur so insistently if it were wrong? Correcting the misconception requires not just providing accurate information but disrupting a visual habit that has been reinforced by exposure to an image that is, itself, ubiquitous precisely because it tells a satisfying story.

The evolution iconography case also has applications in thinking about how representations in other scientific domains distort public understanding — how the "atom as solar system" model generates misconceptions about quantum behaviour, how maps with traditional Mercator projection distort relative size perceptions of continents, how "chemical formulas in hexagons" suggest geometric molecular structure in cases where no such structure exists. Visual scientific literacy — the ability to critically evaluate the conventions built into scientific images — is an underappreciated component of scientific understanding.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published a substantial explainer on this topic in September 2019 under the title "Evolution Doesn't Proceed in a Straight Line – So Why Draw It That Way?" — but as a news and educational piece rather than a formal fact-check. The article was republished from *The Conversation*, authored by biodiversity scholars Quentin Wheeler, Antonio G. Valdecasas, and Cristina Cánovas, and presented a detailed critique of the march-of-progress imagery grounded in both evolutionary biology and the history of science.

The limitation of this approach is that it treats the linear-progress misconception as an intellectual curiosity to be explained rather than a specific false belief to be rated. Snopes's formal fact-check system, which assigns verdicts like "False," "Mostly True," or "Misleading," is better suited to discrete empirical claims than to persistent visual metaphors. The nuanced reality — that the linear image is not a deliberate lie but a historically rooted and cognitively appealing misrepresentation of a complex truth — doesn't map cleanly onto standard fact-check formats.

Science-specific fact-checking and education organisations have been more assertive in addressing the misconception. The National Center for Science Education, Understanding Evolution (a collaboration between the University of California Museum of Paleontology and the National Science Foundation), and similar organisations have dedicated explainer content to the ladder problem, with Understanding Evolution specifically describing the "march of progress" image as a misconception that "hampers both the teaching and public understanding of evolution."

Museum education has increasingly grappled with the problem as well. The American Museum of Natural History's Hall of Human Origins, redesigned in the early 2000s, explicitly presents human evolution as a branching network of species rather than a single lineage, and signage explains why earlier depictions were misleading. This represents a significant shift from earlier natural history museum practice, which often reproduced linear hominid sequences in dioramas and display cases.

The primary gap in fact-checking coverage of this claim is that the misconception is embedded in images rather than sentences, and fact-checking ecosystems are built primarily around textual claims. A meme presenting the march-of-progress image without any accompanying text may spread a false belief about evolution while containing no false statement that can be rated. The visual misinformation problem requires visual correction tools — resources that let people encounter images alongside immediate debunking — that the fact-checking industry is only beginning to develop.
