# The "March of Progress" Image Misrepresents How Evolution Actually Works


## 1. The Claim

There is no single "claim" in the conventional fact-checking sense. Instead, this entry addresses an image — arguably the single most influential scientific illustration of the twentieth century — and the web of misconceptions it has generated and sustained.

The image is formally known as "The Road to *Homo Sapiens*," painted by artist Rudolph Zallinger for the Time-Life book *Early Man* (1965). It depicts a sequence of figures walking in a line from left to right: beginning with a small, hunched, ape-like primate on the far left, progressing through increasingly upright and human-like intermediaries, and culminating in a striding modern *Homo sapiens* on the far right. The figures are arranged in a clear directional progression: earlier forms are smaller, darker, hairier, and more stooped; later forms are taller, lighter, less hairy, and more erect.

The image has been reproduced, parodied, and adapted countless times in textbooks, advertisements, magazine covers, T-shirts, bumper stickers, and internet memes. Its cultural penetration is nearly total: ask anyone to "draw evolution" and they will almost certainly reproduce some version of the March of Progress, even people who could not name a single hominin species.

The implied claim embedded in the image is threefold: (1) evolution is a linear, directional process leading from "lower" to "higher" forms; (2) complexity and sophistication increase monotonically over evolutionary time; and (3) modern humans represent the endpoint, pinnacle, or goal of this process. All three implications are wrong, and their persistence in popular understanding represents one of the most consequential failures of science communication in modern history.

The Snopes article under examination is not a traditional fact-check but a republished article from *The Conversation* by evolutionary biologists Jordi Paps and Cristina Guijarro-Clarke, presenting genomic research that directly contradicts the linear progress model. Their 2020 study, published in *Nature Ecology and Evolution*, showed that major animal lineages — including the one containing humans — are characterized by massive gene losses, not the continuous complexity gains that the March of Progress imagery implies.


## 2. What's Actually True

The March of Progress image, and the linear progress model it embodies, gets evolution fundamentally wrong. Understanding why requires examining what evolutionary biology actually describes, what the genomic evidence shows, and why complexity is a far more complicated concept than a left-to-right sequence of increasingly upright primates suggests.

### Evolution Is a Bush, Not a Ladder

The most fundamental error in the March of Progress is its representation of evolution as a linear sequence: species A gives rise to species B, which gives rise to species C, each one an "improvement" over its predecessor. This is a ladder model of evolution (technically called an "orthogenetic" model), and it was scientifically obsolete by the time Zallinger painted his famous illustration in 1965.

The actual topology of evolutionary history is a densely branching tree — or, more accurately, a bush. Species do not transform into other species in a single line of descent. Instead, ancestral populations split into multiple descendant populations, each adapting to different environmental pressures. Most of these branches go extinct. Some persist for millions of years without dramatic change. A few give rise to further branching events. The resulting pattern looks less like a march and more like a thicket.

Human evolution exemplifies this branching pattern. The hominin family tree includes at least twenty recognized species spanning roughly seven million years, and new species continue to be identified as the fossil record expands. At multiple points in the past two million years, several hominin species coexisted simultaneously: *Homo erectus*, *Homo heidelbergensis*, *Homo neanderthalensis*, *Homo floresiensis* (the "hobbit"), *Homo naledi*, the Denisovans, and *Homo sapiens* all overlapped in time. The March of Progress image erases this diversity entirely, presenting a single lineage of sequential forms rather than the tangled web of coexisting, interbreeding, and independently evolving species that the evidence actually reveals.

The discovery of *Homo naledi* in 2015 — a small-brained, primitive-bodied hominin that survived until as recently as 236,000 years ago, coexisting with large-brained modern humans — is particularly devastating to the linear progress model. If evolution were a march from small-brained to large-brained, *H. naledi* should not exist. Its survival demonstrates that "primitive" and "advanced" forms can coexist indefinitely, each adapted to different ecological niches.

### Complexity Is Not Destiny

The March of Progress image implies that evolution's direction is toward greater complexity — bigger brains, upright posture, tool use, language. But this is a profound selection bias. We are looking at the lineage that produced us and retroactively interpreting its features as the "goal" of evolution. This is like examining the winning lottery numbers and concluding that the lottery was designed to produce that specific combination.

Evolutionary biologist Stephen Jay Gould devoted much of his career to combating this misconception. In his book *Full House* (1996), Gould proposed the "drunkard's walk" model of evolution. Imagine a drunk leaving a bar on a train platform. The bar represents the wall of minimum complexity (life cannot be simpler than the simplest self-replicating molecule). The train tracks represent the maximum complexity that happens to have been achieved so far. The drunk staggers randomly back and forth. Sometimes he moves toward the tracks (increasing complexity); sometimes he moves back toward the bar (decreasing complexity). Neither direction is preferred. But because the wall of minimum complexity is an absorbing boundary — you can't go simpler than the simplest — the statistical tendency over time is for the maximum observed complexity to increase, even though the average complexity may not change much.

This model explains why bacteria — among the oldest and simplest life forms — remain the most abundant and successful organisms on Earth, measured by biomass, species diversity, metabolic versatility, or range of habitable environments. If evolution were a march of progress, bacteria should have been superseded long ago. Instead, they thrive in every environment on Earth, including many where complex organisms cannot survive. Complexity is one survival strategy. Simplicity is another. Evolution has no preference between them.

### The Genomic Evidence: Gene Loss as Innovation

The 2020 study by Paps, Guijarro-Clarke, and Holland, published in *Nature Ecology and Evolution*, provided the most comprehensive genomic support for Gould's anti-progressionist argument to date. By comparing the complete genomes of over 100 organisms (predominantly animals), the researchers traced the history of gene gain and gene loss across the animal tree of life.

Their findings were striking:

**The origin of major animal lineages was associated with massive gene losses, not gains.** The deuterostome lineage (which includes all vertebrates, including humans) and the ecdysozoan lineage (which includes insects and crustaceans) — the two groups traditionally considered the most "complex" — showed the largest number of gene losses in the study. This is the genomic equivalent of discovering that the "most advanced" civilizations achieved their status not by accumulating more technology but by discarding unnecessary tools.

**Gene loss was not a sign of degeneration.** The lost genes were not randomly distributed; they were genes whose functions were no longer needed or whose roles had been taken over by other genetic mechanisms. Gene loss is a form of genomic streamlining — an evolutionary strategy as valid as gene innovation.

**The lophotrochozoan lineage** (which includes mollusks, annelid worms, and other "simpler" animals) showed a roughly equal balance of gene gains and losses — a pattern more consistent with the random walk model than with directional progress.

**The initial emergence of the animal kingdom** was indeed associated with a large burst of genetic innovation — new genes arising at the base of the animal tree. But after this initial expansion, evolutionary trajectories diverged: some lineages gained complexity through new genes, others reduced complexity through gene loss, and neither strategy was inherently "better" than the other.

The researchers explicitly framed their findings in terms of Gould's drunkard's walk: "Life emerged by coming out of the pub and making a large leap in complexity. But after the initial enthusiasm, some lineages stumbled closer to the pub by losing genes, while other lineages drifted towards the track by gaining genes."

### Parasites: Evolution in Reverse

If the March of Progress were accurate, parasitism should be impossible. Parasites are organisms that have evolved from free-living ancestors by losing complexity — shedding appendages, nervous systems, digestive tracts, and even basic metabolic pathways, sometimes reducing themselves to little more than reproductive organs attached to a host. The tapeworm, for instance, has no digestive system at all, absorbing pre-digested nutrients directly through its body wall. Sacculina, a parasitic barnacle, begins life as a free-swimming larva recognizable as a crustacean and then invades a crab, losing virtually all recognizable body structures and becoming a network of root-like tendrils permeating its host's body.

These are not obscure exceptions. Parasitism is one of the most successful ecological strategies in the history of life. By some estimates, parasitic species outnumber free-living species on Earth. Their evolutionary success through simplification directly contradicts the idea that evolution rewards increasing complexity.

### The Racial Subtexts

The March of Progress image carries an additional layer of distortion that is rarely discussed in fact-checking contexts but is essential to an honest epistemological audit. Zallinger's original illustration depicts a progression from dark-skinned, ape-like figures to a light-skinned modern human. The visual grammar is unmistakable: darkness, hairiness, and non-European features are associated with "primitive" stages; whiteness, hairlessness, and European features represent the "progressive" endpoint.

This visual encoding maps directly onto nineteenth-century racial hierarchies that ranked human populations on a scale from "primitive" to "advanced," with Europeans invariably placed at the top. The March of Progress image did not invent these hierarchies, but it gave them a pseudo-scientific visual justification that persists in popular imagination. The image says, without words: evolution's goal was to produce people who look like white Europeans. Everyone else is somewhere further back on the march.

Modern anthropology categorically rejects this framing. All living human populations are equally evolved. The genetic differences between human populations are trivially small compared to within-population variation, and no population is "more evolved" or "closer to our ancestors" than any other. The phenotypic diversity among modern humans reflects adaptation to different environments (skin pigmentation varies with UV exposure, for instance), not differential progress along an evolutionary ladder.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The March of Progress image persists because it satisfies multiple deep cognitive and cultural needs simultaneously, making it resistant to correction even among people who intellectually understand that evolution is not linear.

### Narrative Brain

Human cognition is fundamentally narrative. We understand the world through stories — sequences of events with beginnings, middles, and ends, protagonists and progressions, conflicts and resolutions. The March of Progress fits this cognitive template perfectly: it tells a story with a clear beginning (small, primitive ape), a clear progression (increasingly human intermediaries), and a satisfying endpoint (us). It is evolution compressed into a three-act structure.

The branching tree model of evolution, by contrast, has no protagonist, no direction, no climax, and no resolution. It is a story in which every character is a side character, every plotline is a dead end, and the narrative can end at literally any point without reaching anything recognizable as a conclusion. This is faithful to reality but terrible storytelling, and human brains consistently choose good stories over accurate models when the two conflict.

### Teleological Intuition

Cognitive research in developmental psychology has consistently shown that children spontaneously adopt teleological thinking — the attribution of purpose to natural phenomena. "Rocks are pointy so animals can scratch themselves." "Rain happens so flowers can grow." This intuitive teleology diminishes with education but never fully disappears. The March of Progress image resonates with this deep-seated intuition: evolution has a purpose (producing humans), and every stage in the sequence is moving toward that purpose.

Overriding this intuition requires active cognitive effort, which is why even well-educated people who "know" evolution is non-directional still instinctively think of it as a march of progress. The image provides a visual anchor for the teleological intuition, making it harder to override rather than easier.

### The Textbook Feedback Loop

The March of Progress image has appeared in biology textbooks for over fifty years. Many of these textbooks include caveats explaining that evolution is not actually linear, but the image dominates the visual field while the caveat occupies a footnote. Since visual information is processed faster, retained longer, and recalled more readily than textual information, students walk away with the image burned into their visual memory and the caveat forgotten.

This creates a self-reinforcing cycle: students who learn from the image grow up to write textbooks that include the image (because it is familiar and immediately recognizable), producing a new generation of students who absorb the misconception, who grow up to perpetuate it further. The image has become what meme theorists would call a "cultural replicator" — a unit of information that reproduces itself through the educational system without reference to its accuracy.

### Anthropocentrism

Humans are, unsurprisingly, most interested in the lineage that produced humans. The March of Progress image puts us at the endpoint, which feels natural because we are the ones doing the looking. But this is a perspectival illusion analogous to standing at the end of a corridor and perceiving the walls as converging toward the far end. From our vantage point, everything looks like it was converging on us. From the vantage point of a bacterium, a tapeworm, or a horseshoe crab, the evolutionary story looks entirely different — and just as valid.

Every living species represents an unbroken four-billion-year lineage of evolutionary "success." The horseshoe crab has been essentially unchanged for 450 million years — a duration of evolutionary stability that dwarfs the entire history of the hominin lineage. By the metric of persistence, the horseshoe crab is vastly more successful than *Homo sapiens*. But no one paints a "March of Progress" ending with a horseshoe crab because the image is not about evolutionary success; it is about human self-congratulation.

### The Parody Trap

An underappreciated factor in the image's persistence is its extraordinary success as a meme template. The March of Progress has been parodied thousands of times — showing the "evolution" of music, technology, video game characters, automobiles, beer bellies, and office workers gradually hunching over their desks. Each parody, whether celebratory or satirical, reinforces the underlying visual template: progress is linear, directional, and endpoint-oriented. Every time someone shares a March of Progress parody, they inadvertently propagate the misconception, even if the intent is humor rather than science education.


## 4. Verdict

**Distorted but grounded.** The March of Progress image is based on a genuine phenomenon — human ancestors did evolve from earlier primates, and there are real morphological changes traceable through the hominin fossil record. But the image's linear, directional, progress-oriented framing fundamentally misrepresents the process it claims to depict. Evolution is branching, not linear. Complexity can decrease as well as increase. There is no endpoint, no preferred direction, and no species that represents evolution's "goal." The image has done more to entrench public misunderstanding of evolution than perhaps any other single artifact in the history of science communication.

The Snopes-hosted article (a republication from *The Conversation*) correctly identifies the linear progress model as a misconception and provides strong genomic evidence against it. The EARC mode for this entry is **Augment**: the source article is scientifically accurate but narrow in scope, focusing primarily on the genomic evidence without addressing the image's visual rhetoric, anthropological implications, psychological persistence mechanisms, or racial subtexts.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The March of Progress misconception is not merely an error about evolutionary biology. It is a case study in how visual representations of science can override textual corrections, how cultural narratives can colonize scientific concepts, and how a single image can shape public understanding of an entire discipline for generations.

### Visual Rhetoric and Scientific Authority

The power of the March of Progress image derives partly from its association with scientific authority. It was created for a Time-Life publication — a prestige educational brand — and has been reproduced in contexts that confer institutional credibility: textbooks, museum exhibits, documentary films. The image carries the semiotics of science (detailed anatomical illustration, labeled specimens, left-to-right temporal progression) even though its message is anti-scientific.

This is a broader problem in science communication. The visual language of science — graphs, diagrams, anatomical illustrations, technical formatting — can make any claim seem authoritative regardless of its accuracy. The March of Progress image looks like science in the same way that a lab coat and a clipboard look like medical authority. The semiotics do the persuasive work; the content is almost irrelevant.

### The Problem of Corrective Illustration

One reason the March of Progress persists is that no equally compelling alternative image has replaced it. Phylogenetic trees — the accurate representation of evolutionary relationships — are visually complex, lack a clear protagonist, and do not tell a satisfying story. Cladograms are useful for specialists but impenetrable for lay audiences. The branching bush model that accurately represents evolution is, frankly, ugly compared to the elegant simplicity of the March of Progress.

This reveals a deeper challenge in science communication: accurate representations of complex phenomena are often less visually compelling than inaccurate simplifications. The history of science education is littered with images that are "good enough" to persist despite known inaccuracies — the Bohr model of the atom, the tongue taste map, the simple food chain diagram — because they are easier to draw, easier to understand, and more aesthetically satisfying than their accurate replacements.

Developing visual representations of evolution that are both scientifically accurate and visually compelling remains one of the great unsolved problems of science communication. Until such representations exist and achieve the cultural penetration of the March of Progress, the misconception will continue to propagate regardless of how many articles explain why it is wrong.

### Gould's Unfinished Revolution

Stephen Jay Gould spent decades fighting the linear progress model of evolution, and his work remains the most accessible and rhetorically powerful critique available. *Full House* (1996), *Wonderful Life* (1989), and numerous essays in his *Natural History* magazine column articulated a vision of evolution as contingent, directionless, and fundamentally unpredictable — a vision supported by the fossil record, the genomic evidence, and the basic mathematics of branching processes.

Yet Gould's revolution remains incomplete. His ideas are well known among evolutionary biologists and science-literate readers, but they have not penetrated popular culture with anything approaching the force of the image he opposed. The March of Progress appears on T-shirts and bumper stickers; Gould's drunkard's walk model does not. The image wins because images are culturally transmissible in ways that arguments are not.

The 2020 Paps, Guijarro-Clarke, and Holland study picks up where Gould left off, providing genomic-scale evidence that reinforces the anti-progressionist argument. The finding that human-containing lineages are characterized by massive gene *losses* is a particularly powerful datum because it reverses expectations: we are not the lineage that gained the most genetic complexity; we are the lineage that lost the most. This is an uncomfortable finding for a species accustomed to seeing itself as evolution's crowning achievement, which may explain why it has received less media attention than a finding of this magnitude warrants.

### Implications for Science Literacy

The persistence of the March of Progress misconception has practical consequences for science literacy and public policy. People who believe evolution is directional and progress-oriented are more likely to misunderstand evolutionary medicine (the evolution of antibiotic resistance is not "progress"), ecological conservation (species do not exist on a hierarchy of evolutionary value), and the genetics of human diversity (no population is "more evolved" than any other).

The misconception also provides rhetorical ammunition for creationist arguments. If evolution is supposed to be a march of progress, then any example of apparent "devolution" — e.g., cave fish that have lost their eyes, or parasites that have lost their digestive systems — seems like a counterexample. Creationists regularly cite such cases as evidence against evolution, and the argument resonates precisely because the March of Progress image has already primed the audience to expect unidirectional complexity increase. The image creates the very misconception that its opponents exploit.

### From Image to Worldview

Perhaps the most insidious effect of the March of Progress is not the specific misconception it propagates but the deeper worldview it encodes. The image teaches viewers to see the world in terms of hierarchical progress: some things are "higher" and others are "lower," some are "advanced" and others are "primitive," and the arrow of time points reliably from worse to better. This worldview extends far beyond biology into cultural attitudes about technology, civilization, and human potential.

The idea that history is a march of progress — that later is always better, that change always means improvement, that complexity always trumps simplicity — is a cultural assumption so pervasive that it feels like common sense. But it is not supported by the evidence, either in biology or in human history. Civilizations rise and fall. Technologies are invented and forgotten. Complex societies collapse while simpler ones persist. The actual trajectory of life on Earth, and of human civilization on Earth, looks far more like Gould's drunkard's walk than like Zallinger's confident march.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes article under examination is unusual in the Truth Vault corpus: it is not a traditional Snopes fact-check with a claim, a rating, and an investigation. Instead, it is an editorial republication of an article from *The Conversation*, a platform where academics write for general audiences. The article, by evolutionary biologists Jordi Paps and Cristina Guijarro-Clarke of the Universities of Bristol and Essex respectively, describes their 2020 *Nature Ecology and Evolution* study on gene gain and loss across the animal tree of life.

Snopes published the article with an explicit disclaimer: "This content is shared here because the topic may interest Snopes readers; it does not, however, represent the work of Snopes fact-checkers or editors." This is an honest framing — the article is presented as expert commentary, not as a fact-check — but it also means that the article carries Snopes' institutional imprimatur without having undergone Snopes' fact-checking process.

### Source Quality Assessment

The *Conversation* article is scientifically sound. It accurately describes the study's methods (comparative genomics across 100+ organisms), findings (unprecedented levels of gene loss in major animal lineages), and implications (the linear progress model is genomically unsupported). The article references Gould's work appropriately, uses the drunkard's walk metaphor effectively, and avoids overstating the conclusions.

The underlying study itself is published in a high-impact peer-reviewed journal (*Nature Ecology and Evolution*), conducted by researchers with established expertise in comparative genomics, and consistent with prior work in the field. The scientific claims in the article are well-supported.

### What the Article Does Not Address

The *Conversation* article focuses narrowly on the genomic evidence against the linear progress model. It does not address:

**The visual rhetoric problem.** The March of Progress image is not merely a scientific claim that can be refuted with data; it is a visual representation that operates at a pre-rational level. Genomic evidence published in academic journals does not compete with an image reproduced on a billion T-shirts. The article provides excellent ammunition for someone already engaged in the debate but does nothing to challenge the image's visual dominance in popular culture.

**The educational perpetuation cycle.** The image continues to appear in textbooks, and the article does not address the systemic factors that maintain this perpetuation — publisher inertia, the absence of compelling alternative illustrations, the textbook adoption process that favors familiar images.

**The anthropological and racial dimensions.** The original March of Progress image encodes racial hierarchies that remain culturally active. An epistemologically complete treatment of the image's impact must address this dimension, which the *Conversation* article does not.

**The psychological persistence mechanisms.** The article explains why the March of Progress is wrong but not why people believe it despite being told it is wrong. The cognitive factors — narrative brain, teleological intuition, anthropocentrism — that make the misconception resistant to correction are absent from the analysis.

### The Augmentation Case

This is why the EARC mode for this entry is Augment rather than Enhance. The Snopes-hosted article is not wrong or incomplete in what it covers; it is simply too narrow to constitute a full epistemological audit of the March of Progress misconception. The genomic evidence is one layer of a multi-layered problem that spans visual rhetoric, cognitive psychology, educational policy, racial politics, and the fundamental challenges of communicating scientific complexity to non-specialist audiences.

The ideal fact-checking treatment of the March of Progress would combine the genomic evidence presented in the *Conversation* article with the visual rhetoric analysis, the cognitive psychology of misconception persistence, the educational perpetuation cycle, and the racial subtext that the image has carried since its creation in 1965. No single article has yet achieved this synthesis, which is precisely the gap that the Truth Vault aims to fill.

### A Note on Format

The unusual format of the Snopes article — a republication with disclaimer rather than an original fact-check — reflects both a strength and a limitation of the Snopes model. The strength is that Snopes recognizes the importance of the topic and brings it to a wide audience. The limitation is that the republication format means the article was not subjected to Snopes' editorial process, which might have prompted the addition of broader context, analysis of the image's cultural history, or engagement with the pedagogical dimensions of the problem. Republication extends reach; original analysis extends understanding. Both are valuable, but they are not the same thing.
