# Are These '16 Easy Tests' to Check Whether Food Is 'Fake or Real'?


## 1. The Claim

In June 2019, a Facebook video produced by Blossom, a content brand owned by First Media, posed a question designed to trigger the deepest anxieties of anyone who eats: "Is your food fake or real? Find out with these 16 easy tests at home!" The video accumulated approximately 85 million views and 3 million shares before Facebook removed it on June 10, 2019, following Snopes' investigation and rating. By the time it disappeared, the video had already been replicated, re-posted, and screen-recorded across dozens of secondary accounts, ensuring its content continued to circulate well after the original was pulled.

The video presented a rapid-fire sequence of purported tests that viewers could perform at home to detect food adulteration. These ranged from burning cheese with a lighter (to determine if it was "real" or "processed"), dropping rice into water (to check for plastic contamination), burning vitamin supplements (to distinguish "natural" from "synthetic"), to testing ice cream for washing powder. Each test was presented with clean production values, bright lighting, and the visual grammar of a trusted DIY tutorial—the aesthetic language of platforms like Blossom, 5-Minute Crafts, and their competitors in the viral content-farm ecosystem.

Blossom and its parent company First Media operate within a specific industrial logic: produce high volumes of short, visually engaging, platform-optimized content that triggers sharing behavior. Food content occupies a privileged position in this ecosystem because it combines three of the most reliable viral triggers: bodily anxiety (what am I putting in my body?), moral indignation (are corporations poisoning me?), and actionable novelty (here's a simple test you can do right now). The "16 easy tests" video hit all three with precision. It promised viewers a sense of agency over an opaque industrial food system by offering what appeared to be empirical verification methods—kitchen-counter science that could cut through corporate obfuscation.

The "food purity" anxiety genre has deep historical roots that predate digital media. Concerns about food adulteration fueled the passage of the United States' Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906, and moral panics about food contamination have recurred in every era since. What distinguishes the contemporary version is the speed of propagation and the geographic collapse that digital platforms enable. Content addressing food safety concerns specific to one regulatory jurisdiction can travel instantaneously to audiences in entirely different food systems, stripped of context and amplified by algorithmic optimization for engagement.

First Media, when confronted by Snopes, issued a statement describing the video as "intended only for informational purposes and as entertainment." This disclaimer—a legal hedge common to content farms—reveals the fundamental tension at the heart of viral food-safety content. The video's rhetorical structure was explicitly instructional: it used imperative verbs, step-by-step demonstrations, and categorical verdicts about what results indicated. No reasonable viewer encountering the content in a social media feed would interpret it as entertainment. The disclaimer functioned not as a genuine characterization of the content's intent but as a liability shield, a structural feature of the content-farm business model that allows producers to claim plausible deniability while maximizing engagement through health anxiety.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes consulted Eric Decker, head of the Department of Food Science at the University of Massachusetts at Amherst, who provided expert assessments of the video's claims. His evaluations, combined with analysis from the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and examination of the claims' origins, paint a picture considerably more complicated than the video's binary "fake or real" framing suggested.

**Processed cheese and melting (FALSE).** The video's very first test claimed that processed cheese that is difficult to melt must be "fake." Decker explained that this claim gets the chemistry exactly backward. Processed cheese contains emulsifying salts—typically sodium citrate, sodium phosphate, or potassium phosphate—which disrupt the calcium-mediated protein network in natural cheese. This disruption actually makes processed cheese *easier* to melt into a smooth, homogeneous liquid, rather than harder. Natural aged cheeses like Parmesan, by contrast, often resist smooth melting because their intact protein matrices produce clumping and oil separation when heated. The video's test would, if anything, identify natural cheese as "fake" and processed cheese as "real"—the precise opposite of its stated purpose. This is not a matter of interpretation or degree; the underlying food chemistry directly contradicts the claim.

**Plastic rice (FALSE).** The claim that rice is commonly mixed with or replaced by plastic granules is a persistent hoax with origins in social media posts circulating in Asia and Africa, typically accompanied by videos showing someone setting rice on fire or dropping it in water. No credible evidence has ever established that plastic rice exists as a commercial product at any meaningful scale. The economics alone make the claim implausible: rice is among the cheapest grains globally, while producing plastic granules that convincingly mimic rice in appearance, texture, and cooking behavior would require sophisticated manufacturing at costs exceeding the food product being adulterated. The hoax resurfaces periodically, particularly in regions where food supply chain trust is low, and it has been debunked repeatedly by food safety authorities in multiple countries.

**Ground rocks in baby food (UNPROVEN / MISLEADING).** The video claimed that baby food contains "ground-up rocks" used as a calcium source, framing this as evidence of dangerous adulteration. The underlying chemistry is real but entirely mischaracterized. Calcium carbonate, derived from limestone, is a standard and scientifically validated calcium source used in food fortification worldwide. It is recognized as safe by the FDA, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), and the World Health Organization (WHO). Describing it as "ground-up rocks" is technically accurate at the molecular level in the same way that describing table salt as "ground-up crystals extracted from the earth" is technically accurate—it is true in the narrowest semantic sense while being profoundly misleading about the product's safety, purity, and regulatory status. The framing exploits the naturalistic fallacy, implying that a mineral origin is inherently concerning.

**Synthetic vs. natural supplements (FALSE).** The video claimed that burning vitamin supplements could distinguish synthetic from natural versions, with synthetic supplements supposedly burning while natural ones do not. Decker's assessment was unequivocal: this is, in his words, "just bullshit." Most synthetic vitamins and minerals are chemically identical to their naturally occurring counterparts. Ascorbic acid synthesized in a laboratory is molecularly indistinguishable from ascorbic acid extracted from an orange. Both will burn. Both will dissolve. Both will be metabolized identically by human biochemistry. The flammability of a supplement tablet or capsule is determined by its excipients—binders, fillers, coatings, and carriers—not by whether the active ingredient was synthesized or extracted. A "natural" supplement in a gelatin capsule with cellulose filler will burn readily; a synthetic supplement pressed into a dense mineral tablet may not. The test measures the combustion properties of packaging materials, not the provenance of the active ingredient.

**Meat glue / transglutaminase (MOSTLY TRUE, but misleadingly presented).** The video's claim about "meat glue" was the most factually grounded of the set, but still misleadingly presented. Transglutaminase is a naturally occurring enzyme that catalyzes the formation of covalent bonds between lysine and glutamine residues in proteins. In food processing, microbial transglutaminase (mTG, typically derived from *Streptoverticillium mobaraense*) is used to bind smaller cuts of meat into larger portions, improve texture in processed seafood products, and strengthen dough in baking. The FDA classified transglutaminase as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) in 1998 following review of available toxicological data. It is used openly and legally in the United States, listed on ingredient labels, and subject to standard food safety regulations. The video's alarm about "meat glue" treated a regulated, disclosed food processing technique as a secret adulteration, conflating transparency with concealment.

**Washing powder in ice cream (FALSE).** This claim originated in Indian consumer activism contexts and has no verified basis in the United States food system. A search of the FDA's Center for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition Adverse Event Reporting System (CAERS) database reveals no reports consistent with detergent contamination in commercially sold ice cream in the United States. The claim appears to derive from isolated incidents in India where informal, unregulated ice cream production has occasionally been flagged for contamination, situations that reflect specific regulatory and enforcement conditions in India's informal food economy rather than systemic practices in industrialized food systems.

**Tests 7–16: FSSAI DART guidelines.** The remaining tests in the video—covering adulteration of honey, milk, spices, and other staples—were largely derived from India's Food Safety and Standards Authority (FSSAI) DART (Detect Adulteration with Rapid Tests) toolkit, a set of consumer-facing guidelines published around 2015. These tests are scientifically sound within their intended context. FSSAI developed DART to address specific adulteration patterns prevalent in India's food supply chain, including dilution of milk with water and starch, coloring of spices with non-food-grade dyes, and addition of non-food oils to cooking oil. These adulteration patterns are driven by specific economic and regulatory conditions: fragmented supply chains, limited cold-chain infrastructure, extensive informal-sector production, and enforcement challenges across a vast and diverse territory. The tests are calibrated for the types and severity of adulteration encountered in that context. Presenting them to American or European audiences without any geographic context implied that these problems exist at comparable prevalence in Western food systems—a claim unsupported by evidence and contradicted by the regulatory and surveillance infrastructure of agencies like the FDA and EFSA.

The FDA itself weighed in with an unusual public statement, noting that the practices described in the video "are not legal in the U.S." and that "videos like these can undermine the confidence consumers have in the FDA's role" in ensuring food safety. This statement is notable for its directness: the FDA rarely comments on individual pieces of social media content, and its decision to do so reflects the scale of the video's reach and the perceived damage to public trust in food safety regulation.


## 3. The Epistemic Gap

Snopes' fact-check is competent, thorough, and correct in its individual assessments. The verdict of "Mostly False" accurately reflects the balance of claims. Yet the fact-check, by its structural nature, addresses each claim as a discrete proposition to be evaluated in isolation. This approach—the standard operating method of professional fact-checking—efficiently dispatches the false claims but leaves several deeper epistemic questions unexamined. It is in these gaps that the most interesting analytical work lies.

### Geographic Decontextualization as an Epistemic Category

The most significant gap in both the original video and the Snopes analysis involves what might be called *geographic decontextualization*: the presentation of context-specific food safety information as though it applies universally. This is not merely a factual error but a distinct category of misinformation that requires its own analytical framework.

The FSSAI DART tests were developed through legitimate scientific and regulatory work. India faces genuine food adulteration challenges driven by structural features of its food economy: a large informal sector, limited refrigeration infrastructure in many regions, complex multi-tier supply chains, and enforcement resources that are stretched thin relative to the scale of the market. The DART toolkit was a pragmatic response to these real conditions, designed to empower consumers to perform basic quality checks on products purchased from informal vendors who may not be subject to regular inspection.

When Blossom extracted these tests from their Indian regulatory context and presented them to a primarily North American audience, it performed a specific epistemic operation: it preserved the technical content while stripping away the entire framework of relevance. The tests remained scientifically valid as chemical procedures, but the implied premise—that the adulteration they detect is likely present in the viewer's food—became false. This is not a simple factual error of the kind that standard fact-checking is designed to address. It is a *relocation of relevance*, a category of misinformation that is technically accurate at the procedural level while being systematically misleading at the contextual level.

This pattern of geographic decontextualization is not unique to the Blossom video. It recurs across food-safety social media content, health misinformation, and environmental alarmism. Content addressing genuine problems in one regulatory or geographic context is routinely repurposed for audiences in different contexts, where the same problems either do not exist or exist at vastly different prevalence levels. The global reach of social media platforms creates a structural incentive for this repurposing: content that addresses geographically specific concerns will have a limited audience, while content that implies universal relevance can achieve global viral distribution.

Understanding why food standards differ between jurisdictions requires reference to the international framework that governs food safety: the Codex Alimentarius, jointly administered by the World Health Organization (WHO) and the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO). The Codex establishes international food standards, guidelines, and codes of practice that member nations adopt or adapt according to their specific circumstances. The existence of different national standards—India's FSSAI, the United States' FDA, the European Union's EFSA—does not indicate that some countries have "fake" food while others do not. It reflects the reality that different countries face different food safety challenges depending on their agricultural systems, climate, infrastructure, supply chain structures, and enforcement capacities. The Codex framework is explicitly designed to accommodate this variation while maintaining minimum safety thresholds. Presenting food safety tests from one jurisdiction as universally applicable fundamentally misrepresents how food safety governance works at the international level.

### The Industrialization of Food Anxiety

The Blossom video did not emerge from a vacuum. It was a product of a specific industrial logic: the content-farm model of media production, in which engagement metrics—views, shares, comments—are the primary measure of value and therefore the primary driver of editorial decisions. Within this model, food anxiety content occupies a highly valuable niche because it combines several properties that optimize for engagement.

First, food anxiety content has universal relevance: everyone eats, and therefore everyone is a potential audience member for claims about food contamination. Second, it activates what psychologists call the *behavioral immune system*—the suite of psychological mechanisms evolved to protect organisms from pathogen contamination. Research in disgust psychology has established that contamination-related content triggers strong emotional responses, heightened attention, and increased sharing behavior. The mere suggestion that food might contain non-food substances (plastic in rice, washing powder in ice cream, "ground-up rocks" in baby food) activates disgust responses that are disproportionate to actual risk levels and resistant to rational correction.

Third, food anxiety content offers the appearance of empirical verifiability through simple kitchen-counter experiments. Unlike claims about, say, vaccine ingredients or electromagnetic radiation, which require specialized equipment and expertise to evaluate, the Blossom video's tests could ostensibly be performed by anyone with a lighter, a glass of water, and groceries from their pantry. This apparent accessibility gives the content a democratic, anti-authority appeal: you don't need to trust the FDA or food scientists; you can verify this yourself. The irony, of course, is that the tests are either chemically meaningless (the supplement-burning test), actively misleading (the cheese-melting test), or calibrated for adulteration patterns absent from the viewer's local food supply (the DART-derived tests).

The chemistry of processed cheese illustrates how genuine food science gets inverted in viral content. Emulsifying salts—the sodium citrate and sodium phosphate compounds used in processed cheese—are among the most well-understood and thoroughly studied food additives in the modern food supply. Their function is to sequester calcium ions in the casein protein matrix, thereby disrupting the cross-links that give natural cheese its firm structure. This allows the protein to hydrate more fully, producing the smooth, uniform melt that consumers expect from processed cheese. The process was patented by Walter Gerber and Fritz Stettler in Switzerland in 1911 and refined by James L. Kraft in the United States shortly thereafter. Over a century of food science research, regulatory review, and consumer use has established the safety and function of these compounds. Yet in the Blossom video, the perfectly normal melting behavior of processed cheese was presented as evidence of fakery—a claim that depends on the audience's unfamiliarity with basic food chemistry and their willingness to interpret unfamiliar chemical processes as inherently suspicious.

### Platform Virality and the "Pure Food" Moral Panic

The scale of the video's distribution—85 million views and 3 million shares—demands explanation beyond the content's intrinsic properties. Social media platforms are not neutral distribution channels; they are algorithmic systems optimized for engagement, which in practice means optimized for emotional arousal. Content that triggers fear, disgust, or moral outrage is systematically favored by recommendation algorithms because it produces the behavioral signals (shares, comments, extended view time) that these algorithms treat as indicators of quality.

The "pure food" framing of the Blossom video taps into a moral framework that has deep cultural roots in both Western and non-Western traditions. The concept of food purity carries connotations that extend far beyond chemical composition: it invokes notions of naturalness, authenticity, and moral cleanliness that resonate with contemporary anxieties about industrialization, corporate power, and loss of connection to food production. The video's binary framing—"fake or real"—maps onto a moral binary that is far simpler and more emotionally satisfying than the nuanced reality of modern food processing, where most products exist on a continuum of processing intensity and where "processed" does not mean "unsafe" or "fake."

This moral dimension helps explain why the video achieved such extraordinary reach despite its demonstrable inaccuracy. Sharing the video was not primarily an act of information dissemination; it was an act of moral signaling—a way of expressing concern for one's family and community, skepticism toward corporate food producers, and alignment with values of naturalness and purity. Correcting the video's factual claims, while necessary, does not address the underlying moral and emotional needs that drove its distribution.


## 4. Key Evidence

The following evidence base informs both the Snopes analysis and the expanded epistemic assessment presented here:

**Expert consultation.** Eric Decker, head of the Department of Food Science at the University of Massachusetts at Amherst, provided direct expert assessment of the video's claims to Snopes. Decker's expertise in food chemistry, lipid oxidation, and food quality made him a well-qualified evaluator. His assessments were categorical where the science warranted categorical judgments (the supplement-burning claim is "just bullshit") and more nuanced where the evidence was mixed (transglutaminase is real but its framing as secret adulteration is misleading).

**FDA regulatory response.** The U.S. Food and Drug Administration issued a public statement specifically addressing the Blossom video, noting that the adulterative practices depicted "are not legal in the U.S." and expressing concern that "videos like these can undermine the confidence consumers have in the FDA's role" in food safety oversight. The FDA's CAERS database, which tracks adverse events related to food products, contains no reports consistent with several of the video's claims (e.g., detergent-contaminated ice cream).

**FSSAI DART toolkit (2015).** India's Food Safety and Standards Authority published the DART (Detect Adulteration with Rapid Tests) guidelines circa 2015 as a consumer empowerment tool for markets where formal inspection may be limited. The toolkit is scientifically validated for its intended context and addresses adulteration patterns—milk dilution, spice coloring, oil substitution—that are prevalent in India's food economy but effectively absent from the U.S. regulated food supply.

**Food fraud research.** Moore, Spink, and Lipp (2012, *Journal of Food Science*) established a comprehensive database and typology of food fraud incidents, documenting the most commonly adulterated foods and the economic motivations driving food fraud. Their work demonstrates that food fraud is real but highly contextualized: the types of adulteration encountered vary dramatically by food category, geographic region, and supply chain structure. Everstine, Spink, and Kennedy (2013, *Journal of Food Protection*) extended this work with a systematic taxonomy of food fraud that distinguishes between adulteration, substitution, dilution, concealment, mislabeling, counterfeiting, and diversion—a typological framework that reveals the oversimplification inherent in the Blossom video's binary "fake or real" framing.

**Emulsifying salt chemistry.** The chemistry of emulsifying salts in processed cheese is documented extensively in the food science literature. Kapoor and Metzger (2008, *Comprehensive Reviews in Food Science and Food Safety*) provide a thorough review of the mechanisms by which sodium citrate, disodium phosphate, and related compounds interact with casein proteins to modify cheese texture and melting behavior. This body of research directly contradicts the Blossom video's claim that resistance to melting indicates cheese authenticity.

**Transglutaminase safety.** The FDA's GRAS classification of transglutaminase (1998) followed standard toxicological review procedures. Subsequent research, including reviews published in *Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition*, has confirmed the enzyme's safety profile when used within established guidelines. The enzyme occurs naturally in human blood (as Factor XIII in the coagulation cascade) and in many foods.

**Disgust psychology and behavioral immune system.** Curtis, de Barra, and Aunger (2011, *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*) established the theoretical framework for understanding how contamination-related content activates evolved psychological mechanisms. This research helps explain the disproportionate virality of food-contamination content relative to its actual informational value.

**Codex Alimentarius standards.** The WHO/FAO Codex Alimentarius Commission maintains the international framework for food safety standards that informs national regulatory systems worldwide. The Codex explicitly recognizes that member nations may adopt different standards based on local conditions, making geographic decontextualization of food safety claims a misrepresentation of the international regulatory framework itself.


## 5. What This Means

The Blossom video episode illuminates several broader principles about food safety misinformation that distinguish it from other categories of health-related misinformation.

First, food safety misinformation operates through a different mechanism than, say, vaccine misinformation or climate denial. The latter categories typically involve rejection of scientific consensus—a dispute about what is true. Food adulteration misinformation, by contrast, often involves displacement of genuine concerns from one context to another—a dispute about what is relevant. The FSSAI DART tests are scientifically valid; the question is not whether they work but whether the adulteration they detect is present in the viewer's food. This makes food safety misinformation simultaneously harder to debunk (the underlying science is often real) and easier to propagate (it does not require the audience to reject scientific authority, only to mislocate their trust concerns).

Second, the episode reveals the limitations of platform-level content moderation as a response to food safety misinformation. Facebook removed the Blossom video after Snopes' rating, but the video had already achieved 85 million views and 3 million shares. The content had been downloaded, re-uploaded, screen-recorded, and redistributed across platforms. First Media's "entertainment" disclaimer functioned as a structural defense that complicated removal: if the content is entertainment, not health advice, what is the basis for removal? This legal ambiguity creates a gap that content farms systematically exploit.

Third, the case demonstrates that consumer food-safety education cannot rely solely on debunking individual false claims. The underlying demand—for accessible tools to evaluate food quality—is legitimate. Consumers in the United States and other developed nations are, in fact, several steps removed from food production, and their intuitions about food quality are often poorly calibrated. But the solution is not kitchen-counter chemistry tests; it is improved food literacy that includes understanding of how regulatory systems work, what the actual risks in one's local food supply are, and how to interpret ingredient labels and safety certifications.

The geographic decontextualization problem also has implications for international food safety communication. As food supply chains become more global and as social media erases geographic barriers to information flow, the need for context-specific food safety communication becomes more acute, not less. A consumer in Ohio encountering FSSAI-derived testing recommendations needs a framework for understanding that these tests were designed for different supply chain conditions—not because Indian food safety concerns are unimportant, but because the prevalence and nature of food adulteration differ systematically between regulatory environments.

Finally, the case highlights the asymmetry between content creation and correction in the food safety domain. Blossom's single video reached 85 million people with false or misleading claims. Snopes' correction, while thorough and widely cited, reached a fraction of that audience. The FDA's statement reached fewer still. This distribution asymmetry is not merely a communication challenge; it is a structural feature of platform-mediated information ecosystems that systematically advantages emotionally arousing misinformation over measured correction. Addressing this asymmetry requires not just better fact-checking but fundamental changes to the incentive structures that govern content production and distribution.


## 6. Verdict and Classification

**Snopes Verdict:** Mostly False — confirmed. The majority of the video's 16 claims are false, misleading, or presented without the geographic and regulatory context necessary for accurate interpretation. The partial truth of the transglutaminase claim and the scientific validity of certain DART-derived tests within their intended Indian context prevent a blanket "False" rating but do not rescue the video's overall thrust.

**EARC Mode:** E (Extensive gap — Snopes addresses the surface claims competently but leaves the deeper structural and epistemic dimensions unexplored).

The Snopes fact-check correctly evaluates each of the 16 claims on its individual merits and arrives at an accurate aggregate verdict. However, its claim-by-claim approach does not address the phenomenon's most significant epistemic dimension: the systematic geographic decontextualization of food safety content and the industrial incentive structures that drive it.

**Gap Category: Geographically Decontextualized Food Safety.** This case exemplifies a distinct category of misinformation in which factually grounded content from one regulatory and geographic context is stripped of that context and presented as universally relevant. The category is characterized by several features that distinguish it from simple fabrication:

- The underlying science is often real (FSSAI DART tests are chemically valid)
- The adulteration concerns are genuine in their original context (India's food supply chain faces documented challenges)
- The misinformation arises not from false claims about chemistry but from false implications about prevalence and relevance
- Standard fact-checking tools are poorly equipped to evaluate contextual relevance as distinct from factual accuracy
- The content exploits legitimate consumer desires for transparency and agency in food systems

The Blossom video's 85 million views and 3 million shares represent not merely a failure of individual content moderation but a systemic feature of platform-mediated information ecosystems in which engagement optimization, geographic decontextualization, and food anxiety industrialization combine to produce misinformation at scale. Understanding and addressing this phenomenon requires analytical frameworks that go beyond individual claim evaluation to examine the structural conditions that make such content both producible and distributable.
