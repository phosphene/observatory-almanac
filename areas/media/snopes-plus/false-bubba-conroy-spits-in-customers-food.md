# Bubba Conroy Spits in Customers' Food?


## 1. The Claim

On 5 January 2015, an article titled "'F-CK WHITES': Black Woman Brags via Twitter About Spitting in 'White Peoples Food'" began circulating across conservative social media and political blogs. The article contained a screenshot of what appeared to be a tweet from a user with the handle @BubbaConroy, in which the user purportedly admitted to spitting in customers' food at McDonald's as an act of racial grievance against white patrons — framed explicitly as retaliation for slavery. The accompanying article provided minimal context, consisted of a single paragraph, and noted that the @BubbaConroy Twitter account had been suspended or deleted.

The claim was amplified with tags directing attention to McDonald's corporate account, effectively crowdsourcing consumer anxiety about the safety of fast food. One Twitter post warned: "Thinking of eating @McDonalds today? If so you might want to make sure @bubbaconroy doesn't work there!" The framing was structured to generate maximum alarm without requiring any verification: the suspended account was presented as evidence of suppression rather than as a reason to doubt the claim's authenticity.

The article linked to a Facebook page called "Liberal Chick," described in the Snopes investigation as "a clear parody account designed to satirize stereotypical progressive ideals," characterised by deliberate misspellings and extreme positions clearly designed to mock liberal politics. This relationship between the rumour-generating article and the parody source should itself have been a significant red flag for anyone examining the claim's provenance, but in the viral sharing environment the flag went unnoticed or was disregarded.

David Mikkelson's Snopes investigation, published 8 January 2015 and last updated 7 January 2016, rated the claim definitively False after tracing the @BubbaConroy account to a coordinated 4chan hoax campaign and finding no evidence of any real McDonald's employee matching the description.


## 2. What's Actually True

Nothing in the Bubba Conroy claim is true. There is no credible evidence that any person named Bubba Conroy worked at McDonald's, that any such tweet was genuine rather than fabricated, or that any incident of racial food tampering of this nature occurred at McDonald's in the relevant period.

What is true is that McDonald's, when addressed directly on Twitter about the claim in January 2015, responded: "Hi Tom! We don't tolerate discrimination at McDonald's and we're determining whether this person is an actual employee." This corporate response was measured and appropriately cautious — McDonald's neither confirmed nor denied the alleged employee's existence and committed to investigation. Subsequently, no confirmation of any such employee emerged, and McDonald's made no further public statement suggesting the claim had any substance.

The Snopes investigation provides the most significant piece of positive evidence: a search for the @BubbaConroy Twitter handle at the time of investigation surfaced only two categories of result. The first was individuals reacting to the McDonald's rumour. The second was a specific mention on an archived 4chan /pol/ board thread discussing the coordinated social media hoax campaign of which the Bubba Conroy account appeared to be a component. The handle appeared in a planning discussion thread that also connected to the "End Father's Day" hoax, Operation Bikini Bridge, and other fabricated social media campaigns that 4chan's /pol/ board conducted throughout 2014.

A Reddit discussion from 15 June 2014 in the 4chan subreddit also mentioned the @BubbaConroy account in a list of fake Twitter users, confirming that the account's fabricated nature was documented in the same online spaces that produced it, well before the January 2015 article artificially revived the claim.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty

The Bubba Conroy hoax belongs to a carefully documented type of coordinated disinformation operation that deserves epistemic treatment distinct from ordinary misinformation errors. The operation was not a case of sincere false belief; it was intentional deception with an explicit strategic design articulated by its creators.

The 4chan /pol/ planning threads that the Snopes investigation surfaced made the operation's objectives explicit. One participant described the goals as follows: "(1) how long it naturally takes for your ilk to appreciate an insincere idea among their ranks; (2) how fucking deranged you are in relation to what you can tolerate (many delusional feminists tried to run with that *endfathersday shit); (3) poison the well so further discussion among you in twitter can't happen without suspicion; (4) disclose to the mainstream the mental contortion necessary for the movement to function." This is a sophisticated statement of an information warfare strategy: not merely to embarrass a target group, but to structurally degrade their epistemological commons by making authentic claims within that space indistinguishable from fabricated ones.

The Bubba Conroy claim was specifically designed to exploit the tension between two competing response heuristics. Conservative audiences were targeted to react with racial alarm and outrage based on the apparent evidence of the tweet. Meanwhile, if progressive audiences defended Bubba Conroy on the grounds that the account might be genuine, they would be tricked into defending an invented racial grievance narrative that could then be used as evidence of extremism. If progressive audiences disavowed the account, the hoaxers could claim victory in "exposing" radical racial attitudes. Either response was considered a win by the operation's designers. This is a characteristic feature of what researchers have called "griefing" or "troll epistemology" — operation designs structured so that any response by the target validates the attack.

Marwick & Lewis (2017, "Media Manipulation and Disinformation Online," Data & Society Research Institute) documented the broader ecosystem of such operations, noting that coordinated inauthentic behaviour campaigns from /pol/ and related communities specifically targeted race, gender, and progressive identity politics as productive sites for viral outrage because these topics mobilise both genuine emotional responses and are difficult to discuss dispassionately in a sharing environment. The food tamper hoax variant exploits an additional fearful substrate: public anxiety about the safety of food prepared by anonymous workers in fast food contexts.

The specific date asymmetry in the case is worth noting. The alleged tweet showed a date of June 2014. The article claiming to expose it appeared in January 2015 — seven months later. The Snopes investigation correctly noted that the article provided no explanation for why a tweet from seven months earlier "suddenly warranted a flurry of concern." This temporal gap is in fact a signature feature of reanimated hoax content: fabricated material is produced, allowed to go dormant, and then selectively re-amplified months later in a new social media cycle, with the passage of time obscuring the original source and the earlier debunking. This pattern substantially increases the success rate of hoax amplification by targeting audiences who were not exposed to the original circulation.


## 4. The Social and Institutional Context

The 2014 4chan /pol/ hoax campaign represented a relatively early and well-documented instance of what subsequent researchers would classify as coordinated inauthentic behaviour. DiResta et al. (2018, "The Tactics and Tropes of the Internet Research Agency," Senate Intelligence Committee report) and Linvill & Warren (2020, *Political Communication*) later characterised similar operations from state-aligned actors. The /pol/ operations preceded and in some respects piloted tactics that would be refined and scaled by more sophisticated actors.

The specific operations documented in the Snopes investigation — Operation Bikini Bridge, the End Father's Day campaign, and the Bubba Conroy fiction — shared structural features: fabrication of screenshot "evidence" that could circulate as an image independent of URL verification, creation of social media handles that would be suspended or deleted after achieving viral spread (lending an appearance of suppression-for-truth), and deliberate targeting of topics guaranteed to activate strong emotional responses in identifiable audience segments.

The McDonald's fast food context added an additional layer of exploitability. Food tampering by restaurant workers is a genuine and recurring category of event — real incidents do occur, are reported, and are prosecuted — creating a plausible background against which the fabricated claim could appear credible without requiring further verification. Research on contamination and disgust psychology (Rozin et al., 1986, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) has established that disgust reactions are particularly resistant to disconfirmation: once the possibility of contamination has been considered, disconfirming information often fails to eliminate the aversive emotional response. The viral framing was designed to trigger this psychological mechanism in its target audience.

The Domain Registration and Amplification Infrastructure used by the January 2015 article was itself part of a network of linked fake social media accounts, as the Snopes investigation identified. The article appeared on a domain "linked with a fake social media account masquerading as real," suggesting that the content was not merely a naive reader's mistaken sharing of an old screenshot, but rather an intentional amplification of the hoax material through infrastructure designed for that purpose.


## 5. Verdict Analysis

Snopes' "False" verdict — the clearest possible categorisation — is correct and well-supported. The Bubba Conroy claim fails every available evidentiary test. The Twitter account was fabricated as part of a documented hoax campaign. The alleged tweet was a created screenshot, not a real post. The account was traced directly to /pol/ planning discussions. No corroborating evidence of any actual McDonald's employee engaging in the described conduct was produced by anyone, including by investigators, journalists, or McDonald's itself.

The assignment of EARC mode R (requires additional research) to this entry reflects the genuinely important contextual dimension: the Bubba Conroy case cannot be fully understood as a simple false claim. It is a component of a documented coordinated disinformation campaign, and treating it as a mere factual error mischaracterises its social function and the appropriate epistemic response. Understanding what it actually was — intentional, coordinated, strategically designed racial hoax content — is necessary both for correctly evaluating the claim and for understanding the broader information environment in which similar claims continue to circulate.

The Snopes piece's decision to trace the claim's origins rather than simply asserting its falseness is methodologically correct. In cases of orchestrated hoaxes, establishing the production context is as evidentially important as establishing the falseness of the specific factual claim, because it allows readers to understand the mechanism of deception and to apply that understanding to similar future claims.


## 6. Implications

The Bubba Conroy case has several implications that extend well beyond the specific incident. The first and most immediately practical is for fast food consumers: there is no credible basis for concern about McDonald's food safety arising from this hoax, and the claim should not inform any food safety behaviours or decisions.

More broadly, the case is an important early example of the "screenshot as evidence" problem in social media misinformation. Because Twitter (now X) and similar platforms frequently delete or suspend accounts after viral incidents — sometimes as a result of policy enforcement, sometimes simply due to the account holder's decision — the absence of the original account is routinely misinterpreted as evidence of suppression. The suspended account becomes proof of the claim in the minds of credulous audiences, when in fact it is either evidence of enforcement action or simply part of the original hoax design (accounts are created, achieve temporary viral effect, and are deleted to prevent forensic tracing).

Wardle & Derakhshan (2017, *Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policy*, Council of Europe) distinguished between misinformation (false information shared without intent to deceive), disinformation (false information created and shared with intent to deceive), and malinformation (true information shared to harm). The Bubba Conroy operation is a clear case of disinformation, and its design illustrates why disinformation requires different analytical and response frameworks than misinformation. Fact-checking alone — confirming that no such employee exists — is necessary but insufficient. Exposing the production mechanism, as the Snopes investigation attempted, is equally important.

The racial valence of the hoax is also significant. Food contamination accusations involving racial minorities targeting white customers are a specific category of hate hoax with a long American history predating the internet. Analyses of food contamination fear in racial contexts (Ferber, 2007, *Contexts*) have documented how contamination narratives have been used across different historical periods to regulate racial boundaries and incite discrimination. The 4chan hoax weaponised this historical tradition deliberately, as the planning documentation makes clear. The appropriate response to claims of this type involves heightened skepticism, not merely because false claims of this type circulate frequently, but because the production and circulation of such claims serves identifiable social-harm functions even when they fail to achieve their immediate persuasive goals.
