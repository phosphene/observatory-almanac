# Einstein Humiliated an Atheist Professor with a Brilliant Argument About Cold and Darkness


## 1. The Claim

The story arrives in email inboxes, Facebook shares, and WhatsApp forwards in dozens of variant phrasings, but its structure is always the same. A university professor—confident, dismissive, atheistic—challenges his students with a question about God and evil. He argues that if God created everything, and evil exists, then God must have created evil and is therefore evil. The students are silenced. Then one student (sometimes a physics student, sometimes unnamed) raises his hand.

The student engages with a philosophical reversal. Cold, he explains, is not a real thing—it is the absence of heat. Darkness is not a real thing—it is the absence of light. And evil, following the same logic, is not a thing with independent existence. Evil is the absence of God. Therefore, God did not create evil; evil is simply what remains when God is absent. The professor is defeated. He sits down. The last line of the anecdote identifies the student by name: "Albert Einstein."

This is the story as Snopes documented it from email circulation as early as 1999. The version in the Snopes fact-check database, collected via email, renders the argument in elaborate dialogue, with a physics lecture-style detour through thermodynamics and optics before arriving at the theological punchline. The Einstein attribution appears only in the final sentence, at maximum rhetorical impact, as a celebrity name placed to seal the argument with unimpeachable genius.

The claim circulates in multiple registers. In its simplest form, it is a factual assertion: this event happened, Einstein made this argument. In a more common implicit form, it works as a persuasive piece: whether or not the historical detail is accurate, the argument is attributed to Einstein in order to borrow his intellectual authority for a theological position he never actually held. Both the factual version and the implicit appeal-to-authority version deserve examination.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim is false in every factual particular. No event resembling this anecdote appears in any biography of Albert Einstein, any letter or essay by Einstein himself, any contemporaneous account from the period of his university studies, or any document in the vast scholarly literature on Einstein's life and thought. The story is a fabrication.

Snopes's investigation, published as a fact-check by Barbara Mikkelson in June 2004, establishes the core finding clearly: nothing remotely like the account appears in any credible source, and the account is inconsistent with Einstein's documented views on religion, God, and his own beliefs.

Einstein's actual religious and philosophical views are a matter of substantial historical record, expressed in his own words across dozens of letters, essays, and interviews. He consistently described himself as an "agnostic" or a "religious nonbeliever." In a 1954 letter to philosopher Eric Gutkind—auctioned multiple times and widely cited in scholarship on Einstein's worldview—he wrote explicitly that the word "God" for him was "nothing more than the expression and product of human weakness," and that "the Bible is a collection of honourable, but still primitive legends." In another context, he explained his religious sense as a "cosmic religious feeling" oriented toward the mysteries of the universe, explicitly distinct from any belief in a personal God who intervenes in human affairs, rewards virtue, or hears prayers.

The Einstein of the viral anecdote—the student who argues that evil is the absence of God, defending a specifically Christian theism in a university lecture hall—is so dramatically inconsistent with Einstein's documented theology as to be almost a parody. Einstein never argued for the existence of a personal God. He never described himself as Christian. He expressed, in his own writing, a settled discomfort with the idea of a God who cares about individual human affairs or moral outcomes.

The logical argument at the center of the anecdote—that evil is the absence of God, as cold is the absence of heat and darkness the absence of light—is not original to Einstein, was not made by Einstein, and is not the argument it appears to be. Thermodynamically, the analogy is flawed: heat is a form of energy with measurable physical properties, and its "absence" (absolute zero) is a measurable physical state. Darkness is, more accurately, the absence of photons at detectable wavelengths—a physical rather than purely metaphysical description. Transposing this framework to moral theology does not follow from the physics; it merely borrows the vocabulary of physics to dress a theological assertion in scientific-sounding language without the underlying quantitative rigor that makes physical definitions meaningful.

The theological argument being made in the anecdote—that evil is a privation, the absence of a positive good (God)—is a genuinely ancient one. The "privation theory of evil," associated with Augustine of Hippo and subsequently developed throughout the Catholic philosophical tradition as a response to the Manichean dualism Augustine had earlier practiced, holds that evil has no independent positive existence but is the corruption or absence of being and good. Augustine's formulation in the *Confessions* and *City of God* articulates this position in sophisticated theological terms. Aquinas extended and refined it in the *Summa Theologica*. It is a serious position in the history of theodicy with centuries of philosophical engagement behind it.

The viral Einstein story strips this ancient philosophical argument of its intellectual context, converts it into a debating trick, and places it in Einstein's mouth as if he had originated it and deployed it to humiliate an atheist—an inversion of everything the historical Einstein actually believed and said.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The anecdote has circulated for more than two decades in Christian email chains, Facebook groups, and social media shares because it serves a clear emotional and rhetorical function: it provides a ready-made argument for theistic belief, validated by the most recognizable genius name in popular culture, against the most easily caricatured version of atheist philosophy.

The psychology of this kind of viral content operates on several levels. First, Einstein's name is uniquely powerful as an authority attribution. He occupies an almost singular cultural position as the emblem of scientific genius—to say that Einstein believed something is, in popular imagination, to say that the most qualified intellect in modern history found the claim persuasive. This borrowed authority is precisely what the anecdote's unnamed author was constructing when adding "The young man's name—Albert Einstein" to the final line of what already existed as a generic apologetics story.

Second, the dramatic structure is satisfying in ways that make the story feel true. The arrogant professor defeated by a student's unexpected wisdom is an archetypal narrative that appears across many cultures and traditions—the wise fool confounding the learned fool is a story shape that audiences receive as inherently plausible. When the student turns out to be Einstein, the story acquires the additional resonance of a discovered destiny: of course it was Einstein; only he would have the elegance to think of that.

Third, the argument itself has a surface plausibility that rewards a single reading but does not survive sustained scrutiny. The parallelism—cold is the absence of heat, darkness is the absence of light, evil is the absence of God—has an appealing rhetorical symmetry that can feel more compelling than it actually is when encountered quickly. For readers who already believe the conclusion, the argument feels like confirmation; for readers unfamiliar with the history of theodicy, it may feel novel and persuasive.

Snopes notes, correctly, that the anecdote is "congruent with its reading audience's values" in the sense that it validates a pre-existing theological belief through an appeal to secular scientific authority—using Einstein, the archetypal secular genius, to endorse a theistic position. The strategy of borrowing secular prestige to validate religious claims is common in this genre of viral content; Einstein, Tesla, and Darwin are frequently enlisted for similar purposes through fabricated quotes and misattributed statements.

The story's persistence across more than twenty-five years of viral circulation, despite being debunked by Snopes since 2004, demonstrates a secondary principle: correction rarely catches virality. The debunking requires slightly more effort than the forwarding. Most people who share the Einstein professor story have never encountered the Snopes fact-check, or have encountered it and chosen to continue sharing the story because its rhetorical function matters more to them than its factual accuracy.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE**: The claim is entirely without factual basis. Albert Einstein never made this argument. No event resembling the anecdote appears in any biographical source, correspondence, or contemporaneous account. Einstein's documented religious views were agnostic, not theistic, and are irreconcilable with the position attributed to him in the anecdote. The story's appearance in email chains beginning in 1999 and its subsequent viral spread represent a textbook case of celebrity name-laundering applied to a pre-existing apologetics argument—the practice of inserting a recognizable famous name into an anonymous story to lend it false authority and credibility.

The philosophical argument at the core of the anecdote—that evil is a privation, the absence of a positive good—has genuine intellectual lineage in Augustine, Aquinas, and the broader Catholic philosophical tradition. That lineage is not well-served by the viral anecdote's form, which strips the argument of its theological context, reduces it to a debating trick, and misattributes it to a man whose actual views were incompatible with the conclusion being argued.

The claim rates False by Snopes's standard and by any evidentiary framework applied to historical claims.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Einstein professor anecdote belongs to a well-documented category of viral religious misinformation that combines celebrity fabrication with apologetics content. Understanding this genre—its mechanics, its functions, its persistence—is as instructive as any individual debunking.

The fabricated Einstein quote and story is one of many. Einstein is among the most frequently misquoted figures in the history of internet hoaxes, alongside Abraham Lincoln, Mark Twain, Winston Churchill, and a handful of other culturally canonical figures whose reputations make attribution maximally useful as a credibility signal. The Quote Investigator project, which methodically traces the origins of attributed quotations, has documented dozens of Einstein misattributions spanning a wide thematic range—environmental awareness, spirituality, scientific humility, and political philosophy. The pattern is consistent: a sentiment that would benefit from scientific authority, attributed to Einstein because he embodies scientific authority, regardless of whether he ever expressed the relevant idea.

The religious variant of this genre has particular characteristics. Viral Christian apologetics content of this type—what internet folklore scholars sometimes call "Sacred Electronic Chain Letters" or "Christian Copypasta"—serves community functions within Christian online spaces beyond the purely argumentative. Sharing content that confirms faith, challenges perceived enemies of faith, or demonstrates the rationality of belief is a form of community participation and identity performance. The factual accuracy of the content may matter less to the sharer than its theological and social resonance. Fact-checkers sometimes find this frustrating; sociologists of religion find it illuminating.

The 2014 film *God's Not Dead* took the basic structure of the Einstein professor anecdote and built a feature-length commercial film around it, suggesting that the narrative template had enough cultural traction to sustain a theatrical release. The film was commercially successful within Christian media markets, generating sequels and demonstrating that the student-versus-atheist-professor narrative serves as genuine myth—a story that, regardless of its historical truth, encapsulates a particular community's self-understanding and anxieties. Myths are not diminished by being false; they are, in some respects, defined by being believed independently of evidence. The Einstein anecdote functions as myth within the communities that share it.

The argument structure at the center of the anecdote—evil-as-privation—has a more interesting intellectual history than the viral story suggests. Augustine's privation theory was developed in part as a refutation of Manicheanism, which posited evil as an independent positive force in the cosmos, equal in power to good. Augustine's argument that evil is the corruption of good, and not an independent substance, was philosophically sophisticated and remained influential through the medieval scholastic tradition. Aquinas's elaboration in the *Summa Theologica* distinguished between physical evil (suffering, death, natural disaster) and moral evil (sinful acts of will), applying the privation concept differently to each case. These are real philosophical positions worth knowing about; that they are obscured by a falsified anecdote featuring a physicist who didn't believe in a personal God is an irony that the tradition's actual contributors might find unfortunate.

The viral story also distorts its representation of "atheism" in ways that impede rather than advance genuine engagement. The fictional professor is a cartoon—an arrogant figure making an elementary argument that collapses under the first adversarial question. Real arguments against theism are considerably more sophisticated, and the contemporary atheism-versus-theism debate in philosophical circles examines versions of the problem of evil that neither the fictional professor nor the fictional Einstein would recognize. Reducing complex philosophical conversations to theatrical debates where one side obviously wins tells participating audiences more about their group's sense of itself than about the actual content of the dispute.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This is one of Snopes's cleaner cases. Barbara Mikkelson's 2004 fact-check addresses the claim directly, rates it False, and provides two distinct layers of refutation: the negative evidence (no biographical source contains any such account) and the positive evidence (Einstein's documented views are inconsistent with the position he is claimed to have advocated). The check is well-structured and its conclusion is clear.

What Snopes's treatment does more briefly—and where the Truth Vault format adds value—is the investigation of *why* a false anecdote with such minimal evidentiary support has circulated so persistently for so long. The Snopes fact-check establishes falsity; explaining the social, rhetorical, and psychological functions the false story serves requires a different kind of analysis.

The Einstein professor anecdote is not a case of honest mistake or incomplete information—it is not a misread study or a misattributed finding that later got corrected. It is a fabrication from the outset, created to perform a rhetorical function, and it persists because that function is successfully discharged for its target audience regardless of any fact-checking intervention. This makes it a fundamentally different type of misinformation problem than the distorted-but-grounded claims that populate much of fact-checking territory.

Snopes's 2004 fact-check has not meaningfully reduced the story's circulation over the subsequent two decades. The story continues to appear in Facebook posts, WhatsApp groups, and evangelical Christian media in 2024. This persistence, despite clear and accessible refutation, is a feature worth documenting rather than just a persistent failure. It models what is true of misinformation more broadly: that refutation operates by different mechanics than circulation, and that viral content whose primary function is community identity reinforcement is substantially immune to fact-check correction.

The Einstein case should be read alongside similar documented fabrications—the Marie Curie quote about not fearing the future (unverifiable), the Darwin deathbed conversion story (reliably debunked by his own family members), the Tesla-funded-by-Morgan-to-give-free-energy-to-the-world narrative (a compound of selective facts and fabricated elements)—as part of a pattern of celebrity name-laundering in the service of communities seeking external validation for their values. Understanding the pattern is more useful than individual debunks, and the pattern is something that a compendium format like the Truth Vault is better positioned to document than a single fact-check.
