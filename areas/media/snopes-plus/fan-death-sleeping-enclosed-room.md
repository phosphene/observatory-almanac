# Sleeping with an Electric Fan in a Closed Room Can Cause Death

## §1. The Claim

In South Korea, a persistent and widely held belief asserts that sleeping in a closed room with an electric fan running can cause death. Known as *선풍기 사망* (seonpunggi samang — "fan death"), the belief holds that an electric fan left on overnight in a sealed room will kill the sleeping occupant, typically through one of several proposed mechanisms: hypothermia, oxygen depletion (asphyxiation), carbon dioxide poisoning from the fan motor, or — in what may be the most imaginative variant — the literal "chopping" of oxygen molecules by the fan blades, rendering the air unbreathable.

The belief is not a fringe superstition. It is, or has been, functionally hegemonic across South Korean society. National newspapers regularly report deaths attributed to fan exposure with a tone of sober public health concern. The Korea Consumer Protection Board (KCPB), a government agency, issued a consumer safety alert in 2006 listing electric fans as a summer hazard and warning that "ichthyotoxins" from asphyxiation could result from sleeping with a fan in a closed room (Korea Consumer Protection Board, 2006). South Korean medical examiners have listed "fan death" as an official cause of death in autopsy reports (Levin, 2004). The belief is so deeply embedded that virtually every electric fan sold in South Korea ships with a built-in timer, typically defaulting to intervals of one, two, or four hours, so that the device will automatically shut off during the night — an engineering accommodation to a cultural anxiety with no basis in physics.

The Korean media ecosystem sustains the myth with seasonal regularity. Each summer, news outlets report incidents in which a person is found dead in a room where a fan was running. Headlines rarely question the causal link. As the anthropologist Kim Seung-yeon documented, major Korean newspapers including *Chosun Ilbo*, *Dong-A Ilbo*, and *JoongAng Ilbo* have for decades published articles treating fan death as an established medical phenomenon (Kim, 2007). The absence of critical scrutiny in mainstream Korean journalism means that each summer's reports serve as a fresh infusion of evidentiary warrant for the belief — a self-perpetuating cycle of cultural confirmation.

Outside Korea, the belief is virtually unknown. When it is encountered, as in English-language journalism or expatriate communities, it is typically met with frank incredulity. This stark asymmetry — a belief that is treated as common sense within one society and as plainly absurd outside it — makes fan death one of the most instructive case studies in cultural epistemology available to the contemporary analyst.

## §2. What's Actually True

Each of the proposed mechanisms for fan death can be evaluated against elementary physics and physiology. None withstands scrutiny.

### Hypothermia

The most common claim is that a fan blowing on a sleeping person lowers their body temperature to dangerous levels. This reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of how fans work thermodynamically. An electric fan does not cool air. It moves air. The sensation of cooling that a person experiences in front of a fan is produced by convective heat transfer: moving air accelerates the evaporation of perspiration from the skin surface, which is an endothermic process that draws thermal energy from the body. This mechanism is described by Newton's Law of Cooling, which states that the rate of heat loss from a body is proportional to the difference in temperature between the body and its surroundings (Incropera et al., 2006). Critically, a fan cannot lower a person's core body temperature below the ambient air temperature. In a closed room during a Korean summer — where ambient temperatures routinely exceed 30°C — a fan could not induce hypothermia (defined as core body temperature below 35°C) under any plausible scenario.

In fact, the opposite risk is more realistic. The first law of thermodynamics requires that the electrical energy consumed by the fan motor be dissipated as heat into the room. A typical domestic fan draws 40–75 watts of electrical power; the Stefan-Boltzmann law and basic energy conservation dictate that this energy must ultimately appear as thermal radiation and convective heating within the room. In a small, sealed room with no ventilation, a running fan will incrementally *raise* the ambient temperature, not lower it (Çengel & Boles, 2014). Under conditions of extreme heat and humidity, where the wet-bulb temperature approaches the skin temperature, fans can actually become counterproductive, as they accelerate convective heat *gain* rather than evaporative heat loss — a point documented in public health guidance on heat waves (Jay et al., 2015).

### Oxygen Depletion / Asphyxiation

The claim that a fan "uses up" the oxygen in a sealed room conflates an electric fan with a combustion device. An electric motor converts electrical energy to mechanical rotation via electromagnetic induction. It consumes no oxygen and produces no combustion byproducts. The oxygen concentration in a sealed room will decline gradually due to the metabolic consumption of the room's occupant — a human at rest consumes approximately 250 mL of O₂ per minute (Guyton & Hall, 2015) — but even a small bedroom of 15 m³ contains roughly 3,150 liters of oxygen at standard atmospheric composition (20.9%). At resting consumption rates, it would take well over 100 hours for oxygen levels to fall to a concentration that might produce symptoms of hypoxia (below approximately 16%), and this assumes truly hermetic sealing, which no residential room achieves. Residential buildings have measured air exchange rates of 0.2–1.0 air changes per hour even with windows and doors closed, through gaps around doors, window frames, electrical outlets, and structural joints (Persily, 1998). The fan is irrelevant to this calculation — it neither consumes oxygen nor prevents passive air infiltration.

### Carbon Dioxide Poisoning from the Motor

A variant holds that the fan motor itself emits carbon dioxide. This is physically impossible for an electric motor. CO₂ is a product of combustion (oxidation of carbon-containing fuels) or biological respiration. An electric motor contains copper windings, iron cores, and permanent magnets or electromagnetic coils. No chemical combustion occurs during operation. The only source of CO₂ in the room is the human occupant's exhalation, and as noted above, ventilation rates in residential spaces prevent dangerous accumulation (ASHRAE, 2017).

### "Chopping" of Oxygen Molecules

Perhaps the most revealing mechanism is the claim that fan blades physically sever O₂ molecules, splitting them into individual oxygen atoms that are somehow not respirable. The bond dissociation energy of the O=O double bond in molecular oxygen is 498 kJ/mol (Atkins & de Paula, 2014). To break this bond mechanically, a fan blade would need to impart energy on the order of 8.3 × 10⁻¹⁹ joules per molecule. The kinetic energy of a fan blade tip moving at approximately 15 m/s, distributed across the molecular-scale interaction cross-section relevant to bond disruption, falls short by many orders of magnitude. Furthermore, even if molecular oxygen were somehow dissociated, the resulting atomic oxygen (O) is a powerful free radical that would spontaneously recombine to form O₂ or ozone (O₃) within nanoseconds at atmospheric pressure (Wayne, 2000). The notion that fan blades can chemically alter the composition of the atmosphere is not merely wrong; it reflects a category error about the scale at which molecular bonds and macroscopic mechanical forces operate.

### What Actually Kills People

The deaths attributed to fan death in Korean media are almost certainly the result of other causes. Summer in South Korea brings sustained heat waves, with temperatures frequently exceeding 35°C and high humidity often pushing the heat index above 40°C. Heat-related mortality — including heatstroke, heat exhaustion precipitating cardiac arrest, and dehydration — is a real and well-documented phenomenon (Basu & Samet, 2002). Alcohol consumption, which impairs thermoregulation, is culturally prevalent and is a risk factor in many of these deaths. Cardiac arrhythmias, undiagnosed cardiovascular disease, and other conditions can produce sudden death during sleep. When a body is found in a room with a fan, the fan is the most conspicuous object — but it is the summer heat, not the fan, that is the environmental threat.

## §3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of fan death belief across decades, despite its complete absence of scientific support, invites not merely a fact-check but an epistemological investigation. How can an entire society — a technologically advanced, highly educated nation that ranks among the world's leaders in scientific output — maintain a consensual belief that is trivially falsified by undergraduate physics?

### Social Transmission vs. Individual Verification

The sociologist of science Harry Collins distinguished between what he termed "interactional expertise" and "contributory expertise" — the difference between knowing enough about a field to talk about it fluently and knowing enough to actually contribute new knowledge to it (Collins & Evans, 2007). Most people acquire their beliefs about health and safety through social transmission rather than individual empirical verification. We believe that smoking causes cancer not because we have personally replicated the epidemiological studies, but because trusted institutional authorities tell us so. In Korea, trusted institutional authorities — government agencies, physicians quoted in newspapers, consumer safety bodies — have for decades affirmed the reality of fan death. Any individual Korean who might be inclined toward skepticism faces an enormous social-epistemic headwind: effectively the entire apparatus of cultural common sense.

This dynamic illustrates what the philosopher C.A.J. Coady termed the "epistemology of testimony" — the study of when and why it is rational to accept claims on the authority of others (Coady, 1992). In most cases, deference to social testimony is not only rational but necessary; no individual can empirically verify more than a tiny fraction of the beliefs they hold. The problem arises when the testimonial chain is systematically corrupted — when the cultural consensus itself is wrong, and the normal corrective mechanisms (scientific publication, expert dissent, media criticism) fail to propagate.

### Media Amplification and the Post Hoc Fallacy

Korean media reporting on fan death follows a reliable template: a person is found dead, a fan was on, the fan is implicated. This reasoning instantiates the *post hoc ergo propter hoc* fallacy — the assumption that because event B followed event A, A caused B. The logical structure is identical to the ancient Roman practice of attributing military defeats to the failure to observe proper augury. The philosopher David Hume's analysis of induction is directly applicable here: we can observe constant conjunction (fan on, person dead) without this constituting evidence of causation, because the critical test — does the absence of the fan prevent the death? — is never performed (Hume, 1748/2000). In hundreds of millions of instances each summer, Koreans sleep with fans on and survive. These non-events receive no coverage. The asymmetry of salience between rare deaths and universal survival creates a systematically distorted evidential sample.

The evolutionary psychologists Leda Cosmides and John Tooby have argued that human cognitive architecture is optimized for detecting threats and constructing causal stories about dangers, even at the cost of high false-positive rates (Cosmides & Tooby, 1994). In the ancestral environment, the cost of falsely attributing danger to a novel stimulus was low (unnecessary avoidance), while the cost of failing to detect a real danger was catastrophic (death). This "smoke detector principle," articulated by the evolutionary psychiatrist Randolph Nesse (Nesse, 2005), predicts exactly the pattern we observe: a strong cultural tendency to maintain the belief that fans are dangerous, because the perceived cost of being wrong about the danger (death) vastly exceeds the perceived cost of unnecessary caution (using a timer).

### Official Endorsement and Institutional Lock-In

The Korean government's role is crucial. When the Korea Consumer Protection Board issued its 2006 warning about fan death, it transformed the belief from folk wisdom into officially sanctioned public health guidance. This created what institutional economists call "lock-in" — the self-reinforcing dynamic in which early adoption of a standard (even a suboptimal one) becomes increasingly costly to reverse as infrastructure, expectations, and complementary investments accumulate around it (Arthur, 1989). Fan manufacturers built timers into their products. Consumers came to expect timers. The presence of timers was itself read as evidence that the danger was real — why else would the manufacturer include a safety feature? The result is a closed loop of mutual reinforcement between cultural belief, institutional endorsement, and product design that is extraordinarily resistant to correction.

### The Kernel of Truth Problem

There is a seductive near-truth embedded in the fan death myth that complicates debunking. Fans genuinely can be counterproductive during extreme heat events. When ambient temperature exceeds skin temperature (approximately 35°C) and humidity is high, a fan accelerates convective heat gain without proportionately increasing evaporative cooling, potentially worsening heat stress (Jay et al., 2015). Public health authorities in Australia and Europe have warned against fan use during extreme heat waves for precisely this reason. But this is not "fan death" as Koreans understand it — it is a specific thermodynamic scenario in which the fan's normal cooling mechanism reverses under extreme conditions, and it requires already-dangerous ambient heat. The existence of this real, narrow risk creates a "kernel of truth" that the broader myth parasitizes, making it seem less implausible than it actually is.

Stephen Jay Gould, writing on the persistence of scientific myths, observed that false beliefs survive not because they lack all contact with reality but because they contain just enough truth to make them unfalsifiable by casual observation (Gould, 1991). Fan death fits this pattern precisely: fans are associated with summer, summer is associated with death (from heat), and the fan's presence at the scene of a heat death creates a causal narrative that is wrong in its mechanism but correct in its setting.

## §4. Verdict

**False.** Confidence: **Very High.**

There is no scientifically valid mechanism by which an electric fan operating in a closed room can cause death. The proposed mechanisms — hypothermia, oxygen depletion, CO₂ emission, and molecular disruption — are each refuted by elementary physics, chemistry, and physiology. The belief persists as a cultural institution sustained by media reporting practices, government endorsement, and the self-reinforcing dynamics of social testimony, not by empirical evidence.

This does not mean that people do not die during summer in rooms where fans are running. They do. But the causes are heat-related illness, cardiovascular events, alcohol intoxication, and other medical conditions — not the fan. The fan is an incidental feature of the scene, not the cause of death. Attributing the death to the fan is a textbook instance of the *post hoc ergo propter hoc* fallacy operating at the scale of an entire culture.

## §5. The Wider Picture

### Cross-Cultural Health Myths

Fan death is not unique in kind, only in specificity. Across cultures, deeply held health beliefs persist in the absence of — or in direct contradiction to — scientific evidence. In Germany and much of Central Europe, the concept of *Durchzug* (draft) holds that exposure to moving air, particularly cross-ventilation in a room, causes illness ranging from the common cold to stiff necks and facial paralysis (Henning, 2017). As with fan death, the belief is embedded in everyday behavior: Germans routinely close windows on trains and in offices to avoid drafts, and the practice is socially enforced. In Latin American and Mediterranean cultures, *mal de ojo* (evil eye) attributes illness, particularly in children, to the envious gaze of another person, a belief that shapes social interactions around infants and has associated folk remedies (Dundes, 1981). In Traditional Chinese Medicine, disruptions to *qi* (vital energy flow) are invoked to explain pathologies that biomedical science attributes to entirely different mechanisms (Unschuld, 2010).

What these cross-cultural cases reveal is that the relationship between folk medicine and scientific medicine is not one of simple ignorance awaiting correction. Folk health beliefs are embedded in social practices, institutional structures, and identity. They are maintained not because individuals lack access to contradictory information, but because the belief serves social functions — anxiety management, community cohesion, cultural identity — that mere factual correction does not address. The medical anthropologist Arthur Kleinman argued that illness is always simultaneously a biological event and a cultural construction, and that "healing" in any culture addresses both dimensions (Kleinman, 1988). Fan death, in this light, is not merely a false belief about thermodynamics; it is a cultural institution that manages summertime anxiety about sudden death by providing a controllable cause (the fan, which you can turn off) in place of an uncontrollable one (cardiac arrest, heatstroke, which you cannot predict).

### Korean Media and the Sustainability of the Myth

The Korean media's role deserves particular scrutiny. Investigative journalism in Korea is robust in many domains — political corruption, corporate malfeasance, social inequality — but fan death has remained largely unchallenged in mainstream outlets. Some Korean physicians and scientists have publicly debunked the belief. Dr. Lee Yun-sung, a professor of environmental health at Seoul National University, has stated unequivocally that fan death has no medical basis (Lee, as quoted in Jennings, 2007). The Korea Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (KCDC) has gradually moved away from affirming fan death in its public health communications. Yet the broader media ecosystem continues to report fan-related deaths uncritically, illustrating what the sociologist Pierre Bourdieu described as the power of "doxa" — beliefs so deeply taken for granted that they are not even recognized as beliefs, and therefore cannot be subjected to deliberate scrutiny (Bourdieu, 1977).

### Product Design as Cultural Artifact

The timer-equipped fan is one of the most tangible artifacts of this myth. Every Korean fan manufacturer — Samsung, LG, Shinil, and others — includes an automatic shut-off timer as a standard feature. This engineering decision, originally a response to consumer safety concerns rooted in fan death belief, has become self-reinforcing. The timer is perceived as a safety feature; its presence confirms the danger; the confirmed danger justifies the timer. LG and Samsung have never publicly endorsed the fan death theory, but neither have they publicly disavowed it — they simply sell fans with timers, allowing the product design to speak for itself. This is a case study in how consumer product design can encode and perpetuate cultural beliefs, functioning as what the philosopher of technology Langdon Winner called an artifact with "politics" — a physical object that embeds and enforces social choices (Winner, 1980).

### Local Hegemony vs. Global Consensus

Perhaps the most epistemologically striking feature of fan death is the coexistence of local hegemony and global scientific consensus. There is no debate in the international scientific community about whether fans can cause death; the question has never been taken seriously enough to warrant a peer-reviewed study, because the mechanisms are refuted *a priori* by well-established physics. Yet within Korea, the belief has commanded widespread assent among lay people and, until recently, institutional endorsement from government agencies and media. This asymmetry demonstrates that scientific consensus is not automatically self-propagating across cultural boundaries. The sociologist of knowledge David Bloor's "symmetry principle" — the injunction to explain true and false beliefs by the same types of social causes — is directly applicable: the same social mechanisms (testimony, institutional endorsement, media amplification) that sustain true beliefs in some contexts sustain false beliefs in others (Bloor, 1976). Fan death survives not because Korean culture is less rational than others, but because it occupies a niche in the Korean epistemic ecosystem where the normal corrective pressures of scientific communication have been insufficient to dislodge it.

## §6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

### The Snopes Approach

Snopes addresses fan death in a fact-check entry that rates the claim as "False." The entry describes the belief, notes its prevalence in South Korea, lists the proposed mechanisms, and provides basic scientific refutation. The treatment is clear, accurate, and accessible — hallmarks of Snopes' editorial approach.

What Snopes does well is precisely the debunking function: marshaling the relevant scientific facts, assessing the claim's truth value, and rendering a verdict. Snopes draws attention to the Korean Consumer Protection Board's role, the media reporting pattern, and the cultural specificity of the belief. As a fact-check, it accomplishes its primary purpose — giving a reader who has encountered the claim a reliable assessment of its veracity.

### What Fact-Checkers Don't Engage With

What Snopes does not attempt — and what the format of a fact-check is not designed to provide — is the deeper cultural analysis that fan death demands. Why does this particular false belief persist in this particular society? What does its persistence reveal about the social architecture of belief formation? How does the interaction between media, government, industry, and folk wisdom create self-sustaining epistemic structures that are resistant to correction?

These questions are not within the scope of a fact-check, and it would be unfair to criticize Snopes for not addressing them. But they are precisely the questions that matter most for understanding how misinformation works in practice. The fact-checking model assumes that false beliefs persist because of insufficient access to correct information — that the remedy for misinformation is information. Fan death challenges this assumption. Correct information about basic physics and physiology has been available in Korean for decades. Korean scientists have publicly debunked the myth. Yet the belief persists, because it is sustained not by information deficit but by social structure.

### The Challenge of Culturally-Embedded Beliefs

Fan death illustrates a broader challenge for the global fact-checking enterprise. The tools of fact-checking — evidence gathering, source verification, expert consultation, truth-value rating — are optimized for claims that are empirically testable and socially contested. They are less effective against claims that are empirically false but socially uncontested within a given culture. When a belief is part of the cultural furniture, debunking it requires not just providing contrary evidence but disrupting the social mechanisms that sustain it — media norms, institutional incentives, product design conventions, and the everyday practices of interpersonal communication.

This is not a failure of fact-checking but a recognition of its limits. The Korean fan death myth will not be dissolved by a Snopes article, however accurate. It requires a slow, multi-institutional process of cultural change — one that is, in fact, already underway, as younger Koreans with more exposure to international scientific norms increasingly treat fan death with skepticism (Surridge, 2013). The myth's eventual decline, if it comes, will be a generational process driven by cultural globalization and changing media ecosystems, not by any single act of debunking. In the meantime, fan death stands as a salutary reminder that the truth is not self-executing — that knowing what is true and making that knowledge socially effective are two very different achievements.
