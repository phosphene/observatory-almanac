# Fascism, Corporations, and Merriam-Webster

## 1. The Claim

In April 2018, a widely shared meme published by the Facebook page "Americans Against Fascism" attributed the following claim to filmmaker Adam McKay: "Benito Mussolini created the word 'fascism.' He defined it as 'the merging of the state and the corporation.' He also said a more accurate word would be 'corporatism.' This was the definition in Webster's up until 1987 when a corporation bought Webster's and changed it to exclude any mention of corporations."

The meme's composite claim was that Merriam-Webster deliberately changed its definition of fascism to excise any reference to corporations or corporatism, and that this change was a direct result of a corporate takeover that placed the dictionary under control of interests that did not want the public connecting modern corporations with fascism. The implication was not subtle: a powerful corporate entity had literally censored the historical record to protect its own reputation.

The quote was drawn from a 2010 op-ed McKay wrote for the *Huffington Post*, in which he offered a blistering critique of the U.S. Supreme Court's *Citizens United* decision. In his op-ed, McKay wrote with rhetorical passion rather than scholarly precision, and he described four justices' votes in the case as "treason." The meme extracted his words from this polemical context and presented them as settled fact, stripping away nuance and attribution while adding a conspiratorial narrative about dictionary censorship.

The claim circulated widely because it felt true to many readers: it confirmed suspicions about corporate influence, provided a neat historical parallel between modern capitalism and fascism, and offered a satisfying story about the suppression of inconvenient facts. The meme's appeal lay not in its accuracy but in its emotional and political resonance. As with many successful pieces of political disinformation, it was structured around a kernel of recognizable truth (concerns about corporate power in politics) wrapped in a fabric of falsehood (a specific historical narrative about dictionary censorship).

Snopes investigated the claim in April 2018, with journalist Dan MacGuill rating it "False." The investigation revealed multiple layers of error: Mussolini almost certainly did not give that precise definition; the "corporatism" of Italian fascism bore little resemblance to modern corporations; and the supposed 1987 dictionary censorship never occurred.

## 2. What's Actually True

The claim about Merriam-Webster's ownership is grounded in a real corporate history, but the details were scrambled. Merriam-Webster has indeed changed hands over its long history, but the critical transition cited by the meme—a corporate takeover in 1987 that purportedly led to definition censorship—simply did not occur. Encyclopædia Britannica, Inc., acquired the company that became Merriam-Webster in 1964. The company has operated as Merriam-Webster, Incorporated, since 1982, and it has been owned by Encyclopædia Britannica throughout. There was no 1987 takeover, no new corporate owner with a PR agenda in 1987, and no documented instance of Merriam-Webster's definitions being altered to protect corporate interests at any point in its history.

The claim about Mussolini's definition of fascism has a more complicated relationship with the historical record. The political scientist Roger Scruton's *Dictionary of Political Thought* describes the Italian concept of *corporativismo* as a system in which the economy was divided into state-controlled associations, or "syndicates," representing workers, employers, and the professions. Only one syndicate was permitted per industry, and all officials were either fascist party members or loyal to the regime. The "corporations" in this system united the syndicates within a given industry but made no pretense of autonomy from the state. This was not a system of free-market capitalism; it was a state-directed economic control mechanism.

Mussolini's own writings support this understanding. In his 1927 speech, he declared: "Everything in the state, nothing against the State, nothing outside the state." In *The Doctrine of Fascism* (1932), co-authored with Giovanni Gentile, he wrote: "Fascism desires the state to be strong and organic, based on broad foundations of popular support. The Fascist State lays claim to rule in the economic field no less than in others; it makes its action felt throughout the length and breadth of the country by means of its corporative, social, and educational institutions." This is a vision of total state control over economic activity, not a system that empowers independent private corporations.

It is also true that *Webster's Third New International Dictionary* was published in 1961 and that its definition of fascism has remained essentially stable since then, long before and long after any alleged 1987 changes. The current Merriam-Webster online definition defines fascism as "a political philosophy, movement, or regime (such as that of the Fascisti) that exalts nation and often race above the individual and that stands for a centralized autocratic government headed by a dictatorial leader, severe economic and social regimentation, and forcible suppression of opposition." The word "corporation" does not appear in this definition, but it has not been excised; it was never present in the first place.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's "False" rating was entirely correct, and the investigation by Dan MacGuill was thorough and well-researched. The article effectively explained the distinction between fascist corporatism and modern corporate capitalism—a distinction that is absolutely critical and almost never made in the popular discourse surrounding this claim. The article's description of the state-controlled *corporazione* system in Fascist Italy was accurate and appropriately sourced to Scruton and to Mussolini's own writings. The article also correctly identified that Merriam-Webster had been owned by Encyclopædia Britannica since 1964, definitively disproving the 1987 takeover narrative.

The article's sourcing was appropriate, drawing on both the Encyclopædia Britannica corporate history and the writings of Mussolini, Gentile, and Scruton. The inclusion of the Skeptical Libertarian blog as a source on the Mussolini quote's questionable attribution was reasonable, as was the citation of Political Research Associates for additional context on corporatism.

However, the Snopes article could have been stronger in several dimensions. First, while the article correctly identified the conflation between fascist corporatism and modern corporations, it did not explore the linguistic and conceptual reasons why this confusion is so persistent. The English word "corporatism" shares its Latin root ("corpus," meaning body) with "corporation," and both refer to organized collective entities. The semantic similarity makes the confusion nearly inevitable for nonspecialist readers, particularly when the terms appear in the informal context of political memes rather than academic scholarship. A brief discussion of the etymological relationship would have equipped readers to spot similar conflations in the future.

Second, the article did not sufficiently engage with the legitimate concerns about corporate power in politics that gave the meme its resonance. While debunking a false claim, there is a risk of inadvertently dismissing the real issues that made the claim attractive in the first place. The article could have acknowledged that while the specific narrative about dictionary censorship was false, the broader concern about corporate influence on democratic governance is a subject of genuine and legitimate debate. This would have strengthened the article by distinguishing between debunking false facts and dismissing legitimate concerns.

Third, the article could have explored why certain false narratives about fascism and corporations persist and what they reveal about American political discourse. The repeated appearance of this and similar claims suggests that many Americans struggle to find an adequate vocabulary for describing the relationship between economic and political power, and they reach for "fascism" as a catch-all term of condemnation without understanding its specific historical meaning. This linguistic imprecision is itself a phenomenon worth examining.

Finally, the article did not address the role of the *Citizens United* decision as the broader context for McKay's original op-ed. The case, which held that corporate spending on political campaigns is protected speech under the First Amendment, remains controversial. Understanding this context helps readers see why McKay was writing what he wrote, even as it does not validate the factual errors in his argument.

## 4. Why This Matters

The persistence of the "Merriam-Webster fascism definition" meme matters for several reasons that extend far beyond lexicography. The claim is a textbook example of what might be called "confirmatory disinformation"—false information that spreads not because it is well-crafted or well-sourced but because it confirms what its audience already believes. For readers who are already concerned about corporate influence in politics, the claim that a corporate takeover led to the censorship of a dictionary definition feels instantly plausible, regardless of its evidentiary basis. The emotional truth of the narrative overrides the factual questions.

This case also illustrates the challenge of correcting disinformation when the correction must engage with complex historical and conceptual material. The truth about Mussolini's definition of fascism, the Italian corporatist system, and Merriam-Webster's ownership history is genuinely complicated. It requires readers to understand a distinction between two different meanings of the same root word—"corporation" as a state-controlled body in fascist Italy versus "corporation" as an independent private enterprise in modern capitalism. Communicating this distinction in a format that can compete with a simple, emotionally satisfying meme is extremely difficult.

The meme also reveals something important about the rhetorical use of the word "fascism" in contemporary American politics. The term has become so widely used as a general-purpose political epithet that its specific historical content has been diluted almost to the point of meaninglessness. When every political opponent is called a fascist, the word loses its power to describe actual fascist movements, and the public loses the ability to recognize genuine authoritarian threats. The meme's errors were, in this sense, not merely factual but conceptual: they reflected and reinforced a broader confusion about what fascism actually was and is.

The dictionary aspect of the claim is particularly significant. Trust in authoritative institutions—including reference works—has declined in an era of information fragmentation and political polarization. The claim that a dictionary deliberately changed its definitions to serve corporate interests feeds a narrative in which all institutional authority is suspect and all knowledge is merely a reflection of power. This epistemological nihilism, in which no source can be trusted and every fact is a political construct, is a hallmark of the contemporary disinformation environment. It makes correction impossible not because people cannot access the truth but because they no longer believe that truth exists.

## 5. Research Notes

The 1987 date that appears in the meme is itself a curious detail worth investigating. The choice of 1987 may reflect a conflation with the consolidation of the American dictionary market in the 1980s. In 1984, Encyclopædia Britannica (which already owned Merriam-Webster) integrated Merriam-Webster's operations more closely with its own distribution network, but this was a business reorganization rather than a takeover by a new corporate entity. Alternatively, the date may refer to William J. Bennett's appointment as Secretary of Education (1985-1988) and the subsequent culture wars over curriculum and textbooks, which created public awareness of and sensitivity to battles over the content of reference works. The exact origin of the 1987 date in the meme is unclear, but it appears to be a fabrication or a borrowing from unrelated events.

The Adam McKay op-ed that spawned the meme was published on January 27, 2010, in the immediate aftermath of the *Citizens United* decision (January 21, 2010). The timing is important because it shows that the op-ed was part of a wave of passionate responses to a controversial Supreme Court ruling. McKay's rhetorical excesses—including accusing four justices of treason—should be understood in this context. His argument was not a scholarly contribution to political theory but a polemical intervention in a heated political debate. The meme's error was extracting the factual claims from this rhetorical context and presenting them as objective truth.

The persistence of the Mussolini quote attribution is a case study in the dynamics of "zombie facts"—false claims that are repeatedly debunked but never die. The quote appears in various forms across the internet, attributed sometimes to Mussolini, sometimes to Gentile, and sometimes to "Mussolini and Gentile." The Skeptical Libertarian blog traced the likely origin of the quote to later commentators who paraphrased Mussolini's ideas, and the paraphrase gradually hardened into a direct quotation through repeated use. This is a common mechanism in the creation of fake quotations and one that fact-checkers should be aware of when evaluating claims of the form "[Historical figure] said [memorable phrase]."

The response to Snopes's debunking is also instructive. Followers of the "Americans Against Fascism" Facebook page and other communities that had shared the meme frequently rejected the correction, arguing that Snopes was itself an instrument of corporate interests or that the distinction between fascist corporatism and modern corporations was irrelevant. This response pattern is characteristic of "post-truth" disinformation: the correction is rejected not because it is factually wrong but because it conflicts with a worldview in which all information is politically motivated.

## 6. Verdict

**Snopes Rating: False — Upheld.**

The claim that Merriam-Webster changed its definition of fascism in 1987 after a corporate takeover to remove any mention of corporations is entirely false. Merriam-Webster has been owned by Encyclopædia Britannica since 1964, not since 1987. The definition of fascism has never included the word "corporation" in any standard Merriam-Webster dictionary. And the version of Mussolini's definition cited in the meme—"the merging of the state and the corporation"—is of uncertain attribution and reflects a fundamental misunderstanding of what corporatism meant in Italian fascism.

Snopes correctly identified all of these errors and provided thorough documentation. The "False" rating was appropriate and the investigation was well-sourced. If any criticism is warranted, it is that the article could have done more to address the linguistic reasons for the persistent confusion, the legitimate political concerns that gave the meme its emotional appeal, and the broader pattern of confirmatory disinformation of which this claim is a representative example.

The Truth Vault affirms the Snopes rating while recommending that readers understand the distinction between fascist corporatism and modern corporate capitalism. The word "corporatism" in Mussolini's Italy referred to a state-controlled system of economic organization in which all independent associations were subordinated to the fascist state. It had almost nothing in common with the modern corporate form—an independent, privately-owned legal entity operating within a market economy. Conflating the two based on the similarity of the words "corporatism" and "corporation" is a linguistic mistake that leads to genuine historical misunderstanding.

The larger lesson is about the nature of confirmatory disinformation: claims that feel emotionally true to their target audience will spread regardless of their factual accuracy. The antidote is not simply better fact-checking but also a more sophisticated public understanding of how language works, how historical concepts differ from their modern homonyms, and why the fact that a claim "feels right" does not make it accurate.
