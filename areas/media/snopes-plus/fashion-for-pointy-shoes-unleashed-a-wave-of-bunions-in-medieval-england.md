# Fashion for Pointy Shoes Unleashed a Wave of Bunions in Medieval England


## 1. The Claim

The claim is this: a medieval fashion for absurdly elongated, pointed footwear—known variously as poulaines, crakows, or crakows—caused a measurable epidemic of bunions across England and Western Europe, leaving physical evidence in the skeletal record that persists seven centuries later as a traceable public health injury.

The shoes at the center of this claim were genuinely extreme by any standard. Poulaines, which rose to fashionable dominance in England from roughly the mid-14th century through the 1480s, featured toes that extended anywhere from a few inches to, in the most extreme court examples, two feet beyond the end of the wearer's actual foot. The tips were often stuffed with moss, wool, or hair to maintain their pointed shape. Chains were sometimes attached from the tip of the shoe to the knee, preventing the wearer from taking a full stride. Chroniclers of the period mocked the style with varying degrees of outrage; a notable sumptuary intervention attributed to Edward IV around 1463 attempted to cap toe length at two inches beyond the foot for common people, while the nobility enjoyed somewhat longer allowances. The shoes became not merely a fashion accessory but a social signal system—their preposterous impracticality communicating, without words, that the wearer did not engage in labor.

The claim about bunions goes beyond cultural history. It argues that these shoes produced a specific, diagnosable orthopedic deformity—hallux valgus—at significantly elevated rates in the populations that wore them, and that the skeletal evidence from medieval English burial sites provides forensic confirmation of this causal relationship. This is a precise empirical claim that can be evaluated against archaeological and bioarchaeological evidence, and evaluated it has been, with results remarkable in their specificity.

The framing "a wave of bunions" might sound like tabloid hyperbole, but it maps onto a real statistical pattern in the archaeological record. When researchers from the University of Cambridge examined hundreds of medieval skeletons across different time periods and social contexts, they found exactly the kind of temporal correlation the claim predicts—and the numbers are dramatic enough to warrant the word "wave."


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim is substantiated. A landmark 2021 study published in the *International Journal of Paleopathology*, led by archaeologist Piers Mitchell of the University of Cambridge, examined 177 skeletal individuals recovered from four cemeteries in and around Cambridge: a rural agricultural cemetery, a parish graveyard serving working-class urban residents, a charitable hospital, and the Augustinian friary of St. Andrew the Less, which served clergy and wealthy urban benefactors.

The temporal comparison is the study's most striking finding. Among skeletons dating to the 11th through 13th centuries—before poulaines became fashionable—only 6% of individuals showed signs of hallux valgus. In the 14th and 15th centuries, the peak poulaine era, that figure rose to 27%. That is a fourfold increase in the prevalence of a specific, diagnosable foot deformity, coinciding precisely with the chronological window during which the pointed-toe fashion dominated.

The social stratification data adds a second, powerful layer of evidence. If the causal mechanism was fashion—shoes worn only by those wealthy enough to afford the latest styles—then individuals buried at higher-status locations should show more bunions than those from rural or working-class contexts. That is exactly what the data reveals: only 3% of skeletons from the rural cemetery displayed hallux valgus. The parish graveyard produced 10%. The charitable hospital showed 23%. At the friary, burial ground of wealthy benefactors and clergy, 43% of individuals had bunions. Social status and bunion prevalence tracked each other with unusual consistency, and social status also tracked access to fashionable urban footwear.

The biological mechanism for this relationship is well understood in modern orthopedic medicine. Hallux valgus is a progressive deformity in which the first metatarsophalangeal joint—the joint connecting the big toe to the foot—is forced into a lateral angle. The big toe increasingly points toward the smaller toes, the metatarsal head develops a bony prominence on the medial side of the foot (the "bunion" proper), and the joint itself becomes vulnerable to pain, inflammation, and in advanced cases, significantly impaired mobility. Tight footwear that constricts the forefoot, forces the toes together, and compresses the big toe toward an unnatural angle is among the most well-established precipitating causes in the clinical literature.

Poulaines did precisely this. Their narrow, tapering design forced the toes—particularly the first and second toes—into compressive, lateral contact. The constriction was not merely aesthetic but structural: the pointed shape could only accommodate the foot's natural width up to a point, beyond which the toes had to bend or be compressed. Worn daily over years or decades, beginning in childhood or adolescence in many cases, this mechanical stress would predictably produce the angular joint changes that characterize hallux valgus in mature bone.

The Mitchell team also identified a secondary finding with significant implications: individuals with hallux valgus in the medieval sample showed substantially higher rates of healed fractures, particularly in the bones of the hand and wrist. The pattern is consistent with fall injuries—specifically, the automatic reflexive outstretching of hands to break a fall. In modern clinical literature, hallux valgus is an established risk factor for balance impairment and falls in older adults. The medieval skeletal data suggests this relationship held in the 14th and 15th centuries as well: the fashionable shoes that produced bunions also made their wearers less stable on their feet, more prone to stumbling, and more likely to sustain fall-related fractures as they aged.

The Cambridge study did not stand alone. Independent research had already noted elevated hallux valgus rates in medieval European populations, and bioarchaeological evidence from other English sites had suggested correlations between urbanization, wealth, and foot pathology during the same period. The Mitchell study concentrated and quantified this evidence in a way that made the causal story difficult to dismiss. The convergence of temporal, social, and biomechanical evidence produces a case that satisfies multiple independent evidentiary standards.

Historians of fashion had long suspected the relationship between poulaines and foot health, but the skeletal evidence provides a specificity that documentary sources cannot. Chronicles and sumptuary laws tell us the shoes existed and were controversial; the bones tell us exactly what they did to the people who wore them.

The poulaine fashion declined sharply in the 1480s, replaced first by broader, rounder-toed styles before the square-toed shoes of the Tudor period became dominant. Whether the decline in fashion had any measurable effect on subsequent generations' bunion rates is beyond what current data allows, but the temporal correlation between the style's rise and the spike in hallux valgus is among the cleaner cause-and-effect demonstrations in the entire field of historical epidemiology.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The claim about poulaines and bunions succeeds as a cultural story because it combines several powerful elements: the recognizable dynamics of fashion status signaling, the visceral image of absurdly impractical footwear, and the satisfying moral logic of vanity carrying a physical price. It has the structure of a folk cautionary tale—pride punished by the body—wrapped in the credibility of archaeological science.

The story's circulation received significant boost from the 2021 Cambridge study, which produced findings concrete enough to generate mainstream science journalism. Smithsonian Magazine, The Guardian, Science Daily, and dozens of regional outlets covered the research with genuine enthusiasm. The image of medieval elites hobbling on deformed feet because they wanted to signal status is inherently compelling and easily shareable.

But there is also a subtler reason the claim resonates: it is genuinely illuminating. Fashion history and health history are rarely studied in conjunction in this way, and the poulaine case demonstrates with unusual clarity how social pressures—status competition, conspicuous impracticality, aspirational emulation up the social ladder—can produce population-level health consequences. This is a message with contemporary resonance. High-heeled shoes, narrow dress shoes for men, and tight fashionable footwear of many kinds continue to produce hallux valgus and other foot deformities in modern populations. The medieval poulaine story functions as a deepened historical context for an ongoing pattern.

The claim also benefits from the general credibility boost that comes when archaeology confirms an intuitive suspicion. Many people who encounter the story already believed, sensibly, that such extreme footwear must have damaged people's feet. The study confirms the intuition with data. This kind of narrative, where science validates common sense and adds detail, is uniquely satisfying and tends to stick.

Medieval history in general is prone to distortion—stories about the Middle Ages often flatten complexity into caricature—but the poulaine story is one of the cases where a "medieval weirdness" narrative emerges from genuine evidence rather than myth. It is both colorful and true.


## 4. Verdict

**AFFIRMED**: The claim is substantially accurate. The archaeological record provides strong, multi-layered evidence that the medieval fashion for poulaine shoes produced a measurable increase in hallux valgus across 14th- and 15th-century English populations. The temporal correlation—bunion prevalence rising fourfold from the pre-poulaine era to the poulaine peak—combined with the social stratification data (wealthy benefactors at 43%, rural laborers at 3%) and the well-understood biomechanical mechanism make a compelling and essentially uncontested case.

The word "wave" in the popular formulation is a slight dramatic compression—the increase happened over roughly a century rather than in a sudden outbreak—but it accurately captures the scale of the shift. A fourfold increase in a specific orthopedic deformity across a population constitutes, in any epidemiological framework, a meaningful wave. The causal attribution to poulaine fashion is well-supported by the convergence of temporal, demographic, and biomechanical evidence.

Minor qualifications remain. The Cambridge study drew from 177 skeletons, which is a meaningful sample but not a population census. The assignment of skeletons to social strata depends on burial location, which is a reasonable but not infallible proxy. And the study focused on Cambridge, which means conclusions about "medieval England" as a whole involve inference beyond the specific data.

But these are standard qualifications in bioarchaeology, not grounds for skepticism about the core finding. The evidence is, by the standards of the field, unusually robust, and the claim passes examination.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The poulaine story is embedded in a broader history of fashion as health hazard that extends from antiquity to the present. Understanding this historical dimension illuminates contemporary patterns in ways that purely medical framings often miss.

The history of status footwear and foot deformity is long. Chinese foot binding, practiced for roughly a millennium before its abolition in the early 20th century, is the most extreme example—a deliberate, systematic mutilation of girls' feet beginning in early childhood, producing permanent deformities in the service of beauty standards associated with wealth and marriageability. The poulaine case is obviously less extreme in its methods and consequences, but it shares the structural logic: footwear that signals social status at the cost of the wearer's physical integrity.

The social stratification data in the Cambridge study is philosophically interesting. The rural laborers who could not afford or did not participate in fashionable footwear—the 3% bunion group—ended up with healthier feet than the wealthy benefactors whose bones record the price of fashion at 43%. The poor, in this instance, were physically protected by their exclusion from elite consumption patterns. This inversion of conventional assumptions about wealth and health—that the rich fare better—is not unique to medieval foot biology but is a useful corrective to simplistic narratives.

Sumptuary laws provide a window into how the medieval state attempted to manage these dynamics. The 1463 statute bearing on poulaine toe length was not motivated by public health concerns but by sumptuary ideology—the principle that dress should correspond to rank, and that common people adopting aristocratic fashions constituted a kind of social disorder. The assumption was that ordinary people were damaging the social fabric by wearing shoes they couldn't properly afford; the damage to their feet was not part of the regulatory calculus. This attitude—using fashion regulation to enforce class hierarchy rather than protect health—is a recurring pattern in the history of sumptuary legislation.

The decline of poulaines in the 1480s coincided with broader shifts in late medieval and early Renaissance aesthetics and with the political turbulence of the Wars of the Roses, as new dynasties brought new courtly fashions. The Tudor period produced a dramatically different silhouette, including the broad-toed "duckbill" shoes that spread weight more evenly across the forefoot. Whether Tudor fashion inadvertently produced better foot health is an open archaeological question.

Modern parallels are striking. Epidemiological surveys consistently find elevated hallux valgus rates in populations wearing narrow, fashionable shoes—particularly women, who continue to bear disproportionate social pressure to wear high-heeled, narrow-toed footwear. Studies of hallux valgus in Asian countries following increased adoption of Western-style shoes have documented rapid rises in prevalence, mirroring the medieval pattern. The Cambridge team's identification of fall-related fractures as a secondary consequence of poulaine-induced bunions has direct contemporary relevance: falls and fall-related fractures in older adults represent a significant public health burden, and hallux valgus is an established modifiable risk factor.

The intersection of fashion history, bioarchaeology, and public health is a relatively young academic field, and the poulaine study exemplifies its potential. By reading the bones left behind by historical populations, researchers can reconstruct cause-and-effect relationships across centuries—recovering the unrecorded health costs of choices made in the service of beauty, status, and belonging.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not produced a dedicated fact-check on the claim that medieval pointy shoe fashion caused a bunion epidemic. The source underlying the popular claim—the 2021 Mitchell et al. study in the *International Journal of Paleopathology*—is primary research rather than an internet rumor, which means it does not slot neatly into Snopes's conventional format of debunking false viral claims. When a claim is true, firmly rooted in peer-reviewed archaeology, and simply underreported rather than actively misrepresented, fact-check coverage falls away.

What coverage exists tends to appear in science journalism outlets rather than dedicated fact-check platforms. The Smithsonian, the University of Cambridge's own press communications, and science aggregators like Science Daily covered the 2021 study accurately and in appropriate detail. These outlets conveyed the statistical findings, the social stratification data, and the secondary fracture connection without significant distortion.

At the level of popular retelling, some versions of the story do oversimplify. The "wave" framing can imply a sudden outbreak rather than a decades-long epidemiological trend. Some popular accounts treat the causal relationship as more unambiguously established than the study's authors present it—the researchers themselves use appropriately cautious language about correlation and inference. And some retellings strip away the social class dimension entirely, turning a nuanced story about fashion, inequality, and the distribution of health harms into a simpler "medieval fashion was crazy" narrative.

But these distortions are minor. The core historical claim—that extreme pointy shoe fashion produced elevated bunion rates in medieval English populations, and that this is evidenced in the skeletal record—reflects the scientific evidence accurately. The absence of Snopes coverage here represents a gap in fact-checking infrastructure rather than evidence of any problem with the claim itself. That gap is worth noting: claims that are simply true and historically significant, but not currently circulating as false viral memes, tend to receive less formal fact-checking attention. The Truth Vault exists partly to address exactly this imbalance—to surface claims that have clear evidentiary support and deserve more authoritative public documentation.
