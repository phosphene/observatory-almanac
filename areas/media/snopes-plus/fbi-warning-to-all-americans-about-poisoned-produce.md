## §1 — Claim & Verdict Summary

On 18 July 2016, the website Q Political published an article headlined "FBI Issues Horrifying Warning to Frequent Grocery Shoppers," asserting that the FBI had issued a general, nationwide warning to all American consumers about the possibility that grocery store produce might have been deliberately contaminated with poison. The article opened with emotionally loaded language ("I can't believe this!"; "Every American needs to see this!") and described a "young man" who had visited multiple grocery stores to spray a "poisonous mixture on open food throughout their produce sections."

Snopes rated the claim **False**. What had actually happened was a localized law enforcement and public health event in Ann Arbor, Michigan, in the spring of 2016: the FBI arrested a man suspected of contaminating food at open salad bars and olive bars in several grocery stores in the Ann Arbor metropolitan area, using a spray containing a mouse poison, hand cleaner, and water. The Michigan Department of Agriculture and Rural Development (MDARD) issued a regional advisory recommending that consumers discard salad bar and ready-to-eat foods purchased at specified stores between mid-March and end of April 2016. No one was reported to have been sickened. The advisory was limited in scope, targeted, and issued in May 2016 — nearly two months before Q Political's July article.

The FBI did not issue any warning to "all Americans" about grocery store produce. The specific suspect had been arrested in early May 2016. The story Q Political leveraged was two months old by the time of publication and had already been resolved. The transformation from "regional food safety advisory in Ann Arbor in May" to "FBI warning to all Americans in July" is a textbook case of **scope inflation** in viral news production.

## §2 — Evidential Foundation

The factual substrate of the story, confirmed by the original Detroit Free Press reporting from May 2016, is as follows: law enforcement identified an individual suspected of spraying a liquid mixture — containing mouse poison (brodifacoum or a similar anticoagulant rodenticide), hand cleaner, and water — onto open food displays at multiple grocery stores in the Ann Arbor, Michigan area (Saline, Midland, and Birch Run store locations were also mentioned). The FBI investigated the case because of the potential for widespread consumer harm and the multi-jurisdictional nature of the alleged conduct. The suspect was arrested in early May 2016.

The Michigan Department of Agriculture and Rural Development (MDARD), not the FBI, issued the consumer advisory. MDARD Director Jamie Clover Adams stated: "Out of an abundance of caution and to protect public health and food safety, I encourage consumers to dispose of any foods purchased from salad bars, olive bars and ready-to-eat hot and cold food areas from these stores between mid-March and the end of April." This advisory was explicitly regional, explicitly time-bounded (a six-week window), and explicitly limited to specific store types and food preparation areas (open salad bars, olive bars, hot and cold ready-to-eat sections). MDARD also stated that, based on the composition of the spray mixture at the quantities involved, they did not anticipate adverse health effects on individuals who may have consumed affected food.

Snopes directly contacted MDARD in July 2016 to verify whether Q Political's characterization was accurate. The MDARD representative confirmed that: (1) the agency had issued the original advisory in May 2016 "in an abundance of caution"; (2) no one had been reported sick from the incidents; (3) the suspect had been arrested in early May 2016; and (4) MDARD had confirmed with the FBI that no nationwide warning to grocery shoppers had been issued by the Bureau.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Scope Inflation and the Geography of Risk Communication

The Q Political article transforms a resolved regional public health event into an ongoing, nationwide alert. This transformation involves a specific and reproducible manipulation technique that can be called **scope inflation**: taking a claim that is true within some defined geographic, temporal, or demographic boundary and expanding it to apply universally. The inflated version generates far more fear and shares than the accurate version because it implicates millions of people who were not actually at risk.

Scope inflation in health news is closely related to, but distinct from, several other well-documented misinformation mechanisms. It is different from simple fabrication (the Ann Arbor events were real) and different from misrepresentation of individual facts (the FBI did investigate, a contamination event did occur). What makes it distinctive is that the error lies entirely in the magnitude and generality of the claim, not in the existence of the underlying event.

The risk communication literature has documented the consequences of scope inflation for public behavior. Loewenstein et al. (2001, *Risk Analysis*) found that risk perceptions are often non-linear with respect to scope: people respond to a reported threat affecting 1 in 10,000 people almost identically to how they respond to a threat affecting 1 in 10, a phenomenon the authors called "psychophysical numbing in reverse" — where scope amplification triggers alarm disproportionate to actual changed risk. This means that a claim about "all American grocery shoppers" generates approximately the same emotional response as an accurate claim about "grocery shoppers in Ann Arbor in spring 2016," but the former generates a vastly larger pool of people who feel personally implicated, and thus shares at a far greater rate.

A second epistemic dimension involves **temporal displacement**. The Q Political article published in July 2016 was reporting on events from March-May 2016, using present-tense alarming language ("Reports have now confirmed our worst fears") that implied ongoing, active jeopardy. The suspect had been arrested more than two months earlier. This temporal displacement is a recurring feature of recycled viral content: events that generated localized news coverage at their time of occurrence are rediscovered months later and republished without updating their temporal frame, allowing them to function as "new" threats in the awareness of audiences who missed the original coverage.

The **FBI branding** of the claim adds an additional epistemic layer. The FBI is an agency with high institutional credibility and strong associations with serious crime investigation. Attributing a warning to the FBI — rather than to MDARD or to a local health department — elevates the apparent severity and national significance of the claim. This is a form of what might be called **institutional authority laundering**: attaching a claim to a high-credibility institution that did not actually make it, using the institution's authority to enhance the claim's persuasive force. Pornpitakpan (2004, *Journal of Marketing Research*) documented that source credibility dramatically affects persuasion outcomes; attributing safety warnings to high-credibility federal agencies versus state-level agencies or local authorities significantly increases both belief and sharing behavior.

The epistemic novelty is the recognition that scope inflation, temporal displacement, and institutional authority laundering can each be present in a single article, as they are in the Q Political piece, and that their combination produces a viral unit far more robust than any single technique alone would generate.

## §4 — Institutional & Legal Context

The Ann Arbor food contamination investigation involved several institutional actors whose distinct roles were collapsed in the viral article. The FBI has jurisdiction over federal crimes, including intentional food contamination under 18 U.S.C. § 1365 (tampering with consumer products), which carries penalties of up to life imprisonment if death results. The Bureau's involvement in the Ann Arbor case was investigative — finding and arresting the suspect — not advisory. The FBI does not issue general consumer safety warnings; that role falls to the FDA, USDA, CDC, or relevant state agencies.

MDARD, which did issue the advisory, is a state-level agency with authority over food safety and agricultural product safety in Michigan. Its advisory was an appropriate exercise of its public health communication mandate: it provided targeted, bounded information to consumers who might have been exposed, recommended precautionary disposal of potentially affected foods, and communicated the state's assessment that severe health consequences were unlikely. The advisory was a model of appropriately scoped risk communication.

The federal food tampering law (18 U.S.C. § 1365) was added to the U.S. criminal code in 1983 following the Tylenol murders of 1982, in which someone laced bottles of Tylenol capsules with potassium cyanide, killing seven people in the Chicago area. That case generated massive national media coverage and consumer panic, and was the event that prompted both the federal tamper-resistance packaging regulations (21 C.F.R. § 211.132) and the criminal statute. The FBI's involvement in any food tampering investigation therefore carries the historical weight of one of the most serious product-tampering cases in American history, which may contribute to why attributing a "warning" to the FBI generates disproportionate fear.

The Q Political website was part of a broader ecosystem of politically oriented clickbait sites that published alarmist content designed to maximize engagement from conservative-leaning audiences. Snopes noted that the same article was republished by Q Political again in May 2017, nearly a year after the original publication and more than a year after the underlying events — illustrating the recirculation lifecycle of this type of content.

The MDARD advisory's focus on "open food bars" — salad bars, olive bars, hot and cold ready-to-eat stations — reflects the specific vulnerability of open, unpackaged food to environmental contamination. Grocery stores with open food stations present a different risk profile from packaged food because they lack the sealed packaging that makes product tampering visually evidenced. Post-Tylenol regulatory development focused primarily on packaged goods; the governance of open food stations falls primarily under state and local health code enforcement rather than federal tamper-resistance regulations.

## §5 — What Remains Genuinely Uncertain

**The identity and motive of the suspect** were not publicly disclosed in detail in the sources available to Snopes at the time of the fact-check. Whether the contamination was motivated by commercial extortion (as in some product-tampering cases), random malice, or other factors was not established in public reporting. Understanding motive is relevant because different motivational profiles imply different recurrence risks.

**The specific health effects, if any, from the spray mixture** remain partially uncertain. The mixture of mouse poison, hand cleaner, and water at the concentrations used was assessed by MDARD as unlikely to cause adverse health effects, but no systematic follow-up study of potentially exposed consumers was publicly reported. The toxicity of brodifacoum (a common anticoagulant rodenticide used in mouse poison) at diluted concentrations in the quantities likely deposited on open food is well-characterized in toxicology literature (Barnett et al., 1992, *Toxicology Letters*), but the specific mixture's composition and concentration in this case were not fully disclosed.

**Whether the Q Political article's recirculation in May 2017 reached any audience members who took protective action based on the false information** — for example, avoiding grocery store salad bars — is unknown. The public health cost of false food-safety warnings is measurable in principle: behavioral changes prompted by false alarms can involve unnecessary food waste, altered consumption patterns, and reduced trust in future accurate warnings. This secondary harm from scope inflation in food safety communication deserves more systematic study than it has received.

## §6 — Synthesis & Gap Classification

The FBI poisoned produce story is a well-evidenced false claim: the FBI issued no general warning, the relevant advisory was regional and limited, no one was sickened, and the suspect was arrested two months before the viral article's publication. The transformation from accurate, bounded local public health event to false, universal national threat involves a combination of scope inflation, temporal displacement, and institutional authority laundering that is characteristic of clickbait alarmism in the 2016 American media ecosystem.

**EARC classification: SI (Scope inflation, Institutionally attributed).** The claim's falsity is not about the existence of the underlying events but about the magnitude, geographic scope, institutional attribution, and ongoing relevance ascribed to them. The classification flags the specific mechanism of error so that similar future stories can be evaluated against the same structural template.

**Gap:** The primary epistemic gap is a systematic account of **secondary harm from nationally amplified local alerts**: how many people altered their food behavior in response to the Q Political article across its July 2016 and May 2017 circulation cycles, what those behaviors were, and whether they had measurable effects on grocery purchasing patterns. This is a specific instance of the broader question of how false food safety alarms harm public trust in genuine future alerts — a well-documented concern in risk communication research (Lofstedt & Renn, 1997, *Risk Management*, Palgrave) that lacks empirical grounding in specific viral misinformation case studies. A secondary gap involves the legal and public health system's capacity to correct scope-inflated claims: MDARD's accurate, bounded advisory was far less widely shared than Q Political's inflated version, and no institutional mechanism exists to push accurate corrections to the same audiences who received the false claim.
