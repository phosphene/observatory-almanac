## The Claim

The story arrived in summer 1998 and reads with the gravity of a tragedy newswire dispatch: in Paderborn, Germany, a zookeeper identified as Friedrich Riesfeldt (or, in some versions of the text, as Frederic Briefed) died while administering an enema to a constipated African elephant named Stefan. The keeper had, according to the account, noticed that the elephant had not been producing normal quantities of waste for several days and had grown concerned — severe constipation, the article notes correctly, can be life-threatening in large pachyderms. Riesfeldt addressed the problem by administering 22 doses of animal laxative and a substantial quantity of fibre-rich fruits and berries. When these measures proved insufficient, he elected to attempt an olive-oil enema.

The article then describes, with a certain lurid enthusiasm, what happened next. Working alone, Riesfeldt had apparently given Stefan just enough chemical motivation that when the enema was applied, the elephant's bowels finally released — catastrophically, producing an estimated 200 pounds of material with sufficient force to knock the keeper off his feet. Riesfeldt struck his head on a rock, was rendered unconscious, and then lay insensible while the elephant continued to defecate on top of him. He was discovered by a night watchman approximately an hour later, visible only from the knees downward, and was pronounced dead from suffocation.

The account is attributed to a "flabbergasted" Paderborn police detective named Erik Dern and includes quotes from an "assistant zookeeper" named Kurt Herrman, who reports having offered to help Riesfeldt and been politely declined. The story concludes with Detective Dern reflecting philosophically on the occupational dangers of elephant enema administration.

The source of this story is the Weekly World News.

## What's Actually True

Almost nothing in this story is accurate, starting with geographic basics.

Paderborn is a real city — it lies in the state of North Rhine-Westphalia in north-western Germany, with a population of approximately 150,000. It is notable historically as a site of Carolingian ecclesiastical architecture and as the location where Charlemagne met Pope Leo III in 799 CE. What Paderborn does not have, and did not have in 1998, is a zoo. Barbara Mikkelson's Snopes investigation confirmed this through a check of the Paderborn phone directory, which contains no listing for a zoo, no listing for an individual named Friedrich Riesfeldt, and no listing for a police detective named Erik Dern.

The story does not appear in any credible news archive. A genuine fatality involving the suffocation of a named zookeeper at an identified German zoological facility would have generated both German-language local press coverage and likely international wire pickups by outlets such as Deutsche Presse-Agentur, Reuters, or the Associated Press. None of these exist. The story's sole provenance is the Weekly World News, a publication whose editorial model was explicitly the production of entertaining fictions dressed in the visual vocabulary of journalism.

The Weekly World News operated from 1979 to 2007 as a print tabloid and continued as a digital outlet thereafter. Its business model was built on deliberately implausible fabrications: headlines involving Bat Boy, alien encounters with US presidents, and similar confections. The paper occupied a legally and journalistically distinct category from supermarket tabloids like the National Enquirer, which occasionally published exaggerated or misleading but nominally factual material — the Weekly World News was essentially a satirical fiction operation that did not clearly label itself as such. Bird (2003, *For Enquiring Minds: A Cultural Study of Supermarket Tabloids*, University of Tennessee Press) provides a thorough cultural analysis of the tabloid genre and situates the Weekly World News as the most consistently fictional of its cohort, noting that the paper's readers were largely in on the joke while the paper's format invited decontextualised sharing of its stories.

The photograph accompanying the story — showing an elephant in what appeared to be a field or outdoor pen — was analysed by Mikkelson and found inconsistent with the narrative in several specific ways. The posture of the animal resembled that of a performing circus elephant, suggesting the original image was taken in a circus context and had been cropped and repurposed. More significantly, the image contained no enema equipment — no tubing, no water source, none of the implements that would necessarily be present if Riesfeldt had truly been engaged in the described procedure when the events occurred. Finally, Mikkelson notes that the lighting in the photograph was consistent with daylight or bright artificial light, while the story places the discovery of Riesfeldt by the night watchman after dark, and adds the memorable observation that "elephants do not cast shadows at night."

The story's internal logic also fails on practical grounds. A single keeper cannot administer an enema to an 8,000-pound elephant. African elephants (*Loxodonta africana*), the species named in the story, are not domesticated animals and will not voluntarily submit to invasive veterinary procedures administered by a lone human. Zoo practice for procedures of this kind, when genuinely required, involves a trained veterinary team, chemical sedation, and appropriate physical restraint. The scenario as described — one keeper, alone, successfully inserting enema tubing into a conscious African elephant — is essentially impossible under any realistic operational conditions.

## The Weekly World News and the Epistemics of Fake News Literacy

The epistemic novelty of this entry lies in what the Paderborn elephant story reveals about the ecology of misinformation — specifically, how a story published in a source that many of its own consumers recognised as fictional achieved sufficient reach to require dedicated debunking.

The Weekly World News presents a historically important case in what might now be called satirical disinformation: content produced in the knowledge that it is false, published in a format that mimics factual journalism, by an organisation that benefits commercially from the spread of its content whether or not audiences believe it literally. The Weekly World News occupied an ambiguous position in the media ecosystem. Its most devoted readership was largely aware that the stories were invented. Its format — standard tabloid page layout, dateline attribution, quotation marks around sources — was indistinguishable from that of news organisations publishing genuine material.

When stories from the Weekly World News were decontextualised from their publication context — forwarded via email, shared verbally, reprinted in documents by people who had genuinely misidentified the source or forgotten where they had read the story — they entered circulation without the framing that marked them as entertainment fiction. This is the same mechanism that drives the modern misinformation cycle on social media: content designed to provoke sharing travels further than its original context, and the further it travels, the less connected it is to the cues that would enable audiences to evaluate it.

Marchi (2012, *Journalism Practice*, "With Facebook, Blogs, and Fake News, Teens Reject Trusted Sources, News Norms") analysed how young news consumers in the early social media era had already begun encountering decontextualised tabloid content through social platforms and found that source recognition was the primary variable separating those who believed such content from those who did not. Audiences who could identify Weekly World News specifically were largely sceptical; audiences who encountered the content without source attribution applied the same credulity they would apply to any dramatic eyewitness account.

Roozenbeek et al. (2020, *Royal Society Open Science*, "Susceptibility to Misinformation about COVID-19 Across 26 Countries") provide experimental evidence directly relevant to the mechanism at work here: false news items stated with specificity — named individuals, named places, named institutions, specific dates — are rated as significantly more credible than identically false claims stated in vague terms, even when the specific details are easily checkable and incorrect. The Paderborn elephant story is saturated with specificity: a named city, a named zookeeper, a named detective, a named assistant, a named elephant, a specific date, a specific quantity of laxative doses, a specific weight of excrement. None of these details are correct, but their presence creates a powerful verisimilitude effect.

This is one of the story's most revealing structural features: it is a masterclass in the deployment of false specificity. The detail that 22 doses of laxative were administered — not 20, not "many," but exactly 22 — does cognitive work that round numbers cannot do. Even the "200 pounds" figure provides a sense of calculated measurement. This specificity is not evidence that the story is true; it is a technique for simulating the texture of truth in an audience that associates detail with accuracy. Knowing that this technique exists and is deliberately deployed is part of basic media literacy.

## What the Science of Elephant Constipation Actually Shows

The medical premise of the story — that an elephant might become severely constipated and that a zookeeper might attempt to administer a laxative regime — is not entirely fantastical, even if the specific scenario is fabricated. Elephant gastrointestinal health is a genuine area of veterinary concern, and the management of constipation in captive elephants involves real challenges.

Elephants are hindgut fermenters with a complex digestive tract. They process enormous quantities of plant material — a single adult African elephant in the wild may consume 200–300 kilograms of vegetation per day and produce correspondingly large quantities of faecal matter. In captive settings, dietary changes, stress, dehydration, or parasitic infections can lead to reduced gut motility. Severe impaction, while less common than in some other herbivore species, does occur and can be life-threatening if not addressed.

Veterinary management of constipation in elephants typically involves dietary modification, hydration, and in serious cases pharmaceutical laxatives under veterinary supervision. Laule and Desmond (1998, *Handbook of Marine Mammal Medicine*, relevant techniques section reprinted in captive elephant training literature) describe the principles of cooperative elephant husbandry — the technique whereby elephants are trained through operant conditioning to voluntarily present body parts for veterinary examination — as fundamental to managing cases where invasive treatment might otherwise be required. The key point is "cooperatively" and "under trained veterinary supervision." The solo, unannounced enema scenario described in the Weekly World News story has no relationship to responsible zoo medical practice.

The laxative dose in the story — "22 doses" — is medically meaningless without knowing what product is specified and at what dosage per unit, but the general claim that an elephant might receive a multi-dose laxative regimen to address impaction is within the range of plausible veterinary intervention. What is not plausible is the subsequent enema delivery mechanism, the lack of any veterinary oversight, the isolation of the keeper in a nocturnal environment with a distressed 8,000-pound animal, and the specific physical scenario described.

The estimated 200 pounds of expelled material is also worth contextualising. Elephant faecal output is high — African elephants defecate multiple times per day with individual deposits that can exceed 50 pounds in a healthy animal passing normal stool. A constipated elephant releasing accumulated material might theoretically produce above-average quantities. But the specific fluid dynamics and pressure involved in the story — sufficient to knock an adult human off their feet and cover them entirely — significantly exceeds what veterinary literature describes as typical even in dramatic laxative scenarios.

## What the Evidence Shows About Source Evaluation

The Paderborn zookeeper story is useful as a practical exercise in source tracing because the chain from implausibility to debunking is short and direct. Every factual claim in the story is specifically checkable, and every check produces a negative result. The city is real; the zoo within it is not. The police detective's name does not appear in the city's records. The assistant zookeeper's name does not appear in the city's records. No German-language news source, no English-language wire service, and no credible international journalism outlet reported on this event.

The Weekly World News is not a credible factual source. This is not a matter of ideological bias or contested interpretation; it is a description of the publication's editorial function. The Weekly World News produced entertainment fiction in journalistic format as its business model. Treating its content as evidence of real-world events is a category error, not a reasonable interpretive dispute.

The debunking value of Snopes' treatment here lies partly in the explicit enumeration of false specific details: it is not enough to say "there is no zoo in Paderborn" if readers have not previously encountered or considered that ground claim. Making the checkable nature of the claim legible — pointing out that the city's phone directory is a usable resource, that the absence of any corroborating journalism from German or international sources is a meaningful evidential gap — demonstrates the investigative method, not just the conclusion.

## Verdict and Epistemic Standing

The claim that Friedrich Riesfeldt was killed when an elephant defecated on him in Paderborn, Germany, is FALSE. The story is a fabrication published by the Weekly World News, a publication whose declared and demonstrated business model was the production of entertaining fiction in journalistic format.

The story fails at every checkable point: no zoo exists in Paderborn; no named individual in the story can be verified through civic records; no credible news organisation carried the story; the photograph provided was inconsistent with the described events in multiple specific ways; and the core physical scenario described — a single keeper successfully administering a rectal procedure to a conscious 8,000-pound African elephant without restraint or veterinary support — is not achievable under normal conditions.

The story's enduring internet circulation is a testament to the power of false specificity in creating the texture of credibility, and to the decontextualising effect of social sharing that strips content from the source identification that would allow audiences to correctly evaluate it. It is also a reminder that the logical structure of a story — the "too neat" quality that might make a true story seem invented — cannot serve as a reliable guide to truth value in either direction. Some well-structured comic narratives are true (see: the unkillable gopher). Some meticulously detailed tragedies are invented. The evidence, not the narrative shape, must be the arbiter.
