# Do Hurricanes With Female Names Cause More Deaths?


## 1. The Claim

In June 2014, a research team led by Kiju Jung at the University of Illinois published a paper in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (PNAS) titled "Female Hurricanes Are Deadlier Than Male Hurricanes" (Jung et al., 2014, *PNAS*). The central claim was striking: hurricanes bearing feminine names kill significantly more people than hurricanes bearing masculine names, and the mechanism proposed was implicit gender bias — people unconsciously perceive female-named storms as less threatening, take fewer precautions, and consequently die at higher rates. The paper combined two distinct lines of evidence: an archival analysis of Atlantic hurricane death tolls from 1950 to 2012, covering 94 hurricanes, and a series of four survey experiments in which participants rated the perceived danger of hypothetical storms with masculine or feminine names.

The paper went viral almost immediately. Major outlets including *The Washington Post*, *The Guardian*, *National Geographic*, and dozens of science journalism sites ran the story under headlines that treated the finding as established fact. "Female-named hurricanes kill more than male hurricanes" became the headline, not "study claims" or "researchers suggest." The media framing was remarkably uniform: the finding was presented as a clean, clever, counter-intuitive result that mapped neatly onto broader cultural narratives about gender bias in everyday life. It had the qualities that make science stories travel — a paradoxical finding, an intuitive mechanism, quantitative authority from a prestigious journal, and immediate relevance to public safety. Within days, it was among the most discussed social science findings of 2014.

What the paper actually argued was more conditional than the headlines suggested. Jung et al. did not claim that every female-named hurricane was deadlier than every male-named hurricane. Their archival model used a masculinity-femininity index — nine coders rated each hurricane name on a 1-to-11 scale of perceived femininity — and then modelled death counts as a function of this continuous femininity score while controlling for storm intensity measures including minimum barometric pressure, maximum wind speed, and a normalised damage index. The model found a statistically significant positive relationship: higher femininity scores predicted higher death counts. The four survey experiments then provided what the authors framed as mechanistic support. Participants presented with hypothetical storms bearing female names reported lower perceived risk and weaker intentions to evacuate than participants presented with identical storm descriptions bearing male names.

The claim that reached the public, however, was not about continuous femininity scores or conditional regression models. It was the blunt version: female-named hurricanes are deadlier because sexism kills. This simplified form is what entered the cultural repertoire, and it is the form that subsequent critiques would largely dismantle.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes assigned this claim a verdict of **Unproven**, a designation the fact-checking platform uses when evidence is genuinely inconclusive — neither sufficient to confirm nor sufficient to deny. Snopes writer Alex Kasprak (2017) correctly identified the core tension: a finding published in a prestigious peer-reviewed journal had been substantively challenged by multiple statistical responses published in the same journal, and the original authors had defended their methodology without fully resolving the objections. The Snopes assessment was fair in noting that "this seemingly simple question does not currently have a simple answer."

The archival data are real but deeply ambiguous. The dataset covers Atlantic hurricanes making United States landfall from 1950 to 2012 — a period during which hurricane naming conventions changed fundamentally. From 1950 to 1978, all Atlantic hurricanes received female names exclusively. The World Meteorological Organisation introduced alternating male and female names beginning in 1979. This means that the entire pre-1979 portion of the dataset — nearly three decades — contains only female-named storms. Those decades also coincide with a period when hurricane forecasting was less precise, coastal populations were less mobile, evacuation infrastructure was less developed, building codes were less stringent, and communication systems for storm warnings were more primitive. Any correlation between feminine names and death tolls in the archival data therefore confounds the gender-of-name variable with a broad historical trend toward improving hurricane survival.

The four survey experiments — in which participants rated the perceived threat of hypothetical hurricanes named Victor versus Victoria, or Danny versus Kate — did find that feminine-named storms were rated as slightly less threatening. These results are genuine and internally consistent. However, they measure stated hypothetical intentions in online survey scenarios, not actual behavioural responses to real approaching hurricanes. The gap between what people say they would do in a survey and what they actually do when a Category 4 storm is bearing down on their house is one of the most well-documented problems in behavioural science. Survey-based risk perception studies routinely fail to predict real-world protective behaviour, particularly in high-stakes contexts where situational urgency overwhelms prior impressions (Slovic, 1987, *Science*; Fischhoff et al., 1978, *Environment*).

Two formal critiques published in PNAS within months of the original paper are particularly damaging. Steve Malley's response (Malley, 2014, *PNAS*) re-analysed the same archival dataset restricting attention to the post-1979 period — when alternating male-female names made the comparison legitimate — and found no statistically significant gender effect on death tolls. This is a straightforward and devastating finding: once you remove the confounded pre-1979 data, the effect disappears. Bjorn Christensen and Søren Christensen (Christensen & Christensen, 2014, *PNAS*) identified a different vulnerability. They showed that the statistical significance of the original model depended heavily on a small number of extraordinarily deadly storms: Hurricane Diane (1955), Hurricane Camille (1969), Hurricane Agnes (1972), and Hurricane Sandy (2012). These four storms together accounted for approximately 732 of the roughly 1,900 total deaths in the dataset — 38.5 percent of all fatalities concentrated in four observations out of ninety-four. With those four storms removed, the statistical relationship between name femininity and death toll lost significance. The finding, in other words, was not a robust population-level pattern but an artefact of a handful of extreme events.

Jung et al. published a rebuttal defending their methodology and arguing that removing the deadliest storms was inappropriate because deadly storms are precisely the cases where the gender-bias mechanism should matter most. This rebuttal has some logical force — you should not discard your most informative data points simply because they are influential. But the counter-argument is equally compelling: when your entire statistical conclusion depends on whether four storms happen to have female names, and when three of those four occurred in an era when *all* storms had female names, you do not have a robust finding. You have a coincidence wearing the costume of a pattern.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of the female-named-hurricanes claim despite its statistical fragility reveals several layered epistemic dynamics that extend well beyond this particular study.

**The implicit bias framework is real.** The most important reason people find this claim plausible is that the underlying psychological mechanism — implicit gender bias in risk perception — is a genuine, well-documented phenomenon. Decades of research in social cognition, anchored by the pioneering work of Anthony Greenwald and Mahzarin Banaji on the Implicit Association Test (Greenwald & Banaji, 1995, *Psychological Review*; Greenwald, McGhee, & Schwartz, 1998, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*), have established that people hold unconscious associations between social categories and evaluative attributes. Gender stereotypes, specifically, include the association of femininity with warmth, gentleness, and lower threat, and masculinity with dominance, aggression, and higher threat (Eagly & Steffen, 1984, *Psychological Bulletin*; Fiske et al., 2002, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*). These stereotype associations are measurably present in most populations tested and can influence judgments in laboratory settings.

The hurricane study taps directly into this established literature. If people implicitly associate female names with lower threat, it is not irrational to hypothesise that this association might affect how they respond to storm warnings. The problem is not that the *mechanism* is implausible — it is entirely plausible — but that the *archival evidence* used to demonstrate it in the real world is confounded and statistically fragile. The distinction between "this mechanism exists in principle" and "this mechanism caused these specific deaths" is precisely the kind of distinction that gets lost when a clever study goes viral. People who already accept the reality of implicit gender bias — and they are right to accept it, given the evidence base — experience the hurricane finding as merely *confirmatory* of what they already know, rather than as a novel empirical claim requiring its own separate evidentiary support.

**Stereotype threat and the halo of adjacent findings.** The hurricane claim also benefits from proximity to the extensive stereotype threat literature (Steele & Aronson, 1995, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*), which has shown that activating social stereotypes can affect real performance outcomes. Although stereotype threat concerns a different mechanism — the performance-degrading effects of being stereotyped, rather than the risk-perception effects of stereotyping an external object — the two lines of research share a narrative family resemblance. Both argue that socially constructed categories have measurable, consequential effects on real-world outcomes. Familiarity with stereotype threat research predisposes people to accept structurally similar claims without requiring independent verification. The cognitive operation is one of analogical transfer: "stereotypes affect test scores, so of course they could affect hurricane preparedness."

**First-mover advantage in viral science.** The media epistemics of viral research findings operate on a structural asymmetry that consistently favours initial claims over subsequent corrections. The original Jung et al. paper received wide coverage simultaneously across major outlets in June 2014. The critiques by Malley and by Christensen and Christensen were published in PNAS months later, received fractional coverage, and were typically presented in hedging language — "some researchers question the finding" — rather than with the declarative confidence that accompanied the original. This is a well-studied pattern in science communication. Corrections and qualifications do not travel as well as novel surprising findings (Lewandowsky et al., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*). The original claim occupies first-mover territory in the public mind, and subsequent evidence must overcome both the initial impression and the reduced media attention it receives. By the time the critiques were published, millions of people had already absorbed "female-named hurricanes are deadlier" as an interesting fact, and very few of them encountered the rebuttal.

**Narrative coherence and motivated reasoning.** The claim is also sustained by its narrative elegance. "Sexism is so deeply embedded in our cognition that it literally kills people during hurricanes" is a story with dramatic force, moral clarity, and an implicit call to action. It converts an abstract statistical pattern into a vivid morality tale. People who are committed to drawing public attention to gender inequality — a legitimate and important project — find the claim useful, and useful claims are resistant to revision. This is not a criticism of their goals but a description of how motivated reasoning operates: when a finding serves a valued conclusion, the evidentiary threshold for accepting it drops and the threshold for rejecting it rises.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional status: Unproven, leaning toward Not Supported.**

The Snopes verdict of "Unproven" is directionally correct but imprecise. It treats the evidential landscape as roughly balanced, when the weight of published critique has substantially shifted the balance against the original claim. A more accurate characterisation would be: the archival evidence for a gender-of-name effect on hurricane fatalities is not statistically robust after accounting for known confounds, and the experimental evidence, while internally valid, demonstrates only hypothetical survey responses rather than real-world behavioural differences.

**Confidence: Moderate-to-high that the archival claim is not supported.** The pre-1979 confound is not a minor technicality; it is a structural flaw that the original research design cannot adequately address. The dependence of the finding on four extreme storms, three of which occurred in the all-female-names era, makes the archival result essentially uninterpretable as evidence for a gender-bias mechanism. The experimental evidence that people rate female-named hypothetical storms as slightly less threatening is credible but insufficient to bridge the gap to real-world mortality, and no field study has demonstrated that actual evacuation behaviour differs by hurricane name gender.

**Epistemic basis:** The claim rests on a single study team's analysis, challenged by two independent critiques in the same journal, with no subsequent replication or extension using improved methodology. In the twelve years since publication, no independent research group has produced confirmatory evidence from a different dataset or a different methodological approach. The absence of replication in a literature that generated enormous public interest — and therefore strong incentive for follow-up research — is itself informative. When a high-profile finding generates extensive discussion but no independent confirmation over more than a decade, the Bayesian update should move the posterior toward non-replication.

**What remains genuinely open:** It is entirely possible that hurricane name gender has some small, real effect on risk perception at the margins. The survey evidence is consistent with this possibility, and the implicit bias literature provides theoretical support for the mechanism. But "some small effect on survey responses" and "female-named hurricanes kill significantly more people" are very different claims, separated by an enormous inferential gap. The first is plausible and potentially demonstrable with better experimental methods — particularly field studies tracking actual preparedness behaviour during real storm events. The second is not supported by the available evidence once the archival data are scrutinised with appropriate robustness checks.

**The asymmetry between mechanism and evidence.** It is worth being precise about what fails and what does not. The general proposition that gender stereotypes can influence risk perception is well-supported by decades of social cognition research and should not be dismissed because one particular application of that proposition turned out to be statistically fragile. The failure is not in the implicit bias literature broadly; it is in the specific empirical bridge between laboratory-demonstrated gender-name associations and real-world hurricane mortality outcomes. That bridge was built from confounded archival data and hypothetical survey responses, and it cannot bear the weight of the causal claim placed upon it. Acknowledging this does not require rejecting implicit bias research as a field — it requires recognising that a plausible mechanism is not the same as a demonstrated effect.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The female-named-hurricanes study is a textbook case of several intersecting problems in contemporary social science methodology, and examining it through that wider lens illuminates patterns that extend far beyond meteorology.

**Researcher degrees of freedom and the garden of forking paths.** The original study involved numerous analytical choices, each of which could have been made differently: which years to include, how to code name femininity, which control variables to include in the regression, whether to model deaths as a count variable or log-transformed, and how to handle the extreme outlier storms. Andrew Gelman and Eric Loken's influential framework of "the garden of forking paths" (Gelman & Loken, 2014, *American Scientist*) describes exactly this situation: even without conscious intent to manipulate results, the number of defensible analytical choices available to the researchers is large enough that obtaining a statistically significant result through some combination of choices is far more likely than the nominal p-value suggests. The researchers need not have consciously p-hacked; the problem is structural. When you have ninety-four data points, a continuous femininity index with subjective coding, several possible control variable specifications, and extreme distributional skew in the outcome variable, the actual false-positive rate is much higher than the 5 percent suggested by conventional significance testing.

**The replication crisis lens.** The hurricane study arrived at a particular moment in the social psychology replication crisis. In 2014, the Open Science Collaboration's large-scale replication project was underway but had not yet published its sobering results, which would show that only 36-39 percent of published psychology findings replicated (Open Science Collaboration, 2015, *Science*). The hurricane paper exhibited many of the characteristics that the replication reform movement would subsequently identify as risk factors for non-replication: a surprising and counter-intuitive main finding, a small and idiosyncratic dataset, substantial researcher degrees of freedom in the analytic pipeline, and results that depend on a handful of influential observations. Had the study been published two or three years later, in a post-replication-crisis climate, it would likely have received more sceptical initial coverage and more rigorous peer review. Its timing — just before the full force of the replication reckoning hit — allowed it to benefit from the older norm of treating published-in-a-good-journal as a strong quality signal.

**What better-designed studies would look like.** A convincing test of the hypothesis that hurricane name gender affects mortality would require several features absent from the original study. First, it would need to be restricted to the post-1979 period, when alternating male-female names were used, eliminating the pre-1979 confound entirely. Second, it would need a substantially larger sample — the post-1979 dataset contains fewer than forty directly comparable storms, which provides very low statistical power for detecting the kind of small effect that implicit bias might plausibly produce. Third, and most importantly, it would require behavioural data rather than hypothetical survey responses: actual evacuation rates, shelter usage, preparedness purchasing, or compliance with mandatory evacuation orders, linked to specific storms and their names. Such data exist in principle — emergency management agencies track evacuation compliance — but no study has assembled and analysed them for this question. Fourth, an ideal test might exploit natural experiments: cases where a storm's name was retired and replaced, or where two similar storms with different-gender names struck similar populations in close temporal proximity. The absence of any such study in the twelve years since the original paper is notable.

**What we do know about gender stereotyping and risk perception.** The broader literature on gender and risk perception is extensive and does support several relevant findings, though none that map cleanly onto the hurricane claim. Women are generally perceived as less aggressive and less threatening than men across multiple cultural contexts (Eagly & Wood, 1999, *American Psychologist*). Male names attached to ambiguous stimuli produce higher threat ratings than female names in some experimental paradigms. However, these effects are typically small, are strongest in ambiguous or low-information contexts, and tend to diminish when strong situational cues — such as an approaching Category 4 hurricane with sustained winds of 150 miles per hour — are present. The theoretical case for implicit name-gender bias affecting hurricane response is strongest for marginal storms with ambiguous threat levels and weakest for the most dangerous storms, which is exactly the opposite of what would be needed to produce the large mortality differences the original study claimed. Real hurricane preparedness decisions are overwhelmingly driven by official warnings, local experience, storm intensity forecasts, and community behaviour, not by the implicit gender associations of the storm's name.

**Distributional fragility and the tyranny of outliers.** Hurricane fatalities follow an extremely skewed distribution — a small number of storms cause the vast majority of deaths, while most storms cause relatively few. This distributional property makes any regression analysis of hurricane deaths exquisitely sensitive to the inclusion or exclusion of a handful of extreme events. The Christensen and Christensen (2014) critique demonstrated this directly: removing just four storms — approximately four percent of the dataset — eliminated the statistical significance of the gender effect. This is not a pathological sensitivity test; it is a standard robustness check for exactly this kind of heavy-tailed data. Findings that cannot survive the removal of four percent of their observations are not robust findings. They are artefacts of distributional extremes, which is a different thing entirely.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes assigned the claim a verdict of "Unproven," which in its rating taxonomy sits between "True" and "False" and indicates that available evidence is insufficient to resolve the question definitively. Alex Kasprak's 2017 write-up accurately summarised the original study, the two major PNAS critiques, and the authors' rebuttal. It quoted relevant researchers, noted the pre-1979 naming confound, and concluded that the question "does not currently have a simple answer." As a piece of fact-checking journalism, the Snopes article is competent, honest, and fair to both sides.

**What "Unproven" means epistemically.** Snopes uses "Unproven" as a catch-all for claims where evidence is mixed or insufficient. This is appropriate for claims at an early stage of investigation, where genuinely balanced uncertainty exists. But it is less appropriate for claims that have been substantively challenged and not defended successfully. "Unproven" implies that the jury is still out and further evidence could go either way. In the case of the female-named-hurricanes claim, two independent statistical critiques have identified specific, non-trivial flaws in the original analysis; the original team's rebuttal did not fully resolve those flaws; and no independent replication has been produced in over a decade. This is not balanced uncertainty — it is a finding whose evidential support has been substantially undermined. A more precise verdict would be something like "Not Supported by Current Evidence" or "Original Finding Substantially Challenged," but Snopes' rating system does not include such fine-grained categories. The result is that "Unproven" understates the degree to which the evidence leans against the claim. A reader encountering the Snopes verdict might reasonably infer a 50/50 evidential split, when the actual balance is considerably less favourable to the original study.

**EARC mode R: the finding needs revision, not extension.** In our epistemic audit framework, this claim receives an R designation — indicating that the appropriate next step is revision and critical re-evaluation of the original finding, not extension or application. An E (Extend) mode would be appropriate if the finding were solid and the question were how to build on it. An A (Apply) mode would mean the finding was robust enough to inform policy. An R (Revise) mode recognises that the foundational evidence has been credibly challenged and that the field needs to either produce substantially better evidence or acknowledge that the claimed effect is not established. An R mode also signals that citing this finding as established fact in policy discussions, journalistic articles, or public communication is premature and potentially misleading.

**What was missed in the Snopes analysis.** While the Snopes fact-check was methodologically sound within its scope, several dimensions of the story went unexplored. First, the broader replication crisis context — the fact that findings with exactly these statistical characteristics (small sample, surprising result, researcher degrees of freedom, dependence on outliers) have failed to replicate at very high rates — was not discussed. The Snopes article treated the study as an isolated evidential question rather than locating it within the known base-rate of non-replication in social psychology, which would have shifted the prior probability substantially. Second, the gap between hypothetical survey responses and actual evacuation behaviour was noted but not emphasised. The fact that no study has ever demonstrated that real evacuation decisions vary by hurricane name gender is perhaps the single most important piece of missing evidence, and it deserved more prominence. Third, the media dynamics around viral science findings — the first-mover advantage, the asymmetry between initial coverage and correction coverage, and the cultural utility of the claim for gender-bias narratives — were outside the scope of a traditional fact-check but are essential for understanding why this particular unproven claim persists in public discourse.

**The broader lesson for fact-checking methodology.** The female-named-hurricanes case illustrates a recurring challenge for fact-checking organisations. Binary or near-binary verdict systems (True, False, Mostly True, Unproven) struggle with claims where the original evidence was published in a legitimate venue, received legitimate critique in the same venue, and now sits in an evidential limbo that categorical labels cannot precisely capture. "Unproven" is technically accurate but communicatively misleading. The development of more graduated verdict systems — or at least more extensive narrative contextualisation alongside categorical verdicts — would help readers distinguish between "we genuinely don't know" and "the original evidence has been largely dismantled but the topic is such that definitive disproof is difficult." These are very different epistemic states, and treating them as equivalent under a single label does a disservice to the public's ability to make informed judgments about which factual claims deserve their confidence.
