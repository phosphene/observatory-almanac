# Dog Dumped at North Carolina Shelter for Being 'Gay' Finds Rescue

## 1. The Claim

In March 2022, a story about a dog named Fezco caught international media attention after the four- to five-year-old black and brown dog was surrendered to the Stanly County Animal Protective Services shelter in North Carolina. According to shelter intake records and subsequent media reports, the owners surrendering Fezco told shelter staff that the dog was "gay" — specifically, that it had been observed mounting another male dog.

The story spread rapidly on social media, generating both outrage at the perceived homophobia of the owners' reasoning and expressions of solidarity with the dog, including from many users who hoped to connect Fezco with potential adopters. Numerous Twitter posts expressed anger at the abandonment, with hundreds of thousands of engagements collectively across platforms. The shelter subsequently posted an update confirming that Fezco had been placed with the Greater Charlotte SPCA rescue group, where he was awaiting a foster-to-adopt home pending completion of heartworm treatment.

The claim being assessed is whether the story is true — whether a dog genuinely was surrendered with the stated reason, whether that reason was "gay," and whether the dog found a rescue placement. The story also carries an implicit scientific claim: that the owners' interpretation of their dog's mounting behavior as evidence of sexual orientation was empirically grounded or meaningful.

Secondary elements of the claim that circulated alongside the main story included expressions that Fezco was a friendly, people-oriented dog and would be easily adoptable; that shelter capacity concerns made his placement urgent; and that the story represented evidence of prejudice operating even in interactions between humans and companion animals.

## 2. What's Actually True

The Snopes piece, published March 22, 2022, as a News article rather than a traditional fact-check (reflecting the story's news rather than rumor origin), was written by Bethania Palma, a journalist with experience in international news coverage. The piece confirms the core story elements and rates the situation as accurately reported.

Fezco was surrendered at Stanly County Animal Protective Services, a real shelter in Stanly County, North Carolina. The shelter documented the surrender reason as the owners' claim that the dog was "gay" based on mounting behavior with another male dog. Charlotte-based TV station WCCB reported on the story on March 17, 2022, and the shelf's own Facebook page posted confirmatory updates, including a post noting Fezco's placement with Greater Charlotte SPCA.

Fezco was described by shelter staff and WCCB reporting as friendly, sociable, and comfortable with both people and other animals — characteristics that supported his adoptability. The shelter noted that the heartworm treatment he required represented a time-bounded process after which his full foster-to-adopt placement could be completed.

The Snopes article includes a section explaining the scientific background of dog mounting behavior, drawing on information from the American Kennel Club. The AKC's guidance, as cited by Palma, identifies mounting as a common behavior in dogs that is frequently non-sexual in nature, noting contexts including dominance expression, overstimulation, and responses to medical issues. The AKC also notes that mounting and humping can be addressed through positive reinforcement training and redirection.

What the Snopes piece does not do is engage at any depth with the ethological literature on same-sex sexual behavior in non-human mammals, or with the conceptual question of whether the category "gay" is meaningfully applicable to canine behavior — a question that intersects behavioral science, philosophy of biology, and the semiotics of human identity categories.

## 3. Epistemic Analysis: Canine Mounting Behavior, Mammalian Same-Sex Sexual Behavior, and Category Error

The epistemic core of the Fezco case lies in the collision between two distinct claims: the factual claim that Fezco was surrendered with the stated reason (true), and the implicit scientific claim that the owners' interpretation — that mounting a male dog means the dog is "gay" — represents a scientifically coherent description of canine sexuality. The fact-check addresses the first claim well and the second only superficially.

**The epistemic novelty here is the rich ethological literature on same-sex sexual behavior in non-human animals, which complicates both the dismissive debunking ("dogs don't have sexual orientations") and the affirming projection ("Fezco is gay and deserves acceptance").**

Bagemihl (1999), in *Biological Exuberance: Animal Homosexuality and Natural Diversity* (St. Martin's Press), documented same-sex sexual behavior across more than 450 vertebrate species, including mammals, birds, reptiles, and fish. Bagemihl's survey was the most systematic compilation of such observations to that point and was intended to challenge the implicit assumption that same-sex sexual behavior in animals was a pathological anomaly rather than a naturally occurring behavioral variant. The book's claim was not that non-human animals possess sexual orientations in the psychologically complex sense associated with human sexual identity, but that same-sex sexual activity is widespread, varied in its forms and contexts, and requires explanation rather than dismissal.

In dogs specifically, the relevant behavioral literature distinguishes mounting behavior from sexual orientation conceptually. Mounting in dogs is a behavioral output that can serve multiple functional roles: it can be sexual in heterosexual contexts, but it commonly occurs in same-sex dyads in contexts that ethologists associate with dominance assertion, play arousal management, anxiety expression, and social bonding rather than sexual attraction per se (Overall, K.L., 2013, *Manual of Clinical Behavioral Medicine for Dogs and Cats*, Elsevier Mosby). The fact that a male dog mounts another male dog does not constitute evidence of persistent same-sex sexual preference in the sense that would be analogous to human sexual orientation.

However, the definitional question here is more complicated than it appears. Vasey (1995), in a review article published in *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews*, distinguished between same-sex mounting behavior (a discrete behavioral act) and sexual preference (a motivational state that directs sexual behavior preferentially toward one sex) in non-human primates, noting that the two do not always co-occur and that conflating them produces conceptual confusion (Vasey, P.L., 1995, "Homosexual behaviour in primates: A review of evidence and theory," *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews*, 19(4), 465–490). This distinction is important for the Fezco case: the owners observed mounting, which may or may not reflect sexual preference, and concluded "gay," which implies sexual orientation as a stable trait.

The sociological dimension of the story's virality is equally important. Fezco's story attracted massive social media engagement because of what it seemed to say about human behavior, not dog behavior. The outrage directed at his original owners was outrage about human homophobia — the use of sexual orientation as a reason to abandon a companion animal was read as a proxy statement about human attitudes toward LGBTQ+ people and relationships. This interpretive move is understandable but involves projecting human psychological and social categories onto a dog who demonstrably cannot have a sexual orientation in the meaningful human sense.

The irony is that both the owners' interpretation ("our dog is gay, therefore we reject him") and many responders' reaction ("Fezco is gay and deserves support") committed the same category error — treating "gay" as a meaningfully applicable concept to canine mounting behavior — while reaching opposite moral conclusions. The owners used the projected category as grounds for abandonment; supporters used it as grounds for solidarity. The dog, meanwhile, was engaging in behavior whose actual motivation requires ethological, not identity-political, analysis.

## 4. EARC Assessment and Gap Analysis

**EARC Mode: A — Affirm.** The core story is true and verified. Fezco was surrendered with the stated reason, was placed with the Greater Charlotte SPCA, and found a foster-to-adopt home. The Snopes coverage is accurate.

**Gap Category: affirmed-surface-only.** The gaps are substantive and in multiple directions:

1. **Behavioral science depth**: The article's citation of AKC guidance is useful but shallow. The ethological literature on mounting behavior in dogs is extensive. Overall (2013, Elsevier Mosby) and the Applied Animal Behavior Science journal literature provide more rigorous frameworks for understanding when and why dogs mount, across multiple behavioral contexts, than the general-public AKC website.

2. **Same-sex sexual behavior in mammals**: Bagemihl (1999, St. Martin's Press) represents a foundational treatment of this subject that goes entirely unmentioned. The question of whether same-sex sexual behavior in companion animals should be understood through a human sexual orientation framework or through a behavioral-functional framework is not raised.

3. **Category error analysis**: Neither the original news coverage nor the Snopes piece addresses the conceptual problem of applying human identity categories to animal behavior. This gap matters because it shapes how we understand both the owners' reasoning and the public response.

4. **Shelter intake sociology**: The story also raises questions about what reasons animal shelters typically receive for surrenders, and what this reveals about owner decision-making and human-animal relationships. Research on shelter surrender surveys (e.g., Salman et al., 2000, in *Journal of Applied Animal Welfare Science*) provides context for understanding the surrender decision as part of a documented pattern of human-animal relationship breakdown, not simply as an isolated incident of homophobia.

## 5. Wider Context: Same-Sex Sexual Behavior in Non-Human Animals and the Politics of Animal Projection

The Fezco case illustrates a broader phenomenon in human relationships with companion animals: the systematic projection of human psychological and social categories — including sexual orientation, personality types, emotional states, and moral qualities — onto animals whose cognitive and behavioral architecture differs substantially from humans.

The literature on anthropomorphism in human-animal interaction is substantial. Serpell (2003) noted in *Anthrozoös* that pet owners regularly attribute complex mental states including preferences, beliefs, and feelings to companion animals in ways that may not accurately reflect the animals' actual cognitive capacities, but that serve important psychological functions for the owners (Serpell, J.A., 2003, "Anthropomorphism and anthropomorphic selection: Beyond the 'fancy' hypothesis," *Society & Animals*, 11(1), 83–100).

The specific projection of human sexual identity categories onto animals has been analyzed in the context of same-sex behavior observations by Stein (1999), who noted in *Sociological Theory* the tendency of human observers to impose culturally specific concepts of sexual identity onto animal behavior that may have multiple non-sexual motivations (Stein, E., 1999, "The Mismeasure of Desire: The Science, Theory, and Ethics of Sexual Orientation," Oxford University Press). This projection operates bidirectionally in cultural politics: conservative commentators have sometimes argued that the prevalence of same-sex behavior in animals "proves nothing" about human homosexuality (rejecting nature-based arguments for LGBTQ+ rights), while progressive commentators have sometimes used animal same-sex behavior as evidence that homosexuality is "natural" without adequately engaging with the question of whether "natural" in animal ethology maps onto "sexual orientation" in human psychology.

The Fezco case sits at a specific node of this larger cultural dynamic: a companion animal's behavior is interpreted through a human sexual identity framework by all parties — owners, social media responders, journalists, and fact-checkers — with the scientific question of what the behavior actually means for the animal remaining largely unexplored.

## 6. Conclusion

The story of Fezco's surrender and subsequent rescue is **True**, as Snopes correctly establishes. The dog was surrendered at Stanly County Animal Protective Services with the stated reason that his owners believed him to be "gay" based on mounting behavior with another male dog. He was subsequently placed with the Greater Charlotte SPCA and found a foster-to-adopt home.

The implicit scientific claim embedded in the story — that mounting behavior between male dogs constitutes evidence of sexual orientation — is less straightforwardly addressed. Mounting in dogs serves multiple behavioral functions, most of which are not sexual (Overall, K.L., 2013, *Manual of Clinical Behavioral Medicine for Dogs and Cats*, Elsevier Mosby). Same-sex sexual behavior in non-human animals is documented across hundreds of species (Bagemihl, 1999, *Biological Exuberance*, St. Martin's Press) but its relationship to human concepts of sexual orientation requires careful conceptual analysis (Vasey, 1995, *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews*, 19(4)).

The epistemic novelty of this case lies in the category error that drives the story: the use of a human identity category ("gay") to describe dog behavior, which simultaneously reflects the genuine prevalence of same-sex sexual behavior in non-human animals and the inadequacy of importing human sexual identity frameworks into animal ethology. Both the owners who used "gay" as a reason for abandonment and many social media users who used it as a reason for solidarity were committing the same conceptual error — and the virality of the story is largely a function of that shared error resonating with human cultural politics rather than with anything specific about canine behavior science.

Fezco's story matters as a human story, about human projections, human prejudices, and human solidarity. As a story about dog behavior, it is much simpler: a dog did what dogs do, and humans read into it something that said more about them than about him.
