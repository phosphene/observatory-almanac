# "The Five Stages of Grief Are Universal"


## 1. The Claim

The assertion that grief follows five universal stages — denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance — represents one of the most widely recognized psychological frameworks in popular culture and clinical practice. This claim, derived from Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's groundbreaking 1969 book "On Death and Dying," maintains that bereaved individuals progress through these stages in a predictable sequence as they process loss and ultimately achieve psychological resolution.

The five-stage model has achieved extraordinary cultural penetration, appearing in counseling protocols, self-help literature, medical training, and popular media representations of grief. It suggests that grief follows a natural, orderly progression that can be understood, predicted, and potentially managed through therapeutic intervention. The model implies that "healthy" grief involves experiencing each stage and that failure to progress through the stages indicates pathological or complicated grief requiring professional intervention.

This framework has profound implications for how society understands and responds to bereaved individuals. It shapes expectations about appropriate grief responses, influences the design of bereavement interventions, and provides a common language for discussing loss across diverse cultural and professional contexts. Mental health professionals often use the stages as diagnostic criteria, while bereaved individuals frequently evaluate their own grief experiences against this template.

However, the claim faces substantial challenges from contemporary grief research that documents enormous individual variation in grief responses, questions the sequential nature of the proposed stages, and demonstrates that many bereaved individuals never experience some stages while others experience multiple stages simultaneously or in different orders.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim demonstrates significant fidelity to Kübler-Ross's original clinical observations while misrepresenting the scope and intent of her work. Kübler-Ross developed her five-stage framework based on intensive interviews with terminally ill patients who were facing their own death, not bereaved survivors processing the loss of others. Her original focus was on how dying individuals cope with their approaching death rather than how survivors grieve after loss.

This crucial distinction has been largely lost in popular and even professional applications of the model. Kübler-Ross was describing the psychological trajectory of people confronting their own mortality, which involves different psychological processes than those experienced by bereaved survivors. The extension of her framework to bereavement represents a significant extrapolation that was never part of her original research or theoretical claims.

Furthermore, Kübler-Ross presented her stages as common patterns she observed rather than universal laws of psychological processing. She explicitly acknowledged individual variation and never claimed that all dying patients experience all stages or progress through them in a fixed sequence. Her work was descriptive rather than prescriptive, identifying recurring themes in her clinical observations rather than proposing rigid diagnostic criteria.

The claim's fidelity is further compromised by the transformation of Kübler-Ross's flexible clinical observations into rigid sequential requirements. What began as recognition of common emotional responses became institutionalized as normative expectations about how grief "should" proceed, creating a prescriptive framework that judges individual grief experiences against a supposed universal standard.


The most comprehensive challenge to the universal five stages model comes from George Bonanno's extensive longitudinal research on grief and resilience. His 2009 book "The Other Side of Sadness" synthesized decades of empirical research demonstrating that resilience, not prolonged grief, represents the most common response to loss. Bonanno's studies followed bereaved individuals over extended periods, using standardized measures to track psychological adjustment and identify distinct grief trajectories.

Bonanno's research identified four primary patterns of grief response: resilience (maintaining stable functioning with brief disruption), recovery (initial distress followed by gradual return to baseline), chronic grief (prolonged dysfunction), and delayed grief (initial stability followed by later difficulties). Critically, resilience was the most common pattern, occurring in approximately 50-60% of bereaved individuals, while chronic grief affected only 10-15%.

These findings directly challenge the assumption that "normal" grief involves prolonged emotional disruption and sequential stage progression. Most bereaved individuals maintain reasonable psychological functioning throughout bereavement, experiencing sadness and adjustment challenges without the severe disruption implied by the five stages model.

Paul Maciejewski and colleagues' 2007 study in JAMA provided the most direct empirical test of the five stages model. Following 233 bereaved individuals over two years, they assessed the presence and intensity of denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance at multiple time points. While they found some support for the temporal sequencing Kübler-Ross proposed, individual variation was enormous, with many participants never experiencing certain stages and others experiencing multiple stages simultaneously.

Crucially, the study found that acceptance was high immediately after loss and remained stable over time, contradicting the model's assumption that acceptance represents the final achievement of successful grief work. Depression peaked at six months rather than following other stages sequentially, and many individuals showed high levels of acceptance while also experiencing denial, anger, or bargaining.

Contemporary attachment theory provides alternative frameworks for understanding grief variation. Attachment styles, formed in early relationships, significantly predict grief responses and adjustment patterns. Individuals with secure attachment typically show resilience patterns, while those with anxious or avoidant attachment may experience more complex grief trajectories that don't conform to stage-based expectations.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemological foundation of the five stages model rests primarily on Kübler-Ross's clinical observations and case studies rather than systematic empirical research. Her 1969 book documented patterns she observed through intensive interviews with 200 terminally ill patients, providing rich qualitative data about common psychological responses to approaching death. This clinical foundation offered valuable insights into previously neglected aspects of dying patients' experiences.

However, the model's epistemological limitations become apparent when examined against standards of contemporary psychological research. Kübler-Ross's observations were not subjected to systematic validation, control groups, or statistical analysis. Her case study methodology, while appropriate for exploratory research, cannot establish universal patterns or causal relationships between grief experiences and psychological outcomes.

The extension of the model from dying patients to bereaved survivors occurred without additional empirical validation. This represents a significant epistemological leap that assumes psychological processes in facing one's own death are equivalent to those involved in processing the death of others. These situations involve different emotional challenges, social contexts, and adaptive requirements.

Contemporary grief research employs longitudinal studies, standardized assessments, and diverse samples to examine bereavement processes more rigorously. This research has consistently failed to validate the universality of the five-stage progression, instead documenting enormous individual variation in grief trajectories, timing, and emotional responses.


## 4. Verdict

**Reification: Descriptive Observations Transformed into Universal Laws**

The claim that the five stages of grief are universal represents a classic example of reification — the transformation of flexible clinical observations into rigid universal principles. Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's original work identified meaningful patterns in dying patients' psychological responses, but these descriptive insights have been inappropriately generalized into prescriptive expectations about how all bereaved individuals should experience and process loss.

The evidence clearly demonstrates several key conclusions:

**Original Scope Limitation**: Kübler-Ross studied dying patients facing their own death, not bereaved survivors. The extension of her framework to bereavement represents an unvalidated extrapolation that changes the fundamental psychological context of her observations.

**Empirical Disconfirmation**: Large-scale longitudinal studies consistently fail to validate the universality of five-stage progression. Most bereaved individuals show resilience patterns that don't require progression through emotional stages, while others experience diverse trajectories that don't conform to the proposed sequence.

**Individual Variation**: When stages do occur, they frequently happen simultaneously, in different orders, or with some stages absent entirely. The enormous individual variation in grief responses contradicts claims about universal patterns or sequences.

**Cultural Specificity**: The model reflects Western psychological assumptions that may not apply across diverse cultural contexts where grief is understood and expressed differently.

**Clinical Institutionalization**: Despite limited empirical support, the model became deeply embedded in counseling practice, medical training, and popular culture, creating self-reinforcing expectations about "normal" grief that may not reflect actual psychological processes.

**Phenomenological Reality**: The individual stages do represent real psychological phenomena that many bereaved individuals experience. Denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance are genuine responses to loss, even if they don't occur universally or sequentially.

This assessment categorizes this as "reification" because the model captures authentic psychological experiences while making unwarranted claims about their universality and sequential nature. The stages describe real phenomena that occur for some people in some circumstances, but these observations have been inappropriately elevated to the status of natural laws governing all grief experiences.

The more accurate scientific consensus supports a view of grief as highly individualized, with multiple adaptive pathways and no single "correct" way to process loss. While some individuals may experience something resembling the five stages, many others show resilience, continuing bonds, meaning-making, or other adaptive responses that don't require stage-based progression.

Understanding grief as diverse rather than universal has important practical implications for how society supports bereaved individuals. Rather than evaluating grief experiences against a predetermined template, effective support recognizes and validates the many different ways people successfully navigate loss while identifying the minority who might benefit from professional intervention.

The five stages model's persistence despite contrary evidence reflects its narrative appeal and cultural functions rather than its empirical accuracy. It provides a comprehensible framework for understanding an inherently difficult and unpredictable experience, offering hope for eventual resolution through sequential progress. However, this narrative comfort comes at the cost of misrepresenting the actual diversity and resilience that characterize human responses to loss.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The five stages model intersects with broader debates about the pathologization of normal human experiences and the medicalization of grief. The model's emphasis on sequential progression toward "acceptance" implicitly positions other responses as incomplete or pathological, potentially creating unnecessary distress for bereaved individuals whose experiences don't match the prescribed pattern.

This concern has become particularly acute with the inclusion of "Persistent Complex Bereavement Disorder" in psychiatric diagnostic manuals. Critics argue that the five stages framework contributes to inappropriate pathologizing of diverse but normal grief responses, leading to overdiagnosis and unnecessary clinical intervention for individuals whose grief simply doesn't conform to expected timelines or patterns.

Cultural variation in grief responses poses additional challenges to universal stage models. Different cultures have varying expectations about appropriate grief expression, duration, and resolution. The five stages model reflects Western individualistic assumptions about emotional processing and psychological growth that may not apply across diverse cultural contexts where grief is understood as communal, spiritual, or ongoing rather than individually resolved.

The model's influence extends into legal and policy contexts where expectations about "normal" grief duration affect insurance coverage, bereavement leave policies, and legal proceedings. These applications can create practical difficulties for individuals whose grief experiences don't match institutionalized timelines or expectations.

Professional grief counseling has increasingly moved away from stage-based approaches toward more flexible, individualized interventions that recognize diverse grief trajectories. Contemporary approaches emphasize meaning-making, continuing bonds with deceased individuals, and adaptive coping strategies rather than progression through predetermined stages.

Research on continuing bonds challenges the model's emphasis on "acceptance" as the goal of grief work. Many bereaved individuals maintain ongoing psychological connections with deceased loved ones that provide comfort and meaning rather than representing failure to "move on." These continuing relationships often represent healthy adaptation rather than pathological attachment.

The emergence of complicated grief as a distinct clinical condition separate from depression has provided more nuanced understanding of when grief requires professional intervention. Complicated grief affects a minority of bereaved individuals and involves specific symptoms like intense yearning, difficulty accepting the death, and functional impairment that persist beyond typical adjustment periods.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly examined the universality claims surrounding the five stages of grief, likely because the model exists in a complex middle ground between established clinical practice and evolving scientific understanding. Unlike discrete factual claims about specific events, the five stages model involves interpretation of psychological research, clinical observation, and theoretical frameworks that resist straightforward fact-checking approaches.

This absence represents a significant gap in public fact-checking coverage, given the model's enormous influence on grief counseling, medical education, and popular understanding of bereavement. The model's institutionalization in clinical practice has occurred despite limited empirical validation, creating a situation where widely-held professional beliefs operate without systematic public scrutiny of their evidential basis.

The model's persistence in clinical and popular contexts, despite growing research evidence questioning its universality, exemplifies how compelling narrative frameworks can become entrenched in professional practice even when scientific support becomes increasingly tenuous.
