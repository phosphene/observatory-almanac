# Yes, Photo Shows Flamingos in Zoo Bathroom During Hurricane Andrew


## 1. The Claim

For more than three decades, a photograph has circulated showing dozens of flamingos densely packed inside what is clearly a public restroom — tiled floors, standard bathroom fixtures, and bodies of the lanky pink birds pressed together in an incongruous interior setting. The image periodically resurfaces on social media, and each time it does, the accompanying claim is some version of: during Hurricane Andrew, the Miami Zoo put its flamingos in a bathroom to ride out the storm.

The claim has an almost legendary quality in the context of South Florida's collective cultural memory of Hurricane Andrew. It is the kind of story that people who lived through the storm tell with a mixture of humor and admiration for the ingenuity of wildlife caretakers facing an impossible situation. The flamingos in the bathroom became symbolic not just of animal care under extreme duress but of the broader improvisational human response to a catastrophe that had no real precedent in the modern metropolitan experience of South Florida.

The photograph has been widely shared in different formats, with varying (and sometimes incorrect) captions. At various times, it has been identified as showing: (1) flamingos at Zoo Miami during Hurricane Andrew in 1992 (the correct identification); (2) flamingos at Zoo Miami during Hurricane Irma in 2017 (the period when it first went viral online); and (3) flamingos during Hurricane Georges in 1998. The persistence of these misattributions, combined with the image's general implausibility to those not familiar with zoo hurricane protocols, generated the verification need that the Snopes fact-check addressed.

This entry examines the photograph's authenticity, its documented provenance, the logic and history of zoo hurricane preparedness, and the broader significance of this image as a document of one of the most destructive natural disasters in American history.


## 2. What's Actually True

The photograph is authentic. It was taken by Ron Magill, who served as Zoo Miami's goodwill ambassador and communications director. Magill confirmed the authenticity to Snopes directly: he took the photograph on August 23, 1992, following the landfall of Hurricane Andrew.

Magill's account is detailed and internally consistent. At the time of Hurricane Andrew, Zoo Miami (then operating under the name Miami Metrozoo) maintained a flock of approximately 38 flamingos. The zoo's hurricane protocol required that all flamingos from the exhibit's front lake be rounded up and taken to the ladies' public restroom, which served as a sheltering location whenever a hurricane warning was issued. The choice of location was pragmatic: the restroom had no windows, had a tile floor that facilitated cleanup, and provided an enclosed, climate-buffered space appropriate for holding the birds during extreme weather.

Magill describes the event with characteristic vividness. He had completed the process of moving the flamingos to the bathroom when, struck by the surreal quality of the scene — dozens of large pink birds packed into standard public restroom fixtures — he took out a point-and-shoot camera and documented the moment. "Little did I know at the time how destructive and historic that storm would be or how much exposure that image would get afterwards," Magill told Snopes.

Hurricane Andrew was indeed one of the most destructive natural disasters in American history. A Category 5 storm when it made landfall in South Florida on August 24, 1992, Andrew caused an estimated $26.5 billion in damages (at 1992 values), making it the most expensive natural disaster in U.S. history at that time — a record it held until Hurricane Katrina in 2005. Andrew devastated the communities of Homestead and Florida City, destroyed large portions of the natural habitat of Everglades National Park, and caused 44 direct deaths in the United States (with a disputed higher total when indirect deaths are included). The storm's damage to Zoo Miami itself was substantial: it destroyed a significant portion of the zoo's infrastructure and many of its enclosures.

The flamingo photograph was subsequently published as a two-page spread in Newsweek, becoming one of the most widely reproduced and recognized images of the disaster. WLRN radio documented it in 2012 as an "iconic image" of Hurricane Andrew. Atlas Obscura, in a 2016 retrospective published ahead of the storm's 24th anniversary, described the restroom as the "obvious choice" to hold the flamingos — noting the no-windows structure, tile floor, room for improvised bedding of dumped hay, and a ready supply of fresh water.

The timeline of the image's viral spread is important for understanding the misattributions. The photograph had been in print and limited online circulation since 1992, but it did not go viral in the current social media sense until 2017, when it was reshared extensively ahead of Hurricane Irma as an illustration of Zoo Miami's hurricane preparedness practices. At this point, several outlets — including ABC News Australia and Business Insider — incorrectly captioned the image as depicting Hurricane Georges (1998) rather than Hurricane Andrew (1992). These errors compounded into a small information ecosystem where multiple incorrect versions of the caption coexisted with the correct one, creating the verification need the Snopes fact-check addressed.

Magill has stated that after Hurricane Andrew, Zoo Miami constructed a concrete holding block as part of the flamingo exhibit's permanent infrastructure, providing a purpose-built storm shelter for the flamingos during subsequent storm events. The improvised bathroom protocol was thus a one-time solution born of necessity that eventually prompted a permanent infrastructure improvement.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes fact-check correctly confirms the photograph's authenticity with admirable sourcing: direct confirmation from the photographer, a named date, the original point-and-shoot provenance, and the Newsweek publication record. What it does not explore is the broader institutional context of zoo emergency management that makes the flamingo bathroom protocol not merely an improvisation but a reflection of a systematic approach to the problem of animal care during extreme weather events.

**Zoo hurricane preparedness as an institutionalized practice.** The Association of Zoos and Aquariums (AZA), which represents more than 230 animal care facilities globally, requires all member institutions to conduct annual disaster preparedness drills as a condition of maintaining accreditation. This requirement emerged in part as a direct response to the experiences of major Florida zoos — including Zoo Miami — during the active hurricane seasons of the 1990s and 2000s. The Zoo and Aquarium All Hazards Partnership (ZAHP), a U.S.-based organization that assists facilities in preparing for hazards including hurricanes, earthquakes, and disease outbreaks, formalizes protocols that were once improvised responses to singular events.

The flamingo bathroom protocol, viewed in this light, is not primarily a comic anecdote about ingenuity under pressure. It is a documented instance of an emergent hurricane management protocol that predated the institutional requirements now codified in AZA standards. Ron Magill's description of the bathroom approach as a standing protocol — "whenever a hurricane warning was issued" — indicates that the flamingo relocation procedure had been established as policy before Andrew struck, not invented on the fly.

This matters for understanding what the photograph actually documents. It is not simply a photograph of chaos and improvisation; it is a photograph of a hurricane protocol in action, taken by a wildlife professional who was fulfilling a pre-established emergency procedure. The fact that the procedure involved a public restroom is a function of the constraints that existed before permanent storm infrastructure was built, not a sign of disorder.

**The flamingo-specific considerations.** Flamingos have specific vulnerabilities in hurricanes that make their evacuation a high priority among zoo animals. Unlike large terrestrial mammals, flamingos are subject to extreme physical danger from high winds: their large, kite-like bodies and long legs make them particularly susceptible to being thrown against enclosure fencing or hard surfaces during storm conditions. Their social behavior — flamingos are obligate flocking species that become distressed when separated from their flock — also means that herding them together in an enclosed space is the correct management approach, not an additional stressor. The restroom, which concentrated the flock in a confined but protected space with fresh water access, addressed both the mechanical and social welfare requirements simultaneously.

Flamingo herding is itself a technically demanding procedure. Magill notes in his 2017 Florida Power & Light video that moving the flamingos from the lake is "the last thing zookeepers do" before a hurricane — partly because it is the most stressful element of the preparation, and partly because flamingos can injure themselves if handled incorrectly. Large wading birds have powerful legs and beaks, and a flock of approximately 38 individuals requires coordinated effort from multiple trained staff members.

**The misattribution problem and information hygiene.** The evolution of the photograph's caption history illustrates a specific pattern in how historical images are recycled during recurring events. When Hurricane Irma threatened South Florida in September 2017, every major media outlet that covered Zoo Miami's hurricane preparations found it natural to illustrate their coverage with the most compelling available photograph of zoo animals in hurricane sheltering conditions. The flamingo bathroom photograph was immediately available and unambiguously illustrative. However, its vintage — 25 years old at the time — made it easy to misassociate with the current event rather than with its historical source.

The pattern of misattribution is not unique to this image. Historical photographs of disasters, weather events, and animal behavior are routinely recirculated with incorrect timestamps during recurring events of similar type. The verification burden this creates is substantial: by 2017, the correct caption (Hurricane Andrew, 1992, by Ron Magill) was widely known among South Florida journalists and historical records, but the viral shockwave of Hurricane Irma preparation coverage moved fast enough to propagate incorrect versions before corrections could circulate. The lesson for information ecosystems is that high-salience historical images should carry embedded metadata or canonical caption records accessible to rapid fact-checking, rather than relying on contextual memory that may not transfer across generations of journalists and content creators.


## 4. Verdict

The claim is **true**. The photograph is authentic, its provenance is documented and verified, and its context — approximately 38 flamingos sheltered in the ladies' public restroom of Miami Metrozoo on August 23, 1992, as part of a pre-established hurricane protocol — is confirmed by the photographer himself, Ron Magill, in direct communication with Snopes and in on-record video interviews.

The photograph documents a real event that was both emblematic of the human and institutional response to Hurricane Andrew and a practical instance of a zoo hurricane protocol that has since been formalized across the zoo and aquarium sector. The misattributions to Hurricane Irma or Hurricane Georges are errors that do not affect the photograph's authenticity — they are simply incorrect in date and context.

Snopes verdict: **True**.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Hurricane Andrew remains one of the defining climatic events in American urban history. Its landfall south of Miami on August 24, 1992, brought sustained wind speeds of approximately 165 miles per hour (Category 5 intensity), with gusts reaching 177 miles per hour measured at ground stations. The storm's eye made landfall near Homestead, Florida, a working-class agricultural community that was largely destroyed in the hours following the eye's passage. Approximately 125,000 homes were damaged or destroyed in South Florida, leaving 175,000 people homeless.

The storm prompted a fundamental reconceptualization of emergency management priorities in the United States. The initial response was widely criticized as inadequate and disorganized, and Andrew is frequently credited as the catalyzing disaster that drove the reform of FEMA (the Federal Emergency Management Agency) and ultimately contributed to the creation of more systematic federal disaster response frameworks. As White House Commission on Aviation Safety and Security member Lee Ielpi later summarized, Andrew "showed that the country was not adequately prepared for major disasters" in the way that subsequent events like the September 11 attacks formally demonstrated for terrorism.

For Zoo Miami specifically, Andrew was catastrophic. An estimated 90% of the zoo's trees were destroyed, most of its enclosures were damaged, and significant infrastructure required complete rebuilding. Animals were directly at risk throughout the storm's passage. No zoo animals died during Andrew itself — a remarkable outcome attributable to the systematic hurricane protocols that Magill and his colleagues had developed and executed — but the post-storm recovery required years of work and substantial resources.

The zoo's response to Andrew became a model for other zoological institutions in hurricane-prone regions. The AZA's development of formal disaster preparedness accreditation requirements in the years following Andrew drew directly on the experiences of Florida institutions. Specific protocols for different animal categories — large mammals sheltered in their indoor exhibits, small primates moved to indoor holding, birds managed according to species-specific vulnerability and behavioral needs — became standard across the industry.

Flamingos (*Phoenicopterus* spp.) are native to subtropical and tropical environments in the Americas, the Caribbean, and parts of Africa and southern Europe. The six recognized species range in size from the lesser flamingo (*Phoeniconaias minor*) at approximately 2.6 feet tall to the greater flamingo (*Phoenicopterus roseus*) at approximately 4.5 feet tall with a wingspan up to 5 feet. Their characteristic pink coloration is derived from carotenoid pigments in their food sources — primarily blue-green algae and small crustaceans — and captive flamingos require supplemented diets to maintain coloration. Their social behavior is among the most obligate of any bird species; flamingos in the wild live in flocks numbering from dozens to millions of individuals, and captive flamingos show significant behavioral stress when isolated or held in small groups.

The flamingo bathroom photograph, beyond its humor and its historical documentation value, is a record of successful animal welfare practice under extreme conditions: 38 vulnerable social birds were managed through one of the most powerful hurricane landfalls in recorded history without loss of life, through a combination of institutional protocol, trained professional response, and a public restroom.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by Madison Dapcevich, published August 22, 2022, correctly rates the claim as **True** and provides strong sourcing: direct confirmation from Ron Magill, the Atlas Obscura retrospective, the WLRN documentation, NPR's reporting on zoo hurricane protocols, and the AZA and ZAHP institutional frameworks. The check also usefully untangles the misattribution history, distinguishing the Hurricane Andrew origin from the incorrect Hurricane Irma and Hurricane Georges attributions.

What the fact-check does not fully develop is the broader context of zoo hurricane preparedness as a systematic institutional practice, the flamingo-specific vulnerabilities that make their evacuation a high priority, and the significance of Andrew as the catalyzing disaster for formal emergency management reform in both the zoo sector and national emergency management more broadly. These additions would elevate the piece from accurate confirmation to substantive environmental history.

The identification of Hurricane Andrew's national disaster management significance is absent from the fact-check, which is understandable given the primary task (confirming the photograph), but represents a missed opportunity to contextualize one of the most culturally resonant images of a generation-defining event.

From a fact-checking methodology standpoint, the key principles demonstrated by this case are: (1) direct confirmation from the photographer/originator of a historical image is the strongest form of provenance evidence; (2) named institutional roles (Zoo Miami goodwill ambassador and communications director) add credibility to first-person testimony; (3) timeline reconstruction of the image's circulation is essential for understanding misattribution patterns; and (4) published print distribution records (Newsweek two-page spread) provide strong corroboration independent of social media provenance.

The Snopes treatment is thorough and accurate, representing a high standard of fact-checking for a historical image verification case. The addition of institutional and ecological context would enhance its educational value but does not alter its core evidentiary quality.
