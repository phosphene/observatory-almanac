## The Claim

In December 2020, a Florida manatee that had been spotted ensnared in a bicycle tire in October 2019 was seen again — this time without the tire. A Facebook post shared on December 5, 2020, by the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission (FWC) and Wildlife Research Institute announced the manatee's return to Blue Spring State Park, having apparently freed itself from the entanglement sometime over the preceding fourteen months.

The claim being verified by Snopes — published December 15, 2020 — was that a manatee spotted in 2019 with a bicycle tire wrapped around its body had made a return appearance in December 2020 without the tire. This was rated True. But the story behind the claim is substantially richer than a simple true/false verdict captures: it involves a year-long conservation effort to rescue the animal, an unexpected mechanism by which the tire was eventually severed, and a conclusion that is simultaneously a cause for celebration and a sobering reminder of the scale of the threats facing Florida's manatee population.

The manatee was named "Schwinn" by researchers — a dry reference to the bicycle tire brand — and "Wheelie" by concerned members of the public following the story. It had been first reported in October 2019 in Fernandina Beach in northeast Florida, where observers noticed the rubber tire, apparently from a standard bicycle, encircling the animal's torso. The manatee then migrated approximately 150 miles south to Blue Spring State Park, a spring-fed sanctuary outside of Orlando that is one of the most important winter refuges for Florida manatees.

## What's Actually True

The events reported by the FWC are factually documented and confirmed through multiple institutional sources. The full sequence of events is as follows.

In October 2019, Schwinn the manatee was spotted in Fernandina Beach with a bicycle tire encircling its body. The tire was sufficiently constricting to represent a serious welfare and survival risk — entanglement in foreign objects is one of the documented causes of injury and death in Florida manatees. The animal was reported to wildlife authorities and began attracting public attention.

The manatee migrated south to Blue Spring State Park for the winter of 2019-2020. Wildlife officials from the FWC and affiliated rescue organisations made multiple attempts to capture and rescue the animal during its stay, but the attempts were unsuccessful. As the affiliated nonprofit Save the Manatee Club documented in a news release: "Due to the difficulties involved in making a safe rescue among hundreds of other manatees staying warm in the spring, Schwinn became wary and evaded rescue boats, research canoes, or approaching in-water biologists, making it impossible to safely rescue the tire-encircled manatee." The presence of hundreds of other manatees at the spring refuge during winter months created operational conditions that made targeted capture of one wary individual extremely dangerous for both the rescuers and the surrounding animals.

Schwinn survived the 2019-2020 winter with the tire intact and departed Blue Spring in spring 2020. In February 2020, the manatee was struck by a boat propeller — an injury that, while itself a cause for concern, had an unexpected secondary consequence. The FWC noted: "Ironically, Schwinn the manatee was superficially struck by a boat's propeller in February, which cut through a significant portion of the tire. The cut from the propeller likely weakened the tire, allowing the manatee to swim free sometime later."

When Schwinn returned to Blue Spring for the winter of 2020-2021, detected on a live webcam maintained by the Save the Manatee Club, it was observed without the tire. Wildlife conservationists confirmed the identification through the manatee's distinctive scar patterns and markings, and verified the absence of the tire. The cold front in late 2020 that brought manatees back to Blue Spring in large numbers allowed the confirmation. Schwinn was alive, had survived the entanglement, had survived the propeller strike, and had returned for another winter — bearing scars from the bicycle tire but freed from the entrapment.

The mechanism of liberation — an accidental propeller cut weakening the tire enough to allow escape — is one of the more unusual conservation outcomes on record. It inverts the usual narrative of boat propellers and manatee welfare (propeller strikes are a leading cause of manatee injury and death) by showing a case where a propeller strike, bad in its own right, accidentally provided the physical cutting force needed to free the animal from a different source of harm.

## Why People Believe This

This story generated large public interest because it combined multiple emotionally resonant narrative elements in an unusually tight package.

**The rescue-narrative arc.** Stories of threatened animals being rescued or escaping danger have a deep and well-documented hold on human emotional attention. Research on prosocial emotional responses to animal welfare coverage — drawing on work by Paul Slovic and colleagues (Slovic, 2014, *PLOS ONE*) on the "collapse of compassion" phenomenon in large-scale need — suggests that identified individual animals generate stronger emotional responses than statistical aggregates of animal welfare problems. Schwinn the manatee was a named individual, tracked over time, whose story had a clear narrative arc: entrapment, failed rescue attempts, and eventual (accidental) freedom.

**The irony structure.** The resolution of Schwinn's story is structured around a dark irony: a boat propeller — the primary tool of manatee harm in Florida — accidentally saved this particular manatee from a different source of harm. This ironic resolution is both genuinely true and narratively satisfying in the way that true ironies often are. It does not resolve into a simple moral; it resists reduction to a clean lesson. The boat that struck Schwinn harmed it and helped it simultaneously. This cognitive complexity is part of why the story attracted sustained attention from conservation reporters and public audiences alike.

**The epistemic novelty of accidental conservation.** Conservation outcomes are usually the result of deliberate intervention: rescue missions, habitat protection, population management, legal protections. Schwinn's liberation involved none of these in its final phase — it was accidental, mediated through a harmful event (the propeller strike) that nobody would have chosen. This represents a genuinely unusual type of conservation outcome: not planned rescue but fortuitous physical coincidence. The epistemic novelty is that the conservation story ends not with institutional will but with a kind of bittersweet luck, which sits at odds with the usual framing of wildlife conservation as a managed, intentional human activity.

**The webcam confirmation.** Schwinn's return was confirmed through a live webcam operated by Save the Manatee Club at Blue Spring — a piece of public information infrastructure that allowed the general public to participate in the verification of the story in real time. The webcam made visible something that would otherwise have been accessible only to researchers: the presence and condition of a specific identified individual in a wild population. The public accessibility of this confirmation, and the fact that it was civilians monitoring it who first spotted Schwinn's return, gave the story a participatory dimension that enhanced its emotional engagement.

---

## Verdict

**The claim is true.** A manatee first spotted in 2019 with a bicycle tire wrapped around its body was confirmed tire-free in December 2020 when it returned to Blue Spring State Park. Wildlife officials confirmed that a boat propeller strike in February 2020 had cut through the tire, likely providing the physical weakening that enabled the animal to eventually swim free. Schwinn survived its ordeal and was identified by its distinctive scars upon its return to Blue Spring.

Snopes rated this claim **True**.

The story is genuine in all its particulars and represents a small but real conservation success, arrived at through an ironic and unplanned mechanism.

## The Wider Picture

Schwinn's story is a window into the larger crisis facing Florida's manatee population, and understanding the ecological and policy context transforms the feel-good resolution into something considerably more complex.

**Florida manatees: conservation status.** The Florida manatee (*Trichechus manatus latirostris*) is a subspecies of the West Indian manatee, the largest living member of the order Sirenia. The U.S. Marine Mammal Commission (MMC) considers the Florida manatee "threatened" under the Endangered Species Act and "depleted" under the Marine Mammal Protection Act — the latter designation meaning the population is estimated to be below its optimum sustainable population level. Population counts conducted by the FWC and U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service have estimated approximately 6,000–7,000 manatees in Florida waters, up from dangerously low numbers in the 1970s when comprehensive protection legislation was first enacted.

The recovery of the Florida manatee from the population lows of the early protection era is a genuine conservation success story. However, the population faces persistent and serious threats that make full recovery uncertain. Boat strikes remain the primary documented cause of manatee injury and death in Florida waters, accounting for a substantial proportion of the approximately 800–1,000 manatee deaths recorded annually by the FWC in recent years. Habitat loss through coastal development has reduced seagrass beds — the primary food source for manatees — and warm-water winter refuges. Harmful algal blooms, particularly red tide events in the Gulf of Mexico, have caused mass mortality events. In 2021, a record number of manatees died in Florida waters — more than 1,100 — largely due to starvation linked to the loss of seagrass in the Indian River Lagoon on Florida's east coast, which had been degraded by nutrient pollution and algal blooms.

Research by Tomas et al. (2015, *Endangered Species Research*) on manatee strandings and mortality patterns in Florida documented that boat strikes contribute to over a third of documented human-related manatee deaths in some years, but that the cumulative impacts of habitat degradation, water quality deterioration, and cold-stress events are increasingly significant relative to acute trauma events. The entanglement story — a bicycle tire — represents a class of threat that is individually dramatic but numerically minor compared to these systemic pressures.

**Entanglement as a conservation concern.** Debris entanglement is a recognised threat to the Florida manatee population, though it represents a smaller component of documented mortality than boat strikes. The FWC and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service track entanglement incidents as part of their stranding and rescue databases. Objects that have been documented in manatee entanglement incidents include fishing line, crab trap lines, rope, netting, and miscellaneous debris including — as Schwinn's case illustrates — bicycle tires and other rubber objects discarded into Florida waterways. The Florida wildlife community has documented cases of juvenile manatees, whose smaller size makes them more vulnerable to entrapment as objects that are loose around them at an early age tighten as they grow.

Research by Gerstein et al. (1999, *Marine Technology Society Journal*) on manatee acoustic and behavioural ecology established that manatees' slow, deliberate movement patterns and tendency to interact with novel objects in their environment partially explain their vulnerability to entanglement: manatees investigate foreign objects rather than avoiding them, and the combination of bovine curiosity with slow mobility in debris-laden waters creates entanglement risk that more agile marine mammals might avoid.

**Blue Spring State Park as critical infrastructure.** Blue Spring State Park, the site of Schwinn's winter refuge, is one of approximately 60 natural warm-water refuges used by Florida manatees during winter months. Manatees are physiologically sensitive to cold water — seawater temperatures below approximately 68°F (20°C) begin to cause cold stress, and temperatures below 60°F can cause death. As ocean water temperatures drop during autumn and winter, Florida manatees migrate to warm-water refuges: natural springs (which maintain constant water temperatures year-round of approximately 72°F), warm-water discharge areas at power plants, and other consistently warm locations.

Blue Spring, with its constant 68-72°F spring water flowing into the St. Johns River, attracts hundreds of manatees each winter so predictably that the park is operated in part as a manatee viewing and research site. The park's live webcam infrastructure — which enabled Schwinn's identification — represents a form of public engagement infrastructure that has educational and research value simultaneously. Save the Manatee Club, the nonprofit that operates the webcam in partnership with the FWC, was co-founded by musician Jimmy Buffett and former Florida Governor Bob Graham in 1981 and has been a significant organisational force in manatee conservation advocacy and public education since that time.

**The unresolved question of intentional debris.** Schwinn's bicycle tire presented a particular challenge during the rescue attempts: wildlife officials could not safely sedate and approach the animal in the crowded Blue Spring environment without risking other manatees. The rescue methodology question — how to safely intervene with an entangled manatee in a high-density manatee refuge — is an active area of discussion in marine mammal veterinary medicine. The Save the Manatee Club's account of the rescue failure is candid about the operational constraints, and it raises a systemic question: what interventions are possible for debris entanglement in conditions that don't permit safe individual handling?

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by Madison Dapcevich is structurally simple but provides important context that transforms what might be a two-sentence verification into a substantive account of manatee conservation.

**The institutional sourcing.** The fact-check draws primarily on official statements from the FWC, the Save the Manatee Club, and Blue Spring State Park — all institutional sources with direct knowledge of the events and with consistent public records of their activities. The FWC Facebook post of December 5, 2020, which announced Schwinn's return, is the primary document; the Save the Manatee Club news release is the secondary source. Both are institutional records with public accountability. This sourcing is appropriate for a claim about a specific documented event involving wildlife management agencies.

**The ecosystem context.** The fact-check includes contextual information about the Florida manatee's conservation status — threatened under the Endangered Species Act, depleted under the Marine Mammal Protection Act — and identifies the primary threats (boat collisions, entanglement, habitat loss). This context, brief as it is in the original article, elevates the story from a single animal's tale to an entry point for understanding the broader conservation situation facing the species. The fact-check treats the Schwinn story accurately as both a genuine news event and a teaching occasion.

**The irony acknowledged.** The fact-check notes the ironic mechanism of liberation — the propeller strike — without editorialising. The FWC's own statement uses the word "ironically," acknowledging the unusual quality of the outcome while maintaining the factual focus appropriate to an official communication. Dapcevich preserves this element in the Snopes article, and it is the detail that gives the story its emotional and narrative texture.

**What the "True" rating doesn't capture.** The True verdict confirms that Schwinn was spotted without its tire. It does not capture what is genuinely concerning about the story: multiple rescue attempts failed; the liberation was accidental and mediated through another injury; Schwinn bears permanent scars from the ordeal. The outcome is positive relative to the alternative (a slow death from the tightening tire or from compromised mobility) but not positive as an instance of successful wildlife management. The rescue system developed for Florida manatees, however sophisticated, was unable to help this animal when it needed help. This limitation deserves acknowledgment alongside the celebratory element of the story.

The Schwinn story is ultimately a microcosm of Florida manatee conservation: genuine recovery at the population level, persistent threats, institutional commitment to protection, and outcomes that are sometimes bittersweet rather than cleanly successful. The bicycle tire that wrapped around a manatee in a Florida waterway, and the boat propeller that accidentally cut it loose, both belong to the same story — the story of a large, slow, gentle species navigating a landscape increasingly shaped by human activity and human debris.
