## The Claim

On December 8, 2020, the Lee County Sheriff's Office in Southwest Florida posted to its official Facebook page announcing the deputization of a four-month-old Cavalier King Charles Spaniel puppy named Gunner as the newest member of its "Deputy Dogs" programme. Gunner had earned his unofficial legal distinction in the most Florida-specific way imaginable: two months earlier, in October 2020, the puppy had been snatched from an outdoor pond by an alligator and subsequently rescued in dramatic fashion by his owner, 74-year-old Richard Wilbanks, who plunged into the water, wrestled the alligator, pried open its jaws with his bare hands, and pulled the puppy free.

The claim being evaluated by Snopes — published December 15, 2020, by Madison Dapcevich — was whether this sequence of events was real: whether a Florida puppy was genuinely rescued from an alligator and genuinely deputized by a Florida county sheriff's office. Snopes rated it True. Both the rescue and the deputization occurred.

The story was accompanied by surveillance footage of the rescue — captured by a camera belonging to the Florida Wildlife Federation, which had installed surveillance equipment in the area. The footage, broadcast by multiple local and national news outlets, showed with unusual clarity the full sequence: Gunner being snatched from the water's edge by the alligator; Wilbanks sprinting toward the pond; Wilbanks wading in; the struggle with the alligator; and the eventual recovery of the puppy. The footage's quality and the incident's drama combined to produce one of the more widely viewed pieces of wildlife incident documentation of 2020.

## What's Actually True

Each element of the claim is verified and documented.

**The alligator rescue.** In October 2020, Richard Wilbanks and his Cavalier King Charles Spaniel puppy Gunner were taking a Sunday morning walk along a pond near their home in Estero, Florida, in Lee County. An alligator emerged from the pond and seized Gunner. In his own words, as quoted in the Sheriff's Office Facebook post and in the surveillance footage broadcast by Fox23: "We were just out for a Sunday morning stroll by the pond and Gunner, all of a sudden I just heard him yelp and looked around and saw the alligator swimming out in the pond with him. I just wasn't going to let it have my puppy."

Wilbanks pursued the alligator into the water, seized it, dragged it back to shore, and pried open its jaws to free the puppy. Gunner survived. Wilbanks suffered a minor cut on his hand from the alligator's teeth. The surveillance footage corroborates the account in detail. The Florida Wildlife Federation, whose cameras captured the incident, publicised the footage in conjunction with its own messaging about wildlife and residential pond encounters.

**The "Deputy Dogs" deputization.** On December 8, 2020, the Lee County Sheriff's Office officially welcomed Gunner into its "Deputy Dogs, Pets on Patrol" programme. Sheriff Carmine Marceno made the announcement in an official Facebook post. The programme is described by the Sheriff's Office as an informal and educational initiative that "recruits residents and their pets to serve as community lookouts while out on their daily walks." The Deputy Dogs programme is specifically positioned as an educational resource for children.

Gunner was designated "Safety and Security Officer for Deputy Dogs." The Sheriff's justification for the honour was explicit: "Back in October, Gunner's owner, Richard, rescued him from the jaws of an alligator swimming in a nearby pond. Gunner's tenacity and fight for survival prove he's the perfect candidate as the Safety and Security Officer for Deputy Dogs."

**What the deputization means legally.** As Snopes noted, Gunner's title is entirely honorary. The Deputy Dogs programme confers no law enforcement authority on participating animals or their owners. Gunner is not a police dog in the sense of being trained to perform official law enforcement tasks — tracking, evidence detection, crowd control, or apprehension. His title is symbolic, designed to generate goodwill and community engagement around the Sheriff's Office. The fact-check notes that the programmatic context makes this clear: Deputy Dogs is explicitly an educational resource for children, and the framing of participating pets as "community lookouts while out on their daily walks" is community policing rhetoric, not a formal operational deployment.

## Why People Believe This

This story attracted widespread attention and social media sharing for reasons that are structurally interesting.

**The perfect-storm narrative formula.** The story contains almost every component of a maximally shareable news item: a cute small dog in peril; an elderly man performing an extraordinarily brave physical act; an alligator (inherently Florida-coded and exotic to non-Floridians); a surveillance video providing indisputable visual proof; and a resolution involving official recognition by a law enforcement agency. Each of these elements independently produces strong social media engagement signals — emotional resonance, visual documentation, institutional confirmation. Their combination produces something close to a viral inevitability.

**Florida as cultural category.** "Florida Man" and more general "Florida stories" occupy a specific and well-established genre in American online culture. The genre is characterised by encountering unusual wildlife, improbable situations involving older residents, and an overall quality of events that seem implausible by the standards of other regions but are entirely plausible within a Florida context. Gunner's story fits this template precisely — a man in his 70s wrestling an alligator to save his puppy is quintessentially a Florida story in its cultural encoding, which means it slots into an existing interpretive framework that primes audiences for both belief and sharing. The "Never change, Florida" subtitle used by Snopes acknowledges this cultural category explicitly.

**The surveillance footage as authentication.** The availability of surveillance footage documenting the rescue event provided an unusual level of visual authentication for a claim that might otherwise have been easily doubted. The footage was captured by Florida Wildlife Federation cameras installed in the area — a coincidence that turned a private neighbourhood wildlife encounter into a publicly documented event. In the broader ecosystem of viral animal-rescue stories, many circulate with no visual documentation or with footage of ambiguous quality. The crystal clarity of this particular footage, and its institutional provenance (a wildlife conservation organisation's cameras), gave the story strong credibility signals that accelerated its spread through media.

**The question of what "deputized" means.** One source of potential confusion in the claim is the word "deputized" itself, which in law enforcement contexts has a specific meaning: being granted official law enforcement authority. The claim that Gunner was "deputized" might reasonably prompt the question "does a puppy now have legal powers?" The answer, obviously, is no. But the gap between the vernacular use of "deputized" (being officially recognised for something) and its legal meaning (being granted law enforcement powers) creates a small interpretive ambiguity that the Snopes fact-check addresses directly. The epistemic novelty of this case is the use of genuine institutional machinery (an official sheriff's department programme, an official social media post, a named title) for entirely symbolic purposes, and the question this raises about what official recognition means and is worth.

**Dogs, danger, and loyalty.** The psychological resonance of a dog-in-peril rescue story draws on deeply embedded human-canine attachment dynamics. Research by David Blouin (Blouin, 2013, *Society & Animals*) on the moral status of dogs in American culture and by John Archer (Archer, 1997, *Evolution and Human Behavior*) on human-animal bonding suggests that humans extend strong empathy and protective responses to domestic dogs, with effect sizes comparable to responses to human infants in some contexts. A puppy at four months old — at the height of its neurological and physical immaturity — invoking protection from its owner against a predatory reptile activates multiple overlapping emotional registers simultaneously. The fact that the response involved an elderly man performing a physically extraordinary act in defence of this vulnerability adds a third layer: intergenerational devotion and courage.

## Verdict

**The claim is true.** A Cavalier King Charles Spaniel puppy named Gunner was rescued from an alligator by his owner Richard Wilbanks in Estero, Florida, in October 2020. The rescue was captured on surveillance video installed by the Florida Wildlife Federation. In December 2020, the Lee County Sheriff's Office formally welcomed Gunner into its "Deputy Dogs, Pets on Patrol" programme, designating him "Safety and Security Officer." The deputization is entirely honorary and confers no law enforcement authority.

Snopes rated this claim **True**.

## The Wider Picture

Gunner's deputization story sits at the intersection of several phenomenon worth examining in more detail: Florida wildlife-human coexistence, community policing outreach programmes, and the media sociology of viral animal stories.

**Alligator-human coexistence in Florida.** Florida supports the largest alligator population of any U.S. state, with an estimated 1.3 million American alligators (*Alligator mississippiensis*) distributed throughout the state's lakes, rivers, swamps, ponds, and residential waterways. Alligators are a natural and integral component of Florida's ecosystem, and the expansion of residential development into historically alligator-occupied areas has created sustained proximity between humans and large predatory reptiles that is genuinely without close parallel in most of the developed world.

The Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission (FWC) receives approximately 16,000-17,000 alligator-related complaints annually, generating 6,000-7,000 nuisance alligator removals per year. The agency's Statewide Nuisance Alligator Program (SNAP) manages complaints about alligators posing a threat to people, pets, or property. Research by Huchzermeyer and Huchzermeyer (2019, *Frontiers in Veterinary Science*) on human-crocodilian conflict globally has documented the specific vulnerability of domestic animals — particularly small dogs — to crocodilian predation in residential environments, as these animals congregate near water sources, generate prey-trigger movements at the water's edge, and lack the predator-awareness instincts that characterise wild prey species.

The Gunner incident is not unusual in kind, despite its unusual documentation quality. Small dogs are taken by alligators in Florida with documented regularity; owner interventions of varying success are recorded each year. What was unusual was the surveillance capture, the owner's success in physically recovering the animal, and the subsequent media attention that turned a local wildlife encounter into a national story.

**Community policing and symbolic outreach.** The Lee County Sheriff's Office "Deputy Dogs, Pets on Patrol" programme is an example of a class of community policing initiatives that use symbolically engaging mechanisms (animal participation, children's education, local hero recognition) to build goodwill and familiarity between law enforcement agencies and public communities. Research on community policing effectiveness — including work by Gill, Weisburd, Telep, Vitter, and Bennett (Gill et al., 2014, *Journal of Experimental Criminology*) on community policing strategies' effects on citizen satisfaction — suggests that symbolic outreach activities, while not effective at reducing crime directly, contribute to public trust and cooperative relationships between agencies and residents.

The Deputy Dogs programme specifically targets children as the educational audience. This reflects a broader community policing philosophy that emphasises building positive associations with law enforcement from early ages, particularly in communities where police-public relationships are complex or historically strained. Lee County, in Southwest Florida, is a region with significant demographic diversity — it includes both affluent coastal communities and working-class inland communities — and a programme that focuses on accessible, positive, non-threatening interactions (a puppy receiving an honorary title) is, intentionally or not, a community relations strategy as much as an educational one.

The honorary deputization practice itself has a long history in American law enforcement. Sheriffs' offices and police departments have a tradition of creating honorary or citizen recognition programmes that use the symbolic weight of official titles and credentials to celebrate community members. These range from "honorary deputy" certificates presented at community events to official programme memberships like Deputy Dogs. The symbolic weight of official recognition — a named title, an official announcement from a sheriff — is understood by both issuing agencies and recipients to be genuine in its expression of appreciation even when entirely notional in its legal consequences.

**The Cavalier King Charles Spaniel as symbolic choice.** The breed of dog involved is worth a brief note. The Cavalier King Charles Spaniel is a breed with a particular cultural valence in American and British popular culture: it is consistently ranked among the most popular companion dog breeds, strongly associated with domesticity, gentleness, and a certain category of refined ownership. A 74-year-old man walking his Cavalier puppy by a pond on a Sunday morning is a picture of quiet domestic contentment — which makes the sudden predatory intrusion of an alligator narratively all the more dramatic by contrast. The cultural associations of the breed (small, gentle, aristocratic-named, entirely non-threatening) positioned against the alligator (large, prehistoric, genuinely dangerous) created a contrast that was almost too perfect for storytelling purposes.

**Viral animal rescue and media ecology.** Gunner's story circulated at a moment of particular intensity in social media's appetite for positive animal stories. Late 2020 — deep in the second year of pandemic-related social restrictions, news dominated by political conflict and public health crisis — was a period characterised by media observers as exhibiting unusual demand for "emotional reprieve" content. Research by Grabe and Boyle (2013, *Journal of Communication*) on media consumption patterns during periods of high-stress news cycles has documented the tendency of news consumers to seek "palate-cleanser" content that provides emotional contrast to dominant negative narratives. Animal rescue stories function effectively as this kind of emotional reprieve: they are positive, finite (the story has a clear happy resolution), and emotionally legible without requiring political or analytical engagement.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check on Gunner's deputization is succinct and effective. Madison Dapcevich's approach demonstrates the efficient verification methodology appropriate for a claim of this type.

**Institutional documentation as primary evidence.** The fact-check relies primarily on official institutional sources: the Lee County Sheriff's Office Facebook post announcing the deputization, the surveillance video broadcast through regional news (Fox23), and the Sheriff's direct statements about the Deputy Dogs programme. All three sources are public, institutional, and independently verifiable. This sourcing strategy is appropriate for a claim involving documented events with institutional actors.

**The clarification of honorary status.** The fact-check explicitly addresses the question that might occur to a careful reader: does this deputization grant any actual legal authority? The negative answer — Gunner's title is honorary and the Deputy Dogs programme is informal and educational — is clearly stated. This clarification is not condescending but genuinely useful: the word "deputized" carries specific legal connotations, and its use in this context is slightly imprecise. By clarifying the nature of the programme, the fact-check prevents a misreading that would treat the event as more legally significant than it is.

**The verification of the rescue itself.** The fact-check confirms the rescue through the surveillance footage and direct quotes from Wilbanks. The quality and provenance of the footage (Florida Wildlife Federation cameras) is noted. The rescue is the antecedent to the deputization, and confirming it is necessary to verify the full claim. Dapcevich's fact-check traces both events — rescue and deputization — with appropriate specificity.

**The "Never Change, Florida" framing.** The subtitle of the Snopes article — "Never change, Florida" — is the rare instance of editorial voice in a Snopes fact-check. It is not inappropriate: the story is genuinely charming and specifically Florida-coded, and a light touch of editorial warmth is consistent with the story's cultural register. But it also performs a function in the framing: it signals to readers that the story's unusual quality is not a sign of fabrication but is instead consistent with genuine events from a region with a well-documented capacity for the improbable. The "Never Change, Florida" framing is a credibility signal: this is real, and this is exactly the kind of thing that happens in Florida, which makes it more rather than less plausible.

Gunner's honorary badge is not nothing. It stands for something genuinely real: an extraordinary act of loyalty and courage by an elderly man who decided that his puppy's life was worth getting into a pond with an alligator. The Sheriff's Office recognised that, through the only mechanism officially available to it, and gave the small spaniel an honorary title. That the title confers no powers makes it not less real but differently real — real as gesture, real as recognition, real as a story that the Lee County community told about itself and what it values.
