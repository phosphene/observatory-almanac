## §1 — Claim & Verdict Summary

Beginning in April 2022, a widespread social media campaign asserted that an unusual and suspicious number of fires at food processing facilities across the United States constituted evidence of a coordinated attack on the national food supply. The claim was rated **False** by Dan Evon (2022) of Snopes, who found that upon systematic examination the purported "trend" was neither statistically unusual nor supported by any evidence of arson, sabotage, or coordinated action. The Context note appended to the verdict states the conclusion precisely: almost all fires on circulating meme lists involved explainable accidental causes, the list included events that were not large food processing facilities (including an abandoned building and a butcher shop), the claimed "recent" timeframe spanned more than 16 months, and when Snopes researchers searched for comparable food processing accidents in prior years — 2019, 2020, 2021 — they found an essentially equivalent density of incidents.

A correction published June 9, 2022, amended one numerical detail: the number of fires identified in 2019 represented less than 0.1% (not 1% as originally stated) of the approximately 36,000 food and beverage processing establishments in the United States documented by the USDA's Economic Research Service (USDA ERS, 2019).

## §2 — Origin and Spread of the Claim

The claim first achieved significant circulation in late March and early April 2022, emerging in the direct wake of U.S. President Joe Biden's March 24, 2022 statement — delivered at a Group of Seven meeting in Brussels — that the Russian invasion of Ukraine would cause global food shortages. Bloomberg (2022) reported Biden as saying: "It's going to be real. The price of the sanctions is not just imposed upon Russia. It's imposed upon an awful lot of countries as well, including European countries and our country as well." Ukraine and Russia together account for a substantial share of global wheat supply, and the combination of war disruption, harvest uncertainty, and energy-price-driven input cost increases had genuine structural implications for global grain markets.

The viral food-processing-fire claim appears to have emerged almost immediately after these Biden remarks, functioning as a conspiratorial explanation framework for an impending food shortage that had been given presidential acknowledgment. Evon (2022) noted that a systematic search for social media content about food processing fires before and after Biden's remarks found "practically no claims before Biden's remarks, and an emerging trend of people claiming that there had been an unusual amount of fires at food processing facilities after Biden's remarks." This finding is epidemiologically significant: it identifies the vector of initial spread as reactive conspiracy formation around a legitimately alarming public statement rather than organic discovery of an actual trend.

The specific content circulating took multiple forms: a frequently shared image aggregating various news article headlines about fires at food-related facilities; a list on a website called TimCastIRL that claimed "at least 16 such disasters in the last six months" but opened with a news story from 2019; and various social media posts with lists of 18 to 20 fire incidents. The rhetorical strategy across all versions was identical: aggregation of disparate events, presentation as a coherent list, and implicit or explicit suggestion of coordinated causation.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis and Novel Findings

**Epistemic novelty:** This case is a textbook instance of what cognitive scientists call *apophenia* — the perception of meaningful patterns in objectively random or statistically normal data. Susan Blackmore and colleagues have documented the apophenic tendency as a stable cognitive trait distributed in the human population, with heightened expression under conditions of stress and uncertainty (Brugger & Graves, 1997, *Perceptual and Motor Skills*). The food-processing-fire claim emerged at precisely such a moment: a geopolitical crisis affecting food supply, a simultaneous inflationary period, a pandemic-fatigued information environment sensitized to supply disruption signals, and a presidential statement legitimizing food shortage as a real concern.

The empirical structure of the false trend also exhibits what Kahneman (2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*) termed the *WYSIATI* (What You See Is All There Is) bias: the prominent availability of a list of fire incidents, without comparison to any baseline frequency, produces a compelling subjective impression of abnormality that the absence of a denominator renders impossible to evaluate. No viral claim accompanying the fire list asked: "How many fires at food processing plants occurred in 2021? In 2020? In 2019?" The answer — that Snopes researchers found essentially equivalent density of incidents across all three prior years — was suppressed not through deception but through the simple non-collection of denominator data.

Sunstein and Vermeule (2009, *Journal of Political Philosophy*) argued that conspiracy theories often grow from "crippled epistemology" — limited access to information that makes otherwise implausible theories more plausible within the available informational frame. The food-processing-fire conspiracy exemplifies this: anyone who had only the 2022 fire headlines and no access to the historical baseline would rationally conclude, from the apparent clustering, that something unusual was occurring. The conspiracy theory was, in a limited sense, epistemically rational given constrained information access — and the primary analytical corrective was simply providing the denominator.

The USDA ERS (2019) figure of 36,000+ food and beverage processing establishments in the United States provides the essential denominator. Across a universe of 36,000 facilities, each operating complex industrial machinery, often involving highly combustible organic materials (dust from grain, fat, flour; ammonia refrigeration; petroleum-based cleaning products; volatile oils), the recurrence of fires at the rate of 20–30 per year across identifiable news reports represents a frequency of less than one event per 1,500 facilities per year. This base rate is entirely consistent with ordinary industrial accident frequency and would not be expected to produce visible year-over-year clustering in uncontrolled case counts.

The epistemology of food-industry fire risk also has a well-developed technical literature. Abbasi and Abbasi (2000, *Journal of Loss Prevention in the Process Industries*) documented the elevated fire and explosion risk in food processing plants relative to general manufacturing, attributable to the combustibility of organic particulate matter (grain dust, cocoa dust, powdered milk, sugar dust), pressurized oils, and refrigerant gases. The U.S. Chemical Safety and Hazard Investigation Board (CSB) has documented multiple high-profile food processing facility incidents caused by combustible dust, a recurring and well-known industrial hazard category. This industrial risk literature establishes that food processing fires are not anomalous events requiring conspiratorial explanation; they are recurrent mechanical and safety challenges inherent to the sector's physical processes.

**The rhetoric of the disingenuous timeline:** Evon (2022) flagged a particularly instructive example from the TimCast article, which claimed to document a "strange trend" of fires "in the last six months" but began its list with a 2019 news article. The article then wrote "just days later" about a subsequent fire incident — with "days" actually referring to a span of more than 700 days. This rhetorical chronological compression, presenting years of normal background events as though they constituted a recent cluster, is a well-documented technique in conspiracy theory construction that researchers in the sociology of rumor describe as *temporal conflation* (Fine, 2007, *Rumor Mills: The Social Impact of Rumor and Legend*).

**Research gap:** A critical gap exists in public-facing epidemiological data about food processing facility industrial accidents. Insurance actuarial databases exist (FM Global, Zurich, XL Catlin/AXA XL) and OSHA maintains workplace injury records, but no public single-source federal database provides annual fire/explosion incident frequency for food processing establishments specifically, disaggregated by facility type, scale, and cause. The absence of this public baseline is precisely what makes claims about "unusual trends" both plausible and difficult to debunk efficiently. A prospective, public, systematically maintained incident registry for the food processing sector — analogous to the NFPA's fire statistics reports for the residential and commercial sectors — would substantially reduce the informational conditions under which such conspiracy theories can gain traction.

## §4 — Evidence Examined

**Scale denominator:** USDA Economic Research Service (2019). *Manufacturing*. https://www.ers.usda.gov/topics/food-markets-prices/processing-marketing/manufacturing/. Documents 36,000+ food and beverage processing establishments in the United States, providing the essential baseline denominator for evaluating claimed trend abnormality.

**Industry expert statement:** Tom Super, Senior Vice President of Communications, National Chicken Council, statement to Snopes (2022): "Like any manufacturing plant, there are generally a few fires that occur each year across the country. Most of them are contained rather quickly. And certainly not enough to affect the chicken supply. There are about 200 federally inspected chicken slaughtering plants in the US and thousands more that further process chicken. I would not categorize this as an 'alarming trend.'"

**Conspiracy claim source analysis:** TimCast.com article (archived at https://archive.ph/IXHYG): claimed "at least 16 [food processing fire] disasters in the last six months" but opened list with a 2019 story; referenced "just days later" interval of 700+ days. Demonstrates disingenuous timeline construction.

**2019 fire incident list:** Evon's 2022 research found 26+ news stories about food processing fires, explosions, and accidents from 2019, including: conveyor belt fire at snack production plant in Iowa; three workers injured in explosion at soybean oil facility in Nebraska; cocoa dust fire at Conagra cake mix plant; ADM explosion killing a firefighter in Clinton, Iowa; 5-alarm food factory fire in Chicago; over 3,800 workers displaced by Tyson Foods beef plant fire in Kansas; Sacramento Blue Diamond factory fire; and multiple others. This list is not exhaustive but establishes clear baseline coverage comparable to 2022.

**Geopolitical context:** Bloomberg (2022, March 24). Biden says to expect "real" food shortages due to Ukraine war. Documents the presidential statement that preceded and apparently catalyzed the fire conspiracy claim's viral spread.

**Arson absence:** No incident in the circulating lists was identified by authorities as suspected arson. The Lackawanna County meat processing facility fire, for example, was "ruled an accident" (WNEP.com, 2021).

**Non-qualifying inclusions:** The first item on the most widely shared fire image list involved a building abandoned for years and attributed to homeless occupation (KFOX, 2022). A butcher shop (Conway Daily Sun) was included despite being substantively different in scale and nature from large food processing facilities.

## §5 — Counterclaims, Limitations, and Alternative Interpretations

**Against the conspiracy narrative:** The conspiracy theory's claim that nefarious forces attacked food processing plants to create shortages is not merely unproven but actively contradicted by the available evidence. No instance of suspected arson was identified among the incidents cited. The most impactful facilities reported that their primary production schedules were not affected. The JBS Beef Plant explicitly stated the fire "did not impact the primary production area." If the goal were sabotage, the operational outcomes documented suggest conspicuous failure.

**The coincidence narrative:** A milder version of the claim would hold not that fires were deliberately set but simply that more fires than usual occurred, whether by unusual coincidence or systemic deferred maintenance during pandemic-related operational disruptions. This is a more plausible claim than deliberate sabotage, but it also fails the available evidence: the 2019 baseline (without pandemic pressures) shows comparable incident density, and no insurance actuarial or fire safety organization reported an aggregate trend deviation.

**Limitation — incomplete denominator research:** While Evon's 2022 research provided a 2019 baseline, the investigation acknowledged it did not perform a rigorous year-over-year actuarial comparison against all available official data sources. The definitive refutation of an unusual trend would ideally come from OSHA incident databases or insurance industry actuarial reports showing no statistically significant deviation from historical mean frequencies. This level of rigor was not available to Snopes in the investigation window. The available evidence strongly supports the False verdict but does not provide a statistical significance test of absence.

**Limitation — coverage bias:** News reporting frequency of fire incidents is itself subject to coverage bias — the same fire at a food processing facility may receive disproportionate media attention in April 2022 due to the conspiracy context compared to an identical fire in 2019. The social media amplification of fire stories during the conspiracy claim's circulation potentially created a positive feedback loop in which greater reporting attention made the trend appear more robust than it was, independent of any change in underlying incident frequency.

---

## §6 — Conclusion and Standing

The verdict of **False** is well-supported by multiple converging lines of evidence: (1) documented base rate equivalency between 2022 and 2019 fire incidents; (2) expert industry statements characterizing the frequency as normal; (3) the USDA's establishment-count denominator showing that even 26 fires represent less than 0.1% of facilities; (4) total absence of arson suspicion in any included incident; (5) the inclusion of non-qualifying facilities in widely shared lists; and (6) the clear chronological connection between Biden's food shortage warning and the emergence of the fire conspiracy narrative, suggesting reaction formation rather than independent empirical discovery.

The case stands as a significant example of how genuine macro-level uncertainty — in this case, real war-related global food supply disruption acknowledged by the U.S. president — creates receptive conditions for conspiratorial reframing of normal background industrial events. The 2022 food processing fire conspiracy required no novel events; it required only a pre-existing supply of routine industrial accidents, a list-aggregation format, and an ambient fear of food shortage actively legitimized at the highest political level.

The epistemic gap — the absence of public, maintained, accessible baseline data on food processing facility incidents — remains an ongoing vulnerability that could facilitate similar conspiracies in future crisis periods.

Standing as of 2026-07-21: Verdict unchanged. The 2022 food-processing-fire conspiracy theory has largely faded from active circulation as the immediate Ukraine-conflict food scarcity anxiety has become normalized into broader inflation discourse, but the structural conditions enabling this type of conspiracy (absence of public baseline data, high ambient anxiety about food supply) persist.
