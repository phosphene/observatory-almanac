# Should You Lay 'Buckets of Water' Out For Wildlife Escaping Wildfires?


## 1. The Claim

In December 2017, as multiple devastating wildfires burned across Southern California — incinerating more than 116,000 acres across Los Angeles and Ventura counties — a social media post began circulating widely that purported to relay official government guidance for homeowners. The message read, in its most common form:

> *If you live ANYWHERE near the forest fires that are decimating the California PLEASE be aware that the animals are fleeing the fires and they may show up in your yards. The forestry department is URGING you to bring your animals in at night and let the wild ones pass through. Please put out buckets of water for them — they are scared, exhausted, and have also lost their homes — they need to refuel.*

The post was not new in December 2017. An earlier version had circulated in September 2017 during wildfires in the Pacific Northwest, using nearly identical language with a minor regional adjustment ("our beautiful Pacific Northwest" replacing references to California). The recirculation of the post during the Southern California fires gave it a second viral life and brought it to the attention of readers in areas where wildfires were actively burning.

The claim rests on two distinct components that are worth separating analytically. First, the factual claim that a government "forestry department" is actively urging this behavior — that there is official guidance behind the request. Second, the proposed action itself — putting buckets of water out for wildlife — which might be evaluated independently of whether any government body endorsed it. The viral post collapses both into a single imperative, presenting the action as validated by institutional authority.

Snopes investigated and rated the claim **False** in a December 7, 2017 article by Arturo Garcia, updated December 8 to correct a reference to the U.S. Forest Service.


## 2. What's Actually True

The U.S. Forest Service, when contacted by Snopes, explicitly confirmed that it was not the "forestry department" that allegedly issued this advice. This is the crucial factual determination: the institutional endorsement the post claimed is fabricated. No federal or state forestry agency, wildlife agency, or USDA entity issued guidance urging the public to put buckets of water in their yards for wildlife during the 2017 California wildfires.

More significantly, the wildlife management experts consulted by Snopes actively discouraged the implied course of action — human intervention with wildlife displaced by wildfires. John Griffin, director of urban wildlife programs for the Humane Society of the United States (HSUS), told Snopes that when people instinctively want to help fire-displaced animals, they are "shooting in the dark" because interference with wildlife that is already navigating a crisis may cause additional harms that cannot be anticipated. His quoted reasoning deserves careful attention:

> *"In this situation it's just better not to have an intervention, because [animals] are extremely resilient and they're aware of other food or water sources. Trying to interfere with that, you don't know that interfering won't cause an issue. If they're truly injured and require assistance, that's a different set of circumstances."*

Peter Tira, a spokesperson for the California Department of Fish and Wildlife, offered similar guidance with even less nuance: "If you encounter a wild animal in our neighborhood, leave it alone. Fire or no fire, just let the animals be."

The reasoning behind this professional consensus is ecologically well-grounded. Wild animals that have been displaced by fire are often in an elevated stress state, and human proximity can trigger flight responses, cause stress-induced injury, or draw predators to areas where prey animals are already concentrated and vulnerable. Animals with a history of habituation to humans — particularly those that have encountered supplemental food or water sources — are also more likely to return to suburban environments, creating longer-term problems for the animal (reduced wariness, increased conflict with domestic animals and humans) and for the community.

The specific proposal in the viral post — water buckets — carries an additional practical problem: during active fire events, the animals most affected by water stress are typically those deep within fire perimeters, not those reaching suburban yards. Animals that have successfully reached a residential area have almost certainly also found streams, pools, sprinklers, and other water sources before water deprivation became critical. The water-bucket intervention is therefore remedially redundant for the animals most likely to encounter it.

The claim about wildlife coming to residential yards during wildfires is also, as an empirical matter, less commonly actualized than the post implies. Research on wildlife movement during wildfire events shows that most birds and large mammals move away from fire perimeters across landscape-scale gradients over periods of days, not in concentrated corridors through suburban yards (Nimmo et al., 2019, "Animal Movement in Fire-Affected Landscapes," *Ecology Letters*, 22, 1608–1618). Animals such as rabbits, deer, and bobcats may occasionally enter residential areas during extreme events, but they are unlikely to do so in the concentrated, "fleeing through your yard" pattern that the viral post evokes.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes rating of False is accurate as far as the fabricated-authority component goes: no government body issued this advice, and wildlife experts actively contradict the proposed action. But the fact-check does not engage with several questions that significantly deepen our understanding of why this claim spreads, what it gets wrong about wildlife ecology and disaster response, and what the psychological mechanism is that makes fabricated-authority claims of this type so persistent and resilient.

The most important gap is in the underlying ecology of wildlife and wildfire. The viral post assumes a straightforward relationship: fire → frightened animals → exhausted animals at your fence → water → help. This human-intuitive causal chain is systematically inaccurate in ways that wildlife ecologists have documented extensively.

Many species that reside in fire-prone ecosystems — particularly in California's chaparral, scrubland, and mixed conifer forests — have evolved behavioral and physiological responses to fire that are fundamentally different from what the viral post implies. Rather than panicking in a linear direction away from fire, many animals respond to early fire detection by moving to pre-existing refugia: rock outcrops, burrows, riparian corridors, areas of high moisture content, and other microhabitats that provide natural protection (Pausas & Schwilk, 2012, "Fire and Plant Evolution," *New Phytologist*, 193, 301–303; Engstrom 2010, "Landscape of Fear in Fire-Prone Ecosystems," *Proceedings*, Tall Timbers Fire Ecology Conference). Post-fire, animal movement is not simply "away from fire" but is shaped by resource availability, predator distributions, conspecific behavior, and the patchy structure of fire-modified landscapes.

The role of fire as an ecological process in chaparral systems is also relevant here. California's native fire-adapted ecosystems have been structured by recurring fire for thousands of years, and many of the animals native to these systems have behavioral repertoires that reflect this evolutionary history. A deer or coyote that has lived through multiple fire seasons in chaparral habitat is unlikely to be paralyzed by fear in the way the viral post suggests; such animals have access to culturally learned and instinctively encoded behavioral responses that would not be improved by human intervention.

A second gap in the fact-check is the psychological anthropology of the claim. The "buckets of water for wildlife" meme belongs to a well-documented category of disaster-response viral content that fuses a genuine emotional need — the wish to take protective action during catastrophe — with a behaviorally tractable but epistemically unfounded prescription. Research in crisis communication and disaster psychology has established that individuals experiencing helplessness in the face of catastrophe are strongly motivated to adopt any available behavioral action that feels protective or contributory, even in the absence of evidence for its effectiveness (Fritz, 1961, "Disasters and Mental Health," in *Contemporary Social Problems*, Harcourt, Brace; Solnit, 2009, *A Paradise Built in Hell*, Viking Penguin). This "action bias" is adaptive in many contexts but creates a vulnerability to fabricated-authority claims that offer clear, simple behavioral prescriptions during periods of ambient anxiety.

The fabricated authority component is itself worth more analytical attention than Snopes provides. The post does not name a specific person or department; it refers vaguely to "the forestry department" in a way designed to sound official while being sufficiently generic that no specific agency can be contacted for verification by an ordinary reader. This is a characteristic feature of a class of viral health and safety claims that invoke unnamed institutional authority (Griffin & Dunwoody, 2016, "Impacts of Emotions and Trusted Voices on Perceptions of Scientific Consensus," *Environmental Communication*, 10(1), 114–132). The authority-claim function here is not primarily informational — it is motivational. The post wants people to act, and "the forestry department is URGING you" serves as the motivational frame that converts a suggestion into something that feels like an imperative.

The third gap is in what responsible intervention during wildfires actually looks like for members of the public who genuinely want to help wildlife. The Snopes piece correctly notes that injured animals require specialized care ("a different set of circumstances," as Griffin put it), but it does not identify what those circumstances are or who the appropriate contacts would be. Most states have licensed wildlife rehabilitators and state-certified wildlife rescue organizations that are specifically trained and equipped to handle fire-displaced or fire-injured animals. The National Wildlife Rehabilitators Association (NWRA) maintains a directory, and many local fire response organizations coordinate with wildlife agencies during major events. Suggesting to curious readers that the appropriate response to an injured fire-displaced animal is to contact a licensed rehabilitator rather than to independently supply water would have been more informative than a simple False rating.


## 4. Evidence Assessment

**Evidence confirming the False rating:**
- U.S. Forest Service explicitly denied issuing the advice (Snopes, December 7, 2017).
- John Griffin, HSUS Director of Urban Wildlife: active expert discouragement of the proposed action.
- Peter Tira, California Department of Fish and Wildlife: "leave it alone."
- No documented case of the "buckets of water" intervention providing measurable benefit to fire-displaced wildlife in any scientific or wildlife management literature.

**Evidence for the underlying concern (wildlife distress during wildfires is real):**
- Confirmed large-scale wildlife displacement during major California wildfires, documented by California Department of Fish and Wildlife reports and news coverage.
- Research confirming that wildfire perimeter areas experience significant wildlife stress and increased mortality, particularly for reptiles, some small mammals, and ground-nesting birds (Nimmo et al., 2019; Saab & Powell, 1995, "Timber Management and Neotropical Migratory Birds," *Studies in Avian Biology*, 17).

**Gap:**
- No systematic study exists on outcomes for fire-displaced animals that received supplemental water from private citizens, making the specific intervention claim unresolvable from the base-rate direction.


## 5. EARC Analysis

- **EARC Mode: C (Contested — rated False)** — The fabricated-authority component is confirmed false; the proposed action is actively contradicted by wildlife management expert consensus.
- **Gap Category: fabricated-authority** — The claim deploys a vague but authoritative-sounding institutional voice ("the forestry department is URGING you") as the primary persuasive mechanism; this authority was fabricated and the actual relevant authorities explicitly contradicted the advice.
- **Confidence in Verdict:** High. Multiple independent expert sources and the relevant government agencies agree.
- **Knowledge Gap:** No empirical data on behavioral outcomes for wildlife after citizen water provision during fire events; the psychological mechanism producing this class of viral disaster-mitigation claims is well-documented but rarely addressed in fact-check contexts.
