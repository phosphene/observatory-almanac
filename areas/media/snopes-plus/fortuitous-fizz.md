## The Claim

The claim is a staple of trivia collections, beverage industry histories, and casual cultural knowledge: Coca-Cola became a carbonated drink by accident. A soda fountain clerk at a drugstore — in some tellings working under distraction, in others simply making a routine substitution without thinking — mixed Dr. John Pemberton's new syrup with carbonated soda water instead of the plain water that would have produced a non-fizzy equivalent. The fizz was a happy accident. The resulting popularity of the accidental formulation led Pemberton to adopt it as the standard.

The claim is pleasingly neat and conveys a moral that flatters the listener: remarkable fortunes can rest on trivial errors; the difference between obscurity and world-historical commercial dominance might be a single fumble at a soda fountain. By 1999, when Barbara Mikkelson published the Snopes investigation into this origin story, the "accidental carbonation" narrative had appeared in sources as respectable as the *World Almanac Book of Inventions* (D'Estaing, Valerie Giscard; Pharos Books, 1995, ISBN 0-91181-896-0), *Consumer Reports* (August 1991, p. 30), and a nationally syndicated column by motivational speaker Zig Ziglar (*St. Louis Post-Dispatch*, 8 October 1995, p. D15). Its circulation was not limited to oral folklore — it had been laundered into print by otherwise credible reference sources.

The claim is false.

## What's Actually True

The factual record of Coca-Cola's invention has been reconstructed in considerable detail by two authoritative corporate histories: Frederick Allen's *Secret Formula* (HarperCollins, 1994, ISBN 0-88730-672-1) and Mark Pendergrast's *For God, Country, and Coca-Cola* (Charles Scribner's Sons, 1993, ISBN 0-684-19347-7). Both researchers drew on primary sources including interviews, company archives, and the recollections of people who were present at or connected to the early development of the drink. Their conclusions on the carbonation question are consistent and unambiguous: Pemberton planned from the beginning to mix his syrup with carbonated water.

Allen's account, quoted directly by Mikkelson, is particularly clear. In April and early May of 1886, Pemberton was in active product development. He had created a syrup and was conducting taste tests through a distribution arrangement with Willis Venable's soda fountain, located three blocks from his laboratory on the ground floor of Jacobs' Pharmacy at 2 Peachtree Street in downtown Atlanta — the intersection known as Five Points. Venable billed himself as the "Soda Water King of the South" and operated a twenty-five-foot marble-countered soda fountain that was a local landmark.

Allen documents the specific detail that refutes the accident narrative: according to Frank Robinson, Pemberton's business partner who was present throughout the development period, the syrup was sent to Venable's fountain specifically to be tested as a soda water mixture. As Robinson recollected: "it was taken to Mr. Venable's soda fountain for the purpose of trying it and ascertaining whether it was something the people would like or not." The test purpose was to determine consumer response to the syrup combined with carbonated soda water. This was the plan. There was no accident.

Pendergrast's account adds a further detail that removes any residual ambiguity. Lewis Newman — Pemberton's nephew — and John Turner, who both apprenticed with Pemberton during this period, recalled being "sent down to the drugstore to get a drink of Coca-Cola for Pemberton, since there was no carbonated water at the laboratory." This detail is significant: it confirms that carbonated water was the intended mixing ingredient from the earliest days, and that the reason Pemberton used Venable's fountain rather than making the drinks himself was simply that his laboratory lacked a source of soda water. Pendergrast notes explicitly that this evidence "contradicts the Company dogma that Coca-Cola was accidentally mixed with soda water about a year later."

The context for why carbonated soda water was the obvious, planned ingredient is itself worth explaining. Pemberton was a pharmacist in Atlanta during a period when carbonated soft drinks were an established and growing market. By 1886, Hires Root Beer had been available since 1876, Dr Pepper had launched in 1885, and countless regional soft drink brands were competing for a share of the soda fountain trade. These drinks were all carbonated. The soda fountain was, by its nature, a venue for carbonated beverages — the carbonation was a core part of the product category. Pemberton was developing a product for the soda fountain market. He would have had no more reason to plan for plain-water mixing than a brewer would have reason to plan for a non-alcoholic process.

There is also an important historical context for why Pemberton was developing a new soda water product at all. Atlanta and Fulton County had voted to go dry in 1885, prohibiting alcoholic beverages. Pemberton had previously been marketing French Wine Coca — a near-plagiarism of the highly successful French tonic wine Vin Mariani, which combined Bordeaux wine with Peruvian cocaine leaf extract and was endorsed by figures including Pope Leo XIII and Queen Victoria. When local prohibition eliminated wine as a base, Pemberton's commercial logic required finding a replacement vehicle for his coca preparation. Soda water was the obvious substitute — it was the dominant non-alcoholic base in the burgeoning soft drinks market.

The accident narrative is, then, not merely slightly wrong — it inverts the actual causation. The carbonation was not an afterthought rescued by luck; it was the plan. The specific flavor profile — the combination of coca extract, kola nut, and the array of other flavorants that give Coca-Cola its distinctive taste — was developed precisely to mask the bitter and unpalatable taste of coca and kola when combined with soda water. The formula evolved through deliberate experimentation; the carbonation was the constant, not the variable.

## Why People Believe This

The accidental-carbonation narrative is an excellent case study in the psychology of origin myths, and Barbara Mikkelson's Snopes treatment identifies the core mechanism precisely: there is a cultural need — particularly strong in democratically-minded societies that are also deeply invested in meritocracy narratives — to explain remarkable commercial success as partly attributable to luck.

**The egalitarian consolation of serendipity.** If Coca-Cola's success was the product of an accident — a trivial substitution error at a soda fountain — then anyone might have had the same lucky accident. The distance between ordinary people and extraordinary fortunes is, in this framing, a matter of being in the right place when the right mistake happens. This is a psychologically comforting narrative because it preserves the possibility that luck, rather than exceptional skill or effort, is the variable that separates the merely competent from the spectacularly successful. As Mikkelson observes, invoking serendipity functions to "assure ourselves that a little bit of good fortune is all that separates us from the fabulously rich, people who were just like us until lady luck paid them a visit."

**The epistemic novelty of the "under-design" hypothesis.** The cognitive tendency to underestimate the design that underlies apparently "simple" discoveries is a well-documented cognitive bias. Products that people encounter as simple finished forms — a soft drink, a household item, a piece of everyday technology — rarely carry any trace of the iterative development, failed trials, deliberate planning, and expert knowledge that produced them. Pemberton was not a naive experimenter; he was an experienced pharmacist who had been formulating patent medicines for years. His development of Coca-Cola involved protracted experimentation with flavor profiles, consultations with partners, market testing, and competitive positioning. None of this complexity is visible in the final product. The accident narrative fills the gap left by this invisible complexity with a story that feels complete but assigns creative agency to chance rather than to the craftsmanship that was actually operating.

**The laundering effect of repetition through credible sources.** The *World Almanac Book of Inventions*, *Consumer Reports*, and a nationally syndicated motivational speaker's column are not unreliable sources in general. They are sources that people consult with a reasonable expectation of accuracy. When a claim — even a false one — appears in sources of this kind, it acquires a kind of borrowed authority that makes it significantly harder to dislodge. The accidental-carbonation story had been told and retold in formats that conferred credibility on it, not through the deliberate fabrication of any single person but through the cumulative effect of repeated publication by outlets that did not verify the claim against primary sources. The mechanism here is one of distributed epistemic responsibility: each publisher repeats the story because they encountered it in a credible source, without any individual actor taking responsibility for verifying the original claim.

**The allure of the founding accident narrative as a genre.** The accidental discovery origin story is a recognisable narrative genre with famous exemplars: Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin (mould contaminating a petri dish), Percy Spencer's discovery of the microwave oven (a radar magnetron melting a chocolate bar in his pocket), Post-it Notes (a repositionable adhesive developed by 3M's Spencer Silver, initially considered a failure). Some of these stories are substantially accurate; others are simplified or distorted versions of more complex histories. The genre has its own conventions and pleasures — the oblivious scientist or technician, the surprising result that turns out to be more valuable than the intended one, the moment of recognition. Pemberton's story slotted neatly into this genre. Once the slot was available, the false story could be told and retold with narrative satisfaction.

**The difficulty of verifying negative claims about deliberate intent.** Proving that something was planned rather than accidental is epistemically harder than proving a factual positive claim. To prove that Pemberton planned the carbonation requires access to records of his intentions — recollections of witnesses, documented plans, archival materials. These exist, in the case of Coca-Cola's history, thanks to Allen's and Pendergrast's archival research. But for most consumers of the claim, the archival evidence is not available; what is available is a plausible-sounding accident story. The asymmetry between the ease of telling a false but plausible story and the difficulty of verifying its falseness is a structural condition that favours the circulation of false origin narratives.

## Verdict

**The claim is false.** Coca-Cola was not carbonated by accident. Dr. John Pemberton intended from the earliest stage of product development to mix his syrup with carbonated soda water. His market tests at Venable's fountain in spring 1886 were conducted with carbonated soda water by design. The testimony of his business partner Frank Robinson and of witnesses present during the development period confirms this planning.

The accident narrative is a cultural fabrication — not a deliberate lie by any single actor, but a story that emerged from the psychological need to explain large commercial success through accessible narrative, and that persisted because it appeared in credible sources that did not verify it against primary records. Its falseness was documented by Frederick Allen (*Secret Formula*, 1994) and Mark Pendergrast (*For God, Country, and Coca-Cola*, 1993), drawing on archival and oral history sources. The Snopes fact-check by Mikkelson (1999) brought this documented falseness to a broader audience.

The corrected understanding is, if anything, more interesting than the myth. Pemberton was not a lucky fumbler. He was a professional pharmacist who had been operating in the patent medicine and soft drink market for years, who responded deliberately to the market opportunity created by Atlanta's prohibition law, who developed a flavor profile specifically calibrated for the soda fountain market, and who tested his product through deliberate market trials. His success was not a happy accident. It was the outcome of professional knowledge, deliberate planning, and iterative development applied to a commercially viable product opportunity.

## The Wider Picture

The false origin narrative of Coca-Cola's carbonation illustrates a broader pattern: the systematic under-attribution of skill and design in popular accounts of commercial and technological invention.

**Serendipity as a cultural narrative category.** Research on the history of science and technology has documented a persistent tendency to narrativise discoveries through serendipity frameworks even when the underlying histories are more deliberate and structured. Royston Roberts' *Serendipity: Accidental Discoveries in Science* (Wiley, 1989) catalogues genuine cases alongside popular distortions. The genuinely accidental discoveries — and there are real ones — tend to occur in contexts where deliberate experimental work has created the conditions for the accidental result to be noticed and understood as significant. Fleming's penicillin contamination, for example, was a genuine accident, but Fleming's ability to recognise its significance depended on years of deliberate research that had already primed him to understand what he was seeing. The distinction between accident and design is rarely as clean as origin narratives suggest.

**The history of carbonated beverages.** The technical history of carbonated soft drinks provides context that the accident narrative ignores. The deliberate commercial production of carbonated water dates to the late 18th century. Joseph Priestley produced the first systematic account of artificially carbonating water in 1767 (published in his pamphlet "Directions for Impregnating Water with Fixed Air," 1772), and Johann Jacob Schweppe founded his carbonated water business in 1783, which became the first commercially successful carbon dioxide—infused water company. By the mid-19th century, soda fountains dispensing carbonated beverages were established fixtures of American pharmacy culture. The "soda jerk" — the fountain operator — was a recognised trade role. Pemberton was operating within an already-mature industry that took for granted the combination of flavored syrups with carbonated water.

**Corporate mythology and brand history.** The Coca-Cola Company itself contributed to the contested state of its own history through decades of official narratives that emphasised select elements (including, at times, versions of events closer to accident than deliberate design) and suppressed or downplayed others. Pendergrast's access to company archives was hard-won, and his account contradicts what he calls "Company dogma" on several points. The history of Coca-Cola's self-presentation is a fascinating sub-field in which the distinction between corporate myth and corporate history is itself part of what needs investigation.

The Coca-Cola Company has, over the decades since Pendergrast's and Allen's publications, largely moved away from versions of the accidental carbonation story in official communications. But the false narrative continues to circulate in popular reference works, trivia compilations, and casual accounts — demonstrating that corrections to established false claims require active and repeated effort to propagate, while the false claims themselves spread and persist with much less effort required.

**Implications for fact-checking practice.** The accidental-carbonation story illustrates a specific challenge for fact-checkers: false claims that appear in credible reference works are significantly harder to dislodge than false claims that circulate only through informal channels. The *World Almanac* and *Consumer Reports* are, in general, reliable sources. Finding that they contain a specific false claim requires checking them against primary sources rather than simply against other secondary sources. This is methodologically demanding: it requires knowing which primary sources exist and having access to them, which few general-audience fact-checkers can reliably accomplish for all claims.

Mikkelson's treatment is notable for drawing directly on Allen's and Pendergrast's primary archival work rather than simply asserting the false claim's falseness. This approach — citing the historical research that refutes the popular account — is essential for this category of fact-check. Without the underlying archival research, the claim that the carbonation was deliberate would itself be an assertion, and the circular argument between rival assertions would be undecidable. The archival grounding is what makes the verdict possible.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes treatment of this claim is one of Mikkelson's more explicitly analytical early pieces. It does not merely establish the verdict and provide the evidence; it also addresses the psychological question of *why* the false narrative persists. This dual function — factual correction plus psychological explanation — is an underappreciated aspect of good fact-checking practice.

**The rating.** Snopes rates the claim "False" — accurate and appropriate. The factual record is clear: Pemberton planned the carbonation. The archival evidence from Allen and Pendergrast is unambiguous. The "False" rating is warranted.

**The psychological analysis.** Mikkelson's observation about the cultural need to attribute commercial success to luck is one of the most analytically interesting passages in Snopes's early catalogue. It identifies a genuine cognitive mechanism — the egalitarian consolation of serendipity — that predicts not just this specific false belief but an entire class of invention mythology claims. This generalisation makes the article valuable beyond its immediate fact-checking function.

**What the analysis misses.** The Snopes article does not discuss the broader pattern of corporate history distortion, the role of Coca-Cola's own official communications in shaping the received account, or the specific mechanisms by which the false claim was laundered through reference works. These are not deficiencies so much as evidences of the article's scope — it is a targeted piece of claim investigation rather than a comprehensive cultural history of Coca-Cola myth-making.

**The sourcing.** The article's reliance on Allen (1994) and Pendergrast (1993) is appropriate and sufficient for the factual verdict. Both books are well-sourced corporate histories based on archival research and first-hand testimony. The quotes from both are directly relevant and clearly sourced. The contemporaneous false accounts (from *World Almanac*, *Consumer Reports*, and Ziglar's column) are identified with publication details, which allows the reader to assess the breadth of the false claim's circulation. This combination — refutations from primary-research books alongside citations of the false account's source trail — is the appropriate structure for this genre of fact-check.
