# "Fortune Cookies Are a Chinese Tradition"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: Fortune cookies — the crisp, folded pastries containing paper slips bearing aphorisms, predictions, or "lucky numbers" — originated in China as a cultural tradition and arrived in the United States as part of the culinary heritage of Chinese immigration.

**Implicit corollary**: Fortune cookies are therefore an authentic feature of Chinese cuisine and a genuine cultural export of China, in the same category as dishes such as dim sum, chow mein, or Peking duck — foods that migrated to America while retaining their Chinese cultural roots.

**How the claim is encountered**: The fortune cookie's apparent Chinese-ness is transmitted by repeated association rather than explicit assertion. Every fortune cookie arrives at the end of a Chinese restaurant meal. Every on-screen Chinese restaurant scene involves fortune cookies. The packaging of fortune cookies sold in supermarkets features Chinese design motifs. No Chinese restaurant menu advertises fortune cookies as Japanese-American. The claim is not usually made as a positive argument but absorbed as ambient background knowledge through hundreds of accumulated experiences of receiving a fortune cookie in a distinctly Chinese-American dining context.

**Variants of the claim**: Some versions go further, connecting fortune cookies to specific Chinese historical practices — stories of messages baked into mooncakes during the Yuan dynasty, or of Buddhist temples offering paper fortunes inside folded pastries. These more elaborate variants construct a continuous lineage stretching from medieval China to the modern Americanized fortune cookie. They have the effect of transforming the cookie from mere food into cultural artifact, lending the false origin story an aura of historical depth.

**The paradox of authentic inauthenticity**: A rich irony underlies the fortune cookie's cultural status. The cookie is now so thoroughly associated with Chinese-American cuisine that it is practically a symbol of it — a metonymic stand-in for "Chinese restaurant" in popular culture and commercial representation. And yet fortune cookies are not served in China, are rarely known or eaten there, and have no active role in Chinese culinary tradition. When fortune cookies were introduced to China in the early 1990s during expansion of American-style restaurant culture, they were received as a novelty import — an American thing. This inversion — the "quintessentially Chinese" food being experienced as foreign by Chinese people — is one of the cleanest markers of a cultural misattribution.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Japanese confectionery antecedent**:

The historical predecessor of the American fortune cookie is a Japanese confection called *tsujiura senbei* (辻占煎餅), a sesame- and miso-flavored cracker folded around a paper fortune slip. *Tsujiura senbei* have been documented in Japan since at least the 19th century and possibly earlier; illustrated woodblock prints from Kyoto depicting fortune-telling crackers have been dated to the late Edo period (Nakamachi, M., 1998, "The Fortune Cookie: The Real History," *Gastronomica*, 2(3): 38–47). The crackers were associated with Shinto shrine practices and festival culture, sold near temple complexes where fortune-telling was a standard religious service. The physical design — a folded cracker or wafer with a paper slip tucked inside or held within the fold — is unmistakably homologous with the American fortune cookie.

**California, early twentieth century**:

Fortune cookies in the United States appear in the historical record in northern California between approximately 1907 and 1920. The primary competing origin claims center on two Japanese-American businessmen in the San Francisco Bay Area.

The first is Makoto Hagiwara, a Japanese landscape designer who served as the head gardener of the Japanese Tea Garden in Golden Gate Park. According to historical documentation assembled by food historian Yasuko Nakamachi and corroborated by archival research at the California Historical Society, Hagiwara was serving fortune cookies at the Tea Garden as early as 1907–1908, with the cookies made by a local Japanese bakery, Benkyodo. The Benkyodo company, founded by brothers Suyeichi and Juzaburo Okamura, was a Japanese-American confectionery business that produced a range of Japanese-style pastries for the San Francisco market (Manabe, N., 2008, *Comparative American Studies*, 6(4): 359–378, documenting the Benkyodo-Hagiwara connection).

The second claim centers on David Jung, a Chinese-American immigrant who founded the Hong Kong Noodle Company in Los Angeles. Jung claimed to have invented the fortune cookie in 1918 as a philanthropic measure — distributing cookies with Bible verses to unemployed men on the streets of Los Angeles. The Jung claim has been championed by the Los Angeles Chinese-American business community and was famously the subject of a 1983 mock trial in San Francisco's Court of Historical Review, which ruled in favor of the Hagiwara/Japanese origin (United Press International, 1983). However, the Jung claim lacks documentary corroboration at the level of the Hagiwara/Benkyodo evidence.

**The scholarly consensus**:

Academic food historians have reached substantial convergence on the Japanese-American origin story. Nakamachi's research (1998) in particular is considered the most methodologically rigorous investigation, pairing physical-cultural analysis of Japanese fortune-telling crackers with archival documentation of California Japanese-American businesses. Her conclusion — that fortune cookies descend directly from the *tsujiura senbei* tradition and were first produced in the United States by Japanese-American bakers — is widely cited in subsequent scholarly literature (Coe, A. & Coe, S.D., 2009; Diner, H.R., 2001, *Hungering for America*, Harvard University Press, pp. 194–196).

**The wartime displacement mechanism**:

The single most important factor transforming fortune cookies from a Japanese-American confection into a "Chinese" food was the forced incarceration of Japanese Americans during World War II. Following Executive Order 9066 (issued February 19, 1942), approximately 120,000 Japanese Americans — regardless of citizenship — were removed from the Pacific Coast and confined in internment camps (Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians, 1982, *Personal Justice Denied*, U.S. Government Printing Office). This forced relocation shuttered Japanese-American businesses across California, including the bakeries and confectioneries that had been producing fortune cookies. The supply chain for fortune cookies was severed.

Into this vacuum, Chinese-American restaurant owners and food suppliers stepped. Fortune cookies had been adopted by Chinese-American restaurants as convenient end-of-meal treats during the 1920s and 1930s, purchased from Japanese-American bakeries. When those bakeries disappeared, Chinese-American entrepreneurs built their own production capacity. By the time Japanese Americans returned from internment and attempted to reestablish their businesses, the fortune cookie supply infrastructure was entirely in Chinese-American hands and had been for years. The cookies were by then so thoroughly embedded in the Chinese-American restaurant experience that their Japanese origin was simply not visible to the postwar American public (Lee, E., 2015, *The Making of Asian America*, Simon & Schuster, pp. 184–190).

**China today**:

Fortune cookies are not part of Chinese culinary culture in China. They are not baked there at scale, not served in restaurants there as a tradition, and are not recognized as Chinese by Chinese nationals. When Wonton Food Inc., the world's largest fortune cookie manufacturer (producing approximately 4 million cookies per day from its Queens, New York facility), attempted to introduce fortune cookies to China in 1992 through retail distribution, market surveys found that Chinese consumers regarded them as alien and distinctly American (Shih, J., 1992, *Asian Wall Street Journal*, archived reporting). The product line was discontinued.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — invisible cultural displacement and the power of context-based attribution**:

The fortune cookie myth is epistemically unusual because it does not rest on any single stated falsehood. No one tells people "fortune cookies were invented in China." Instead, the belief is generated by context: the cookie arrives within a system — the Chinese-American restaurant meal — that marks it as belonging to that system's cultural heritage. This is a form of attribution-by-association, and it is both cognitively automatic and extraordinarily resistant to correction.

Human cognition is strongly context-bound in cultural attribution. When we consume a food within a particular ethnic cuisine context, we automatically assign it cultural membership in that cuisine. The context does not merely accompany the information; it constitutes the information's meaning. A diner does not consciously reason: "I received this cookie at the end of a Chinese meal, therefore it must be Chinese." The inference is pre-conscious, categorical, and runs below the threshold of deliberation. This is adaptive cognition because context typically is a reliable signal of cultural origin — most foods served in Chinese restaurants do have roots in Chinese culinary tradition. The fortune cookie is an exceptional case, but the mind has no automatic mechanism for flagging exceptions to reliable context-dependent inferences.

The Japanese-American origin story is also rendered invisible by a structural asymmetry in American cultural memory. Japanese-American history received thoroughgoing suppression during and after World War II, not only through physical incarceration but through the cultural erasure that followed. The internment generation largely assimilated to American majority culture, Anglicizing names and minimizing ethnic distinctiveness. Their businesses, interstitial in the American economy before the war, were not reconstituted in anything like their original form. The fortune cookie's Japanese antecedents disappeared along with the social network and institutional memory that would have maintained the oral transmission of its origin story. In the absence of that memory, the cookie was orphaned from its history and adopted by the visible culinary tradition that presented it to American consumers.

There is also a deeper pattern at work involving the legibility of Asian cultures to American consumers. Through most of the twentieth century, the internal distinctions between Asian ethnic communities — Japanese-American, Chinese-American, Korean-American — were often invisible to white American consumers of Asian food and culture. "Asian" and "Chinese" were often used interchangeably in casual discourse, particularly in culinary contexts. This perceptual conflation made it easy for a Japanese-American confection to be absorbed into "Chinese food" without triggering any noticed contradiction. The granularity of distinction that would have been required to notice the Japanese origin was simply not present in the cultural categories available to most American consumers.

Finally, the fortune cookie's origin story is complex, multi-causal, and features as its central mechanism an episode — Japanese-American internment — that is itself underrepresented in public historical consciousness. Correcting the fortune cookie myth properly requires engaging with this history, which adds affective and cognitive weight to the correction. For a casual false belief with no negative practical consequences, this is a high threshold for belief revision.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE**: Fortune cookies are not a Chinese tradition. They originate in Japanese-American confectionery culture in early twentieth-century California, descending from the Japanese *tsujiura senbei* tradition. Their current association with Chinese-American restaurants is an artifact of World War II forced incarceration of Japanese Americans, which displaced Japanese-American bakers from the supply chain and handed production to Chinese-American entrepreneurs who served a generation of American diners without historical attribution.

**The Snopes ruling**: Snopes rates this claim False, tracing the Japanese-American origin with reference to Nakamachi's historical research and noting that fortune cookies are not present in Chinese culinary culture as practiced in China.

**What would need to be true for the claim to be correct**: The claim could be salvaged only if evidence emerged of a distinct Chinese antecedent predating the Japanese *tsujiura senbei* tradition and the California Japanese-American bakers — a historical record (recipes, illustrations, textual references) showing fortune-bearing folded pastries in Chinese culinary or ceremonial practice before 1900. No such evidence exists in the food history literature. The mooncake-message stories that sometimes accompany the Chinese-origin claim are documented as mythology rather than history; they refer to messages baked into solid cakes (not folded wafers), and even if true would not constitute an origin for the specific folded-pastry-with-paper format that defines the fortune cookie.

**Practical significance**: The fortune cookie myth is low-harm but non-trivially interesting as a case of cultural misattribution with structural causes rooted in ethnic discrimination. It erases a specific episode of Japanese-American economic displacement and cultural contribution that deserves historical visibility. The myth's correction is minor in practical terms but significant as an act of historical accuracy.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The fortune cookie sits within a large category of "inauthentic ethnic foods" — dishes that American diners associate with the culinary traditions of immigrant groups but that were developed in the United States and have no genuine counterpart in the source culture. American Chinese food in general is a rich example: dishes such as General Tso's chicken, chop suey, beef with broccoli, and egg foo young were all either invented in America or substantially transformed there and have little or no presence in regional Chinese culinary traditions (Anderson, E.N., 1988, *The Food of China*, Yale University Press).

The fortune cookie represents a particularly concentrated version of this phenomenon because the misattribution involves not one but two ethnic groups: a Japanese-American origin is hidden by a Chinese-American transmission, with both the hiding and the transmission shaped by historical forces (internment, postwar assimilation, immigrant business competition for consumer access) that have nothing to do with cuisine and everything to do with political economy. The cookie is, in this reading, a small edible monument to American immigration history.

The myth also illustrates the general dynamic by which visible cultural communities absorb the cultural products of less-visible communities when those communities face legal suppression. Scholars of American food history have identified parallel mechanisms in other contexts: the absorption of African-American culinary innovations into "Southern cooking" marketed by white restaurateurs, the erasure of Indigenous food knowledge in mainstream American agriculture, and the incorporation of Mexican-American food labor and technique into "Tex-Mex" cuisine with credit flowing away from the actual innovators. The fortune cookie's history is a legible, non-inflammatory, and non-tragic version of a widely documented pattern.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes**: Labels the claim False, providing citation of Nakamachi's research and noting the wartime displacement mechanism. Gives appropriate emphasis to the historical suppression of Japanese-American culture as explanatory context.

**Scholarly treatment**: The fortune cookie origin story has attracted notable academic attention disproportionate to the apparent magnitude of the question, precisely because it is a clean case study for food history methodology (tracing undocumented cultural flows) and for the historical sociology of Asian-American communities. Jennifer 8. Lee's *The Fortune Cookie Chronicles* (2008, Twelve Books) is the most widely read book-length treatment, synthesizing the historical record for a general audience and explicitly framing the question as a window into Asian-American history more broadly.

**Popular culture handling**: The fortune cookie's origin has been covered in mainstream media including *The New York Times*, *Smithsonian Magazine*, and NPR's food programming. Coverage uniformly confirms the Japanese-American origin. Interestingly, polling data cited in some coverage suggests that awareness of the cookie's Japanese-American origin has increased substantially since Lee's 2008 book, making this one of a small number of food myths where popular correction has actually achieved measurable impact on belief distribution.

**Chinese-American community responses**: Reactions within Chinese-American communities to the "fortune cookies aren't Chinese" narrative have been mixed. Some community voices have embraced the corrected history as an opportunity to acknowledge shared history with Japanese-American communities and to reflect on the dynamics of Asian-American ethnic group relations. Others have pushed back, arguing that the decades of Chinese-American adoption, modification, and commercial development of the fortune cookie constitute a legitimate claim to cultural ownership regardless of origin, and that origin-based attribution misses the significance of the cookie's evolution as a Chinese-American cultural artifact. This debate itself reflects genuine complexity about what "cultural ownership" of a food means in immigrant American contexts.
