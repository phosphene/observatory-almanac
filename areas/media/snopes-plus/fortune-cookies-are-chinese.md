# Fortune Cookies Are a Chinese Tradition

## 1. The Claim

At the end of a meal at a Chinese restaurant in the United States, the check arrives accompanied by small, folded cookies — light, crisp, mildly sweet, each containing a slip of paper with a proverb, a prediction, or a lucky number. Fortune cookies have become so thoroughly identified with Chinese food in the American imagination that questioning their origins seems almost pedantic. They appear in Chinese restaurant scenes in films and television, are marketed as Chinese novelties, and occupy a firm place in the popular conception of Chinese culinary culture.

The claim that fortune cookies are a Chinese tradition — or at minimum, that they originated in China — is almost universal among people who have not specifically researched the question. Even more sophisticated versions of the belief acknowledge Chinese-American origin while assuming the item entered American culture through the Chinese immigrant community.

The reality is considerably stranger and considerably more interesting.

## 2. What's Actually True

Fortune cookies originated in Japan.

The closest documented ancestor of the American fortune cookie is the *tsujiura senbei* — a grilled rice cracker containing a slip of fortune paper, produced by shrine bakeries and street vendors in and around Kyoto. These cookies appear in Japanese woodblock prints from the 19th century and were sold at Shinto shrines, particularly the Fushimi Inari Taisha — the great shrine complex south of Kyoto famous for its thousands of torii gates. They were made by hand over charcoal grills, and their fortunes (*tsujiura*) were folded inside the biscuit rather than inserted into a pocket.

The tsujiura senbei were made from a darker dough, flavored with sesame and miso, and were considerably less sweet than their American descendants. But their form — a folded wafer enclosing a fortune slip — is unmistakably the prototype of the American fortune cookie.

How they arrived in America is a matter of some documented history and persistent dispute. The most credible origin account credits Makoto Hagiwara, the Japanese-American landscape designer who was the primary creator and long-term caretaker of the Japanese Tea Garden in San Francisco's Golden Gate Park. Hagiwara began serving fortune cookies at the Tea Garden's tea house in approximately the 1890s or early 1900s, using cookies supplied by a local Japanese bakery. The cookies were intended to thank visitors for their patronage and may have contained notes expressing gratitude rather than generic fortunes.

A competing origin claim centers on David Jung, a Chinese immigrant who founded the Hong Kong Noodle Company in Los Angeles around 1916 and claims to have invented the fortune cookie independently as a way to distribute biblical scripture to the poor. The 1983 "Mock Trial of the Fortune Cookie" in San Francisco — held at the Court of Historical Review, an institution that exists primarily for historical amusements — ruled in favor of Hagiwara's claim, though the verdict carries no legal weight and the Los Angeles claimants have never conceded.

Subsequent food history research, including Jennifer 8. Lee's comprehensive 2008 book *The Fortune Cookie Chronicles: Adventures in the World of Chinese Food*, places the Japanese origin on substantially firmer historical and documentary ground. Lee also documented the remarkable aftermath of World War II, in which Japanese-Americans were interned under Executive Order 9066 (1942), their businesses were seized or shuttered, and Chinese-American entrepreneurs — many of whom were exempt from internment as citizens of an Allied nation — filled the void in Japanese restaurant supply chains. Fortune cookies, which had been primarily associated with Japanese-run businesses, were adopted by Chinese restaurant owners during and after the war and became associated with Chinese cuisine almost by accident of wartime displacement.

By the time the internment ended and Japanese-Americans were released and permitted to rebuild, fortune cookies had been thoroughly absorbed into the cultural identity of Chinese restaurants. The Suyeoka family, who had operated a fortune cookie operation before the war, found the market had moved on. Chinese-American entrepreneurs had mechanized production — Shuck Yee's invention of the fortune cookie folding machine in 1964 made mass production viable — and the cookies were now indelibly associated with Chinese-American food culture.

Today, fortune cookies are essentially unknown in mainland China. Chinese visitors to the United States frequently express surprise at encountering them as a "Chinese" food. When a major Chinese company began importing American fortune cookies to China in the 1990s as an exotic curiosity, the novelty of the product was the selling point — Chinese consumers encountered fortune cookies as an American thing, not a familiar one.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The belief in Chinese origin is almost entirely a product of context. Fortune cookies are served in Chinese restaurants. Chinese restaurants are where Americans encounter fortune cookies. The association is experienced so consistently and so repeatedly that it naturally produces an inference of origin: the food must come from the culture whose restaurants serve it.

This is a reasonable inference in most culinary contexts, and it reflects a broader cognitive pattern in which cultural authorship is assigned based on contemporary association rather than historical investigation. Pizza is Italian, sushi is Japanese, tacos are Mexican — these associations are broadly correct at the level of cultural origin even if the specific forms are products of emigration and adaptation. The fortune cookie is an exception: it is presented in Chinese restaurants, but it originated elsewhere and was absorbed into Chinese restaurant culture through a specific historical event that most diners know nothing about.

The role of Japanese-American internment in the fortune cookie's cultural migration is rarely discussed in popular contexts, which means that even people who know fortune cookies have a disputed history often do not know the wartime dimension. Lee's *Fortune Cookie Chronicles* brought this history to wider attention, but the book's reach, however impressive for a work of food history, is modest compared to the frequency with which fortune cookies are served and encountered.

Marketing has done nothing to correct the association. Fortune cookies are packaged with Chinese characters and imagery, sold in stores as "Chinese fortune cookies," and presented by many restaurant chains as part of a authentically Chinese dining experience. The commercial infrastructure reinforces the cultural attribution regardless of historical accuracy.

There is also a specific cultural dynamic at play. Chinese-American restaurant culture in the United States developed its own distinct identity — a hybrid cuisine that is neither authentically Chinese nor simply American but something original, shaped by immigration, adaptation, and the specific demands of non-Chinese customers. Fortune cookies fit naturally into this hybrid tradition. They belong to Chinese-American culture in the sense that Chinese-American restaurants have made them their own, even if they did not originate there. This creates a genuine ambiguity: is it wrong to call something Chinese-American if it has been thoroughly incorporated into Chinese-American cultural practice? The answer depends on whether the question is about origin or about belonging.

The simplest explanation for the belief's durability, however, is that no one asks. Fortune cookies arrive at the end of a pleasant meal, eaten without much thought. The question of where they came from is not one that most diners have occasion or motivation to pursue.

## 4. Verdict

The claim is **false in its standard form**. Fortune cookies did not originate in China and are not a Chinese tradition. They appear to have originated in Japan, were introduced to the United States by Japanese immigrants (most credibly Makoto Hagiwara at San Francisco's Japanese Tea Garden), and were absorbed into Chinese restaurant culture during World War II when Japanese-American businesses were disrupted by internment.

Fortune cookies are genuinely Chinese-American in the sense of belonging to the hybrid cultural tradition of Chinese-American restaurant cuisine. But they are not Chinese in the sense of originating in or having historical roots in China. Mainland Chinese people do not recognize them as part of their culinary heritage, and Chinese visitors to the United States typically encounter them as a novelty.

The Japanese origin is documented. The wartime mechanism of cultural transfer is documented. The claim as commonly stated — that fortune cookies are a Chinese tradition or a Chinese invention — is wrong.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Before moving to the broader cultural dynamics, it is worth addressing a practical implication of the origin question: the fortune cookie industry today. The United States consumes approximately three billion fortune cookies annually, produced primarily by a small number of large manufacturers. Wonton Food Inc. in Queens, New York is the largest manufacturer, producing approximately four million cookies per day. The industry is overwhelmingly Chinese-American at the level of ownership and operation, which reinforces the association of fortune cookies with Chinese-American business culture even as their ultimate origin is Japanese. The slip-printing and mass distribution of fortune messages has itself become an industry: companies like Wonton Food manage enormous databases of fortune texts and periodically retire old fortunes and introduce new ones. The message inside a fortune cookie is a product of copywriting and inventory management, not tradition. This layer of industrial reality sits behind the romantic image of a handmade confection containing a personal prediction, and it is worth noting as a reminder that the consumer experience of cultural tradition is often substantially mediated by commercial production.

The fortune cookie's history is a remarkable case study in how cultural associations are formed and how historical events shape them in ways that become invisible over time.

The internment of Japanese-Americans during World War II was one of the more significant civil liberties violations in 20th-century American history. More than 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry were removed from their homes and businesses and confined in camps, without trial and without evidence of disloyalty, on the basis of ethnicity alone. The Supreme Court upheld the policy in *Korematsu v. United States* (1944), a decision not formally repudiated by the Court until 2018. The economic consequences for Japanese-Americans were devastating. Many lost businesses they had spent decades building.

The fortune cookie story illustrates one small, strange way in which this displacement altered American culture: an item that would likely now be recognized as Japanese-American became Chinese-American because the historical actors who might have maintained Japanese cultural attribution were removed from the picture. This is not the most important consequence of internment by any measure. But it is a concrete example of how historical injustice shapes cultural inheritance in ways that persist long after the injustice itself is no longer visible.

Lee's *The Fortune Cookie Chronicles* uses the fortune cookie as a lens through which to examine the larger history of Chinese food in America — its origins, its adaptations, its relationship to Chinese immigration and labor history, and its role in American popular culture. The book is a model of how food history can illuminate social history, and its treatment of the fortune cookie origin debate is thorough and balanced.

The story also raises questions about cultural ownership and attribution. If a food item has been adopted by a community and made genuinely its own — if it has been integrated into the culture's practices, aesthetics, and identity — at what point does origin cease to be the primary determinant of cultural belonging? Chinese-American restaurant culture has made fortune cookies its own in a way that is real and substantive. The historical record matters, but the living culture has its own legitimacy.

Fortune cookies are also genuinely interesting as a historical artifact of the Japanese craft confectionery tradition. The tsujiura senbei tradition at Fushimi Inari and other shrines predates the American fortune cookie by centuries. Visitors to the Fushimi Inari shrine complex can still find bakers making fortune-bearing crackers by hand on iron griddles — a direct connection to the tradition that produced the American fortune cookie. The two items look and taste quite different, but the structural idea — a baked good enclosing a written fortune — is the same, and the lineage is traceable.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The fortune cookie origin question presents several distinct fact-checking challenges. The claim is not usually presented as an explicit assertion to be tested — no one publishes a news article claiming fortune cookies are Chinese — but as a background assumption that underlies countless cultural references, marketing decisions, and casual statements. Correcting a background assumption requires different strategies than correcting an explicit claim.

Fact-checkers who have addressed the fortune cookie origin typically frame it as a "surprising truth" piece rather than a debunking of a viral claim. The story has a built-in hook: the food most associated with Chinese restaurants in America is actually Japanese in origin. This hook makes the story worth telling in a popular format.

The historical evidence is relatively strong for food history. The tsujiura senbei tradition is documented in Japanese historical sources and in surviving practices at Kyoto shrines. The Hagiwara connection is documented in San Francisco Tea Garden records and local historical sources. Jennifer 8. Lee's investigative journalism brought substantial additional documentation and conducted interviews with surviving witnesses and family members. The David Jung alternative claim exists but is not well-documented.

The wartime transfer is the most important and least-discussed element. Explaining the internment connection transforms the story from a simple factual correction (it's Japanese, not Chinese) into a historical narrative with moral weight. This makes it more compelling and more memorable — people are more likely to retain and transmit a story than a correction.

One risk in fact-checking this topic is oversimplification. The statement "fortune cookies are Japanese, not Chinese" is more accurate than the common belief but still incomplete. A full account acknowledges the Japanese origin, the Chinese-American adoption, the specific historical circumstances of that adoption, and the genuine sense in which fortune cookies now belong to Chinese-American culture. Fact-checkers who reduce this to a simple national attribution swap ("actually they're Japanese") do not fully serve their audience.

The broader lesson is that cultural attributions of food are often more complicated than they appear — the result of immigration, adaptation, displacement, and commercial development rather than straightforward cultural transmission. Fortune cookies are an unusually well-documented example of a general phenomenon: the food we associate with a culture is often the product of a more tangled history than the association suggests.

Food historians have documented many such cases. The "Irish" potato arrived in Europe from South America. "Belgian" chocolate developed its distinctive character through industrial techniques refined in the 19th and 20th centuries from cacao that was grown by enslaved and colonized workers in West Africa and Central America. "American" barbecue draws on traditions brought by enslaved Africans who synthesized Indigenous American and West African cooking techniques. These tangled histories do not diminish the cultures that have made the foods their own; they enrich the story of how food travels, adapts, and accumulates meaning. The fortune cookie is one of the more precisely documented examples of this general pattern, and the Japanese-American internment connection gives it a moral weight that transforms it from a culinary curiosity into a minor window onto American history.

For anyone who wants to see the original, the tsujiura senbei tradition is alive and accessible. Visitors to the Fushimi Inari Taisha shrine in Kyoto can still find vendors making fortune crackers over charcoal griddles by hand — the same tradition, centuries old, that produced the prototype of the American fortune cookie. The two items have diverged considerably in flavor, texture, and visual form. But the idea — a baked good that delivers a written fortune — connects them across an ocean, a century, and the most significant forced displacement in American domestic history.
