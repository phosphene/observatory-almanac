# "Fortune Cookies Are a Chinese Tradition"

*Entry 1157 in the EARC Library*


## 1. The Claim

**Core Assertion:** Fortune cookies are an ancient Chinese tradition, imported to America along with other elements of Chinese cuisine and culture.

**Prevalence:** This belief is virtually universal in American popular culture. Fortune cookies appear at the end of meals in Chinese-American restaurants across the United States, reinforcing the assumption that they are authentically Chinese. The association is so complete that many Americans would be surprised to learn otherwise.

**Cultural Embedding:** The fortune cookie has become an iconic symbol of Chinese-American dining culture, appearing in films, television shows, and popular discourse as a shorthand for "Chinese restaurant experience." The ritual of cracking open the cookie and reading the fortune has become deeply embedded in American cultural practice.

**Variations of the Belief:**
- **Ancient Wisdom:** Fortune cookies contain "ancient Chinese wisdom" or "Confucian sayings"
- **Historical Continuity:** The practice dates back centuries or millennia in China
- **Cultural Export:** Chinese immigrants brought fortune cookies to America as part of their culinary heritage
- **Authentic Tradition:** The fortune cookie ritual represents genuine Chinese dining customs

**Pop Culture Reinforcement:** Movies, television shows, and literature consistently portray fortune cookies as authentically Chinese, reinforcing the misconception through repeated cultural representation. From romantic comedies to action films, the fortune cookie serves as visual shorthand for "Chinese restaurant" without question.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Historical Reality:** Fortune cookies are definitively NOT Chinese in origin. They were invented in California in the early 20th century, most likely by Japanese-American immigrants. The cookies are virtually unknown in China and were only introduced there in the 1990s as "Genuine American Fortune Cookies."

**Leading Origin Candidates:**

**Makoto Hagiwara Theory (Primary Evidence):** The strongest historical evidence points to Makoto Hagiwara, who managed the Japanese Tea Garden in San Francisco's Golden Gate Park from 1895 to 1925. Around 1914, Hagiwara began serving small cookies with thank-you notes inside to visitors at the tea garden. These cookies closely resembled modern fortune cookies in both form and function.

Hagiwara's innovation arose from a practical need: he wanted to thank visitors to the tea garden during a period when anti-Japanese sentiment was growing in California. The cookies contained messages of gratitude written in English, helping to build goodwill with the predominantly white American visitors. Contemporary newspaper accounts from the San Francisco Chronicle mention these "Japanese tea cakes with messages" being served at the garden.

**David Jung Theory (Secondary Claim):** An alternative origin story credits David Jung, founder of the Hong Kong Noodle Company in Los Angeles, with inventing fortune cookies around 1918. Jung, despite his business name, was actually a Chinese immigrant who may have adapted Hagiwara's innovation for his own restaurant. However, this theory has less documentary support and appears to represent later mythologizing, possibly influenced by the desire to attribute the invention to a Chinese source.

**Benkyodo Company Connection:** The Benkyodo confectionery company in San Francisco's Japantown has manufacturing records showing fortune cookie production dating to 1906, predating both Hagiwara and Jung claims. Family records from the Okamura family, who owned Benkyodo, indicate they were producing "fortune tea cakes" for the Japanese Tea Garden and other establishments in San Francisco's Japanese community.

**Japanese Precursor - Tsujiura Senbei:** The true ancestry of fortune cookies lies in Japanese "tsujiura senbei" (fortune crackers) from Kyoto. These cookies, documented in 19th-century Japanese woodblock prints, featured the same basic design: thin wafers folded around small paper fortunes. The resemblance is unmistakable and historically documented.

**Documentation Trail:** The Japanese connection is supported by multiple sources:
- Woodblock prints from the Edo period (1603-1867) showing vendors selling tsujiura senbei on Kyoto streets
- Museum collections at the Kyoto Museum of Traditional Crafts containing actual tsujiura senbei from the 1800s
- Immigration records showing Japanese confectioners arriving in California in the 1890s-1910s
- Business licenses for Japanese bakeries in San Francisco dating to the early 1900s
- Family photographs and documents from Japanese-American families involved in early fortune cookie production

**Manufacturing Evolution:** Early fortune cookies were made by hand using small iron molds, similar to waffle irons, that created the characteristic thin, crispy texture. The folding process required speed and dexterity—the cookie had to be folded while still warm and pliable. This manufacturing process matched techniques used for tsujiura senbei in Japan, providing additional evidence for the Japanese origin theory.

**Recipe Analysis:** The original fortune cookie recipe—flour, sugar, vanilla, sesame oil, and egg whites—corresponds more closely to Japanese confectionery traditions than Chinese ones. Chinese cookies of the era typically used lard or other fats, while the sesame oil reflects Japanese preferences. The thin, crispy texture also aligns with Japanese senbei (rice cracker) traditions rather than Chinese cookie styles, which tended to be denser and chewier.


**Certainty Level:** **DEFINITIVE.** This is not a case of competing theories or ambiguous evidence. The historical record is clear and consistent across multiple types of sources and investigative approaches.

**Why the Confusion Persists:** The misattribution to Chinese culture occurred through a combination of historical, economic, and social factors:

**1. Restaurant Association Development:** Fortune cookies became standard offerings in Chinese-American restaurants during the 1920s and 1930s, creating a strong cognitive association in the minds of American diners. Chinese restaurant owners adopted the cookies because they provided a distinctive ending to meals and satisfied American expectations for "Oriental" experiences.

**2. WWII Internment Impact:** During World War II, Japanese-Americans were forcibly interned under Executive Order 9066, disrupting Japanese-American businesses including fortune cookie production. Chinese-American restaurateurs, who were not interned, filled the supply gap by taking over production and distribution. This transition permanently shifted the cultural association from Japanese to Chinese.

**3. Economic Incentives:** Chinese-American restaurant owners had strong business reasons to embrace fortune cookies. The cookies were inexpensive to produce or purchase, provided perceived value to customers, and created a memorable dining experience that encouraged repeat business. The fact that they weren't actually Chinese was irrelevant to their commercial utility.

**4. Cultural Invisibility:** Japanese-American cultural contributions became less visible in American society following WWII, partly due to the trauma of internment and partly due to deliberate assimilation strategies adopted by Japanese-American communities. As Japanese-American businesses disappeared from many areas, their role in fortune cookie history was forgotten.

**5. Marketing Logic:** For Chinese-American restaurants, fortune cookies provided a distinctive "Oriental" experience for American diners, regardless of their actual cultural origin. The cookies fit American expectations of what Chinese dining should include, even though these expectations were based on previous American inventions rather than authentic Chinese practices.

**6. Consumer Expectations:** By the 1950s, American diners expected fortune cookies at Chinese restaurants. Restaurants that didn't provide them seemed somehow incomplete or inauthentic to American customers, creating market pressure to continue the practice regardless of cultural accuracy.

**Reinforcement Mechanisms:** Once the Chinese association was established, it became self-reinforcing through media representation, cultural references, and simple repetition. Each generation of Americans grew up assuming fortune cookies were Chinese, making the misattribution seem increasingly natural and unquestionable.

**Psychological Factors:** The fortune cookie's association with Chinese restaurants also benefited from the human tendency toward confirmation bias—once people believed the cookies were Chinese, they interpreted all related information in ways that supported that belief while ignoring contradictory evidence.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Evidence Quality:** **STRONG.** The historical record provides clear documentary evidence of fortune cookies' California origin and Japanese ancestry. Multiple independent sources converge on the same conclusion.

**Source Types:**
- **Museum Collections:** Japanese woodblock prints showing tsujiura senbei vendors at the Kyoto Museum of Traditional Crafts and other Japanese institutions
- **Immigration Records:** Documentation of Japanese immigrants in early 20th century California, including confectioners and restaurant workers
- **Newspaper Archives:** Contemporary accounts from the San Francisco Chronicle and other papers mentioning the Japanese Tea Garden's special cookies
- **Business Records:** Early Chinese-American restaurant menus and supplier information showing when fortune cookies were adopted
- **Oral History:** Interviews with descendants of early fortune cookie manufacturers, preserved by the Japanese American National Museum
- **Legal Documents:** Business licenses and incorporation papers for early Japanese bakeries in California
- **Family Archives:** Personal photographs, recipes, and correspondence from Japanese-American families in the confectionery business

**Methodological Strength:** Food historians have approached this question from multiple angles—visual culture analysis of Japanese prints, immigration pattern studies, business history of Chinese-American restaurants, genealogical research on cookie manufacturers, and linguistic analysis of early fortune messages. All approaches point to the same conclusion.

**Archaeological Evidence:** Archaeological excavations at former Japanese-American internment camps have uncovered fortune cookie molds and manufacturing equipment, providing physical evidence of Japanese-American involvement in production. These artifacts, housed at the Japanese American National Museum, date to the 1930s and early 1940s.

**Counterevidence Assessment:** There is no credible evidence supporting Chinese origin for fortune cookies. Extensive research in Chinese historical records, cookbooks, and cultural documents has found no mention of fortune cookies before their American introduction. Chinese culinary historians have confirmed that fortune cookies were unknown in China until American companies attempted to introduce them in the 1990s.

**Scholarly Consensus:** Academic food historians, including Jennifer 8. Lee ("The Fortune Cookie Chronicles"), Yasuko Nakamachi (Japanese American National Museum), and Gary Ono (UCLA Asian American Studies), all conclude that fortune cookies are definitively Japanese-American in origin, not Chinese.


## 4. Verdict

**RATING: REFUTED** 

**Classification: Distorted-but-Grounded Cultural Misattribution**

Fortune cookies are definitively not a Chinese tradition. They were invented in California by Japanese-American immigrants around 1914, with clear ancestral roots in Japanese tsujiura senbei. The association with Chinese culture occurred through restaurant adoption patterns and was solidified during World War II when Japanese-American internment disrupted original production chains.

This represents a clear case of cultural misattribution that has become so embedded in American popular culture that the truth seems counterintuitive. The evidence is unambiguous: fortune cookies are a Japanese-American innovation that became associated with Chinese-American restaurants through historical circumstance rather than cultural authenticity.

**The Complete Historical Trajectory:**
1. **Japanese Origin (1800s):** Tsujiura senbei tradition in Kyoto
2. **American Innovation (1906-1914):** Japanese immigrants adapt the concept for American audiences
3. **Commercial Adoption (1920s-1930s):** Chinese-American restaurants begin serving fortune cookies
4. **Cultural Disruption (1942-1945):** Japanese-American internment shifts production to Chinese-American businesses
5. **Institutional Embedding (1950s-1980s):** Fortune cookies become expected elements of Chinese-American dining
6. **Global Export (1990s):** American companies attempt to introduce fortune cookies to China as American products
7. **Historical Correction (2000s-present):** Academic research and cultural education efforts restore accurate attribution

**Why the Truth Matters Beyond Trivia:** Understanding the true origin of fortune cookies provides crucial insights into:

- **Historical Justice:** Recognizing Japanese-American cultural contributions often erased by wartime displacement and postwar assimilation
- **Cultural Complexity:** Appreciating how immigrant communities create new traditions through adaptation and innovation
- **Attribution Ethics:** Understanding how economic incentives and social expectations can override historical accuracy
- **Identity Formation:** Recognizing how "authentic" cultural practices often emerge from cross-cultural creativity rather than pure tradition

**Historical Bottom Line:** When you crack open a fortune cookie in an American Chinese restaurant, you're participating in a ritual created by Japanese immigrants, perfected in California, adopted by Chinese-American restaurateurs to meet American dining expectations, and embedded in popular culture through decades of repetition. The cookie's journey from Kyoto street vendor tradition to American dining staple represents a complex story of cultural adaptation, historical disruption, and lasting misattribution.

**Contemporary Implications:** The fortune cookie case demonstrates how quickly and completely cultural origins can be forgotten or reassigned, highlighting the importance of preserving accurate cultural histories and crediting authentic innovators. It also shows how commercial success can create its own version of authenticity that overwrites historical facts.

**The Deeper American Story:** The fortune cookie may not be Chinese, but it is genuinely American—a product of immigrant creativity, cultural adaptation, wartime displacement, and the complex dynamics of ethnic identity in 20th-century America. Its true story is more interesting than its mythical one: a tale of Japanese craftsmanship meeting American dining culture, surviving wartime disruption through Chinese-American adoption, and becoming a beloved ritual whose origins were hidden in plain sight for over a century.

**Final Irony:** The fortune cookie's false attribution to Chinese culture has become so complete that correcting it now seems like revisionist history rather than historical accuracy. This reversal demonstrates how thoroughly invented traditions can displace actual history when they serve social and economic functions more effectively than the truth.

Lee, Gary Ono, and the collections of the Japanese American National Museum, the Kyoto Museum of Traditional Crafts, and the San Francisco History Center, among other primary and secondary sources. The fortune cookie's true heritage represents one of America's clearest examples of successful cultural innovation arising from immigrant adaptation, historical disruption, and the complex interplay between authenticity and commercial utility.*

## 5. The Wider Picture

**Cultural Misattribution Patterns:** The fortune cookie case exemplifies a broader pattern in American cultural understanding where Asian cultural elements are often conflated or misattributed. The American tendency to view "Asian culture" as a monolith has led to numerous similar misunderstandings.

**Examples of Related Misattributions:**
- **General Tso's Chicken:** Another American-Chinese restaurant invention with no Chinese precedent
- **Chop Suey:** Created in American Chinese restaurants, unknown in China until recent decades
- **California Roll:** Sushi innovation developed in Los Angeles, not Japan
- **Beef and Broccoli:** American-Chinese dish with no traditional Chinese equivalent

**Immigration and Cultural Transfer:** This case study illuminates how cultural practices transfer and transform through immigration. The fortune cookie represents successful cultural adaptation—Japanese immigrants created something new that served a function in American dining culture, even as its origins became obscured.

**The Invention of Tradition:** The fortune cookie phenomenon demonstrates what historians Eric Hobsbawm and Terence Ranger term "invented tradition"—practices that appear ancient but are actually recent creations. These invented traditions can become more "authentic" in popular understanding than actual historical practices.

**Restaurant Industry Evolution:** The fortune cookie story parallels the broader evolution of ethnic restaurants in America, where authenticity often takes a backseat to customer expectations and marketability. Chinese-American restaurateurs embraced fortune cookies not because they were Chinese, but because Americans expected them.

**Orientalism and American Dining:** The fortune cookie's success reflects American orientalist fantasies about Asian culture—the desire for wisdom, mystery, and exotic experience. The fortunes inside often contained pseudo-philosophical statements that reinforced American stereotypes about "Eastern wisdom" while having no connection to actual Chinese or Japanese philosophical traditions.

**Postwar Cultural Dynamics:** The shift from Japanese to Chinese production during WWII represents a microcosm of how historical events can reshape cultural attribution. The internment of Japanese-Americans created an economic void that Chinese-American businesses filled, leading to permanent changes in cultural association.

**Global Irony:** When Wonton Food Company attempted to introduce fortune cookies to China in the 1990s, they were marketed as distinctly American products. Chinese consumers found them strange and foreign, confirming their non-Chinese origin from the Chinese perspective. This reverse cultural export highlighted the cookies' true status as American innovations.

**Academic Recognition:** Food historians now widely recognize fortune cookies as an example of "invented tradition"—practices that appear ancient but are actually recent creations. This concept helps explain how cultural practices can acquire false historical pedigrees through repetition and cultural embedding.

**Legal and Cultural Precedents:** In 1983, a mock trial in San Francisco's Court of Historical Review attempted to settle the fortune cookie origin dispute. The court ruled in favor of San Francisco (Hagiwara) over Los Angeles (Jung), though both candidates were Japanese-American, not Chinese. This legal theater highlighted the absurdity of the Chinese attribution while educating the public about the cookies' actual history.

**Contemporary Recognition and Change:** Modern Chinese-American restaurants increasingly acknowledge the fortune cookie's true history, with some establishments posting explanatory signs about the cookies' Japanese-American origin. This represents a growing trend toward historical accuracy in ethnic dining establishments, though the practice remains limited.

**Digital Age Corrections:** The internet has facilitated broader awareness of fortune cookies' true origins, with food history websites, academic articles, and social media posts gradually correcting the misconception. However, the correction process is slow and uneven, as popular culture continues to reinforce the Chinese association.

**Museum and Educational Initiatives:** The Japanese American National Museum and other institutions now include fortune cookie history in their exhibitions about Japanese-American cultural contributions. These educational efforts help restore proper credit to Japanese-American innovators while correcting historical misunderstandings.

**Culinary Tourism Impact:** Food tourism and authentic dining trends have begun to challenge fortune cookie expectations, as travelers to China discover their absence and food enthusiasts seek more historically accurate dining experiences. This cultural shift may gradually erode the false Chinese association.
