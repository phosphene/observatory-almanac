# "Fortune Cookies Are a Chinese Tradition"


## 1. The Claim

Fortune cookies are universally associated with Chinese restaurants in the United States, leading to the widespread belief that they represent an authentic Chinese culinary tradition. Diners at Chinese-American restaurants receive fortune cookies with their meals as a standard conclusion to the dining experience, reinforcing the assumption that this practice reflects traditional Chinese hospitality customs. The cookies themselves—crisp, folded wafers containing printed fortunes or wise sayings—are often viewed as examples of ancient Chinese wisdom traditions translated into American restaurant culture.

This association is so strong that fortune cookies have become symbolic of Chinese-American cuisine itself, featured prominently in popular culture depictions of Chinese restaurants and frequently referenced in discussions of Chinese food traditions. Many Americans consider fortune cookies as authentically Chinese as chopsticks, tea ceremonies, or lunar New Year celebrations.

The claim extends beyond simple culinary tradition to broader cultural authenticity: fortune cookies are often presented as examples of how Chinese immigrants preserved their cultural heritage through food practices, adapting traditional elements to American contexts while maintaining essential cultural meaning. In this narrative, fortune cookies represent successful cultural transmission and adaptation rather than invention or appropriation.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim that fortune cookies are Chinese is inaccurate based on extensive historical research:

**Japanese Origins**: Fortune cookies derive from Japanese tsujiura senbei (fortune crackers), which have been sold at temples and shrines in Japan since at least the 19th century. Yasuko Nakamachi's 2008 research at Kanagawa University traced the cookies to Japanese temple traditions in Kyoto, where similar folded crackers containing fortunes were part of religious practices. The Japanese version used darker dough and contained more savory flavoring compared to the sweeter American adaptation.

**Japanese-American Innovation**: The fortune cookie as known in American restaurants was developed by Japanese immigrant bakers in California in the early 20th century. Key figures include Makoto Hagiwara, who served them at the Japanese Tea Garden in San Francisco's Golden Gate Park around 1914, and Suyeichi Okamura, whose Benkyodo bakery in San Francisco's Japantown produced fortune cookies for local restaurants. These early versions maintained closer ties to Japanese tsujiura senbei traditions while adapting ingredients and production methods for American palates.

**World War II Transfer**: The critical transition occurred during World War II Japanese internment (1942-1945). Executive Order 9066 forced Japanese-American families into internment camps, closing Japanese-owned businesses including bakeries that had been producing fortune cookies. Chinese-American restaurant owners, who were not subject to internment, adopted the practice of serving fortune cookies, filling the market gap left by closed Japanese businesses.

**Post-War Establishment**: By the 1950s and 1960s, fortune cookies had become firmly established as a Chinese restaurant tradition in American diners' minds. The Japanese origins were largely forgotten or never widely known, while the practice became thoroughly integrated into Chinese-American restaurant culture. Chinese immigrants arriving after 1965 encountered fortune cookies as an established expectation of American diners rather than as part of their own cultural tradition.

**Contemporary China**: Fortune cookies remain virtually unknown in mainland China as a traditional practice. When they appear in modern China, they are marketed as "American" novelties or Western imports. Chinese visitors to American Chinese restaurants often express surprise at fortune cookies, confirming their absence from traditional Chinese dining practices.


Historical research and contemporary cultural evidence strongly support the Japanese-American origins of fortune cookies:

**Academic Consensus**: Food historians, including Jennifer 8. Lee's research documented in "The Fortune Cookie Chronicles" (2008) and Yasuko Nakamachi's academic work, consistently identify Japanese origins for American fortune cookies. This research includes interviews with surviving family members of early Japanese-American bakers and examination of historical business records from San Francisco's Japantown.

**Archaeological Evidence**: Physical evidence includes early fortune cookie molds and production equipment found in former Japanese-American bakeries, photographs of Japanese Tea Garden fortune cookie service from the 1910s and 1920s, and documentation of Japanese temple fortune traditions that clearly predate American Chinese restaurant adoption.

**Contemporary Japanese Recognition**: Japanese cultural institutions and food historians recognize American fortune cookies as descended from Japanese traditions, while acknowledging the American adaptations and development. This recognition includes museum exhibits and cultural education materials that trace the connection.

**Chinese Cultural Absence**: Systematic examination of traditional Chinese dining customs, historical Chinese cookbooks, and contemporary Chinese restaurant practices in China shows no evidence of fortune cookie traditions. Chinese culinary historians and cultural experts consistently identify fortune cookies as foreign to traditional Chinese food culture.

**Internment Documentation**: Historical records of Japanese-American internment include documentation of closed businesses, including bakeries that had served fortune cookies. Post-war business records show Chinese-American restaurants adopting practices previously associated with Japanese establishments.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The misattribution of fortune cookies reflects several systematic patterns in how cultural origins are assigned and remembered:

**Visible Association Over Historical Documentation**: The strong contemporary association between fortune cookies and Chinese restaurants overwhelms historical evidence about actual origins. Most diners encounter fortune cookies exclusively in Chinese restaurant contexts, making the Chinese connection seem self-evidently authentic. This visible association pattern explains many cultural misattributions where current context obscures historical development.

**Immigration Narrative Overlay**: The fortune cookie misattribution fits appealing narratives about immigrant cultural preservation and adaptation. Americans want to believe that ethnic restaurants represent authentic cultural transmission, making the "Chinese tradition" explanation more emotionally satisfying than the more complex story of inter-ethnic cultural transfer during wartime displacement.

**Erasure Through Invisibility**: Japanese-American experiences, including internment, receive less cultural visibility than Chinese-American immigration narratives. The World War II context that explains fortune cookie transfer is uncomfortable historical territory that many prefer to minimize or ignore. This erasure makes alternative origin stories more palatable and believable.

**Cultural Homogenization**: Popular American culture tends to homogenize Asian cultures, making internal distinctions between Chinese, Japanese, Korean, and other traditions less visible. This homogenization makes it easier to assign fortune cookies to "Asian tradition" generally and "Chinese tradition" specifically without careful attention to actual cultural boundaries.

**Commercial Reinforcement**: Chinese restaurant owners had obvious commercial incentives to embrace fortune cookies once they became customer expectations, regardless of cultural authenticity. Explaining the Japanese origins would serve no business purpose and might confuse diners who expected "Chinese" traditions at Chinese restaurants. Market pressures favor maintaining cultural misattributions when they serve commercial interests.


## 4. Verdict

**REPLICATE**: The fortune cookie origin story represents a clear case of cultural misattribution with well-documented historical evidence that can be straightforwardly presented to correct widespread misconception.

**Key Elements for Replication:**

1. **Historical Documentation**: Present the clear timeline from Japanese tsujiura senbei through Japanese-American bakery development to World War II transfer to Chinese restaurants. Include specific dates, locations, and key figures with photographic evidence where available.

2. **Internment Context**: Explain how Executive Order 9066 and Japanese-American internment created the conditions for cultural practice transfer, emphasizing this as historical fact rather than abstract cultural evolution.

3. **Contemporary Evidence**: Document how fortune cookies are viewed in modern China (as American imports) and Japan (as descended from traditional practices but Americanized), providing contemporary cultural perspectives that support the historical evidence.

4. **Broader Pattern Recognition**: Place fortune cookie misattribution within the broader context of ethnic food myths, helping readers develop better frameworks for evaluating cultural authenticity claims.

5. **Cultural Sensitivity**: Address the misattribution respectfully, acknowledging both Japanese-American contributions and Chinese-American adaptation while avoiding blame or cultural criticism.

The fortune cookie story is unusually well-documented for food history, with clear evidence, identifiable historical actors, and specific historical events that explain the cultural transfer. Unlike many food origin stories that involve gradual evolution or disputed claims, the fortune cookie trajectory can be traced through specific businesses, individuals, and historical moments.

This case study provides an excellent example for developing better cultural literacy about ethnic food traditions, immigration history, and the difference between contemporary cultural associations and historical development. The story also offers opportunities to discuss Japanese-American experiences, World War II internment impacts, and patterns of cultural adaptation and misattribution without requiring complex interpretive frameworks.

The replication should emphasize that cultural practices can move between communities through historical circumstances rather than just gradual evolution, and that understanding these movements requires attention to specific historical events rather than assumptions based on contemporary associations. Fortune cookies represent successful cultural adaptation by Chinese-American restaurateurs rather than preservation of Chinese tradition—a distinction that honors the actual cultural creativity involved rather than perpetuating inaccurate origin stories.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Fortune cookie misattribution exemplifies broader patterns of cultural misassignment that affect how Americans understand ethnic food traditions and immigration history:

**Parallel Misattributions**: Similar misattributions include chicken tikka masala (British-Indian creation, not traditional Indian), General Tso's chicken (American-Chinese invention, unknown in China), and Caesar salad (Mexican creation, not Italian). These examples share the pattern of contemporary association overwhelming historical accuracy.

**Model Minority Dynamics**: The fortune cookie story intersects with "model minority" narratives that obscure Asian-American diversity and internal differences. Treating all Asian immigrants as culturally equivalent makes it easier to misattribute practices between different ethnic communities while maintaining stereotypical expectations about "Asian" traditions.

**Internment Memory**: Fortune cookie history provides a concrete example of how Japanese-American internment affected cultural transmission and business ownership. The transfer of fortune cookie production represents broader patterns of economic displacement and cultural erasure that affected Japanese-American communities during and after World War II.

**Authenticity Economics**: The commercial success of "authentic" ethnic cuisine creates incentives for restaurants to maintain cultural misattributions when they serve customer expectations. Explaining that fortune cookies aren't traditionally Chinese would serve no commercial purpose and might disappoint diners seeking "authentic" experiences.

**Cultural Documentation Gaps**: The fortune cookie story highlights how cultural practices can be misattributed when historical documentation is incomplete or inaccessible. Japanese-American community records were disrupted by internment, making it easier for alternative origin stories to develop without systematic contradiction.

**Food as Cultural Identity**: Fortune cookies demonstrate how food practices become markers of cultural identity regardless of historical accuracy. Once established, these associations resist correction because they serve contemporary identity functions rather than historical accuracy goals.

**Immigration Wave Effects**: Different waves of Chinese immigration encountered fortune cookies at different stages of their American establishment. Early Chinese immigrants (pre-1920s) would have been contemporary with Japanese fortune cookie development, while post-1965 immigrants encountered fortune cookies as established Chinese restaurant expectations, leading to different understandings of the practice's origins.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not systematically addressed the cultural origins of fortune cookies, despite this representing a classic case of widely held cultural misconception with clear historical evidence available for verification. The absence reflects the tendency for food history claims to receive less attention than political or celebrity-focused misinformation, even when food origin stories have significant implications for understanding cultural identity, immigration history, and patterns of cultural attribution.

This gap is particularly notable because fortune cookie origins involve well-documented historical events—Japanese immigration to California, World War II internment, and the transfer of business practices between Asian-American communities—that provide clear evidence for tracing the practice's actual development.
