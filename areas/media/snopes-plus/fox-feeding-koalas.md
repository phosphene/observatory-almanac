## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In January 2020, as the Australian bushfire crisis dominated global news coverage, a 30-second video circulated on social media platforms including Facebook, YouTube, and Twitter purporting to show a mother fox nursing koala joeys that had been orphaned by the catastrophic wildfires then raging across southeastern Australia. The framing was emotionally resonant: a wild fox, normally a predator of small marsupials, was supposedly crossing species lines to nurture fire victims in an act of interspecies maternal compassion.

Snopes investigator Dan MacGuill (2020) assigned this claim the rating **Miscaptioned**. The video does not show koala joeys under any circumstances. It shows a red fox (*Vulpes vulpes*) nursing her own cubs. The footage bears no connection whatsoever to Australia or its bushfire crisis: it was recorded on April 1, 2009, by Québécois nature photographer Luc Durocher at Parc Jean-Drapeau on Notre Dame Island in Montréal, Canada. The video was first posted to Facebook on December 26, 2014, and recirculated periodically over subsequent years under a rotating cast of false captions—in 2016 it was claimed to show a fox suckling bear cubs; in 2018, fox and monkey joeys. The koala version is only the most recent iteration in a pattern of repeated miscaptioning.

This entry examines the identification markers that distinguish fox cubs from koala joeys in the footage, the psychology of emotionally resonant miscaptioning during humanitarian crises, the history of recaptioning cycles in social media misinformation, and what the video's repeated reinvention reveals about the structural conditions that allow the same piece of footage to generate viral engagement under entirely different false premises across multiple years.

## §2 The Footage, Its Origins, and the Anatomy of the Misidentification

The footage under examination is an approximately 30-second clip showing an adult red fox lying on the ground while multiple smaller animals nurse from her. The animals nursing are clearly fox cubs, identifiable by multiple features observable in the video itself: the reddish-orange fur coloration consistent with young vulpines, the pointed facial structures visible in portions of the video showing the animals at angles sufficient for profile identification, the proportionality of the cubs' bodies relative to the mother, and the posture and movement style consistent with young canids rather than marsupials.

Koala joeys (*Phascolarctos cinereus*) at a developmental stage where nursing would be observable externally (i.e., out of the pouch and beginning to feed) are morphologically quite distinct. They have rounded faces with large eyes, a pug-nosed snout, and a body shape characteristic of marsupials—stocky, with rounded ears extending above the head and clinging posture typical of animals adapted to holding onto eucalyptus branches and their mothers' backs. The animals in the video share none of these features.

The original video was identified by MacGuill (2020) through source tracing to Luc Durocher's Facebook page, where it was first posted on December 26, 2014. Durocher confirmed to Snopes via email that he filmed the sequence at precisely 7:02 a.m. on April 1, 2009, at Parc Jean-Drapeau, a municipal park on Notre Dame Island in the city of Montréal, Quebec, Canada. The park, situated on an island in the St. Lawrence River, is well-known for its urban wildlife populations and is a documented habitat for red foxes.

Durocher is a prolific nature photographer who has documented fox families at Parc Jean-Drapeau extensively, posting multiple videos to YouTube and photographs to Flickr and PBase showing the same vixen and her cubs from multiple angles at different times. This additional footage makes it unambiguous—the animals are fox cubs in various stages of development, completely typical of the species.

When Snopes contacted Durocher on January 27, 2020, he reposted his decade-old original video to Facebook, providing yet another timestamped verification point. The news site India Today initially published an article with the headline "Mama Fox Feeds Milk to Baby Koalas in Viral Video From Australia. This is Motherhood, Says Twitter" but subsequently deleted the article and published a correction.

The misidentification exploited two conditions simultaneously: the emotional salience of the Australia fires (which saturated attention in January 2020 with genuine footage of burnt koalas and other animals) and the visual difficulty of species identification in amateur wildlife footage where the subjects are small, close to the ground, and partially obscured.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Crisis Conditions as Misinformation Amplifiers and the Recaptioning Cycle

The epistemic novelty in this case resides at the intersection of two phenomena that are individually well-documented but whose combined dynamics are underappreciated in fact-checking literature: **humanitarian crisis emotional salience as a misinformation amplifier**, and **recaptioning cycles as a distinct form of persistent viral content**.

On the first phenomenon: studies of information propagation during disaster events have consistently documented that emotional arousal—particularly the mixture of distress and desire for positive resolution that characterizes humanitarian crisis coverage—dramatically increases sharing propensity regardless of epistemic quality. Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018), writing in *Science* (American Association for the Advancement of Science), documented across a large dataset of Twitter diffusion that false news stories spread faster, farther, broader, and deeper than true ones, and that emotional novelty (including emotional rescue stories) is a primary driver. This general finding applies with amplified force during crisis events, when audiences are specifically primed to seek comfort and when positive counterstories (animal rescued, species helping species) generate psychological relief that motivates sharing before deliberate evaluation.

The koala miscaptioning case represents an example of what might be called **opportunistic recaptioning**: taking an emotionally neutral or mildly positive piece of footage (a fox nursing her cubs) and attaching it to a high-salience humanitarian narrative (Australian fire victims) to generate crisis-amplified virality. The original footage on its own generated modest and entirely legitimate engagement when Durocher posted it. The miscaptioned version, launched during peak global attention to the Australian fires, generated an immediate and massive viral response.

On the second phenomenon: the recaptioning history of this specific video is remarkable and underexplored. MacGuill (2020) documented at least three prior iterations before the Australian fires version: bear cubs (2016), monkeys (2018, multiple accounts), and koalas (2020). This pattern is characteristic of a class of footage that retains perpetual re-viral potential because its core content is genuinely emotionally engaging—the sight of a mammal nursing young is cross-culturally positive—and because it requires no editing beyond caption substitution for each new deployment.

The epistemically significant point is that each successive recaptioning exploits a fresh audience that did not encounter the previous iterations. The four-year gap between the footage's original posting and its first major misuse round (2016 bear cubs), and the further gaps to the 2018 monkey version and 2020 koala version, suggest a deliberate or semi-deliberate pattern of waiting until sufficient audience turnover has occurred to make the recaptioning effective again. This is not unique to this video: Wardle (2017), writing in *First Draft News*, described the phenomenon of "zombie content"—media assets that die down and then resurface under new false framings—as a structurally underappreciated category of persistent misinformation.

The implication for fact-checking methodology is that reverse image and video search should be the default first step for any emotionally resonant animal footage circulating during a crisis, specifically because crisis conditions generate the highest recaptioning incentive. The Snopes investigation correctly performed this sourcing but could have explicitly documented the three prior recaption cycles as evidence of the zombie-content pattern.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

MacGuill's (2020) investigation successfully identified the original footage, confirmed its Montreal origin with the photographer, and traced its December 2014 first posting. Several threads merit additional attention:

**The recaptioning authorship question**: Who originally attached the "Mama Fox Feeds Baby Koalas in Australia" caption to the video is not established. The Snopes investigation identified several Facebook pages and YouTube accounts that spread the miscaptioned version in late January 2020—specifically "Did You Know" on Facebook, "Let's Make it Viral" on YouTube, and Greenpeace Australia's Instagram account through a secondary reshare. However, which of these (or another account) was the initial source of the specific Australian fires framing is not determined.

**The India Today correction chain**: India Today initially published the story and subsequently deleted it, replacing it with a fact-check. The mechanics of how a national news organization published the story without verifying the footage in the first place—given that the footage had been attributed to Durocher, a Canadian photographer, on his public Facebook page for six years—is not analyzed. This represents a gap in understanding of how mainstream media verification failures interact with social media viral cycles.

**The fate of the accounts**: As of MacGuill's reporting (January 27, 2020), the Facebook pages "Did You Know" and "Angell Dear" and the YouTube channel "Let's Make it Viral" still had the miscaptioned versions online; only the specific Twitter accounts mentioned had deleted their posts. Whether those accounts subsequently deleted or corrected the content, and whether any accounts faced platform-level consequences, is not documented.

**Durocher's intellectual property interests**: The photographer spent ten-plus years having his copyrighted footage repeatedly misused under false captions. Whether he sought or received any intellectual property remedy for the systematic misattribution across platforms is unaddressed.

## §5 The 2019–2020 Australian Bushfire Crisis and the Misinformation Ecology It Generated

The Black Summer bushfires of 2019–2020 were a historically anomalous event that reshaped both Australian fire policy and global ecological discourse. Beginning in June 2019 and burning with exceptional intensity through February 2020, the fires burned approximately 24 million hectares (over 59 million acres) across multiple Australian states—an area larger than the United Kingdom—killing an estimated one billion animals according to widely circulated estimates (Dickman, 2020, as reported by multiple science outlets in early 2020, though formal peer-reviewed publication of these figures came later; Wintle et al., 2020, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*, Springer Nature, discussed the ecological scale of the event).

The koala occupied a specific symbolic position within the fire crisis coverage. The species had been listed as vulnerable to extinction in Queensland and New South Wales prior to the fires, and footage of injured and dehydrated koalas being given water and treated by rescuers achieved extraordinary global reach through January 2020. This created a specific audience prepared to receive and share koala-adjacent content, particularly content framed as evidence of animal resilience or cross-species compassion.

The fox-koala miscaptioning was far from the most significant misinformation to emerge from the fires. Verified misinformation researchers documented false claims about arson (substantially amplified relative to actual arson statistics), misattributed photographs from other fires presented as Australian bushfire imagery, and false claims about government response. The fox video was unusual in being affectively positive—a comforting false story rather than a frightening or politically charged one—which may account for its rapid initial correction by India Today and its relatively shorter peak virality cycle compared to more politically charged fire misinformation.

The ecological reality of fox-koala interaction is also salient background here: the red fox (*Vulpes vulpes*) is, in fact, an invasive species in Australia introduced in the 1800s, and is one of the significant predators of koala joeys and other native marsupials (Dickman, 1996, *University of Sydney* report on introduced predators). A fox genuinely nursing koala joeys would therefore be biologically extraordinary, not merely heartwarming—it would require a fox to suppress normal predatory drive toward a species that foxes actively hunt. This biological implausibility was not foregrounded in the initial viral circulation but is part of the background context that attentive fact-checkers should have recognized as a red flag.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: MISCAPTIONED**

The video does not show a fox suckling koala joeys orphaned by Australian bushfires. It shows a red fox nursing her own cubs on Notre Dame Island in Montréal, Canada, filmed by nature photographer Luc Durocher on April 1, 2009, and first publicly posted in December 2014. The video has been recaptioned with false species and location information at least three times since 2016 prior to the Australian fires version. The footage bears no connection to Australia, the 2019–2020 bushfires, or koalas.

**EARC Classification: C (Contextual — crisis-amplified recaptioning)**

The analytical gap is contextual: understanding why this footage recirculates with new false captions every two to three years, and why the Australian fires version achieved its particular virality at scale, requires situating the footage within the emotional ecology of the crisis event and within documented patterns of zombie-content recaptioning in social media misinformation. The core debunking (it's fox cubs, not koalas, filmed in Canada) is straightforward; what requires additional context is the structural dynamic that keeps this footage active as a misinformation vehicle across years.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Identity and motivation of the specific account(s) that originated the Australian fires framing for the video
- Whether the "Did You Know" Facebook page and "Let's Make it Viral" YouTube channel subsequently corrected or removed the content
- Whether Luc Durocher pursued any intellectual property remedies for repeated misuse of his footage
- Full accounting of the recaptioning distribution network across platforms in each of the documented false-caption cycles (2016, 2018, 2020)
