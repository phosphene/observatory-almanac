# "Frankenstein Is the Name of the Monster"


## 1. The Claim

One of the most persistent misconceptions in popular culture holds that "Frankenstein" is the name of the monster in Mary Shelley's 1818 novel. This error appears everywhere from casual conversation to published works, creating a widespread belief that the reanimated creature bears the same name as its creator, Victor Frankenstein.

The claim manifests in various forms: some people believe Frankenstein was always the monster's name, others think Mary Shelley gave the creature this name in the novel, and still others assume that "Frankenstein" has become an acceptable alternative name through common usage. The misconception is so pervasive that many dictionaries now include "Frankenstein" as a noun meaning "a monster" or "something that destroys its creator."


## 2. What's Actually True

While Snopes correctly identifies the factual error, their analysis remains somewhat surface-level in addressing why this particular misconception has achieved such remarkable persistence and cultural acceptance. The assessment treats the naming confusion primarily as a matter of public literacy about classic literature, missing the deeper literary and philosophical implications of the creature's namelessness.

Snopes accurately describes what happens in Shelley's novel but doesn't fully explore the thematic significance of the naming issue. The creature's lack of a name is not an oversight or omission by Shelley—it's a deliberate literary choice that carries significant weight in the novel's exploration of identity, responsibility, and what it means to be human.

Additionally, while Snopes mentions the 1931 film adaptation, they don't adequately trace the evolution of the name confusion through various adaptations and cultural references, nor do they examine how this error might actually illuminate aspects of the original work that casual readings might miss.


The literary evidence supports a reading of the creature's namelessness as thematically central to Shelley's novel. The creature's lack of name reflects its liminal status—neither fully human nor other, neither accepted by its creator nor recognized by society. This nameless state contributes to the creature's tragic isolation and its eventual turn toward violence.

However, the popular conflation of creator and creature, while textually inaccurate, may capture something that Shelley intended readers to recognize: the intimate connection between Victor and his creation. The creature is, in many ways, an extension of Victor's identity—his scientific ambition, his hubris, his capacity for both creation and destruction made manifest.

Literary critics have long noted that Victor and the creature function as doubles or mirror images throughout the novel. They share similar intellectual capacities, both become isolated from society, both are capable of violence, and both are driven by consuming obsessions. The creature explicitly tells Victor, "You are my creator, but I am your master," suggesting a relationship so intertwined that their identities become nearly inseparable.

From this perspective, the popular error of calling the creature "Frankenstein" might actually reflect an intuitive understanding of the novel's deeper themes about the relationship between creator and creation, the responsibility that comes with making life, and the ways in which we are defined by what we bring into the world.


## 3. Why People Believe This

Mary Shelley's "Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus" was published in 1818 when Shelley was just 20 years old (though she began writing it at 18). The novel emerged from the famous ghost story competition at Villa Diodati in 1816, where Shelley, Percy Bysshe Shelley, Lord Byron, and John Polidori challenged each other to write supernatural tales.

The textual evidence regarding the creature's namelessness is unambiguous. Throughout the novel, Victor Frankenstein refers to his creation using various descriptive terms:

- "creature" (used dozens of times)
- "daemon" (reflecting the classical sense of a spirit, not necessarily evil)
- "fiend"
- "wretch"
- "being"
- "monster"
- "my creation"
- "the work of my own hands"

Crucially, the creature itself is acutely aware of its nameless state and expresses anguish about this lack of identity. In one of the novel's most poignant passages, the creature tells Frankenstein: "I am thy creature: I ought to be thy Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom thou drivest from joy for no misdeed." The reference to Adam is significant—Adam was the first named human in Judeo-Christian tradition, and the creature sees itself as deserving of similar recognition and identity.

The creature's eloquence and literary sophistication further emphasize the tragedy of its namelessness. Shelley portrays the creature as highly intelligent and articulate, having learned to read through studying Milton's "Paradise Lost," Plutarch's "Parallel Lives," and Goethe's "The Sorrows of Young Werther." The creature can engage in philosophical discourse about identity, morality, and responsibility, yet it remains nameless and therefore, in a fundamental sense, without social existence.

Shelley's deliberate choice to leave the creature unnamed reflects broader themes in the novel about responsibility, recognition, and what constitutes personhood. Victor's refusal to name his creation parallels his broader abdication of responsibility for it. Naming is an act of recognition and acceptance—parents name children, humans name pets, societies name citizens. Victor's failure to name the creature represents his fundamental failure to acknowledge it as a being deserving of care, recognition, or place in society.


## 4. Verdict

**Enhancement Confirmed**: While the claim is factually incorrect, the widespread conflation of creator and creature illuminates central themes in Shelley's novel about identity, responsibility, and the relationship between maker and made.

The technical facts are clear: in Mary Shelley's 1818 novel, "Frankenstein" refers to Victor Frankenstein, the creator, while the creature remains unnamed throughout the text. Snopes correctly identifies this as a factual error that has become widespread through popular culture and media adaptations.

However, a deeper analysis reveals that this "error" may actually enhance understanding of the novel's central themes rather than simply obscuring them. The popular assumption that the creature should bear Frankenstein's name reflects an intuitive understanding of the intimate connection between creator and creation that Shelley deliberately crafted.

The creature's namelessness is not accidental but thematically significant. It represents Victor's failure to accept responsibility for his creation and the creature's tragic exclusion from human society. When readers assume the creature is named Frankenstein, they make visible the relationship that Victor tries to deny—that the creature is, in a fundamental sense, his offspring and bears his identity.

The enhancement perspective recognizes that cultural "errors" can sometimes illuminate aspects of original works that surface readings might miss. The Frankenstein name confusion forces attention to questions of identity, parenthood, responsibility, and recognition that are central to Shelley's novel.

Rather than simply correcting the misconception, we might use it as a pedagogical tool for exploring why names matter, what it means to create life (artificial or otherwise), and how responsibility extends beyond the moment of creation to ongoing recognition and care.

The widespread acceptance of "Frankenstein" as the monster's name also demonstrates how stories evolve in cultural memory and how popular understanding can diverge from textual accuracy while still capturing essential truths about the work's meaning and significance.

Mary Shelley created a story rich enough to support both the original text and its cultural transformations. The name confusion, rather than diminishing the novel, may actually testify to its psychological insight and enduring relevance. The creature that Victor refuses to name has, through cultural memory, claimed his creator's name—a fitting resolution to one of literature's most profound explorations of what we owe to what we create.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Frankenstein name confusion exists within a broader pattern of how classic literature gets transformed, simplified, and sometimes misremembered in popular culture. Similar examples include the widespread belief that Sherlock Holmes said "Elementary, my dear Watson" (he didn't, at least not in Doyle's original stories), or that Hamlet's famous soliloquy begins "To be or not to be, that is the question" (it's actually "To be, or not to be—that is the question").

These textual "errors" often reveal something about how stories evolve in cultural memory and what elements the public finds most memorable or meaningful. The Frankenstein name confusion is particularly interesting because it has achieved such widespread acceptance that it has, in effect, created a parallel cultural text alongside Shelley's original novel.

**Adaptation History**: The transformation of "Frankenstein" from creator to creature can be traced through various adaptations:

The 1931 Universal Studios film, directed by James Whale and starring Boris Karloff as the creature, established many of the visual and narrative conventions that still define popular understanding of Frankenstein. Karloff's creature, with its flat-top head, bolts in the neck, and lumbering gait, became the definitive image of "Frankenstein's monster" for generations of viewers who never read Shelley's novel.

Subsequent films, television shows, and other adaptations continued to blur the line between creator and creature. Many adaptations title themselves simply "Frankenstein" while focusing primarily on the monster, contributing to the name confusion. The 1994 Kenneth Branagh film "Mary Shelley's Frankenstein" attempted to return to the source material but still couldn't escape the cultural weight of the name association.

**Dictionary Evolution**: The persistence of the name confusion has led to its partial legitimization in reference works. Merriam-Webster, for example, now includes "Frankenstein" as a noun meaning "a monster in the shape of a man" and "something that becomes dangerous to its creator." This dictionary recognition reflects the reality that language evolves through usage, even when that usage diverges from original sources.

**Literary Analysis**: Scholars have noted that the name confusion may serve pedagogical purposes, making visible themes that casual readers might otherwise miss. When students encounter the "error" that Frankenstein is the creator, not the creature, they're forced to think more carefully about the relationship between the two characters and the significance of naming in the novel.

The confusion also highlights the novel's exploration of parenthood and responsibility. Just as children bear their parents' names, the creature might be said to bear Frankenstein's name by right of creation. Victor's failure to provide a name—or to accept responsibility for his creation—becomes more apparent when readers assume the creature should share his name.

**Cultural Psychology**: The persistence of the name confusion despite widespread correction attempts suggests something deeper about how cultural memory works. The error may persist because it serves useful psychological or narrative functions, similar to other persistent misconceptions that capture emotional or symbolic truths even when factually incorrect.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes correctly rates this claim as "False," noting that in Mary Shelley's original 1818 novel, "Frankenstein" refers exclusively to Victor Frankenstein, the scientist who creates the creature. The monster itself remains unnamed throughout the text, referred to variously as "creature," "daemon," "fiend," "wretch," and other descriptive terms, but never given a proper name.

Snopes accurately traces the source of confusion to popular adaptations, particularly the 1931 Universal Studios film starring Boris Karloff, which visually cemented the public image of Frankenstein's monster while contributing to the name confusion. The fact-check also notes that this error has become so common that it's achieved a kind of cultural legitimacy despite being textually inaccurate.
