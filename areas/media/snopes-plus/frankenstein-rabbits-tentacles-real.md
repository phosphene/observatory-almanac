# 'Frankenstein' rabbits with horns or tentacles on faces are real — but there's nothing to fear

## 1. The Claim

In August 2025, social media users across multiple platforms encountered photographs and descriptions of wild rabbits exhibiting grotesque facial growths — dark, tentacle-like or horn-like protrusions extending from the animals' heads and faces. The reactions were immediate and alarming. "Experts Warn of Infected 'Frankenstein' Rabbits with Tentacles Invading the US," announced one post on X that accumulated over one million views. Another post viewed nearly eight million times declared: "MAJOR CONCERN... Rabbits spotted invading parts of the US with weird tentacles sprouting from their heads due to virus... Colorado officials are warning: 'DO NOT TOUCH.'" A third post, viewed more than 24 million times, described "tentacle-like growths" on rabbits specifically identified as being in Fort Collins, Colorado.

The cascade continued: Instagram posts about the supposedly invading rabbits were liked nearly 100,000 times, more than 45,000 times, and more than 25,000 times across different accounts. A Facebook post was reacted to over 10,000 times. The language was consistently apocalyptic — "invading," "MAJOR CONCERN," "Frankenstein," and "experts warn" — suggesting an unprecedented wildlife emergency requiring urgent public attention.

The compound claim embedded in this viral moment was several-layered: that these rabbits existed and were being sighted in Colorado; that the phenomenon represented something new or escalating; that experts had issued warnings warning people away from the animals; and that the condition, whatever its cause, posed potential risks to humans or other species encountering the affected rabbits.

At the intersection of these claims sits a much older, quieter scientific story — one that was known to wildlife biologists and veterinarians for nearly a century before it became a trending topic on social media in the summer of 2025.

## 2. What's Actually True

The underlying biological phenomenon is entirely authentic. There is a real virus — the Shope papillomavirus (SPV), also known as cottontail cutaneous papilloma virus — that infects wild rabbits and produces exactly the kinds of dark, irregular growths described in the viral posts. The virus causes papillomas, or wart-like tissue growths, that can develop into the black, horn-like or tentacle-like protrusions visible in photographs of infected animals. Colorado Parks and Wildlife (CPW) has publicly documented the condition on its website, confirming that affected cottontail rabbits have been observed in Colorado.

However, essentially every element of the alarmist framing was false. The virus was not new. It was not "invading" the United States. No experts had warned of an unprecedented outbreak. The virus poses no risk to humans or to non-rabbit species.

The science behind these corrections is well-established. The Shope papillomavirus was first described in 1933 by virologist Richard Shope, who documented it in wild rabbits from Kansas and Iowa — giving the virus one of its names. A 2015 peer-reviewed study by Escudero Duch et al. ("A Century of Shope Papillomavirus in Museum Rabbit Specimens," *PLoS ONE*, 2015) examined museum specimens of rabbits and determined that the virus had been present in North American wild rabbit populations for at least a century prior to 2015. The study located the virus's endemic range "almost exclusively in the US Midwest" — a region that encompasses Colorado. In 2013, a USDA-commissioned report by Root et al. ("Cottontail Rabbit Papillomavirus Infection in a Desert Cottontail [Sylvilagus Audubonii] from Colorado, USA," *Journal of Wildlife Diseases*, 2013) documented the capture of a desert cottontail infected with the Shope papillomavirus in Larimer County, Colorado, in August 2011. Fort Collins — where the 2025 social media sightings originated — is the largest city in Larimer County.

Colorado Parks and Wildlife explicitly states on its web page dedicated to cottontail rabbits that the Shope papillomavirus is specific to rabbits and "does not cause disease in other species." PetMD and ScienceDirect sources confirm that the virus is spread primarily by biting arthropods — ticks and mosquitoes — rather than through direct contact with affected animals, and that outbreaks are most common in summer and fall when arthropod populations peak. While the disease is more severe in domestic rabbits (where growths have a three times higher rate of becoming malignant than in wild rabbits, according to ScienceDirect), wild cottontails typically experience growths that resolve spontaneously after the rabbit overcomes the infection. The growths become dangerous primarily if they develop near the mouth and interfere with eating or drinking.

The Smithsonian Institution has noted that rabbit papillomavirus infections may historically have contributed to the legend of the antlered jackalope — the folkloric creature of American Western mythology — because infected rabbits bearing irregular horn-like growths were sighted and described well before the virus's formal scientific identification.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The August 2025 social media panic about Frankenstein rabbits represents a well-documented class of information environment failure: the viral rediscovery of known ecological phenomena by users without context, amplified by algorithmic incentives for engagement through fear and novelty. Understanding how this class of failure operates requires examining both the specific features of the SPV case and the broader patterns of ecological misinformation in digital media environments.

**Species-Appropriate Disgust and Monstrous Framing**: Evolutionary psychology research has established that human disgust responses serve as behavioral immune systems, generating automatic aversive reactions to stimuli associated with pathogens, bodily deformity, and contamination (Curtis, Aunger & Rabie, 2004, "Evidence that Disgust Evolved to Protect from Risk of Disease," *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*). Images of animals with irregular, proliferating tissue growths activate these disgust responses reliably and powerfully, independent of any factual assessment of disease risk. The language of "tentacles" — appendages associated with deep-sea predators and horror genre creatures — supercharges this response by invoking a culturally loaded visual lexicon of the monstrous.

The framing of infected rabbits as "Frankenstein rabbits" amplifies this effect by explicitly invoking cultural schema of unnatural creation, scientific transgression, and the blurring of species boundaries. Mary Shelley's Frankenstein operates in popular culture as a shorthand for fears about technology, nature gone wrong, and the creation of the grotesque through boundary violation. Applying this label to an animal exhibiting the normal consequence of a viral infection transforms a routine ecological observation into a narrative event with horror genre valence.

**The Architecture of Viral Alarmism**: The specific posts that drove the 2025 panic shared structural features common to high-performing viral alarmism: compressed, declarative language ("MAJOR CONCERN"); official-sounding attribution ("experts warn"); escalating sensory description ("tentacles sprouting from their heads"); and a geographic specificity that makes the phenomenon feel immediate and proximate ("invading parts of the US," "Fort Collins, Colorado"). These structural features are characteristic of what Chou et al. (2018, "Fact-Checking as Risk Communication," *Journal of Medical Internet Research*) identified as the "false urgency pattern" — content that performs the informational style of emergency communication without fulfilling its factual obligations.

The use of "invading" deserves particular attention. Invasion biology — the scientific study of how introduced species proliferate and displace native ones — carries strong normative and threat associations in popular media. The word "invading" applied to animal populations implies a directional, intentional movement posing territorial threat. The Shope papillomavirus bears none of these characteristics: it is not a new introduction to North American ecosystems, it does not travel through rabbit dispersal in ways that constitute an "invasion," and it had already been documented in Larimer County, Colorado, for at least fourteen years before the 2025 sightings.

**Ecological Amnesia and the Perpetual Rediscovery**: The most structurally important feature of this episode is not the alarmism itself but the underlying ecological amnesia that makes such alarmism possible. Ecological amnesia — the gradual loss of baseline knowledge about what was once normal in a natural environment — was first formally described by fisheries biologist Daniel Pauly (1995, "Anecdotes and the Shifting Baselines Syndrome of Fisheries," *Trends in Ecology & Evolution*) in the context of declining marine species, but the concept applies to all forms of ecological knowledge. When most of the social media users who encountered the 2025 posts had no prior awareness that a century-old papillomavirus routinely produced horn-like growths in wild Colorado rabbits, the sighting appeared as a genuinely new and alarming event.

This knowledge gap is not primarily a failure of individual users. It reflects a structural feature of modern media ecosystems: local natural history knowledge — the accumulated understanding of what is normal in a particular ecosystem — is preserved primarily in specialized scientific literature, local wildlife management agencies, and the diminishing population of naturalists with direct field experience. None of these knowledge holders are well-positioned in the attention economy that shapes viral content creation and consumption.

The result is a systematic cycle: ecological phenomena that are well-known to wildlife biologists but invisible to general audiences are periodically "discovered" by social media users encountering them for the first time, reframed as emergencies or invasions through alarmist language, amplified by algorithmic engagement optimization, and subsequently corrected by fact-checkers — only to be rediscovered and reframed again when the next generation of users encounters similar phenomena without contextual knowledge. The Shope papillomavirus rabbit story in 2025 is structurally identical to the periodic viral panics about "zombie deer disease" (chronic wasting disease, known to wildlife managers since 1967), "brain-eating amoeba" (Naegleria fowleri, present in warm freshwater bodies throughout the southeastern United States), or "zombie caterpillars" infected by baculovirus — all phenomena with decades-long documented histories that generate recurring viral alarmism when encountered without ecological context.

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Mixture — true core, false framing
**Confidence:** High
**Epistemic Basis:** Peer-reviewed virology and wildlife biology
**Phenomenological Status:** Genuine biological phenomenon; alarmist social context is manufactured
**Mechanistic Status:** Well-established (Shope papillomavirus, arthropod transmission, species-specific)
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Low — the "Frankenstein" framing generates fear without improving understanding; the accurate scientific framing generates useful information about wildlife health and biosafety

The affected rabbits are real and their condition is real. Images circulating on social media in August 2025 accurately depicted wild cottontail rabbits exhibiting papillomas produced by a genuine viral infection that has been documented in North American rabbit populations for at least a century. There is nothing fabricated about the animals' appearance.

Everything surrounding the biological fact, however, was either false or substantially misleading. The Shope papillomavirus is not new. It is not spreading in an "invasion" pattern. It does not pose risks to humans, other mammals, or any non-rabbit species. It has been documented in Colorado specifically since at least 2011, and in the broader US Midwest since its formal scientific description in 1933. The alarmist framing — "invading," "MAJOR CONCERN," "experts warn," "Frankenstein" — was wholly manufactured by the viral communication layer superimposed on an ordinary ecological observation.

The specific claim that "experts warned" is particularly worth examining. Wildlife biologists and veterinarians have been aware of and monitoring Shope papillomavirus for decades. What they consistently say is essentially the opposite of an alarm: this is a known, endemic, species-specific condition; wild rabbits typically recover; people should not handle affected animals primarily because of general wildlife handling guidelines; and domestic rabbit owners should take sensible precautions about exposure to biting insects during peak season. This expert knowledge was accurately represented in the Colorado Parks and Wildlife web page that was widely cited during the 2025 episode. The "warning" reframed a routine wildlife health advisory into an emergency alert.

The Mixture verdict from Snopes accurately captures the dual character of the claim: real phenomenon, false alarm. What it cannot fully capture within its format is the systemic dimension of the episode — the way that periodic viral rediscovery of known ecological phenomena reveals structural vulnerabilities in how ecological knowledge is preserved and transmitted through modern digital media environments.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Shope papillomavirus and its cultural history illuminate several deeper scientific and sociological questions worth pursuing beyond the immediate fact-check.

**A Century of Papillomaviruses**: The papillomavirus family (Papillomaviridae) encompasses more than 200 known viral types that infect mammals, birds, and other vertebrates, causing a range from benign warts to malignant carcinomas depending on the host species and viral strain. Human papillomaviruses (HPV) became widely familiar through their association with cervical cancer and the development of HPV vaccines, making them one of the best-known examples of a virus-cancer connection. The discovery that viruses could causally contribute to cancer — a finding now known to apply to approximately 15–20% of all human cancers globally (de Martel et al., 2012, "Global Burden of Cancers Attributable to Infections," *The Lancet Oncology*) — began with early papillomavirus research, including Shope's work on the cottontail rabbit papillomavirus in the 1930s.

Richard Shope (1901–1966) was among the most influential virologists of the 20th century, making foundational contributions not only to papillomavirus biology but also to influenza virology and the study of swine influenza. His 1933 documentation of the cottontail rabbit papillomavirus provided early evidence that viruses could induce stable heritable changes in cells — a conceptual precursor to the modern understanding of viral oncogenesis. The virus that bears his name was subsequently used in laboratory research as an early model system for studying papillomavirus biology, viral tumor induction, and host immune responses. This research lineage contributed directly to the scientific understanding that eventually enabled the development of vaccines against human HPVs.

Escudero Duch et al. (2015, "A Century of Shope Papillomavirus in Museum Rabbit Specimens," *PLoS ONE*) demonstrated the remarkable scientific utility of museum collections: by examining preserved specimens held in natural history museum collections dating back to before the virus's formal identification, the researchers were able to document the virus's presence in tissues collected decades earlier than any virological surveillance could have detected it. This approach — retrospective detection of pathogens in archived biological specimens — has become increasingly important in emerging disease surveillance, allowing researchers to establish baseline distributions and temporal trends for pathogens of concern before they become clinically significant.

**Cottontail Biology and Population Dynamics**: Eastern and desert cottontail rabbits (Sylvilagus floridanus and Sylvilagus audubonii respectively) are among the most ecologically significant small mammals in North American grassland and shrubland ecosystems, serving as primary prey species for a wide range of predators including coyotes, foxes, raptors, and the threatened ferruginous hawk. Cottontail population dynamics are characterized by high reproductive rates, short individual lifespans, and dramatic interannual population fluctuations driven by predator-prey cycling, weather, and disease (Chapman & Flux, 1990, "Rabbits, Hares and Pikas," IUCN).

Viral diseases including the Shope papillomavirus, myxomatosis (introduced historically in European rabbit populations), and rabbit hemorrhagic disease are part of the natural epidemiological landscape of wild cottontail populations. At typical infection prevalences, these diseases function as components of ecosystem regulation rather than as threats to population viability. The appearance of papillomatosis in individual animals draws attention precisely because its symptoms are visually dramatic — horn-like facial growths are far more visible and striking than the internal effects of many other viral infections — but does not indicate elevated population-level risk.

**Host-Pathogen Specificity**: The species-specificity of the Shope papillomavirus is one of its most epidemiologically important characteristics and deserves scientific clarification. Papillomaviruses as a group are highly host-specific: each type infects only a narrow range of host species, with most types infecting a single species or closely related species cluster. This host specificity is determined by the virus's surface proteins and the corresponding receptor molecules on host cells, which must match for successful cellular infection (Doorbar et al., 2012, "The Biology and Life-Cycle of Human Papillomaviruses," *Vaccine*).

For the Shope papillomavirus, species specificity means that human infection is not merely unlikely but essentially impossible through normal contact with affected animals. The virus's surface proteins are adapted to bind cellular receptors found in rabbit epithelial cells — not the analogous receptors in human cells. This biological barrier is robust and has not been overcome in the decades of documented virus-human contact during wildlife management operations, veterinary examinations, and casual observation. The appropriate public health guidance — "don't handle wild animals" — is a general wildlife biosafety guideline, not a specific anti-SPV protective measure.

**The Jackalope Connection**: The Smithsonian Institution's observation that Shope papillomavirus infections may have contributed to jackalope mythology deserves expansion. The jackalope — a folkloric creature depicted as a jackrabbit with pronghorn antelope horns — first appeared in American popular culture in the 1930s, with the earliest documented taxidermy of the creature created by Douglas Herrick of Douglas, Wyoming, in 1932. The timing of the jackalope's cultural emergence is strikingly coincident with Shope's formal documentation of the cottontail papillomavirus in 1933, suggesting that observations of horn-bearing rabbits were circulating in the cultural environment that generated the legend.

This connection represents an unusual case where folk natural history — the collection and interpretation of anomalous animal observations by non-scientists — correctly captured a genuine biological phenomenon but interpreted it through a mythological rather than pathological framework. The jackalope as cultural artifact represents preserved ecological witnessing: people encountered horned rabbits, found the phenomenon noteworthy and memorable, and preserved the observation in a cultural form that persisted across generations. The observation was accurate; only the explanation was wrong.

**Social Media Ecology and Information Exposure**: Research on information diffusion through social media platforms has consistently found that false or misleading content spreads faster, further, and more persistently than accurate information (Vosoughi, Roy & Aral, 2018, "The Spread of True and False News Online," *Science*). The mechanism proposed by Vosoughi and colleagues involves novelty: false information tends to be more novel — more surprising and unexpected — than true information, which may already be familiar to many users. True stories like "known virus continues to cause known symptoms in known animal population in known location" fails the novelty test categorically. False stories like "EXPERTS WARN: Frankenstein rabbits with TENTACLES invading America" pass it with maximum scores.

This structural feature of the information environment means that accurate ecological information about ordinary wildlife phenomena will systematically underperform alarmist misrepresentations of those same phenomena in any attention economy. Fact-checks correct the record for users who seek them out, but the fact-checked corrected version of the story reaches a far smaller audience than the original alarmist version.

**Comparative Cases of Ecological Misrepresentation**: The pattern illustrated by the Frankenstein rabbit panic appears with remarkable regularity. Chronic wasting disease in deer — a prion-based neurodegenerative disease endemic to North American wild deer populations since at least the 1960s — generates periodic media cycles of "zombie deer" alarm when news organizations encounter the condition, typically framing existing, stable, monitored endemicity as a new crisis. Red tide algal blooms along Florida's Gulf Coast produce recurring cycles of alarm coverage despite being a documented feature of Florida's coastal ecology for centuries. The murder hornet panic of 2020 responded to the documented first detection of Asian giant hornets in the Pacific Northwest, a genuine ecological concern, with media coverage that dramatically overstated human risk and generated public fear disproportionate to actual threat.

In each case, the underlying ecological phenomenon was real and scientifically documented. In each case, the alarmist media framing misrepresented the novelty, geographic reach, or risk profile of the phenomenon. In each case, fact-checkers verified the core claim while correcting the framing. And in each case, a structurally similar episode has occurred again when a new cohort of social media users encountered the phenomenon without contextual knowledge.

What separates the Shope papillomavirus rabbit case from a situation warranting genuine alarm is precisely the features that wildlife biologists assess systematically: Is the agent new to the region? (No.) Is the affected population experiencing atypical mortality? (Not documented.) Does the agent have demonstrated potential for spillover to other species? (No.) Is the geographic range expanding in ways that suggest novel ecological dynamics? (Not evidenced.) All four assessments point to ordinary endemicity, not invasion.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim as **Mixture**, with the "What's True" component being that the Shope papillomavirus is real and produces the observed growths in wild rabbits, and the "What's False" component being that the virus is new to the US, that it poses risks to humans or other species, or that it is "invading" in any meaningful sense.

The Snopes analysis by Emery Winter (published August 13, 2025) draws on a comprehensive set of sources: Colorado Parks and Wildlife's official species page for cottontail rabbits; the 2015 Escudero Duch et al. study in PLoS ONE; the 2013 Root et al. USDA report in the Journal of Wildlife Diseases; the Smithsonian Institution's public natural history content; and PetMD and ScienceDirect entries on cottontail rabbit papillomavirus. The sourcing accurately represents the state of the scientific literature and institutional wildlife management consensus.

The citation of the peer-reviewed literature — specifically the Escudero Duch et al. (2015) study establishing a century of documented presence — is the most important factual contribution of the fact-check. It anchors the debunking not in assertion but in historical scientific record, making it difficult for the "new invasion" framing to survive contact with the facts.

The Snopes treatment correctly identifies the core false elements of viral spread: the "invasion" framing, the "experts warn" framing, and the claim of human risk. It also correctly notes the genuine differential concern for domestic rabbit owners, who have legitimate reason to minimize their pets' exposure to biting insects in areas where the virus is circulating. This nuance — the virus is benign in wild rabbits but potentially more serious in domestic ones — is essential and appropriately included.

What the Mixture format captures well is the dual-character nature of the claim: there is genuine biological reality here, and responding with a blanket False verdict would itself be misleading. The papillomavirus-infected rabbits with horn-like growths are real animals, their condition is real, and the images people saw were authentic. The Mixture verdict correctly prevents overcorrection toward dismissal of the underlying phenomenon while clearly designating the alarmist invasion framing as false.
