## The Claim

Benjamin Franklin is said to have advocated for the turkey — not the bald eagle — to be America's national bird. The story appears reliably at Thanksgiving dinners, in American history classrooms, on trivia websites, and in popular books about the Founding Fathers. It is presented as evidence of Franklin's idiosyncratic judgment, his practical sense, or — depending on the teller — his charming eccentricity.

The claim is **false as stated**, but not without a real foundation. Franklin did, in a private letter, express admiration for the turkey and dissatisfaction with the bald eagle. The myth arises from a specific distortion: a private, after-the-fact grumble is transformed into a formal policy position or legislative advocacy.

## What's Actually True

### The Letter: Franklin to Sarah Bache, 26 January 1784

Every version of this myth traces back to a single primary source: a letter from Benjamin Franklin to his daughter Sarah Bache, written on 26 January 1784 — more than a year and a half after the Great Seal of the United States was formally adopted on 20 June 1782.

The letter is preserved in the Founders Online archive at the National Archives, and the relevant passage is worth quoting at length because it is often excerpted in ways that amplify one half and suppress the other:

> For my own part I wish the bald eagle had not been chosen as the representative of our country. He is a bird of bad moral character. He does not get his living honestly. You may have seen him perched on some dead tree, where, too lazy to fish for himself, he watches the labour of the fishing hawk; and when that diligent bird has at length taken a fish, and is bearing it to his nest for the support of his mate and young ones, the bald eagle pursues him, and takes it from him.
>
> With all this injustice, he is never in good case, but like those among men who live by sharping and robbing he is generally poor and often very lousy. Besides he is a rank coward: the little king bird not bigger than a sparrow attacks him boldly and drives him out of the district. [...]
>
> I am on this account not displeased that the figure is not known as a bald eagle, but looks more like a turkey. For in truth, the turkey is in comparison a much more respectable bird, and withal a true original native of America. Eagles have been found in all countries, but the turkey was peculiar to ours, the first of the species seen in Europe being brought to France by the Jesuits from Canada, and served up at the wedding table of Charles the ninth.
>
> He is besides, (though a little vain and silly tis true, but not the worse emblem for that) a bird of courage, and would not hesitate to attack a grenadier of the British guards who should presume to invade his farm yard with a red coat on.

This passage is vivid, funny, and characteristically Franklinesque — playful, moralistic, and making a pointed political analogy through animal behaviour. But note what it is not:

1. It was written **after** the Great Seal had been adopted; it is not a proposal, it is a retrospective complaint.
2. Franklin says he is **"not displeased"** that the seal figure looks more like a turkey than an eagle — he is not saying it should have been a turkey.
3. His treatment of the turkey is genuinely admiring, but he also notes the turkey is "a little vain and silly" — this is not an unqualified endorsement.
4. There is no statement anywhere in this letter that he **advocated** for the turkey to be the national symbol.

### Franklin's Actual Role in the Great Seal Process

The myth is further complicated by Franklin's actual participation in the seal design process, which has been studied in detail by the U.S. State Department.

On 4 July 1776 — the same day the Declaration of Independence was adopted — the Continental Congress appointed a committee to design a seal for the new nation. The committee consisted of Benjamin Franklin, John Adams, and Thomas Jefferson. These were arguably the three most prominent intellectual figures among the Founders.

The committee struggled. According to State Department records, they eventually turned to a consultant — Pierre Eugène du Simitière, a Swiss-born artist and naturalist living in Philadelphia — for help with the design. The first committee's proposals were submitted to Congress on 20 August 1776, but Congress ordered the report "to lie on the table," indicating disapproval.

The design proposed by this first committee did **not feature a bird at all**. The proposals centred on Biblical and classical imagery: Moses parting the Red Sea (Franklin's suggestion), the Judgment of Hercules (Adams's preference), and a complex multi-element design from Jefferson. None of these came close to adoption.

Two subsequent committees took up the work before the final seal was finalised. By the time a bald eagle appeared in the design, Franklin had no involvement in the process. The bird was introduced by the second and third committees and their consultants, and the final design was submitted by Secretary of the Continental Congress Charles Thomson in 1782, who incorporated the eagle from an earlier design but gave it the specific American character the seal now bears.

Franklin never formally proposed a turkey. He never formally proposed any bird. His committee did not include birds in its submission. And his famous letter of 1784 was a private communication written after the seal had been in use for eighteen months.

### The Verdict on the Claim

Snopes rates this claim **False**, with important context: Franklin opposed the bald eagle as a national symbol but never specifically advocated that a turkey be its replacement. That verdict is correct.

The Franklin Institute (in Philadelphia) has addressed this myth directly, concluding the same: the turkey story is a distortion.

## Why People Believe This

### The Letter Is Real and the Sentiment Is Genuine

The myth survives in part because it is not entirely fabricated. Unlike some myths that have no factual basis whatsoever, this one has a primary source document that really does show Franklin expressing the views attributed to him — a preference for the turkey's character over the eagle's. People who have actually read the letter sometimes conclude that, while Franklin may not have formally advocated for the turkey, he clearly *preferred* it, which makes the claim feel substantially true even when it is technically false.

This is the defining structure of the "distorted-but-grounded" category: an authentic kernel of evidence with a conclusion that goes further than the evidence actually supports.

### Franklin as America's Favourite Eccentric Founder

Benjamin Franklin occupies a distinctive place in American historical mythology. Unlike Washington (severe, marble, paternal) or Jefferson (brilliant but morally compromised), Franklin is remembered as the practical jokester, the inventor of the homely proverb, the man who flew a kite in a thunderstorm, the self-made printer from Boston who dined with kings. His character invites humanising anecdotes, and the turkey story is exactly the kind of thing people feel Franklin *would* have done.

This is a well-documented cognitive mechanism in historical memory: claims that are consistent with a figure's established character feel more credible regardless of evidential basis. Franklin's wit and irreverence are so firmly established that a story about him engaging in elaborate ornithological character assassination reads as plausible.

### The "National Bird" Frame Is an Anachronism

The Great Seal features a bald eagle, but the United States does not officially designate a "national bird" through formal legislation in the same way it designates, say, a national anthem or a national motto. The Bald Eagle Protection Act of 1940 established the bald eagle as a protected species, and it has long been considered the de facto national bird, but the frame of "Franklin wanted a different national bird" applies a category that wasn't clearly formulated in that way during the founding period.

The mythology elides this distinction. People familiar with the idea of national symbols think of the Great Seal design as equivalent to choosing a national bird. Franklin's complaint about the seal's eagle then becomes a complaint about the national bird choice, which then becomes an advocacy position for an alternative. Each transformation involves a small imprecision; cumulatively, they produce a false claim.

### Telephone Effects and Popularised History

The story has been repeated so many times — in American textbooks, in Thanksgiving-themed journalism, in travel writing about Philadelphia — that it has acquired the texture of established fact. Repetition is itself a source of perceived credibility. By the time someone encounters this claim in adulthood, they may have heard it from teachers, relatives, and television specials without ever encountering the correction.

The Franklin Institute notes that this is one of the most commonly asked questions they receive about Franklin, suggesting a very high base rate of public belief in the claim. This is consistent with the mechanisms above: a plausible story attached to a beloved historical figure, repeated in credible-seeming contexts, resistant to the friction of correction.

## Verdict

**False as stated, grounded in a real document.** Benjamin Franklin never formally advocated that the turkey be the national bird or the symbol on the Great Seal. He was not a participant in the decisions that led to the bald eagle's inclusion on the seal. His famous private letter of January 1784 — written after the seal was already in use — expressed strong personal distaste for the eagle's "moral character" and genuine admiration for the turkey, but it was a private opinion, not a policy position. The claim converts a retrospective personal grumble into a historical counterfactual that never existed.

The distinction matters: Franklin was a writer of extraordinary rhetorical skill. His letter about the eagle and the turkey is a piece of entertainment — a funny moral fable delivered to his daughter. Reading it as a position paper in a policy debate misunderstands both the genre of the document and the context in which it was written.

## The Wider Picture

### What Franklin Actually Believed About National Symbols

Franklin's letter reveals something genuinely interesting about his thinking on national symbolism, beyond the turkey/eagle surface controversy. His critique of the bald eagle is explicitly *moral and behavioural* — it is not merely an aesthetic preference but an argument that national symbols should embody virtues worth emulating. An eagle that steals fish from other birds, he argues, projects the wrong values.

This is a serious argument in the philosophy of political symbolism, one that echoes debates about national emblems that continue today. Should national symbols represent what a nation is, what it aspires to be, or what it already has? Franklin's turkey endorsement (such as it is) falls clearly in the "aspiration" camp: the turkey is, in his framing, industrious, native, courageous, and appropriately modest.

The bald eagle became entrenched as America's symbol for reasons that had little to do with Franklin's views: its impressive appearance, its existing use in heraldic traditions, its association with Roman and later European imperial iconography (which the new republic was simultaneously appropriating and trying to distinguish itself from), and the sheer momentum of committee-driven design.

### The Great Seal's Complex Genealogy

The Great Seal of the United States is itself a fascinating example of design by committee. The process took six years and involved fourteen men across three separate committees. The final design fused elements from different proposals, incorporated misunderstandings about heraldic convention, and was finalised under time pressure.

Charles Thomson, who produced the final design, took the bald eagle from William Barton's design but gave it a more naturalistic American orientation. The result is a seal that its creators would have described as intentional and unified, but which close inspection reveals as a composite of competing ideas. Franklin's committee, with its Moses and Hercules imagery, would have produced a very different national symbol had it prevailed.

This complexity — the contingent, committee-driven reality of the seal's origin — is entirely missing from the Franklin turkey myth, which narrates the event as a single founding moment where one great man made a fateful choice.

### Founding Father Mythology More Broadly

The turkey story is one example of a larger category: myths about the Founding Fathers that distort the historical record by projecting contemporary concerns backward. Other examples include the persistent myth that the Declaration of Independence was signed on July 4 (it wasn't; most signatures were appended on August 2), that Thomas Jefferson was a religious skeptic who rejected Christianity outright (he was a complicated Deist who produced a version of the Gospels with miracles removed, but the full picture is more nuanced), and that Alexander Hamilton and Aaron Burr's duel was simply personal animosity (it was embedded in a complex political context involving the 1804 New York gubernatorial election).

What these myths share is a tendency to personalise and simplify the contingent, complicated, and often messy historical reality. Franklin opposed the eagle; therefore he proposed the turkey. Hamilton insulted Burr; therefore they fought. The actual histories are richer, stranger, and more instructive, but they are also harder to remember and harder to tell at a dinner table.

### Why the Myth Is Harmless But Still Worth Correcting

The consequences of believing the turkey myth are low. No one is harassed, no policy is distorted, no community suffers because people think Franklin wanted a different national bird. The myth is in the "charming trivia" category of misinformation rather than the "consequential falsehood" category.

But correcting it matters for methodological reasons. The structure of the claim — a real primary source, a genuine sentiment from a real historical figure, converted into a false conclusion — is exactly the structure of much more consequential distortions. Learning to distinguish "Franklin expressed this opinion" from "Franklin advocated this position" is training in the kind of close reading that matters greatly when the stakes are higher.

The turkey story is also a useful case study in what documents actually say versus what people claim they say. The letter to Sarah Bache is freely available online, concise, and highly readable. Anyone who reads it can verify the claim themselves. The persistence of the myth despite the document's accessibility suggests that the social transmission of the story is so powerful that primary source access is insufficient on its own to correct it.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this **False**, providing the Franklin letter in substantial quotation and noting the critical contextual gap: the letter was written after the seal was adopted, and Franklin never formally proposed the turkey as an alternative. The rating and its explanation are both accurate.

The assessment also correctly notes that Franklin's first committee — the only committee he served on — did not include a bird in its proposal at all. This is a crucial evidentiary point that demolishes the narrative even before reaching the letter.

The one slightly underexplored dimension in the standard fact-check treatment is the broader context of the Great Seal's design history, which illustrates the full complexity of how the eagle came to be on the seal and how absent from that process Franklin actually was. Understanding that the decision involved fourteen men over six years, and that Franklin's committee's proposal was rejected outright, reframes the question from "should it have been a turkey?" to "what series of decisions led to the eagle, and who made them?"

**The epistemological residue:** The myth's staying power is a useful reminder that even well-established falsehoods backed by clear primary sources are difficult to dislodge once they enter the social memory of a culture. The correction is available, credible, and straightforward. The myth persists anyway. This should make us appropriately humble about the limits of fact-checking as a tool for reshaping public belief.
