# Benjamin Franklin Wanted the Turkey to Be the National Bird of the United States

## The Claim

The story is among the most durable pieces of American founding mythology: Benjamin Franklin, polymath, diplomat, and elder statesman of the Revolution, passionately campaigned for the wild turkey to replace the bald eagle as the national bird of the United States. The claim circulates in schoolrooms, Thanksgiving dinner conversations, history podcasts, and viral social media posts with metronomic regularity. It appears in trivia games, editorial cartoons, and even the marketing of bourbon brands. In most retellings, Franklin is cast as a lovable eccentric — the kite-flying sage who thought America's symbol should be a farmyard bird rather than a raptor. Some versions escalate the claim into outright legislative action, asserting that Franklin formally proposed the turkey during the design of the Great Seal or fought a losing battle in the Continental Congress.

The myth rests on a kernel of real documentary evidence. Franklin did, in fact, write critically about the bald eagle and favorably about the wild turkey. The letter exists, it is authenticated, and it is freely available in the archives of the National Archives via Founders Online. This is what makes the claim so sticky: it is not fabricated from whole cloth. It is fabricated from selectively quoted cloth — real words, stripped of their context, stitched into a narrative that Franklin himself would not have recognized.

Snopes rates the claim **False**, and that rating is correct. But the full story — why Franklin wrote what he wrote, what the letter was actually about, how the Great Seal was really designed, and how the myth took on a cultural life of its own — is far richer and more instructive than a simple true-or-false verdict can convey.

## What's Actually True

### The Letter to Sarah Bache

On January 26, 1784, Benjamin Franklin sat in his quarters in Passy, a village on the outskirts of Paris where he had served as American minister to France since 1778. He was seventy-eight years old, in declining health, and increasingly homesick. He wrote a long, discursive letter to his daughter, Sarah (Sally) Bache, in Philadelphia.

The letter was not about the national bird. It was not about the Great Seal. It was about the **Society of the Cincinnati** — a newly formed fraternal organization for officers who had served in the Continental Army and their descendants. Franklin was alarmed by the Society's hereditary membership structure, which he saw as an affront to republican principles. The idea that military distinction could be passed from father to son like a title of nobility struck him as precisely the kind of aristocratic corruption the Revolution had been fought to abolish.

Franklin devoted the bulk of the letter to a sustained, often witty critique of hereditary honors. He calculated, with characteristic mathematical playfulness, that after nine generations a member's descendant would have only 1/512th of the original officer's blood — the remaining 511 parts coming from "peasants and tradesmen." He called the principle "mathematically absurd."

It was in the course of this argument — and only in this course — that Franklin turned his attention to the Society's insignia, which featured an eagle. Here, and only here, is the passage that launched the myth:

> "For my own part I wish the Bald Eagle had not been chosen as the Representative of our Country. He is a Bird of bad moral Character. He does not get his Living honestly. You may have seen him perch'd on some dead Tree near the River, where, too lazy to fish for himself, he watches the Labour of the Fishing Hawk; and when that diligent Bird has at length taken a Fish, and is bearing it to his Nest for the Support of his Mate and young Ones, the Bald Eagle pursues him and takes it from him."

Franklin continued:

> "With all this Injustice, he is never in good Case but like those among Men who live by Sharping & Robbing he is generally poor and often very lousy. Besides he is a rank Coward: The little King Bird not bigger than a Sparrow attacks him boldly and drives him out of the District."

And then the famous passage about the turkey:

> "I am on this account not displeas'd that the Figure is not known as a Bald Eagle, but looks more like a Turkey. For in Truth the Turkey is in Comparison a much more respectable Bird. He is besides, though a little vain & silly, a Bird of Courage, and would not hesitate to attack a Grenadier of the British Guards who should presume to invade his Farm Yard with a red Coat on."

Read in its original context, the passage is unmistakably a piece of rhetoric, not a policy proposal. Franklin was making a satirical comparison between the eagle and men who "live by Sharping & Robbing" — a barb aimed at the pretensions of hereditary military aristocracy. The turkey's "courage" against a redcoat is a joke, playing on the bird's territorial aggression and the color of British military uniforms. Franklin was being funny. He was also being politically serious — but about the Society of the Cincinnati, not about ornithological symbolism.

Crucially, Franklin never said he wanted the turkey to be the national bird. He said he was "not displeas'd" that the Society's poorly engraved eagle looked more like a turkey, and he used the comparison to extend his satirical argument. The distinction between these two positions — ironic rhetorical commentary versus earnest advocacy — is the entire gap between the myth and the reality.

### The Actual Design of the Great Seal

The history of the Great Seal further demolishes the claim. The seal was designed through three successive committees between 1776 and 1782, and Franklin's involvement was limited to the first.

**The First Committee (1776).** On July 4, 1776 — the very day the Declaration of Independence was adopted — the Continental Congress appointed Benjamin Franklin, John Adams, and Thomas Jefferson to design a national seal. The committee also engaged Pierre Eugène Du Simitière, a Swiss-born artist and heraldry expert in Philadelphia, as a consultant. The proposals that emerged from this committee contained no birds whatsoever. Franklin proposed a dramatic Biblical scene: Moses parting the Red Sea while Pharaoh's army drowned, with the motto "Rebellion to Tyrants is Obedience to God." Jefferson suggested the Children of Israel in the wilderness on one side and Hengist and Horsa, the legendary Saxon chiefs, on the other. Adams proposed Hercules at the Crossroads, choosing between Virtue and Sloth. Du Simitière contributed a shield design with elements from six European nations represented in the American colonies. Congress reviewed the committee's amalgamated proposal in August 1776 and tabled it. No eagle. No turkey. No bird of any species.

**The Second Committee (1780).** A new committee was appointed in 1780, consisting of James Lovell, John Morin Scott, and William Churchill Houston. They engaged Francis Hopkinson, a signer of the Declaration and a talented artist, as their consultant. Hopkinson introduced a shield with thirteen red and white stripes and a constellation of thirteen stars, but the central figure on his design was a seated figure of Liberty, not a bird. Congress tabled this proposal as well.

**The Third Committee and Charles Thomson (1782).** A third committee — Arthur Middleton, John Rutledge, and Elias Boudinot — was appointed in May 1782. Their consultant was William Barton, a Philadelphia lawyer with expertise in heraldry. Barton introduced the bald eagle into the design, placing it as a crest atop a pillar. When Congress again found the result unsatisfactory, they handed all three committees' work to Charles Thomson, Secretary of Congress. Thomson was the figure who actually synthesized the final design. He placed Barton's eagle at the center, holding an olive branch and arrows, with the motto *E Pluribus Unum* on a scroll in its beak and a constellation of stars above its head. Congress approved Thomson's design on June 20, 1782.

Franklin was in Paris during the work of the second and third committees. He had no involvement in the selection of the eagle, no vote on the final design, and — beyond his 1776 contribution of the Moses scene — no input into the Great Seal at all. The letter to Sarah Bache was written a year and a half after the seal's adoption. He was commenting after the fact, parenthetically, in the course of an argument about something else entirely.

### Franklin's Rhetorical Method

Understanding why the letter reads the way it does requires understanding Franklin as a writer. He was, by 1784, one of the most celebrated prose stylists in the English-speaking world. His *Poor Richard's Almanack* had made him famous for sharp, epigrammatic wit. His political satires — including pieces like "Rules by Which a Great Empire May Be Reduced to a Small One" (1773) and "An Edict by the King of Prussia" (1773) — demonstrated a mastery of irony, deadpan absurdism, and the kind of rhetorical misdirection that invites the reader to mistake the satirical surface for a sincere argument.

The turkey passage is vintage Franklin: a mock-serious comparison that uses animal behavior as a lens for human moral failings. The eagle is lazy, dishonest, cowardly — like the European aristocrats whose hereditary honors Franklin despised. The turkey is "vain & silly" but brave enough to fight — like the common republican citizen Franklin championed. The passage is a fable in miniature, a parable about character and merit, embedded in a private family letter. To extract it and present it as a policy position is to misunderstand not just the letter but the man.

## Why People Believe This

The myth persists for reasons that are themselves instructive about how historical misinformation operates.

**The quote is real.** Unlike many pieces of Founders mythology — the cherry tree, "I cannot tell a lie," and various fabricated deathbed utterances — the Franklin turkey quote comes from an authenticated, easily accessible document. This gives the myth an armor of credibility that purely invented stories lack. People who encounter the claim can verify that Franklin really did write favorable things about the turkey and unfavorable things about the eagle. The fabrication lies not in the words but in their framing.

**The myth flatters American self-image.** The story casts Franklin as charmingly contrarian, a democratic populist who preferred the humble, practical turkey to the imperial eagle. This fits neatly into the broader mythology of the Founders as plain-spoken farmer-philosophers, suspicious of European pomp. The turkey — associated with Thanksgiving, the harvest, and homespun American identity — becomes a symbol of anti-elitist values. Never mind that Franklin was, by 1784, a cosmopolitan bon vivant who had spent a decade in the salons of Paris; the myth prefers its folksy version.

**Context requires effort; slogans do not.** Reading the full letter to Sarah Bache takes time and attention. Understanding its argument about the Society of the Cincinnati requires historical background that most casual readers do not have. The decontextualized quote — "the Turkey is in Comparison a much more respectable Bird" — is punchy, memorable, and self-contained. It travels well across media precisely because it has been stripped of the nuance that would complicate it.

**Repetition breeds credibility.** The claim has been repeated in enough authoritative-seeming contexts — history textbooks, museum gift shops, television documentaries, even the websites of some historical societies — that many people encounter it as established fact before they ever encounter a correction. Each repetition adds a thin layer of apparent authority, until the accretion feels as solid as bedrock.

**The myth fills a narrative gap.** The actual process of designing the Great Seal — six years, three committees, multiple consultants, and a final synthesis by a congressional secretary — is bureaucratic and undramatic. The myth replaces this institutional story with a personal one: a great man, a bold proposal, a colorful debate. The human brain prefers stories about individuals making choices to stories about committees filing reports. The turkey myth gives the Great Seal an origin story with a protagonist, a conflict, and a punchline.

**Thanksgiving amplifies the signal.** Every November, the myth enjoys a seasonal resurgence. Journalists, bloggers, and social media users in search of Thanksgiving-adjacent content rediscover the turkey quote and circulate it anew. The holiday provides a recurring cultural occasion for the myth to be retold, shared, and reinforced, creating an annual cycle of misinformation that fact-checks struggle to interrupt.

## Verdict

**False.** Benjamin Franklin never proposed, advocated for, or campaigned to make the turkey the national bird of the United States. He was not involved in the selection of the bald eagle for the Great Seal. His comments about the turkey appear in a single private letter to his daughter, written in 1784 — more than a year after the Great Seal was adopted — in the course of an argument about hereditary military honors and the Society of the Cincinnati. The passage is rhetorical satire, not a policy proposal. To claim that Franklin "wanted" the turkey as national bird is to misrepresent the letter's content, context, purpose, and tone.

The rating is unambiguous, and Snopes is correct to assign it. But the verdict alone misses what makes this case valuable: the mechanism of distortion. The raw material is genuine. The words are Franklin's. What is false is the editorial frame placed around them — the omission of context, the inflation of satire into advocacy, and the transformation of a parenthetical aside into a defining position. This is not a case of someone making things up. It is a case of someone selectively quoting things that are true in a way that produces a conclusion that is false. That mechanism is worth understanding in its own right.

## The Wider Picture

### The Manufacture of Founders Mythology

The Franklin turkey myth belongs to a broader pattern in which the words of the American Founders are selectively quoted, decontextualized, and repurposed to serve narratives they never intended. This pattern is pervasive and bipartisan. Jefferson's "tree of liberty" letter (to William Stephens Smith, 1787) is routinely quoted to support positions Jefferson was not advancing in the letter. Madison's Federalist No. 46 is cited in ways that ignore its actual argument about federal-state relations. Washington's Farewell Address is mined for lines about foreign entanglements while its equally emphatic warnings about partisan faction go unmentioned.

The common mechanism is always the same: a real document, a genuine quotation, an act of surgical extraction that removes the words from the argument they were embedded in, and a new frame that assigns them a meaning the author did not intend. The result is a kind of historical ventriloquism — the Founder appears to speak, but the words have been rearranged in someone else's mouth.

Franklin is particularly vulnerable to this treatment because he wrote so much, so wittily, and in so many registers. His irony invites misreading. His aphorisms invite decontextualization. His range — from scientific papers to bawdy humor to political satire to diplomatic correspondence — means that almost any position can find a Franklinian quote to support it, provided one is willing to ignore the surrounding paragraphs.

### What the Letter Actually Reveals

Read properly, the letter to Sarah Bache is not a quirky footnote about turkeys. It is a serious political document that reveals Franklin's deepest republican commitments. His target was not the eagle but the principle of hereditary distinction — the idea that honor could be inherited rather than earned. Writing from France, where he had witnessed firsthand the consequences of an entrenched aristocracy, Franklin saw the Society of the Cincinnati as a potentially dangerous seed. American officers, fresh from a war fought against monarchical privilege, were creating a hereditary order that mimicked the very structures they had overthrown.

The eagle-and-turkey passage functions within this larger argument as a satirical emblem. The eagle — a predator that steals from the industrious, a bully that flees from smaller birds — represents the aristocratic parasite, living off others' labor and claiming unearned dignity. The turkey — vain but brave, willing to fight — represents the ordinary citizen whose courage comes not from bloodline but from character. Franklin was not talking about birds. He was talking about the soul of the republic.

This reading is not speculative. It is what the letter says, plainly, when you read the whole thing. The myth survives precisely because most people never do.

### The Cultural Afterlife

The turkey myth has taken on a cultural life that now operates independently of the historical record. It has become a piece of American folklore — something "everyone knows" in the same way that everyone knows George Washington had wooden teeth (he didn't; they were made from hippopotamus ivory, human teeth, and metal) or that Paul Revere shouted "The British are coming!" on his midnight ride (he didn't; the colonists still considered themselves British, and the operation required stealth, not shouting).

These myths persist not because evidence supports them but because they perform cultural work. They provide memorable, humanizing stories about figures who might otherwise remain remote and monumental. Franklin-the-turkey-advocate is more approachable than Franklin-the-political-theorist-critiquing-hereditary-oligarchy. The myth domesticates a complex thinker into a lovable eccentric, and in doing so, it makes the Founding era feel accessible and folksy rather than intellectually demanding and politically fraught.

There is a cost to this domestication. When we replace Franklin's actual argument — a sharp, principled warning about the corruption of republican government by hereditary privilege — with a charming anecdote about turkeys, we lose access to ideas that remain urgently relevant. Franklin's critique of unearned status, of institutions that perpetuate advantage across generations, of the tendency for revolutionary movements to calcify into new forms of aristocracy — these are not quaint eighteenth-century concerns. They are live questions in any democratic society. The turkey myth doesn't just misrepresent what Franklin said. It obscures what he was thinking about, and why it mattered.

### Counterfactual Temptations

Part of the myth's appeal is the counterfactual it invites: *What if* America's national bird were the turkey? The thought experiment is irresistible and harmless as a parlor game. But it rests on a false premise. The choice was never between the eagle and the turkey. The choice was between Moses parting the Red Sea, Hercules at the Crossroads, a seated figure of Liberty, and various heraldic configurations — none of which involved a turkey. The eagle entered the design process in 1782 through William Barton's heraldic expertise, drawing on a long European tradition of using eagles as symbols of sovereignty. The turkey was never a candidate, never proposed, and never debated. The counterfactual is entertaining, but it is built on the myth rather than the history.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim **False** and provides a competent summary of the letter's context and the Great Seal's history. The rating is correct, and the core debunking is sound. Other fact-checking organizations — including the Smithsonian's *National Museum of American History* and the Franklin Institute in Philadelphia — have published similar corrections, noting the letter's actual subject and the absence of any formal turkey proposal.

The standard fact-check approach works well here at the level of verdict: the claim is false, and the evidence is clear. Where the typical treatment falls short is in the depth of context. Most fact-checks quote the relevant passages from the letter but do not reconstruct the full argument about the Society of the Cincinnati, which is essential to understanding *why* Franklin wrote what he wrote. Without that context, the correction can feel arbitrary — "he didn't mean it that way" — rather than anchored in a recoverable reading of the actual text.

The case also illustrates a structural limitation of the fact-check format when applied to "distorted-but-grounded" claims. A binary true/false verdict handles outright fabrications well. It handles distortions less well, because the thing being checked is not a discrete factual proposition but an interpretive frame. Franklin did write favorably about turkeys and unfavorably about eagles. The claim is not that he didn't; the claim is that these comments constituted advocacy for the turkey as national bird. The falsity lies in the inference, not the evidence. Communicating that distinction — *every word is real, but the conclusion drawn from them is wrong* — is harder than debunking an invented quote, and it requires more space, more context, and more willingness to engage with the text as a text rather than as a collection of extractable data points.

This is the broader challenge of historical misinformation: when the raw material is authentic, the debunking must operate at the level of interpretation rather than verification. It must reconstruct context that the myth has stripped away. And it must do so compellingly enough to compete with a version of the story that is shorter, funnier, and easier to remember. The turkey myth will persist not because the evidence supports it, but because it is a better story than the truth — unless the truth is told with equal vividness and depth.

The fact-checking community would benefit from treating cases like this not merely as claims to rate but as case studies in the mechanics of distortion. The Franklin turkey myth is a near-perfect teaching example of how selective quotation works, how context changes meaning, and how a real document can be made to say something its author never intended. That lesson extends far beyond any single historical anecdote. It applies to every domain in which real evidence is repackaged to support false conclusions — which is to say, it applies to the entire landscape of modern misinformation.
