# Humans Possess Free Will Independent of Deterministic Processes

## 1. The Claim

The claim under examination is one of the oldest and most consequential propositions in human intellectual history: that human beings possess genuine free will — a capacity for conscious, autonomous choice that operates independently of, or at least is not fully reducible to, deterministic physical processes. In its strongest formulation, sometimes called *libertarian free will*, this holds that when a person deliberates and chooses, there is a metaphysically real moment at which the outcome is not fixed by prior causes, and the agent could have done otherwise in exactly the same physical circumstances.

This is not a fringe claim. It is the default assumption embedded in legal systems worldwide, in religious doctrines across traditions, in everyday moral discourse, and in the phenomenology of ordinary experience. When someone says "I chose to do X," the folk concept presupposes that the choosing was not merely the deterministic output of antecedent neural states — that the agent was the genuine author of the action in some irreducible sense.

The claim matters because nearly everything in moral responsibility, criminal justice, praise, blame, and self-conception rests on it. If free will is illusory, as some neuroscientists and philosophers have argued, the implications cascade through ethics, law, and public policy. If it is real in any robust sense, we need to understand how a physical brain generates it.

What makes this claim epistemologically loaded — and what places it permanently outside the reach of conventional fact-checking — is that its truth conditions are not empirically specifiable in any straightforward way. Unlike a claim about vaccine efficacy or election fraud, "humans have free will" cannot be resolved by pointing to a dataset. The disagreement is not about facts but about what kind of fact would count as an answer.

## 2. What's Actually True

Several things are empirically established, even if the ultimate question remains open.

**The brain makes decisions before conscious awareness reports them.** This has been demonstrated repeatedly since the landmark work of Libet et al. (1983, *Brain*), who showed that a cortical readiness potential (Bereitschaftspotential, or RP) precedes the reported moment of conscious intention to act by approximately 350–500 milliseconds. Participants instructed to flex their wrist "whenever they felt like it" showed measurable neural preparation well before they reported the urge to move. This finding has been replicated and extended by Soon et al. (2008, *Nature Neuroscience*), whose fMRI paradigm detected patterns of brain activity in frontopolar and parietal cortex that predicted a participant's choice of which button to press up to 7–10 seconds before the person reported awareness of having decided. Fried et al. (2011, *Neuron*) provided single-unit recording evidence from the supplementary motor area (SMA), showing that progressive recruitment of neurons preceded reported conscious will by several hundred milliseconds, with prediction accuracy rising sharply in the final 500ms before reported intention.

**Conscious will can be dissociated from action.** Daniel Wegner's extensive research program, synthesised in *The Illusion of Conscious Will* (Wegner, 2002, MIT Press), demonstrated that the sense of willing an action and the causal production of that action are distinct processes. Through experiments with "helping hands," ideomotor suggestion, and other paradigms, Wegner showed that people reliably experience authorship of actions they did not cause and fail to experience authorship of actions they did cause. The feeling of willing is, in Wegner's framework, an inference — a post-hoc interpretation the brain constructs when the thought of an action and the action itself co-occur with appropriate timing and exclusivity.

**Determinism at the macro scale is well-supported but not absolute.** Classical physics is deterministic; given complete knowledge of initial conditions, future states are in principle predictable. But quantum mechanics introduces genuine indeterminacy at the subatomic level. Whether quantum indeterminacy "scales up" to affect neural processes in any way relevant to decision-making is deeply contested. Robert Kane (1996, *The Significance of Free Will*, Oxford University Press) has been the most prominent philosopher to argue that quantum indeterminacy in neural networks during moments of effort and deliberation could provide the metaphysical "gap" that libertarian free will requires. Most neuroscientists and physicists regard this as speculative, since thermal noise at biological temperatures is generally thought to wash out quantum coherence effects in neural tissue. But the question is not settled.

**Compatibilist free will is coherent and may be all that matters.** A long tradition in philosophy — running from Hume through Frankfurt (1971, *The Journal of Philosophy*) and Dennett (2003, *Freedom Evolves*, Viking) — argues that the kind of free will worth wanting is not independence from causation but rather a specific kind of causal structure. On Frankfurt's hierarchical mesh theory, an agent acts freely when their first-order desires (wanting to do X) are endorsed by their second-order desires (wanting to want to do X). A drug addict who wants heroin but wishes they didn't want it acts unfreely; a person who desires coffee and is content with that desire acts freely. The key move is that freedom is not about escaping the causal order but about the internal architecture of motivation. Dennett extends this by arguing that the capacity for reflective self-control, information-gathering, and deliberation — capacities that evolved and that vary across agents — is what constitutes freedom in any sense relevant to moral responsibility.

**The experience of free will is real as a phenomenon.** Whatever the metaphysics, human beings universally report the experience of deliberation, choice, and authorship. P.F. Strawson's influential work on reactive attitudes (Strawson, 1962, *Proceedings of the British Academy*) argued that practices of holding people responsible — feeling resentment, gratitude, moral indignation — are constitutive of human social life and cannot coherently be abandoned simply because determinism might be true. Even Derk Pereboom, who defends *hard incompatibilism* — the view that free will is incompatible with both determinism and indeterminism and therefore does not exist (Pereboom, 2001, *Living Without Free Will*, Cambridge University Press) — acknowledges that abandoning the concept requires significant revisions to moral practice, not merely an intellectual adjustment.

## 3. The Libet Problem and Its Discontents

The Libet experiment has dominated popular discourse about free will for four decades, and its standard framing has become a kind of cultural shorthand: "neuroscience has shown that our decisions are made unconsciously before we're aware of them, therefore free will is an illusion." This framing is, at best, a drastic oversimplification. At worst, it is a category error that confuses a finding about the timing of neural preparation with a conclusion about the metaphysics of agency.

The standard narrative runs as follows. Libet et al. (1983, *Brain*) showed that the readiness potential — a slow negative cortical potential originating primarily in the SMA and pre-SMA — begins ramping up roughly 550ms before a voluntary action, but the subject's conscious "urge" to act (as self-timed via a clock-watching method called the Libet clock or "W-time") occurs only about 200ms before the action. The RP, therefore, precedes conscious awareness by approximately 350ms. The conclusion drawn: the brain has already "decided" before you are aware of deciding. The extension: conscious will is epiphenomenal.

Soon et al. (2008, *Nature Neuroscience*) appeared to intensify the problem dramatically. Using multivariate pattern analysis on fMRI data, they showed that activity patterns in frontopolar cortex and precuneus could predict which of two buttons a participant would press up to 7–10 seconds before the participant reported awareness of having chosen. The prediction accuracy was modest — approximately 60% versus the 50% chance level — but the temporal gap was striking. John-Dylan Haynes, the senior author, built an influential research program around this paradigm, extending it to more complex decision contexts and arguing that the neural antecedents of decisions are unconscious and temporally remote from awareness.

Fried et al. (2011, *Neuron*) added single-neuron resolution, recording directly from human SMA during neurosurgical procedures. They found a progressive buildup of firing rates in a population of neurons that preceded the reported time of conscious intention, with a threshold-crossing event occurring roughly 500ms before reported awareness. The data seemed to tell a consistent story across scales: EEG, fMRI, and single-unit recordings all pointed to unconscious neural preparation preceding conscious will.

**The epistemic novelty: Schurger's stochastic accumulator reframe.**

Here is where the standard narrative breaks down, and where the Truth Vault must introduce a correction that most popular accounts of the Libet paradigm still miss.

Aaron Schurger, Jacobo Sitt, and Stanislas Dehaene (Schurger et al., 2012, *PNAS*) proposed a fundamentally different interpretation of the readiness potential. Their argument, grounded in computational modelling and confirmed by experimental data, is that the RP does not reflect a specific unconscious "decision" to move. Instead, it reflects the ongoing stochastic fluctuation of neural activity in motor preparation circuits — a noisy, spontaneous oscillation that occasionally crosses a threshold, at which point a movement is initiated.

In their model, participants in self-paced movement tasks are not deciding "when to move" in any deep sense. They are waiting for an instruction (move whenever you feel like it) and their motor system is undergoing continuous sub-threshold fluctuations. Sometimes these fluctuations, driven by neural noise, happen to ramp upward and cross the action threshold. The "decision" is better understood as the crossing of a stochastic threshold by a random walk — not as the output of an unconscious deliberative process.

This reframe is devastating to the standard Libet narrative for a precise reason: if the RP is noise-driven accumulation rather than unconscious decision-making, then it is not evidence *against* free will at all. It is evidence about the dynamics of motor initiation in a task that, crucially, involves no meaningful choice. The Libet paradigm asks people to flick their wrist whenever they feel like it — there is no competing option, no deliberation, no stakes. The very design of the experiment strips away everything that makes decision-making feel like decision-making. It then discovers that what remains — the bare initiation of a meaningless movement — looks like a noisy threshold crossing. This should not be surprising, and it should not be used to draw conclusions about the kind of reflective, deliberative agency that compatibilists (and most people) care about.

Schurger et al.'s model also explains a puzzle that the standard narrative could not: why the onset time of the RP is so variable across trials. If the RP reflected a discrete unconscious decision, its onset should be relatively consistent. But it is not — it varies widely, exactly as a stochastic process would predict. The model quantitatively accounts for the distribution of RP onset times, lending it strong empirical support.

**The deeper question this reveals.** Once the measurement-level argument against free will is dissolved — once we recognise that the Libet paradigm does not show what popular accounts claim it shows — the philosophical question snaps back into focus. The empirical neuroscience of motor timing neither supports nor refutes free will. It is orthogonal to the real issue, which is whether the kind of reflective, higher-order endorsement described by Frankfurt (1971, *The Journal of Philosophy*), the evolutionary and information-theoretic account developed by Dennett (2003, *Freedom Evolves*, Viking), or the agent-causal libertarianism defended by Kane (1996, *The Significance of Free Will*, Oxford University Press) is the right framework for understanding human agency.

The question of whether compatibilist free will — the capacity for reflective self-governance, reasons-responsiveness, and hierarchical endorsement of one's own motivational states — is what actually matters for moral responsibility is not a neuroscience question. It is a philosophical question about what we mean by "free" and what we need from the concept to sustain practices of praise, blame, and accountability. Strawson's (1962, *Proceedings of the British Academy*) argument that reactive attitudes are constitutive of moral life, not optional add-ons that can be discarded if determinism turns out to be true, remains powerful precisely because it locates the question at the right level — the level of interpersonal recognition, not the level of cortical potentials.

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Undecidable by empirical methods alone. The claim "humans possess free will independent of deterministic processes" is not the kind of proposition that admits of straightforward empirical confirmation or refutation. Its truth conditions depend on contested definitions of "free will," "independence," and "deterministic processes," each of which is the subject of active philosophical debate with centuries of accumulated argument.

**Confidence:** Low confidence in any extreme position. The neuroscientific evidence does not establish that free will is illusory, contrary to popular accounts. Nor does it establish that libertarian free will exists. The strongest empirical finding — that neural preparation precedes conscious awareness of intention — has been substantially recontextualised by Schurger et al. (2012, *PNAS*), and its implications for free will are far less clear than the standard narrative suggests.

**Epistemic Basis:** The claim sits at the intersection of empirical neuroscience (which studies the timing and neural substrates of decision-making), philosophy of mind (which analyses the conceptual structure of agency, authorship, and moral responsibility), and fundamental physics (which determines whether the universe is deterministic at every level). No single discipline can adjudicate it. The relevant empirical findings are robust but their philosophical interpretation is contested. The philosophical frameworks are sophisticated but empirically underdetermined.

**Phenomenological Status:** Free will is real as a phenomenological datum. Human beings universally experience deliberation, the weighing of reasons, the sense of authorship over actions, and the feeling that they could have done otherwise. This experience is not refuted by showing that neural activity precedes its conscious report — the experiential reality of deliberation and choice is not contingent on its temporal priority over subpersonal neural processes.

**Mechanistic Status:** No known physical mechanism has been identified that would support libertarian free will — that is, a capacity for choices that are neither determined nor random. Kane's (1996, Oxford University Press) quantum indeterminacy proposal remains speculative. Compatibilist free will — freedom understood as reflective self-governance, reasons-responsiveness, and hierarchical endorsement — is mechanistically plausible and consistent with everything known about neural architecture, but it does not satisfy the folk conception of "independence from deterministic processes."

**Folk Wisdom Value:** High. The folk concept of free will, while philosophically unsophisticated, tracks something real about human cognitive architecture: the capacity for deliberation, prospective simulation, impulse inhibition, and higher-order evaluation of one's own desires. Whether this capacity constitutes "real" free will depends on which philosophical framework one adopts, but the phenomena the folk concept points to are genuine and functionally important.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The free will debate is not merely an academic exercise. Its stakes are practical, immediate, and high.

**Criminal justice.** Legal systems worldwide presuppose that defendants could have chosen otherwise — the concept of *mens rea* (guilty mind) assumes a capacity for voluntary, conscious choice. If libertarian free will is illusory, the retributive justification for punishment collapses. Pereboom (2001, *Living Without Free Will*, Cambridge University Press) argues explicitly for this conclusion: if no one is truly responsible for their actions, punishment can only be justified on consequentialist grounds (deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation), not on the grounds that the offender "deserved" it. This is not a hypothetical concern — defence attorneys have already begun citing neuroscience findings in mitigation arguments, and courts are grappling with how to interpret brain imaging evidence.

**Public health and self-conception.** Studies have shown that when people are primed to disbelieve in free will, they become more likely to cheat, less likely to help others, and more fatalistic about their own behaviour (Vohs and Schooler, 2008, *Psychological Science*). Whether or not free will is "real" in a metaphysical sense, belief in it appears to be functionally important for prosocial behaviour and self-regulation. This raises a disturbing possibility: even if the scientific evidence eventually pointed clearly toward hard determinism, communicating that conclusion publicly could be actively harmful.

**Artificial intelligence.** As AI systems become more sophisticated, the question of whether and how to attribute agency, responsibility, and autonomy to non-biological systems becomes urgent. If free will is understood compatibilistically — as a set of functional capacities (deliberation, reasons-responsiveness, self-monitoring) rather than as a metaphysical property — then the question of whether advanced AI systems possess a form of free will is not absurd. This has implications for liability, rights, and governance frameworks that are already being debated in policy circles.

**The scientific self-image.** The free will debate is, at bottom, a question about what kind of beings we are. Are we sophisticated biological machines whose every thought and action is the inevitable output of prior physical states? Are we agents with genuine causal powers that cannot be reduced to subpersonal mechanisms? Or — as the compatibilists argue — is the dichotomy itself confused, and is the real question not whether we escape causation but whether the causal structure that constitutes us is of the right kind to deserve the name "freedom"? How a culture answers this question shapes its art, its politics, its institutions, and its self-understanding.

The neuroscience will continue to advance. Future experiments with higher temporal and spatial resolution, combined with more ecologically valid decision paradigms (moving beyond wrist-flicking to genuine dilemmas), may reveal new features of the neural architecture of decision-making. But they will not settle the free will debate, because the debate is not ultimately about neural timing. It is about what we owe each other and ourselves as agents navigating a world we did not choose to enter.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

They don't — and they can't, not cleanly.

The architecture of fact-checking — claim identification, source verification, evidence weighing, verdict assignment — is designed for empirically resolvable propositions. "Did this politician say X?" "Is this statistic accurate?" "Did this event happen?" These are questions with truth conditions that can be operationalised: you find the transcript, check the dataset, verify the record.

The free will claim is categorically different. It is not that the evidence is insufficient (though it is underdetermined); it is that no conceivable evidence would settle the question as conventionally posed. The claim "humans possess free will independent of deterministic processes" contains at least three terms ("free will," "independent," "deterministic processes") whose definitions are themselves the subject of unresolved philosophical dispute. Fact-checking requires fixed definitions to evaluate against; here, the definitions are what's contested.

Snopes, to its credit, has historically avoided deeply philosophical claims, focusing on viral misinformation, urban legends, and political claims with verifiable truth conditions. The hypothetical Snopes page for this claim does not exist (the URL returns 404), and this is arguably the correct editorial decision. A "True" or "False" verdict would be epistemically irresponsible; an "Unproven" verdict, while technically accurate, risks implying that the question is merely awaiting better evidence when in fact it may be constitutively unanswerable by empirical methods alone.

The gap this exposes in fact-checking methodology is significant. As public discourse increasingly involves claims that are epistemologically loaded — claims about consciousness, moral realism, the nature of perception, the ontological status of mathematical objects — the binary or trinary verdict framework that serves fact-checkers well for empirical claims becomes inadequate. What is needed is a richer epistemic vocabulary: a way of marking claims as *contested at the conceptual level*, not merely *unresolved at the evidentiary level*. The EARC framework's "C" (Contested) designation, applied to this entry, is one attempt to provide exactly that distinction.

The fact-checking community would benefit from engaging more directly with philosophy of science and epistemology — not to become philosophers, but to develop more nuanced tools for handling the growing category of public claims that resist straightforward empirical adjudication. The free will question is the paradigm case, but it is not the only one. Claims about the hard problem of consciousness, the reality of moral facts, the interpretation of quantum mechanics, and the nature of mathematical truth all share the same structural feature: they are genuine intellectual questions where reasonable, well-informed people disagree not because they lack evidence but because they disagree about what would count as evidence.

Until fact-checking develops frameworks for this category, the most honest response to "Do humans have free will?" remains: *that depends on what you mean, and the disagreement about what we mean is the real question*.
