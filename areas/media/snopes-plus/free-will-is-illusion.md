# Is Free Will an Illusion?

## 1. The Claim

"Free will is an illusion." The sentence appears in popular science books, TED talks, Reddit threads, and increasingly in public discourse. It is often presented not as a philosophical position but as a scientific finding—something neuroscience has definitively established. The standard narrative goes roughly as follows: Benjamin Libet ran experiments in the 1980s and discovered that the brain initiates movement before the person is consciously aware of deciding to move. Therefore, consciousness is not the cause of our actions. Therefore, free will—the idea that we could have done otherwise—is an illusion.

This is one of the most consequential claims in contemporary public discourse about human nature. If true in its strong form, it has profound implications for criminal justice, moral responsibility, self-understanding, and the nature of deliberation. If it is contested, or if "free will" means something more nuanced than the popular version assumes, then the confident public claim deserves scrutiny.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

### The Libet Experiments

In 1983, Benjamin Libet and colleagues at UCSF published a study that became one of the most cited and debated experiments in neuroscience. Participants were instructed to flex their wrist whenever they felt like it and to report the position of a clock hand at the moment they first felt the urge to move. EEG recordings tracked a brain signal called the Bereitschaftspotential—the "readiness potential"—a slow negative-going wave in motor cortex that had been discovered in the 1960s by Kornhuber and Deecke.

Libet found that the readiness potential began, on average, about 550 milliseconds before the wrist movement. But participants reported their conscious intention to move only about 200 milliseconds before the movement. The implication appeared stark: the brain was "preparing" the movement more than a third of a second before the person consciously decided to move. The decision, it seemed, was made unconsciously, and consciousness was only informed afterward.

Libet himself was careful about his conclusions. He noted that participants could "veto" their movement up until about 200 milliseconds before execution—suggesting that while unconscious processes might initiate action, conscious volition could still intervene. He described this as a "conscious veto power" that preserved a role for free will. Many popular accounts of his work dropped this nuance.

### The Schurger Reinterpretation

In 2012, Aaron Schurger, Jacobo Sitaram, and Stanislas Dehaene published a paper in *PNAS* that fundamentally reframed the Libet finding. They proposed that the readiness potential is not a preparatory motor signal at all—it is the slow accumulation of spontaneous neural noise that occasionally crosses a threshold required to trigger movement.

In their model, participants in the Libet paradigm are instructed to move "whenever they feel like it" with no specific target. This creates an unusual decision context: there is no external signal to wait for, no optimal time to act, and no cost to acting early or late. In such a context, movement initiation would naturally be triggered by random fluctuations in neural activity crossing a threshold. The readiness potential is what that stochastic accumulation looks like in an EEG average.

If Schurger et al. are right, then the readiness potential tells us nothing about conscious decision-making. It is not "the brain deciding before the mind knows"—it is spontaneous neural noise reaching a firing threshold in an artificially unconstrained task context. The temporal precedence of the readiness potential over conscious intention is a statistical artifact of averaging across trials in a task specifically designed to produce spontaneous, uncued movements.

Subsequent research has refined and partly contested Schurger's model, but it significantly weakened the evidentiary force of Libet's original interpretation. The clean narrative—"neuroscience has proven you don't really decide anything"—rests on an interpretation of the readiness potential that is now disputed even among neuroscientists.

### The Philosophical Contest

Parallel to the neuroscience debate, philosophy has its own longstanding dispute about free will that is not resolved by brain scans in either direction.

**Hard determinism** holds that every event, including every human decision, is causally necessitated by prior events. Given the state of the universe at any moment and the laws of physics, everything that will happen is already determined. Because we could not have done otherwise, moral responsibility in its traditional sense is incoherent. We do not praise a boulder for falling or blame a virus for infecting—and on this view, we should not praise or blame humans either. Robert Sapolsky's 2023 book *Determined: A Science of Life Without Free Will* is the most thorough contemporary statement of this position, integrating genetics, neuroscience, hormonal biology, and developmental psychology to argue that every behavior is the product of factors entirely outside the agent's control.

**Compatibilism** holds that free will and determinism are not actually in conflict—that "free will" properly understood does not require the ability to violate causal laws, but rather the ability to act in accordance with one's own reasons, values, and deliberations without being coerced. On this view, the question is not whether your brain initiates movement before you consciously experience willing it—it is whether your actions flow from your character, your reasoning, and your intentions in a way that makes them *yours*. Daniel Dennett, Harry Frankfurt, Susan Wolf, and P.F. Strawson are among the most influential compatibilist philosophers. They argue that hard determinists are attacking a "libertarian" conception of free will—uncaused causation, ghostly mental intervention in physical processes—that most serious philosophers of mind have long since abandoned.

**Libertarian free will** (in the philosophical sense, unrelated to the political sense) holds that human agents genuinely transcend causal determinism in some way—through quantum indeterminacy, through consciousness acting as a non-physical causal agent, or through some other mechanism. Most philosophers and neuroscientists find this position difficult to defend empirically. But its complete refutation would require a level of mechanistic certainty about consciousness that current neuroscience does not possess.

### Where the Contest Actually Stands

The popular claim "free will is an illusion" conflates several separate questions:

1. **Is determinism true?** Probably yes at the macro-level, though quantum mechanics introduces genuine indeterminacy at the micro-level (whether this propagates to macro-level neural decisions is unknown and contested).

2. **Does determinism entail that free will is an illusion?** Only if you define "free will" as requiring libertarian uncaused choice. Compatibilists deny this entailment.

3. **Does neuroscience show that conscious deliberation doesn't cause behavior?** The Libet interpretation that seemed to show this has been substantially contested. Current neuroscience does not have a consensus view on the causal relationship between conscious intention and action.

4. **Should we revise our practices of moral responsibility?** This is a normative question that empirical neuroscience alone cannot answer—it depends on what we think moral responsibility is *for*.

Neither the neuroscience nor the philosophy has produced a verdict that would justify the confident popular claim. The question is genuinely open.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### The Appeal of Determinism

Hard determinism has a seductive simplicity. If everything is caused, then the agent is just a nexus of prior causes—genes, upbringing, neurons, hormones—and the idea that *you* decided anything in some deeper sense dissolves. This is, for many people, not frightening but liberating: it means that you cannot be blamed for your nature, that punishment for its own sake is irrational, and that the proper response to bad behavior is understanding and remediation, not judgment.

Sapolsky's framing in *Determined* is partly motivated by this compassionate impulse. If criminals are products of developmental trauma, poverty, and neurobiology, then punitive incarceration is not justice but cruelty. This is a compelling moral argument. But it requires the hard determinist conclusion, and that conclusion remains contested.

### The Pop-Science Pipeline

The Libet results spread through pop science precisely because they are dramatic and counterintuitive. "Your brain decides before you do" is a headline that writes itself. Neuroscientist Sam Harris's 2012 book *Free Will* made the Libet argument accessible to millions of readers. It was short, clear, and confident, and it cited genuine scientific research. What it did not adequately engage with was the compatibilist tradition or the Schurger reinterpretation (which appeared the same year).

Pop-science accounts of neuroscience consistently overstate certainty and understate contestation. The Libet finding became a "proof" rather than a disputed empirical result embedded in unresolved interpretive debates.

### The Cultural Moment

The determinism debate has also caught a cultural tailwind from a broader skepticism about agency. In an era of algorithmic curation, data-harvesting, and behavioral prediction markets, the claim that "we don't really choose" resonates with the feeling many people have of being nudged, manipulated, and controlled by systems they cannot see or resist. Free will skepticism has become, in some circles, the psychologically honest position—the view that refuses to flatter human beings with the illusion of self-determination.

This cultural resonance does not make the claim more true. But it helps explain why it spreads.

## 4. Verdict

**Contested. Neither confirmed nor refuted by current evidence.**

The claim "free will is an illusion" is a philosophical position, not a scientific finding. The neuroscientific evidence most commonly cited—the Libet readiness potential—has been significantly reinterpreted and no longer supports the strong conclusion it was once taken to establish. The philosophical question of whether determinism eliminates meaningful agency is actively debated between compatibilists and hard determinists, with both positions held by serious scholars.

What is true: the brain does a great deal of processing below the level of conscious awareness. Conscious deliberation is not the initiator of every action. Many of the factors that shape our behavior—genetics, early environment, neurochemistry—are not chosen by us. These are genuine and important empirical findings.

What is not established: that these findings eliminate the kind of agency that matters for practical deliberation, moral responsibility, or the causal efficacy of reasoning. The philosophical argument that they do depends on a definition of "free will" that most contemporary philosophers of mind do not accept.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### The Stakes for Justice

The policy implications of the free will debate are not merely academic. If hard determinism is right—if people genuinely cannot do otherwise given their neurobiology and environment—then the retributive rationale for criminal punishment (you deserve to suffer for what you chose to do) collapses entirely. What remains is consequentialist justification: incarceration is justified only insofar as it prevents future harm or enables rehabilitation.

This shift has advocates across the political spectrum. Sapolsky dedicates substantial portions of *Determined* to arguing for reform of the American criminal justice system along these lines. Neuroscientist David Eagleman has made similar arguments. The debate is not merely abstract: several jurisdictions have begun allowing neuroscientific evidence in sentencing proceedings, and courts have struggled with whether neurological evidence of reduced impulse control should mitigate criminal sentences.

The tension is this: if hard determinism is correct and we cannot justifiably punish, we may also have trouble justifiably praising, rewarding, or holding people to commitments. Compatibilists argue that our reactive attitudes—gratitude, resentment, admiration, indignation—are not eliminable from human social life and that a just system of responsibility can be grounded in them even under determinism. Hard determinists argue that reactive attitudes are themselves biological products of evolution and have no special normative authority.

### The Consciousness Problem

One reason this debate resists resolution is that it is entangled with the hard problem of consciousness—the question of why and how physical processes give rise to subjective experience. We do not have a satisfactory scientific account of how neural firing produces the felt experience of deliberation, intention, or choice. Until we do, claims about what consciousness "really does" in the causal chain—including claims that it does nothing—remain speculative.

The Libet paradigm measures neural correlates of intention. It does not measure consciousness itself. The inference from "the readiness potential precedes the reported intention" to "consciousness is causally irrelevant" requires assumptions about the relationship between neural activity and consciousness that are not established. Schurger's reinterpretation removed even the empirical step in this inference, but the philosophical gap would remain even if Libet's original interpretation were reinstated.

### What Compatibilism Offers

Compatibilism is often dismissed by hard determinists as definitional sleight of hand—changing the meaning of "free will" to rescue a concept from refutation. Dennett has spent decades pushing back on this charge. His argument, in brief, is that the "libertarian free will" targeted by determinists was never a coherent concept to begin with: the idea of an agent whose choices are genuinely uncaused is not a scientifically respectable notion that neuroscience has refuted, but rather a philosophical confusion that generates false problems.

On Dennett's view, what we actually care about when we care about free will is: Can I act on my reasons? Can I respond to new information and revise my behavior? Am I the kind of entity that can be reasoned with, held to account, and trusted with responsibility? These capacities are real, measurable, and not threatened by determinism.

Frankfurt's influential 1971 paper "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person" adds a further layer: what matters for moral responsibility is not whether our first-order desires are determined, but whether we can form second-order desires (desires about our desires) and whether our actions align with those. A person acts freely in the relevant sense when what they do reflects who they want to be, regardless of whether that want is itself determined.

These are not merely semantic maneuvers. They identify real properties of agents that matter for how we should treat each other, and they distinguish humans meaningfully from rocks, thermostats, and simple reflex arcs.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

### The Unusual Structure of This Claim

Most fact-checking addresses empirical claims: was this statistic accurate, did this event happen, did this person say this thing. "Free will is an illusion" is not straightforwardly an empirical claim—it is a philosophical conclusion that draws on empirical evidence. This puts it in an unusual category for fact-checkers, who may be better equipped to verify data than to adjudicate contested philosophical frameworks.

As a result, this specific claim is rarely fact-checked in the conventional sense. Science journalists have written critical pieces about overstated Libet interpretations, and philosophers have written popular rebuttals to Sapolsky and Harris. But neither Snopes nor PolitiFact nor similar organizations has issued a formal verdict.

### The Science Journalism Problem

The strongest corrective work has come from science journalists and philosophers writing for general audiences. Pieces in *The Atlantic*, *Aeon*, and *Quanta Magazine* have engaged seriously with the Schurger reinterpretation and the compatibilist tradition, offering more accurate accounts of the state of the debate than the pop-science books that created the consensus.

The problem is reach. *Free Will* by Sam Harris sold hundreds of thousands of copies. A *Quanta* article about the Schurger reinterpretation reached a fraction of that audience. The asymmetry of penetration between confident, simple claims and nuanced corrections is a structural feature of the information environment, not a fixable error by individual journalists.

### What an Adequate Fact-Check Would Look Like

An adequate treatment of this claim would need to accomplish the following:

1. **Distinguish the neuroscientific claim from the philosophical claim.** The Libet finding (contested as it is) addresses whether conscious intention precedes neural preparation—not whether determinism is true or whether agency is meaningful.

2. **Accurately report the state of the Libet evidence.** The Schurger reinterpretation is now more than a decade old and has not been refuted. Any treatment of the free will neuroscience debate should address it.

3. **Take compatibilism seriously.** Dismissing compatibilism as "changing the subject" requires engaging with Dennett, Frankfurt, and Wolf—not simply asserting that libertarian free will was the only thing anyone cared about.

4. **Be honest about uncertainty.** The consciousness problem is genuinely unsolved. We do not know how subjective experience relates to neural causation. Confident claims in either direction outrun the evidence.

5. **Identify the policy stakes.** The criminal justice implications are real and should be named. The claim is not merely academic.

The honest verdict is: contested, live, and consequential. Neither side has won. The popular "illusion" narrative overstates the scientific consensus and underrepresents the philosophical complexity. But the hard determinist position is held by serious scholars and is not obviously wrong. The intellectually honest position is to hold the question open.
