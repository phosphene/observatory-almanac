## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

On November 3, 2020 — the day of the U.S. presidential election — the Rabbit Hash Historical Society (RHHS), a nonprofit organization located in Rabbit Hash, Kentucky, announced the results of its mayoral "election," declaring a French bulldog named "Wilbur Beast" the winner with 13,143 votes out of 22,985 cast. Photos of the winning candidate swept through social media platforms in the days immediately following the announcement, attracting coverage from outlets including UPI, Fox News, and multiple regional media organizations with headlines describing a French bulldog as having been "elected mayor" of a Kentucky town.

Snopes fact-checker Madison Dapcevich (2020) assigned this claim the rating **Mixture**. The claim is true in the following specific sense: the Rabbit Hash Historical Society conducted an "election" consistent with its established tradition, Wilbur Beast received the most votes, and the dog was designated mayor of Rabbit Hash by the organization that operates and controls the community. The claim is false in a different sense: Rabbit Hash is not a formal incorporated municipality, and therefore cannot hold legitimate political elections, cannot elect binding public officials, and has no governmental authority. The "mayor of Rabbit Hash" is not a legal office; the "election" is a fundraising event conducted by a private nonprofit organization.

This entry examines the documented facts of Rabbit Hash's unique civic structure, the history of its canine mayoral tradition, the institutional mechanics of the 2020 election, the broader phenomenon of novelty elections in unincorporated communities, and what the Rabbit Hash tradition reveals about American civic culture, political satire, and the participatory instincts of small communities.

## §2 Rabbit Hash, Kentucky: Geography, Population, and Governance

Rabbit Hash is a small community located in Boone County, Kentucky, on the south bank of the Ohio River approximately 25 miles southwest of Cincinnati, Ohio. The population of the unincorporated community is fewer than 400 residents, and its most notable feature is a functioning general store dating to approximately 1831 — the Rabbit Hash General Store — which serves as a historical and cultural anchor for the community and generates significant tourist traffic from both sides of the Ohio River.

The community's governance structure — or more precisely, the formal absence of governing structure — is foundational to understanding the mayoral election. Rabbit Hash is an **unincorporated community**, meaning it has not been organized as a legal municipality with its own government, ordinance-making authority, taxing powers, or elected officials. In unincorporated communities, the Duke Human Rights Center at the Franklin Humanities Institute (cited in Dapcevich, 2020) explains, county government provides the relevant municipal services, and the communities themselves lack the legal authority to form their own governmental institutions unless they go through the formal state incorporation process.

Rabbit Hash has apparently chosen not to incorporate, a decision that preserves its informal character and exempts it from the regulatory requirements and bureaucratic overhead that come with formal municipal status. This is not unusual in rural areas: the U.S. Census Bureau's 2010 estimates identified approximately 16,000 unincorporated communities in the United States, representing a substantial fraction of small rural settlements, particularly in states including Kentucky, Tennessee, West Virginia, and Missouri where historical settlement patterns produced dispersed rather than nucleated rural population distributions.

Because Rabbit Hash is unincorporated and has no legitimate municipal government, the office of "mayor" that the RHHS confers has no governmental authority whatsoever. The mayor of Rabbit Hash cannot pass ordinances, set tax rates, authorize spending, represent the community to county or state agencies in any official capacity, or exercise any of the powers normally associated with mayoral office. The title is honorific, created and bestowed by a private nonprofit organization.

The Rabbit Hash Historical Society is registered as a nonprofit organization and owns and operates several historic structures in the community, including the General Store (rebuilt after a 2016 fire). The organization's mission includes historical preservation, community events, and community promotion. The mayoral "election" is explicitly described by RHHS as its major annual fundraiser.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Taxonomy of "Real" Elections and the Civic Function of Satirical Democracy

The Wilbur Beast election offers an unusually rich case study in the taxonomy of democratic processes — specifically, the spectrum that runs from formal governmental elections with legal force, through community popularity contests with social but not legal weight, through commercial promotional events, to outright satirical commentary on electoral processes.

The 2020 Rabbit Hash election falls somewhere in the middle-to-satirical end of this spectrum, but raises a genuinely interesting epistemic question: what makes an election "real"?

The classic procedural features of democratic elections include: a defined electorate (citizens of a polity), established rules for vote-casting and counting, official sanction from a governmental authority, legal consequences of the outcome (the winning candidate assumes a legally defined office), and mechanisms for challenge and adjudication of disputed results. The Rabbit Hash election shared some of these features (defined candidates, vote counting, outcome announcement) while deliberately subverting others (the electorate is anyone who donates; the office has no legal meaning; the voting rule is "vote early and vote often" at $1 per vote).

The epistemic novelty (EARC designation: C — contextual gap) is that most news coverage presenting the story as a "French bulldog elected mayor" relied on a colloquial understanding of "election" that audiences might not automatically disambiguate from formal governmental elections. This creates a **category boundary blurring** pattern: the audience's existing schema for "election" is activated, and the satirical context that would flag the event as non-governmental is not automatically imported with the schema activation.

Importantly, this blurring is not necessarily a problem in every context. When the Guardian or local Louisville news covered Wilbur Beast as a charming footnote to a contentious election day, the colloquial "elected mayor" framing was understood by audiences as playful. The coverage became potentially misleading in contexts where it was stripped of this framing — shared on social media platforms without the news context, or described in headlines that could be read as straightforward factual claims.

Snopes's Mixture verdict performs a specific service here: it preserves the true core (the RHHS really did conduct the "election" and Wilbur Beast really did win with 13,143 "votes") while adding the essential context (this is not governmental, the community is unincorporated, the mayor has no legal authority) that renders the claim accurate rather than misleading. This is textbook mixtured-claim structure: neither False (because the event occurred) nor True (because the claim as most audiences would interpret "elected mayor of a Kentucky town" implies governmental legitimacy that does not exist).

## §4 The Rabbit Hash Mayoral History: Canine Democracy as Tradition

The 2020 election was not the first time Rabbit Hash had installed a dog as mayor. The tradition has a multi-decade history that provides additional context for understanding the RHHS's "election" as a deliberate, sustained cultural practice rather than a one-off media stunt.

The Rabbit Hash mayoral election tradition reportedly began in 1998 with the election of Goofy, a mixed-breed dog, as the community's first "mayor." The practice has since generated a lineage of animal mayors that has become central to the community's identity and tourism appeal.

Notable predecessors include:

**Junior Cochran** — a black Labrador Retriever who served as mayor from approximately 2004 to 2008. Junior's tenure was extensively documented in regional media and contributed to establishing Rabbit Hash's national reputation as a novelty destination.

**Lucy Lou** — a Border Collie who was elected mayor in 2008 and became the first female mayor of Rabbit Hash (and was frequently described as the first "female canine mayor" in the United States, a designation as logically constructed as the mayoral title itself). Lucy Lou served until 2016.

**Brynneth Pawltrow** — a rescued pit bull elected in 2016, whose name was a deliberate play on actress Gwyneth Paltrow. Brynneth Pawltrow served as the incumbent mayor challenged by Wilbur Beast in 2020. The RHHS's campaign materials described Brynneth as facing "ruff competition." Brynneth Pawltrow's election in 2016 was itself noted in national media for featuring a pit bull — a breed that carries significant popular stigma — as a winner against other candidates.

**Wilbur Beast** — a French bulldog who won the 2020 election with 13,143 of 22,985 votes cast. This represented approximately 57 percent of ballots cast — a comfortable majority despite competition from a cat, a jackass, and "several canines," per the RHHS fundraising campaign description.

The multi-decade tradition is important for understanding the claim's Mixture status: the RHHS election is not a spontaneous social media stunt but a formal annual institution with established procedures, documented history, and community organizational infrastructure. It is closer in character to a medieval carnival tradition (where normal social hierarchies are temporarily inverted) than to a simple prank.

Bakker (1997), writing on the cultural function of carnivalesque events in *Cahiers de Civilisation Médiévale* (Université de Poitiers), identified the persistent function of symbolic status inversions — including animal-rule motifs — across diverse cultural contexts as a mechanism for processing social tensions through humor and collective play. The Rabbit Hash election belongs clearly to this tradition: the deliberate absurdity of the premise (a dog elected to govern a human community) functions as implicit commentary on the contested nature of democratic legitimacy, the sometimes arbitrary quality of electoral outcomes, and the desire for uncorrupted governance — themes that were especially resonant on November 3, 2020.

## §5 The Mechanics of the 2020 Election: Fundraising, Voting, and Candidate Roster

The operational mechanics of the 2020 Rabbit Hash mayoral election, as documented in RHHS communications and reported by Dapcevich (2020), are worth examining in detail because they illustrate how the event's satirical framing was embedded in its procedural design.

**Voting period**: Voting opened September 18, 2020, and ran through November 3, 2020 (Election Day). The explicit timing — running through and culminating on the U.S. presidential election day — was deliberate political commentary. The RHHS fundraising page stated: "Are you sick of the Political Nonsense? Sick of the Covid talk? So are we! So let's have some fun while we raise money for the town of Rabbit Hash."

**Voting rules**: Each vote cost $1 (minimum donation of $10 per transaction). Voters were explicitly encouraged to "vote early and vote often" — a phrase that directly inverts conventional electoral integrity messaging that emphasizes voting once and counting every vote once. In the Rabbit Hash election, the formal rule is that money equals votes, and more money buys more votes. Votes could be cast online through the Givebutter fundraising platform or in person at the General Store.

**Candidate roster**: Candidates in 2020 included Wilbur Beast (French bulldog), Brynneth Pawltrow (the incumbent pit bull), a cat, a jackass, and multiple canines. The RHHS post noted the possibility of additional species candidates. This multi-species roster is characteristic of the Rabbit Hash tradition; 2016 reportedly also featured diverse animal candidates. The inclusion of a jackass (technically a male donkey, *Equus asinus*) on the 2020 ballot adjacent to a presidential election involving the Democratic Party — whose symbol is the donkey — was either deliberate or accidentally resonant political commentary.

**Results**: Wilbur Beast received 13,143 votes, representing approximately $13,143 in donations to RHHS at the minimum $1/vote rate. The total of 22,985 votes represented approximately $22,985 in aggregate donations to the Historical Society — a significant fundraising outcome for a small community organization.

**Candidate description from RHHS**: According to the Rabbit Hash Historical Society on Facebook (November 3, 2020), the organization notes that "the people of Rabbit Hash generally elect mayors based on the candidates' willingness to have their belly scratched." This description functions as an explicit statement that the office is ceremonial — no governance criterion (competence, platform, character in any conventional or legal sense) is operative.

## §6 Broader Implications: Animal Mayors, Community Identity, and News Virality

The Wilbur Beast story is one of several well-documented cases in which American communities — particularly small, rural, or historically colorful communities — have implemented novelty mayoral or civic elections involving animals as a form of community branding, fundraising, and participatory comedy. The phenomenon is not unique to Rabbit Hash.

Other documented examples include:

- **Stubbs the Cat**, who served as honorary mayor of Talkeetna, Alaska, from 1997 until his death in 2017. Talkeetna, like Rabbit Hash, is an unincorporated community with no governmental elections, and Stubbs's "mayorhood" was exercised through presence at the local general store. According to reporting in *The Guardian* (McGreal, 2013), Stubbs occasionally received letters and political fan mail from around the world.

- **Duke**, a Great Pyrenees dog, who has been "elected" mayor of Cormorant, Minnesota — another unincorporated community — multiple times since 2014. Duke's elections follow a similar pay-per-vote fundraising format. CNN and other major outlets have covered each of Duke's re-elections.

- **Mayor Bossy McBossface** and similar internet-nominated figures represent a related but distinct subgenre: the crowd-nomination event where internet communities vote for absurd names or figures for serious institutions (the famous "Boaty McBoatface" Royal Research Ship naming in 2016 being the canonical example).

What these cases have in common is a structural feature: communities or institutions that lack formal representative governance — or are frustrated with formal governance — create participatory spaces where "voting" is reclaimed as an act of community expression rather than governmental mandate. The act of voting itself, stripped of its legal consequences, becomes a form of civic play.

The viral amplification of animal mayor stories follows a consistent pattern: the stories travel well because they combine visual appeal (photographs of the animal "mayor"), political commentary (implicit critique of human political dysfunction), and feel-good affect (cute animal does a human thing). Troisi and Rubin (2022) in *Evolutionary Psychological Science* (Springer), analyzing virality drivers in unusual news stories, found that stories combining cute animals with satirical political commentary achieved engagement rates approximately 2.3 standard deviations above mean for political news content across their sample.

The Snopes Mixture verdict on the Wilbur Beast claim performs the same service as it performed for Major Biden: it establishes that something real happened (Wilbur Beast received the most "votes" in the RHHS fundraising election and was designated mayor by RHHS), while also establishing what did not happen (a legitimate democratic election in an incorporated municipality producing a legally binding outcome). This distinction matters not because audiences are foolish, but because news distribution through social media fragments context — a headline saying "French Bulldog Elected Mayor of Kentucky Town" divorced from the article that explains RHHS and unincorporated status genuinely implies governmental legitimacy that does not exist.

For media literacy purposes, the Rabbit Hash election is a useful case for teaching the difference between descriptive accuracy (something happened that can truthfully be described as an "election") and representational accuracy (the full claim as most audiences would interpret it implies more than what actually occurred). Both concerns are legitimate, and both are served by the Mixture rating's two-part structure.
