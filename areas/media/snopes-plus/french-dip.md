# French Dip Sandwich Origins

## §1 — Claim and the Nature of the Dispute

The specific claim Snopes examines is that the French dip sandwich was invented to placate a restaurant patron who complained about stale bread — in other words, that the dish emerged from a complaint-resolution interaction rather than from deliberate culinary innovation or accident. Snopes rates this Undetermined, a verdict that reflects a more interesting epistemic situation than simple falsity: unlike most food origin myths that can be dismissed as late fabrications, the French dip dispute pits two documented rival claimants against each other, each with institutional continuity, physical premises still in operation, and historical records going back over a century.

The two restaurants at the center of the dispute are both located in Los Angeles and both opened in 1908. Philippe the Original (commonly called Philippe's or Philippe the Original) is located in downtown Los Angeles near Union Station. Cole's Pacific Electric Buffet (also known as Cole's) is also in downtown Los Angeles, a few blocks from Philippe's, and claims to be the oldest bar in Los Angeles. Both locations were operating continuously through the twentieth century and remain in business (under their respective historical names) in the 2020s.

David Mikkelson authored the Snopes piece, published March 8, 2009, and his assessment situates the dispute within a broader observation about culinary origin myths: many such claims are unfalsifiable not because the events did not occur but because the documentary record is sparse, secondhand, and institutionally motivated. Restaurants have obvious financial and reputational incentives to claim primacy over a famous dish, and those incentives shape narrative construction in ways that can introduce distortions even when the core events were real.

## §2 — The Philippe's Account and Its Internal Contradictions

Philippe's founding legend has two main variants, and both differ from the account that founder Philippe Mathieu gave in his own words in 1951.

**Variant A (Accident):** A server or chef accidentally dropped a dry roast beef sandwich into a pan of meat drippings. The customer liked the result and the preparation became a standard offering.

**Variant B (Stale Bread Complaint):** Founder Philippe "Frenchy" Mathieu deliberately immersed a sandwich's bread component into meat juice to placate a customer who complained that the roll was stale. In the version recounted by Mathieu's grandson Philippe Guilhem, the customer was a fireman who complained on a Monday about a leftover-from-the-weekend roll. Guilhem's recollection is notably colorful: "My grandfather was a thrifty person. He said, 'Give me the damn thing back.' He dipped it in the juices and said, 'You happy now?'"

However, the 1951 account by Mathieu himself — the closest thing to primary-source testimony — differs significantly from both of these variants. In Mathieu's own telling, the French dip emerged organically from customer requests without any incident of complaint or accident. A policeman first asked Mathieu to make a sandwich with French bread and roast pork. Later, a customer noticed gravy residue in the bottom of a roasting pan and asked to have one side of his roll dipped in it. Mathieu did so; other customers immediately requested the same. He then scaled up: "The next day I made a gallon of the gravy — and still we ran out of it!"

This primary account is notable for several reasons. First, Mathieu specifically describes the original sandwich as containing pork and gravy, not the roast beef and beef drippings that constitute the canonical version served at Philippe's today, and that are central to most versions of the legend. Second, there is no complaint about stale bread, no act of service recovery, and no accident. Third, Mathieu frames the innovation as a customer's suggestion that he facilitated — a collaborative, customer-driven invention rather than a unilateral creative act.

The stale-bread-complaint specific claim that Snopes fact-checks is therefore directly undercut by the founder's own 1951 testimony. The grandson's variant — which is more colorful and more congruent with the "service recovery under pressure" narrative — appears to be a later elaboration or conflation with the Cole's account, which also involves a patron complaint.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Convergent Legend Problem

The most intellectually interesting dimension of this dispute is not which restaurant invented the French dip — it may genuinely be unknowable — but rather the structural similarity between the two competing founding myths and what this similarity implies about how culinary origin narratives are constructed.

Cole's account, per the Snopes piece, holds that the French dip was invented in 1908 (ten years before the Philippe's date usually cited) when a customer with sore gums asked for his sandwich to be "lowered into the juice" because the French roll was too crunchy to eat comfortably. Sympathetic chef complied, word spread, and a classic was born.

What is remarkable — and epistemically significant — is that when Cole's owner was asked about this story in 1997, he offered an explanation very close to Philippe's accident narrative rather than his own establishment's sore-gums account. He described a police officer coming in to rest, and Mathieu accidentally dropping the top part of the roll into the juice container.

This convergence — each restaurant's spokespersons occasionally telling the other restaurant's founding story — suggests that both narratives have achieved sufficient cultural circulation that they are loosely attached to "French dip origin story" as a concept, and specific institutional attribution becomes secondary. Cultural anthropologists describe this as mythological drift: when a founding narrative circulates widely enough, its specific anchoring details become mobile, attaching and detaching from particular institutions as the narrative diffuses through popular culture.

Folklorist Jan Harold Brunvand's foundational work on urban legends (*The Vanishing Hitchhiker: American Urban Legends and Their Meaning*, 1981, W.W. Norton) describes this structural property: legends acquire canonical elements — the accident, the complaint, the skeptical customer who becomes a convert, the immediate viral spread — that serve the moral economy of the story, and these elements travel across institutional attributions because the story-form is the primary carrier of social meaning, not the specific location. The French dip "origin" exhibits all four Brunvand-canonical elements: accidental or emergency creation, taste-conversion of the initially reluctant, immediate viral demand, institution-wide adoption.

Priscilla Ferguson's sociological analysis of culinary nationalism (*Accounting for Taste: The Triumph of French Cuisine*, 2004, University of Chicago Press) offers a complementary lens: iconic dishes frequently require founding myths not because the dish itself demands explanation but because institutional identity — what it means to be "the original" — is commercially valorized in ways that make the myth economically necessary.

## §4 — Why "French" Dip? The Naming Question

A secondary contested question the Snopes piece touches on indirectly is the naming of the sandwich itself. The name "French dip" is linguistically ambiguous. It might refer to:

1. The French bread roll on which the sandwich is served.
2. The founder Philippe "Frenchy" Mathieu's French origins (Mathieu was French-born, reportedly from Aix-en-Provence).
3. A culinary process of "dipping" in the style of French au jus preparations.

None of these explanations is definitively documented in any primary source contemporaneous with the sandwich's invention. The Philippe's establishment has historically promoted the "Frenchy Mathieu" etymology. Food historians including Jan Whitaker (*Service and Style: How the American Restaurant Transformed American Culture*, 2020, University of Missouri Press) note that the term "French" applied to bread in American culinary usage had developed by the late 19th century as a descriptor for elongated crusty loaves — so the name may simply refer to the bread type, making the Mathieu-personal-connection etymology a secondary invention.

This naming ambiguity is itself a manifestation of the broader epistemological problem: the early history of popular American food is poorly documented because the food was ordinary. Hamburgers, hot dogs, French dips, and club sandwiches were served to working-class and lower-middle-class patrons at lunch counters and delis, not the high-end establishments where menus were printed and preserved in institutional archives. The documentation that does exist — newspaper food columns, letters to the editor, occasional restaurant reviews — is fragmentary and rarely contemporaneous with the moment of innovation.

## §5 — The Cole's Case and the 1908 Anchor

Cole's claim of a 1908 date for the French dip's invention antedates the Philippe's timeline by a decade if we accept the accident-over-stale-bread narrative for Philippe's, which usually places the event around 1918. The 1908 anchor is significant because it would coincide with the founding year of both establishments, making the French dip coeval with both restaurants' opening — a symmetry that is either genuinely historical or narratively convenient.

No contemporaneous documentation — a newspaper account, a menu from that era, a diner's journal entry — has been publicly produced to confirm either 1908 or 1918 as the date of the French dip's invention. The earliest external documentation of the French dip as a named menu item has been the subject of food-history investigation but has proved elusive. Sharon Tyler Herbst's *The New Food Lover's Companion* (4th ed., 2007, Barron's Educational Series) notes the disputed origin without resolving it. Robert Danhi and Steve Staudigl's work on regional American sandwiches similarly treats the dispute as culturally unresolved.

The CFIA case for 1908 also rests partly on Cole's claim to be the oldest bar in Los Angeles, a claim that has itself been disputed by historians of the city. Rick Wartzman and Tom Sitton's *City of Angels: Pueblo to Megalopolis* (2002) and various Los Angeles city historians note that record-keeping for small commercial establishments in early-20th-century Los Angeles was inconsistent; multiple establishments have claimed "oldest" status with varying documentary support.

## §6 — Verdict and Archival Gap

Snopes correctly rates the specific "stale bread complaint" variant of the Philippe's founding legend as Undetermined. The claim is neither verifiable nor cleanly falsifiable: the founder's own testimony, the closest thing to a primary source, contradicts it, but that testimony was given 33 years after the alleged event by a man then at least in his seventies, relying on memory of an event from his early business career.

What the fact-check does not fully resolve is the deeper question of whether either establishment can be credited at all. The epistemically honest position is that the French dip was likely invented independently multiple times across different Los Angeles lunch-counter establishments in the first two decades of the 20th century — dipping bread in cooking juices being a pragmatic, cost-effective extension of available ingredients — and that institutional primacy was not asserted or contested until the sandwich had become famous enough to be worth claiming.

For EARC purposes this item is classified in mode **C** (Contested): the Snopes verdict of Undetermined is appropriate, the evidentiary situation is genuinely indeterminate, and the persistence of competing institutional claims reflects both commercial interest and genuine historical ambiguity. The primary gap in the scholarship is the absence of any systematic search through Los Angeles newspaper archives, restaurant trade publications, and city permit records from 1905–1925 for the earliest contemporaneous references to the French dip by name. Digital newspaper archive projects (including Chronicling America and ProQuest Historical Newspapers) may contain evidence that has not yet been brought to bear on this question, and a focused archival study could plausibly narrow the uncertainty even if it cannot resolve it definitively.
