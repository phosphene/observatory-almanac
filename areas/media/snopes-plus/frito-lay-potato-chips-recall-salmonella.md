## §1 — Claim Anatomy

On 21 April 2017, Frito-Lay voluntarily recalled two products: Jalapeño Flavored Lay's Kettle Cooked Potato Chips and Jalapeño Flavored Miss Vickie's Kettle Cooked Potato Chips. The recall was triggered not by any positive detection of Salmonella in Frito-Lay products, but by a supplier's prior recall of a seasoning blend containing jalapeño powder that the supplier had identified as potentially contaminated. Frito-Lay's own statement was explicit that no Salmonella had been found in the seasoning as supplied to Frito-Lay, and that the action was taken "out of an abundance of caution." No illness related to this recall had been confirmed.

This recall, which had been registered with the FDA and was publicly documentable, was reported on 21 April 2017 by a website called Daily Mox. The Daily Mox report reproduced Frito-Lay's press release text and the FDA notice, but surrounded this material with alarmist framing — including a headline asserting that the situation constituted "A HEALTH HAZARD SITUATION WHERE THERE IS A REASONABLE PROBABILITY THAT THE USE OF THE PRODUCT CAN CAUSE SERIOUS, ADVERSE HEALTH CONSEQUENCES."

The core factual problem Snopes engaged in June 2017 was therefore the exaggeration of severity: Frito-Lay's own language ("no Salmonella was found," "abundance of caution," "no illness confirmed") was inconsistent with the "serious adverse health consequences" framing deployed by Daily Mox. Snopes investigated and characterised the claim as real but overstated.

A secondary problem emerged in July and August 2017, when the same content was recirculated with fresh urgency under a new headline: "Alert!! One Of America's Most POPULAR Potato Chip Brands Just Issued MASSIVE Recall — Throw Them Away NOW." By this point, the recall was months old. Products had been off shelves and consumers advised since late April. The recirculated article presented three-to-four-month-old information as immediate news. This recirculation, not the original April report, was the event Snopes classified as "Outdated" — a verdict that captures not the factual accuracy of the underlying recall but the temporal misrepresentation of its currency.

## §2 — Verification Record

Snopes investigator Dan MacGuill verified the existence and details of the recall through two primary documentary sources. First, Frito-Lay's own press release, published 21 April 2017 on the company's website, explicitly described the scope of the recall, the products affected, the causal chain (supplier seasoning recall → precautionary downstream recall), and the absence of confirmed illness. Second, the FDA's recall database contained an entry for the Frito-Lay action, providing the official regulatory record of the event.

MacGuill confirmed that the basic facts reported by Daily Mox were accurate: Frito-Lay had voluntarily recalled two jalapeño-flavored products on 21 April 2017 due to potential Salmonella in a seasoning ingredient. The recall was real, registered, and documentable.

MacGuill also verified the supplier context. The recall chain began with Frito-Lay's supplier of jalapeño powder-containing seasoning blend, which had itself issued a recall after detecting potential Salmonella in its own products. Frito-Lay's precautionary withdrawal of its jalapeño chip products was a downstream response to this upstream supplier recall, not a response to any detection within Frito-Lay's own supply chain. This causal structure is material to the severity assessment: a precautionary recall triggered by a supplier's event, with no downstream positive detection and no confirmed illness, represents a different tier of food safety risk than a recall triggered by positive pathogen detection in finished products or by illness cluster investigation.

The "serious adverse health consequences" language reproduced by Daily Mox in its headline appears to derive from the formal FDA recall classification system, in which recalls are categorised by risk level. Class I recalls, the most serious tier, are defined as situations with "a reasonable probability that the use of the product will cause serious adverse health consequences or death." However, the FDA's own database entry for the Frito-Lay recall did not classify it as Class I, and the "reasonable probability" standard is a worst-case regulatory threshold applied for conservative precautionary purposes rather than a factual statement of expected patient harm.

When the same content recirculated in July and August 2017, MacGuill identified the recirculation as the presentation of months-old information as urgent current news, warranting an "Outdated" classification — a Snopes-specific verdict category acknowledging that a claim's factual content may have been accurate at a prior time but is being presented in a temporally misleading way.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis

This case introduces a category of misinformation that is underrecognised in conventional fact-checking frameworks: temporal misrepresentation, or the misdating of true claims. The April 2017 Frito-Lay recall was real. The July–August 2017 recirculation of that recall as current news was epistemically deceptive because it presented an already-resolved regulatory action as an ongoing, urgent consumer threat.

The epistemic novelty here lies in the concept of recirculation as its own form of time-indexed misinformation. Most misinformation typologies focus on content falsity — claims that are factually inaccurate, fabricated, or misleading. The outdated-recirculation category challenges this typology by presenting a case where the content itself may be factually accurate while the frame of temporal currency — "just issued," "NOW," "ALERT" — is false. The claim's truth value is relative to a time index, and the misrepresentation consists precisely in the mismatch between the time index implied by the presentation and the actual time of the event.

This form of misinformation is particularly common in food safety reporting, where recalls have a specific temporal structure: an initial announcement period of high relevance, a middle period during which products are being withdrawn from shelves, and a resolved period after which the actionable window for consumers has closed. Recirculation of recall content in the resolved period generates unwarranted anxiety — some readers will rush to check their pantries for products that have not been sold for months — while providing no genuine public health value.

The severity-framing distortion offers a second epistemically significant feature. Regulatory language designed for worst-case conservative risk assessment — "reasonable probability of serious adverse health consequences" — was lifted from its regulatory context and presented to a general audience as a factual risk characterisation. This context-stripping transforms regulatory precaution into epidemiological prediction, producing a significant and systematic overstatement of actual consumer risk.

A third feature worth examining is the supply chain causal structure of the recall itself. The contamination risk flowed from a jalapeño powder supplier to a seasoning blend manufacturer to Frito-Lay. At each link in this chain, the presence of Salmonella was a possibility, not a confirmed finding. By the time the potential risk reached the consumer, it had been attenuated through multiple uncertainty layers, each of which the Daily Mox coverage ignored entirely. The framing presented the end product — the potato chip — as the site of confirmed danger, when in reality the chain of potential exposure involved probabilities at each step, none of which had materialised in confirmed illness.

## §4 — Scientific and Empirical Background

Salmonella is one of the most common causes of foodborne illness in the United States. The CDC estimates that Salmonella causes approximately 1.35 million infections, 26,500 hospitalisations, and 420 deaths annually in the United States, making it a substantial and genuine public health burden. The vast majority of Salmonella infections are caused by Salmonella enterica serovars, with Salmonella typhimurium and Salmonella enteritidis being the most commonly implicated in outbreak investigations.

The association of Salmonella with foods such as jalapeño powder may seem counterintuitive to consumers accustomed to thinking of the pathogen primarily in association with raw meat and poultry. However, Salmonella is capable of surviving in low-moisture environments including dried spices and seasoning blends. The drying process reduces water activity sufficiently to slow bacterial growth but does not reliably kill all Salmonella cells present; heat inactivation during processing is the primary control measure.

The Peanut Corporation of America Salmonella outbreak of 2007–2009 established that even shelf-stable, dry-processed food products could serve as vehicles for Salmonella distribution at national scale. That event, which involved the deliberate shipment of product known to have tested positive for Salmonella, resulted in over 700 confirmed cases across 46 states and at least nine deaths.

The Frito-Lay situation in 2017 was structurally distinct from the Peanut Corporation case in every material respect. No Salmonella had been confirmed in Frito-Lay products. No illness had been linked to Frito-Lay products. The recall was precautionary and supplier-triggered. The products in question are heat-exposed during manufacture — the kettle cooking process involves high temperatures that would substantially reduce (though not necessarily eliminate) any Salmonella introduced through seasoning applied post-cooking. The specific sequential exposure pathway from contaminated jalapeño powder at a supplier facility to harmful dose at the point of consumer consumption involved multiple attenuating factors that were absent from the alarmist coverage.

The FDA's recall classification system is relevant context. Class I recalls apply to products "where there is a reasonable probability that the use of the product will cause serious adverse health consequences or death." Class II recalls cover products that "may cause a temporary adverse health consequence" with "remote probability of serious adverse health consequences." Class III recalls are minor violations unlikely to cause adverse consequences. The FDA's database characterisation of the Frito-Lay recall and the Salmonella risk profile of the specific exposure scenario would provide the appropriate context for consumer guidance, but this level of specificity was absent from the viral reporting.

## §5 — Misinformation Mechanics

The mechanics of this case involve two distinct phases of misinformation generation: the original April 2017 exaggeration by Daily Mox and the July–August 2017 recirculation as fresh news. Each phase operates through a different mechanism.

In the first phase, the mechanism is severity inflation through decontextualised regulatory language. The Daily Mox article imported the FDA's formal risk classification language into a headline without acknowledging that this language is a standardised precautionary formulation rather than an empirical assessment of actual consumer risk. The gap between regulatory language (designed to be conservative) and observed epidemiological risk (none, in this case) was erased by the presentation, leaving readers with the impression that the product posed a documented, serious, confirmed health threat.

In the second phase, the mechanism is temporal decontextualisation. The July–August recirculation added no new facts, cited no new developments, and provided no information suggesting the situation had changed since April. It presented a resolved regulatory action using present-tense urgency markers ("just issued," "NOW," "THROW THEM AWAY") that implied the situation was current and unresolved. For a consumer encountering this article in August 2017, the logical inference — that the recall had just been announced and that immediate action was required — was false.

The combination of these two phases is characteristic of a specific category of viral food safety content: an original article that exaggerates severity, followed by delayed recirculation that adds temporal misrepresentation to the severity distortion. The second-generation viral spread often reaches a different audience than the first, potentially many months later, ensuring that the distorted version continues to circulate long after any practical relevance has expired.

Snopes's "Outdated" verdict category is specifically designed to address the second-phase problem. Rather than characterising the claim as False (which would be inaccurate, since the recall was real) or True (which would fail to convey the temporal misrepresentation), "Outdated" signals that the content was accurate when originally reported but is being presented deceptively in terms of currency. This represents a sophisticated attempt to calibrate verdict categories to the real structure of the misinformation.

Importantly, Snopes investigators also noted the general principle that consumers should always verify product recall claims through authoritative primary sources — specifically, the FDA's recall database (fda.gov/safety/recalls) and the CPSC's recall portal — rather than relying on secondary reporting, which is subject to the distortions documented in this case.

## §6 — Verdict and Takeaways

**Verdict: Outdated.** The Frito-Lay recall of Jalapeño Flavored Lay's Kettle Cooked and Miss Vickie's Kettle Cooked potato chips was a real, documented event registered with the FDA on 21 April 2017. The recall was precautionary, triggered by an upstream supplier event, and involved no confirmed Salmonella detection in Frito-Lay products and no confirmed illness. The core factual content of the Daily Mox report was accurate, though the severity framing was materially overstated.

When the same content was recirculated in July–August 2017 as fresh, urgent news, the temporal framing was false. The recall was months old, already acted upon, and no longer actionable for consumers. The dramatic urgency markers — "just issued," "THROW THEM AWAY NOW" — implied a contemporaneous emergency that did not exist.

The gap classification is type **R** (Relevance): the claim's factual kernel was real but the temporal and urgency framing rendered the recirculated content epistemically irrelevant and behaviorally misleading. Consumers who acted on the July–August recirculation were responding to a false frame of immediacy applied to a historical event.

This case argues for a more textured understanding of food recall misinformation. The question "is there really a recall?" is only part of the relevant inquiry. Equally important are: when was it issued? Has it been resolved? What level of risk does it represent? Were any illnesses confirmed? Was the product actually contaminated or was the recall precautionary? Alarm-driven food safety content systematically elides these distinctions, producing a simplified "dangerous product — act now" narrative regardless of whether the specific situation warrants that response.

For researchers and communicators, the case illustrates the specific challenges posed by the outdated-recirculation category: it does not fit standard true/false typologies, it requires temporal indexing to assess, and its harmful effects — unwarranted consumer anxiety, erosion of trust in legitimate recall communications — are real despite the factual accuracy of the underlying event.
