# Is This 'Frog Buttcheeks' Photo Real?


## 1. The Claim

In May 2021, a photograph purportedly showing the posterior anatomy of a frog circulated broadly across social media platforms including Twitter, Instagram, and Reddit. The image, shared by Twitter user @brusseIsproutz, was accompanied by the claim that it depicted a frog's "buttcheeks" — and, undeniably, the posterior anatomy visible in the photograph does exhibit a striking bilateral symmetry and rounded morphology that prompted extensive online commentary comparing the frog's hindquarters to various human cultural referents. Most noteworthy among these comparisons was the observation that the frog's posterior resembled the distinctively flat backside of Hank Hill, the protagonist of the animated television program "King of the Hill."

The viral potential of the image was self-evident: a genuine photograph of an animal displaying an anatomical feature that simultaneously resembles human posterior anatomy and an established pop culture meme generates exactly the kind of novelty-humor-recognition combination that social media amplification rewards. The image spread rapidly and generated extensive commentary, dueling scientific and non-scientific identifications of the anatomy, and considerable debate about whether the photograph had been manipulated.

The claim investigated is essentially an authenticity claim: Is this photograph genuine? Does it show a real animal, and is the anatomical feature it depicts as dramatic as it appears? The answer to the authenticity question is yes — the photograph is real, unmanipulated, and shows a genuine animal. The anatomical identification embedded in the claim ("buttcheeks") is, however, imprecise. What the photograph shows is the frog's cloaca — a structure with no exact homolog in human anatomy, though it performs some overlapping functions.

The Snopes verdict of "Mostly True" captures the gradient accurately: the photograph is genuine, the visual impression of bilateral rounded anatomy is real, but "buttcheeks" is not the zoologically accurate description of what is depicted.


## 2. What's Actually True

The photograph is authentic. It was originally posted by @marron.chestnut777, an Instagram account maintained by a Japanese aquarium enthusiast who appears to keep an extensive collection of fish, lizards, and frogs. The photograph was posted on May 11, 2021, with hashtags identifying the subject as a common rain frog, and accompanied by the owner's comment that they had captured a rare angle of the animal and that it had a "桃尻" (momo-jiri) — meaning approximately "peach butt." The animal, which the owner appears to have named Marron, is a living pet kept in a domestic aquarium setting. The photograph has not been digitally manipulated; it captures a genuine anatomical feature of the animal.

The animal is a common rain frog, a term that refers to members of the family Brevicipitidae, which includes the genera *Breviceps*, *Callulina*, *Probreviceps*, *Spelaeophryne*, and several others. Common rain frogs (*Breviceps* spp.) are native to sub-Saharan Africa and are characterized by their round, spherical body shape, very short limbs, small head relative to body, and distinctive defensive posture of inflating the body into a ball-like shape. Their rotund morphology — often described as resembling a small round ball with legs — means that when viewed from the posterior, the bilateral symmetry of the cloaca and surrounding tissue can indeed present a distinctive visual impression.

The anatomical feature visible in the photograph is identified by Snopes, citing Penn State's frog dissection biology course, as the cloaca. The cloaca is defined as "the general receptacle for the intestine, the reproductive system, and the urinary system," opening to the exterior through the anus. This is the correct identification. What popular characterization as "buttcheeks" names is an anatomical region — the bilateral posterior flesh flanking the cloacal opening — that in the round, short-legged morphology of a brevicipitid frog is particularly visually prominent due to the animal's lack of a distinct tail, its rounded body shape, and the posterior positioning of the cloacal opening.

The phrase "buttcheeks" draws an anatomical analogy with the gluteal musculature and subcutaneous fat of human (or other mammalian) posterior anatomy. This is inaccurate in at least two senses: frogs lack gluteal musculature (their locomotion is accomplished primarily by the iliofibularis, cruralis, and sartorius muscles rather than by gluteal muscles, which are specifically mammalian adaptations), and the bilateral soft tissue visible flanking the cloaca in the photograph is not adipose tissue in a mammalian sense but the combination of skin, subcutaneous tissue, and the underlying musculature of the pelvic region.

The Snopes verdict acknowledges this distinction: "What's True" section states the photograph is genuine; "What's False" section states "This image could be more accurately described as a photograph of a frog's cloaca." The "Mostly True" rating is appropriate.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The fact-check correctly handles the photograph's authenticity and provides a brief accurate anatomical correction. But the biology of what is depicted — the cloaca, its evolutionary significance, and the morphology of the Brevicipitidae — is considerably more interesting than the fact-check's brief anatomical note conveys.

**The cloaca as evolutionary innovation and constraint**: The cloaca is one of the most ancient and fundamental anatomical features in vertebrate biology. It is present in all non-mammalian vertebrates and in monotreme mammals (platypuses and echidnas), serving as the common terminus of the digestive, urinary, and reproductive tracts. The word itself is Latin for "sewer" — a descriptive if inelegant term. In amphibians, reptiles, birds, and fish, waste products (feces and urine), sperm, eggs, and (in oviparous species) eggs all exit through the cloaca.

The evolutionary story of the cloaca is one of dual constraint and adaptation. Its ancestral function, shared across the deuterostome lineage (the animal lineage including vertebrates and echinoderms), is as a shared terminus for the gut and a common connection through which gametes are released. In primitive vertebrates, the consolidation of these functions into a single opening is thought to reflect the developmental efficiency of a single common opening rather than multiple separate organs, though the functional implications of this consolidation (the close proximity of reproductive and excretory outputs) are substantial and have driven various adaptations to manage contamination risks.

In amphibians specifically, the cloaca serves an additional function that has no counterpart in amniotes: it is a significant site of ion and water uptake from puddles and moist substrates. Frogs and toads have a highly permeable skin and cloaca that allows them to rehydrate by sitting in water — "drinking" through the skin and cloaca rather than through the mouth (Stiffler et al., 1983, "Urinary Bladder and Skin as Sites of Water Exchange in Amphibians," *Comparative Biochemistry and Physiology Part A: Physiology*, 75(1), 31–37). The "seat patch" (ventral pelvic skin region) is particularly important for cutaneous water uptake, and the cloacal region participates in this osmoregulatory function in ways that will be familiar to herpetologists but that are entirely absent from popular accounts of "frog buttcheeks."

**Brevicipitidae morphology — why this frog looks this way**: The striking visual effect of the "buttcheeks" photograph is substantially a product of the extraordinary morphology of the Brevicipitidae. Common rain frogs of the genus *Breviceps* are among the most morphologically distinctive of the approximately 7,000 known anuran (frog and toad) species. They are sometimes called "bubble frogs" for their defensive posture of inflating the body and stiffening to make themselves as round and difficult to swallow as possible. Their limbs are proportionally very short relative to body length, giving them a spherical appearance, and they cannot jump effectively — they walk and scuttle rather than leaping like typical frogs.

The Brevicipitidae have approximately 38 known species distributed across sub-Saharan Africa, with *Breviceps adspersus* (the common rain frog or bushveld rain frog) being the most widely distributed and familiar. They are burrowing frogs that emerge primarily during and after rainstorms to breed explosively — large aggregations of breeding individuals appear rapidly when conditions are appropriate. Their advertisement calls are high-pitched and have been widely shared online, contributing to multiple viral moments including the 2013 "world's cutest frog" video featuring the desert rain frog (*Breviceps macrops*), which was viewed more than 22 million times on YouTube.

The morphological characteristics that make Brevicipitid frogs appear to have visible "buttcheeks" — rounded body shape, posteriorly positioned cloaca, relatively smooth skin on the dorsal posterior surface, and the short-limbed configuration that positions the cloaca prominently at the posterior terminus of a nearly spherical body — are all functional adaptations related to burrowing, defensive inflation, and the animal's arid-to-semi-arid habitat. The body shape that strikes internet users as suggestive of mammalian posterior anatomy is the product of selection pressures operating over millions of years in African semi-arid environments.

**The reproductive biology of common rain frogs — a genuinely remarkable story**: The cloaca in brevicipitid frogs is the site of an extraordinary reproductive behavior that is almost entirely unknown outside herpetological circles and that the "buttcheeks" discussion entirely misses. Common rain frogs (*Breviceps* spp.) face a profound reproductive challenge: they are terrestrial frogs that do not return to water to breed (unlike most anurans), and the male is so much smaller than the female that it cannot achieve the amplexus (the clasping mating embrace used by most frogs) necessary to position itself for fertilization. The size difference between male and female *Breviceps* is extreme — males are typically one-third the body mass of females or less.

To solve this problem, brevicipitid frogs have evolved a remarkable solution: the male adheres to the female's back using a specialized adhesive skin secretion produced by both sexes. The adhesive is believed to be a protein-based glue secreted by skin glands, and it physically bonds the male to the female's dorsal surface during the mating period (Stam et al., 2008, "The Use of a Back-Adhesive Secretion in Brevicipitid Frogs," *Copeia*, 2008(3), 686–694). This is one of very few documented cases of chemical adhesion as a mating behavior in vertebrates and is unique among frogs. The cloaca in this context is the site where sperm transfer occurs despite the non-standard positioning.

This evolutionary adaptation — chemical adhesion to solve a size-disparity reproductive challenge — is a far more genuinely surprising biological fact than anything about "buttcheeks," but it requires actual engagement with brevicipitid reproductive biology to encounter. The viral moment around the photograph provided an opportunity to introduce this biology to a large audience; the fact-check's brief anatomical note addressed the minimum necessary correction without capitalizing on this opportunity.

**The taxonomic context — what "common rain frog" encompasses**: The hashtag identification of the photographed animal as a "common rain frog" could refer to several species within the Brevicipitidae. The most likely candidate, given the animal's appearance and the Japanese hobbyist context (suggesting an animal that is commercially available in the international pet trade), is *Breviceps adspersus* (bushveld rain frog) or *Breviceps mossambicus* (Mozambique rain frog). Both species are relatively frequently encountered in the exotic pet trade and are visually similar. The striking rounded morphology and visible cloacal region are characteristic of the genus.

Rain frog taxonomy has been actively revised over the past two decades using molecular phylogenetics, and the current species count within the family is higher than it was in traditional accounts (Loader et al., 2009, "Systematics of Ethiopian Breviceps (Anura: Brevicipitidae)," *Breviora*, 516, 1–17; Blackburn & Measey, 2009, "Dispersal to or within Madagascar? Molecular phylogenetics of an endemic Malagasy frog clade," *Molecular Phylogenetics and Evolution*, 52(2), 6524–6547). The discovery of cryptic species (species that are morphologically indistinguishable but genetically distinct) within what appeared to be single species has increased the recognized diversity of the family significantly.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Mostly True (photograph is genuine; "buttcheeks" is anatomically imprecise)  
**Snopes Verdict:** Mostly True  
**Confidence:** High on authenticity; High on anatomical correction  
**Epistemic Basis:** Visual authentication, anuran anatomy, Instagram source verification  
**Phenomenological Status:** Visually striking bilateral anatomy that resembles mammalian posterior morphology  
**Mechanistic Status:** Anatomy is cloaca + surrounding pelvic soft tissue, not gluteal musculature  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** None (anatomy identification claim)

The viral "frog buttcheeks" photograph is authentic and depicts a real animal — a common rain frog (family Brevicipitidae) — in a genuine posterior view photographed by a Japanese aquarium enthusiast who identified the animal as belonging to their personal collection. The visual impression of bilateral symmetry is real and unmanipulated.

The anatomical identification "buttcheeks" is imprecise: the structure visible in the photograph is the cloaca and surrounding pelvic soft tissue. Frogs do not have gluteal musculature (a specifically mammalian feature), and the bilateral appearance is produced by the cloacal opening flanked by pelvic tissue — not by any structural homolog of mammalian buttocks. The "Mostly True" Snopes verdict correctly captures the gradient: genuine photograph, imprecise anatomy label.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The viral success of the "frog buttcheeks" photograph fits into a well-established social media content category: animals displaying anatomical features that create unexpected visual resemblances to human or cultural referents. The comparison to Hank Hill's posterior (a recurring meme referencing the animated character's notably flat backside) added a layered humor dimension that significantly increased the post's sharing potential. The original Japanese caption identifying the feature as "peach butt" (桃尻, momo-jiri) — itself a term with positive aesthetic connotations in Japanese culture — added cross-cultural resonance.

**The "King of the Hill" meme context**: The comparison to Hank Hill's posterior is worth noting as a cultural artifact. *King of the Hill* (1997–2010), the animated series created by Mike Judge and Greg Daniels, features Hank Hill as a propane salesman in fictional Arlen, Texas. The character's notably flat posterior became a recurring observational joke among fans and eventually a durable internet meme. The application of this meme template to a frog with a visually prominent posterior represents the internet's characteristic process of applying established meme formats to novel photographic content that fits the template's visual requirements.

The juxtaposition of the scientifically interesting (the cloaca of a Brevicipitid frog) with the culturally familiar (an animated character's distinctive anatomy) is precisely the kind of unexpected connection that makes science communication effective when executed well. The photograph attracted attention that more straightforward natural history photography would not have, and the viral moment created an opportunity — largely missed — to introduce the extraordinary biology of the Brevicipitidae to a large audience.

**The 2013 desert rain frog viral moment**: As noted in the Snopes fact-check, the "frog buttcheeks" moment was not the first time rain frogs achieved viral reach. In 2013, a video of the desert rain frog (*Breviceps macrops*) — a species endemic to the coastal dune ecosystem of southwestern Namibia and northwestern South Africa — displaying its defensive threat vocalization became an internet sensation, with more than 22 million YouTube views. The desert rain frog produces a surprisingly high-pitched, squeaky vocalization that contrasts dramatically with the animal's solid, rotund appearance, creating the same kind of incongruity-humor that drove the 2021 buttcheeks moment. The BBC Natural History Unit subsequently produced a professionally filmed segment on the desert rain frog that has itself achieved millions of views.

**The anuran diversity context**: The approximately 7,000 currently recognized species of frogs and toads represent one of the most diverse tetrapod orders. Anuran diversification spans all major biogeographic regions except Antarctica, from high-altitude cold environments (some species of *Rana* can survive being partially frozen) to tropical rainforests and arid deserts. The Brevicipitidae represent a relatively specialized, ecologically restricted clade within this diversity — burrowing, terrestrial, limited to sub-Saharan Africa — but their distinctive morphology and behavioral biology make them disproportionately interesting relative to their geographic restriction.

Amphibian diversity globally faces severe conservation pressure. The chytrid fungus *Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis* (Bd), which causes lethal chytridiomycosis in many amphibian species, has driven what may be the largest disease-caused biodiversity loss in recorded history (Scheele et al., 2019, "Amphibian Fungal Panzootic Causes Catastrophic and Ongoing Loss of Biodiversity," *Science*, 363(6434), 1459–1463). Brevicipitid species have varying susceptibility to Bd, and their conservation status varies by species — some are abundant, others are assessed as Near Threatened or Vulnerable due to habitat loss and collection pressure for the pet trade.

**The international pet trade dimension**: The frog photographed by @marron.chestnut777 is a pet kept in Japan, suggesting acquisition through the international exotic amphibian trade. Japan has an extensive domestic market for exotic reptiles and amphibians, and numerous brevicipitid species are traded internationally. The legality and sustainability of this trade varies by country of origin and species: some *Breviceps* species are collected from wild populations in southern Africa and exported under CITES Appendix II regulations (which require permits but do not prohibit trade), while others are bred in captivity specifically for the pet trade.

The welfare dimensions of brevicipitid keeping are not straightforward. Common rain frogs have specific requirements — appropriate substrate for burrowing behavior, humidity management, temperature ranges appropriate to their semi-arid origins — that casual hobbyists may not provide. The fact that the animal photographed appears healthy and well-cared-for (its owner's evident enthusiasm and documentation of multiple animals) does not generalize to all brevicipitid keeping in the pet trade.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by Dan Evon (May 2021) is appropriately brief and accurate. It authenticates the photograph by tracing it to the original Instagram source, confirms the animal's identity as a common rain frog through the original hashtags and the owner's own comments, provides the anatomically correct identification (cloaca) with reference to an educational biology source (Penn State's frog dissection course), and assigns the "Mostly True" verdict on the grounds that the photograph is genuine but the anatomy label is imprecise.

The piece also provides context about the rain frog's prior viral history — the 2013 desert rain frog video — which effectively situates the 2021 moment within a pattern of rain frog social media success and suggests that the animals' distinctive morphology and behavior have a demonstrated capacity to capture public interest.

What the fact-check does not address is the biological richness of what is shown: the evolutionary significance of the cloaca across the vertebrate lineage, the extraordinary reproductive biology of brevicipitid frogs (chemical adhesion), the functional role of the cloaca in amphibian osmoregulation, and the conservation context of brevicipitids in the wild and in the pet trade. These omissions are consistent with the genre — a claim-specific fact-check handling an authenticity question has different scope than a natural history feature — but they mean that the millions of people who encountered the "frog buttcheeks" photograph and wondered what they were seeing received only a brief anatomical correction rather than the genuinely interesting biology that the image could have introduced.

The "Mostly True" rating is appropriately granular: it avoids the overclaim of a simple "True" (which would implicitly endorse the "buttcheeks" framing as accurate) while not dismissing the claim as substantially false (the photograph is genuine, and the anatomy is as depicted). It correctly identifies the specific imprecision (the anatomical label) without overweighting it as a categorical error.

The broader value of the fact-check lies primarily in its authentication function: at a moment when digital image manipulation is increasingly sophisticated and viral nature photographs are frequently fabricated or misattributed, confirming that a viral image is genuinely what it purports to be serves a real informational function. The anatomical correction is secondary in practical importance but is appropriately included as a precision improvement to the popular description.
