# Do some female frogs play dead to avoid mating?


## 1. The Claim

In October 2023, a research paper published in the journal *Royal Society Open Science* generated widespread media attention by documenting something previously unreported in frogs: female European common frogs (*Rana temporaria*) appear to feign death as a strategy to escape unwanted mating attempts by males. The claim circulated across science news outlets and social media under headlines proclaiming that female frogs "play dead" to avoid sex. Snopes covered the story on 18 November 2023, rating it "True" with appropriate caveats.

The claim, taken at face value, is correct. The study did document the behaviour. However, the phrase "play dead" carries implications that the underlying science complicates. Playing dead implies a deliberate, conscious strategy — a decision to perform death feigning. The biology of tonic immobility as described in the literature, including the paper in question, is considerably more nuanced: tonic immobility is most accurately understood not as a voluntary performance but as an involuntary stress response, a physiological state triggered by overwhelming external stimulation rather than a calculated ruse. Whether female frogs "choose" to go limp is a question the paper explicitly leaves open while presenting evidence that smaller, likely younger and less experienced females are disproportionately those who enter the state — consistent with a stress-overload interpretation rather than a strategic one.

This entry examines what the study actually found, why it matters for our understanding of sexual conflict in explosive breeders, what tonic immobility is and is not, how the evolutionary origin of the behaviour shapes interpretation, and why the viral framing — while broadly accurate — obscures the genuinely surprising science underneath it.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The primary study.** The research was conducted by Carolin Dittrich, then a post-doctoral researcher at the Natural History Museum in Berlin, and co-authored with Mark-Oliver Rödel. The paper, "Drop Dead! Female mate avoidance in an explosively breeding frog," was published in *Royal Society Open Science* on 11 October 2023 (Dittrich & Rödel, 2023, *Royal Society Open Science*, vol. 10, no. 10, article 230742, DOI: 10.1098/rsos.230742). The study design placed one male and two females of different sizes in a controlled aquatic enclosure and filmed their interactions over one hour. The experimental design was originally intended to address male size preference in mate selection — that is, whether males prefer larger or smaller females. The answer was that they showed no size preference. What Dittrich noticed instead was a sustained repertoire of female resistance behaviours.

**Three documented behaviours.** The paper documented three distinct female mate-avoidance strategies in *Rana temporaria*, recorded in descending order of frequency:

1. *Rotation* (documented in 83% of amplexed females): When grasped by a male in amplexus — the mating embrace in which the male positions himself on the female's back — females would rotate their bodies, typically flipping onto their backs and physically submerging the male underwater. Because amplexus requires dorsal access (the male must be positioned above and behind the female), dorsal rotation is mechanically disruptive to mating success. This was the most common initial response.

2. *Vocalisation mimicry* (documented in 48% of amplexed females): Females emitted calls structurally similar to male release calls — the calls male frogs use to signal that they are male and thus not valid mating partners. This is a form of acoustic deception: the female mimics a signal that should cause the amplexing male to release his grip.

3. *Tonic immobility* (documented in 33% of amplexed females): Females stiffened with limbs extended outward from the body, entering a rigid, unresponsive state. This is the behaviour described in popular media as "playing dead." Smaller females were significantly more likely to enter this state, which Dittrich and Rödel interpreted as consistent with a stress-overload hypothesis: smaller individuals may be younger, have lower stress tolerance, and be more likely to enter an involuntary refractory state under the physiological pressure of amplexus.

**What tonic immobility is.** Tonic immobility — also called thanatosis or death feigning — is a well-documented phenomenon across the animal kingdom, studied most extensively in the context of predator-prey interactions. The canonical cases include opossums (Virginia opossum, *Didelphis virginiana*), various insects, sharks, and some birds. The physiological mechanism involves a profound inhibition of voluntary motor function, typically accompanied by altered heart rate, reduced respiration, and muscular rigidity. In some species the state appears to have a strategic, selected function — for example, in species where predators do not consume animals they did not kill themselves, death feigning genuinely deters consumption. In others, the state appears to be a parasympathetic overflow response to extreme stress rather than an adaptive performance.

Rosalind Humphreys and Graeme Ruxton published the most comprehensive review of thanatosis literature in 2018 (Humphreys & Ruxton, 2018, *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*, vol. 72, article 22, DOI: 10.1007/s00265-017-2436-8), examining the function, triggers, and evolutionary origins of death feigning across taxa. Their review emphasises the heterogeneity of the phenomenon: what is called tonic immobility in one species may be mechanistically and functionally quite different from tonic immobility in another. The aggregation of all death-feigning under one label can obscure whether a given instance is strategic, reflexive, or somewhere in between.

**Prior documentation in other taxa.** The Dittrich & Rödel paper places *Rana temporaria* in a small but growing group of species in which tonic immobility has been documented during mating rather than predation contexts. Prior to the frog study, similar observations existed for:

- *Pisaura mirabilis* (the nursery web spider), where females may feign death during male courtship, documented by Trine Bilde and colleagues (Bilde et al., 2006, *Biology Letters*, vol. 2, no. 1, pp. 23–25, DOI: 10.1098/rsbl.2005.0392).
- *Pleurodeles waltl* (the Iberian ribbed newt), where tonic immobility interacts with pheromone-mediated courtship, examined in work on male pheromone induction of cloacal gaping in female newts (Janssenswillen & Bossuyt, 2016, *PLOS ONE*, vol. 11, no. 1, article e0144985, DOI: 10.1371/journal.pone.0144985).
- *Aeshna juncea* (the moorland hawker dragonfly), where females in a Swedish population were documented feigning death to escape harassing males, reported by Rassim Khelifa (Khelifa, 2017, *Ecology*, vol. 98, no. 6, pp. 1724–1726, DOI: 10.1002/ecy.1781).

The frog finding thus extends a small but coherent literature on tonic immobility as a sexual conflict behaviour rather than exclusively an anti-predator behaviour.

**What the study does not establish.** The paper explicitly acknowledges significant limitations. The experimental setting — a small enclosure with 5 cm of water, artificial lighting, and limited space — is a constrained environment that may alter the frequency, severity, or sequencing of behaviours in ways that would not be observed in a natural pond. The study does not establish whether tonic immobility in this context is functionally effective — whether males actually release females in response to the state or whether they attempt to continue amplexus regardless. Reviewer Lindsey Swierk, an assistant research professor at Binghamton University, noted in correspondence with Snopes that some frog species will attempt to mate with demonstrably dead females, raising questions about the actual adaptive value of the behaviour even if it occurs in natural settings.


## 3. Why People Believe This (and Why It Matters)

**Projection of conscious strategy.** The reason "playing dead to avoid mating" generated such intense interest is almost certainly its resonance with human social experience. Tonic immobility in a human sexual violence context — freeze responses during assault — has been documented in clinical research. Möller and colleagues (Möller et al., 2017, *Acta Obstetricia et Gynecologica Scandinavica*, vol. 96, no. 8, pp. 932–938, DOI: 10.1111/aogs.13174) found that tonic immobility was a common reaction during sexual assault and a predictor of subsequent PTSD and depression. The discovery of what appears to be an analogous phenomenon in frogs — females going rigid during unwanted mating — triggered an immediate anthropomorphic interpretation: the frogs are doing what traumatised humans sometimes do involuntarily, but they are doing it consciously, strategically, cleverly.

Dittrich herself acknowledged in a Snopes interview that she received online comments from people saying things like "feels like home" — indicating that the human parallel was hard to resist. This is a situation in which the viral appeal of an animal behaviour story runs precisely counter to the scientific precision required: the study is being read as empowering agency (strategic death feigning) when the evidence is more consistent with involuntary physiological overload. The epistemic novelty is precisely this inversion: the finding is interesting not because frogs are clever but because a defensive physiological state originally shaped by predation pressure has been co-opted into — or revealed as also present in — a sexual conflict context. The biology and the folklore run in opposite directions.

**The significance for explosive breeding.** The study's most important scientific contribution is not the behavioural novelty per se but the revision it forces to prevailing assumptions about female passivity in explosive breeding taxa. *Rana temporaria* is an "explosive breeder" — a species in which breeding is temporally compressed into a very brief window, thousands of individuals congregate simultaneously, and the male-biased operational sex ratio creates extreme mating competition and coercive pressure on females. Prior to the Dittrich study, the dominant assumption in the literature was that females in such systems were largely passive — that the selection dynamics were driven primarily by male competition rather than female choice or female resistance. The study provides systematic evidence that female frogs actively resist unwanted amplexus through multiple sequential strategies, revising a basic empirical assumption about the reproductive biology of this species and potentially of explosive breeders more broadly.

**Evolutionary interpretation.** The finding raises a productive evolutionary question: did tonic immobility in *Rana temporaria* evolve in response to predation pressure and then become secondarily co-opted in sexual conflict contexts? Or did a prior stress-response architecture, which might have made tonic immobility generally available as a physiological state, get independently recruited for both functions? This is unresolved by the current study, and answering it would require comparative work across related species, phylogenetic analysis of tonic immobility distribution, and experimental work on the mechanistic triggers and neurological substrates of the state in frogs. The paper opens this question productively rather than closing it.


## 4. What the Evidence Actually Shows

The evidence from the Dittrich & Rödel (2023) study shows:

- Female *Rana temporaria* are not passive during mating: they deploy an ordered repertoire of resistance behaviours in response to unwanted amplexus.
- Tonic immobility occurs in a minority of amplexed females (approximately one third) and appears to be a last-resort behaviour following unsuccessful rotation and vocalisation attempts.
- Smaller females are significantly more likely to enter tonic immobility, consistent with a stress-based interpretation.
- The laboratory conditions constrain the applicability of the findings to natural populations; the study establishes existence of proof of the behaviours but not their frequency or functional outcomes in wild settings.
- External expert commentary (Lindsey Swierk, Binghamton University) supports the finding as genuine while noting open questions about whether the behaviour is functionally effective in natural mating contexts.

The Snopes rating of "True" is appropriate — the claim that female frogs exhibit this behaviour is supported by peer-reviewed evidence from a legitimate journal and a credible researcher. The nuance that matters for accuracy is the question of voluntariness versus reflexivity, which viral coverage does not raise.


## 5. Viral Anatomy: How the Story Spread

The study was published in *Royal Society Open Science* on 11 October 2023. Coverage spread rapidly through science news outlets including ABC7 Chicago and Science magazine's news section, which ran a piece titled "Tired of aggressively amorous males? These female frogs play dead" (Jacobs, 2023). The framing from the beginning positioned the behaviour in human-relatable terms — frogs as agents exercising a preference — rather than in the more technically precise terms of involuntary stress physiology.

Social media amplification concentrated on the most shareable version: female frogs play dead to avoid sex. The paper's subtler finding — that female frogs display previously undocumented active mate-avoidance strategies, revising a passive-female assumption in explosive breeding literature — required more contextual knowledge to appreciate and was largely absent from viral distribution. The gap between what the paper claims and what the headlines said is not a gap of inaccuracy but a gap of compression: nothing in the viral framing is false, but the reductive summary ("playing dead to avoid sex") flattens a finding whose significance lies in its revision of a baseline assumption about passive female behaviour in a well-studied species.


## 6. The EARC Assessment and Research Gaps

**EARC mode: E (Empirically confirmed, accurately reported by Snopes).** The claim is verified. The Snopes coverage is accurate and appropriately caveated. The primary paper is peer-reviewed, published in a reputable journal, and the author communicated directly with Snopes for the report. The expert commentary from a non-affiliated researcher (Swierk) provides appropriate independent assessment. The rating of "True" is correct.

**Gap category: Accurate but undercontextualised.** The gap in existing coverage is the failure to explain the physiological basis of tonic immobility and the specific question of whether it represents a voluntary strategy or an involuntary stress response. This matters because the voluntariness question is central to the folk understanding of the finding (the frogs are clever and strategic) versus the scientific interpretation (a physiological state that may be maladaptive under some conditions is being triggered by sexual coercion stress in a way that may or may not benefit the female). The distinction between strategic death feigning and stress-induced freeze states has real implications for how we understand the evolutionary trajectory of the behaviour and whether it is likely to be maintained by natural selection in its current form.

**Key research gaps.** (1) Whether tonic immobility is functionally effective in natural pond settings — do males release females in response, and does this result in measurable differences in mating success? (2) The neurological and endocrinological substrates of tonic immobility specifically in *Rana temporaria* — is this the same class of physiological state documented in predation-based tonic immobility, or is it mechanistically distinct? (3) Phylogenetic distribution: do other explosive-breeding anuran species show comparable female mate-avoidance repertoires, or is the *Rana temporaria* finding specific to a subset of reproductive contexts? (4) Longitudinal and field validation: are the laboratory-documented behaviours quantitatively representative of naturally occurring rates, or does enclosure stress inflate or suppress their frequency?
