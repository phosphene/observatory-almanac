## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A widely shared photograph, circulating online since at least 2013, depicts what appears to be a small frog encased in ice or coated with heavy frost. The image is typically accompanied by text reading: "The Alaskan tree frog. Freezes solid in winter, its heart stopping, then thaws in spring and merrily hops off." The viral framing treats the photograph as documentary evidence of a specific named species—the "Alaskan tree frog"—and presents the animal's freeze tolerance as a simple, charming curiosity. Versions of the meme have circulated on Facebook, Tumblr, Reddit, and various content-aggregation sites, accumulating millions of collective views over a period of years.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2015) assigned this claim the rating **Miscaptioned**. This verdict is more nuanced than a simple "True" or "False" because the claim bundles a genuine biological phenomenon with a fabricated species name and a fraudulent photograph. Specifically, what is true is that a species of frog found in Alaska—the wood frog, *Rana sylvatica*—can indeed survive for extended periods with up to two-thirds of its body water completely frozen, its heart stopped, and its breathing ceased. What is false is that the photograph used to illustrate this claim does not depict a real frog at all: it appears to show a garden ornament or decorative figurine coated with frost or ice. Additionally, there is no animal called an "Alaskan tree frog." Alaska has only two native frog species, neither of which is a tree frog in the hylid sense: the Columbia spotted frog (*Rana luteiventris*) and the wood frog (*Rana sylvatica*), according to the Alaska Department of Fish and Game (2008).

The Miscaptioned rating captures the compound nature of the error: the underlying biological claim has genuine scientific support, but the specific evidence presented—both the photograph and the species name—is fabricated. This makes the frozen frog meme an unusually instructive case study in how misinformation can operate as a vector for real scientific knowledge, and how the epistemic status of a claim can depend critically on which components of a composite assertion one evaluates.

This entry examines the fabricated image and species name, the genuine cryobiology of *Rana sylvatica*, the broader phenomenon of freeze tolerance across the tree of life, and the epistemically novel category of the "miscaptioned true fact"—viral content that launders genuine science through falsified evidence.

## §2 The Image and Its Errors: No "Alaskan Tree Frog" Exists

The photograph central to the viral claim shows what appears to be a small frog in an ice-encrusted or frost-covered state, sitting on a surface of snow or ice. The image is visually striking—it depicts a perfectly formed frog shape, completely covered in a uniform layer of white frost crystals, with an almost sculptural quality. This sculptural quality is, in fact, the first diagnostic clue: a real frog that has undergone natural freeze tolerance does not look like this. Real frozen wood frogs, as documented in laboratory photography from cryobiology research (Costanzo et al., 2013, *Journal of Experimental Biology*), exhibit a darkened, somewhat shrunken appearance with partial ice formation visible beneath the skin, not a uniform external frosting that preserves every surface detail in pristine crystalline form.

Mikkelson (2015) identified the image as most likely depicting a garden ornament—a ceramic or resin frog figurine—that had been left outdoors and accumulated a coating of frost. The uniformity of the frost coating, the perfectly preserved posture (real frozen frogs are typically found in leaf litter or soil depressions, not posed on open ice surfaces), and the absence of the characteristic skin texture changes associated with genuine freeze-thaw cycling all support this identification. The image, in short, is not a photograph of a biological specimen at all; it is a photograph of a decorative object that happens to resemble what a casual viewer might imagine a frozen frog would look like.

The species name "Alaskan tree frog" is itself a fabrication with no taxonomic basis. Alaska is home to exactly two frog species, as documented by the Alaska Department of Fish and Game (2008): the Columbia spotted frog (*Rana luteiventris*), found in southeastern Alaska, and the wood frog (*Rana sylvatica*), which ranges across most of the state and is the species responsible for the genuine freeze-tolerance phenomenon. Neither species is a tree frog. True tree frogs belong to the family Hylidae and are characterized by toe pads adapted for arboreal locomotion; neither Alaskan species possesses these adaptations. The wood frog is a terrestrial and semi-aquatic ranid frog that hibernates on the forest floor, typically buried in leaf litter rather than in aquatic environments—a critical behavioral detail, because it is precisely this terrestrial overwintering habit that exposes the animal to subzero temperatures and has driven the evolution of freeze tolerance.

The invention of the name "Alaskan tree frog" may reflect a conflation with other well-known freeze-tolerant amphibians, such as the gray tree frog (*Hyla versicolor*) of eastern North America, which is also freeze-tolerant (Storey & Storey, 1985, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*). However, *Hyla versicolor* does not occur in Alaska. The fabricated name creates a phantom taxon—a species that sounds plausible to non-specialists but corresponds to no real organism—and attaches to it a real biological capacity drawn from a different, correctly named species. This taxonomic laundering is a common feature of viral natural history misinformation.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Miscaptioned True Fact — When Misinformation Launders a Real Phenomenon

The frozen frog meme represents an epistemically distinctive category of misinformation that deserves its own analytical treatment: the miscaptioned true fact. Unlike straightforward false claims (where the underlying assertion is wrong) or manipulated media (where real footage is altered to support a false narrative), the miscaptioned true fact presents a genuine scientific phenomenon through fabricated evidence. The biological claim—that a species of frog in Alaska can survive extended freezing—is robustly supported by peer-reviewed research spanning decades. The evidence presented to support that claim—the photograph and the species name—is fabricated. The truth of the underlying phenomenon is being laundered through false documentation.

This creates a distinctive epistemological problem for fact-checkers and science communicators. A straightforward "False" rating would be misleading, because the core biological claim is well-established science. A straightforward "True" rating would be irresponsible, because the specific evidence presented is fabricated and the species name is invented. Snopes addressed this by deploying the "Miscaptioned" rating—a verdict that acknowledges the composite nature of the claim and directs attention to the specific elements that are false without dismissing the elements that are true.

The epistemic novelty here extends beyond the individual case. Miscaptioned true facts function as a specific mechanism by which real scientific knowledge enters popular circulation in a degraded epistemological state. The audience that encounters the frozen frog meme comes away with a genuine piece of biological knowledge—that some frogs can survive freezing—but acquires it through a channel that has trained them to accept fabricated evidence as documentation. The person who shares this meme believing the photograph is real has learned a true fact and simultaneously practiced a false epistemology. They have arrived at a correct conclusion via an invalid pathway, and this invalid pathway will not reliably produce correct conclusions in future encounters with similar claims.

This phenomenon has been studied in the misinformation literature under related framings. Lewandowsky et al. (2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) documented that misinformation creates "continued influence effects"—even when a specific false claim is corrected, the broader narrative framework in which the claim was embedded tends to persist. In the frozen frog case, the continued influence is not a false belief about frogs (since the core claim is true) but a degraded evidentiary standard: having accepted a garden ornament as a photograph of a real frozen frog, the viewer's threshold for accepting future fabricated "evidence" of true claims has been lowered.

Science communication research by Scheufele and Krause (2019, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) has documented that the pathway by which scientific knowledge reaches public audiences matters for downstream epistemic behavior. Knowledge acquired through authoritative, well-sourced channels tends to produce audiences who seek similar channels for future questions; knowledge acquired through viral misinformation—even when the knowledge itself is accurate—tends to produce audiences who are more susceptible to future misinformation, because the initial encounter rewarded uncritical acceptance rather than source evaluation.

The frozen frog meme is therefore not epistemically harmless simply because the underlying biology is correct. It is an instance of accurate information delivered through a mechanism that systematically degrades the audience's ability to distinguish accurate from inaccurate information in the future. The true fact is the bait; the degraded epistemology is the hook.

## §4 The Cryobiology of Rana sylvatica: Freeze Tolerance Mechanisms

The genuine science underlying the frozen frog claim is remarkable and well-documented. The wood frog (*Rana sylvatica*) is the most extensively studied freeze-tolerant vertebrate, and the physiological mechanisms by which it survives freezing represent one of the most striking adaptations in vertebrate biology.

Wood frogs overwinter on the forest floor, typically beneath leaf litter, where they are exposed to ambient air temperatures that in interior Alaska can drop to −20°C or below (Costanzo et al., 2013, *Journal of Experimental Biology*). Unlike aquatic-hibernating amphibians that avoid freezing by remaining in water above 0°C, wood frogs tolerate the formation of ice within their bodies. Up to two-thirds of total body water can be converted to ice during a freezing episode. During this frozen state, the heart stops beating, breathing ceases, blood circulation halts, and the animal shows no detectable brain activity—by most conventional physiological criteria, the frozen wood frog is indistinguishable from a dead animal.

The key to survival is the accumulation of cryoprotectant solutes in tissues and organs prior to and during the onset of freezing. In the wood frog, the two primary cryoprotectants are glucose and urea (Costanzo et al., 2013). When ice nucleation begins on the skin surface—often triggered by contact with ice crystals in the surrounding environment—the wood frog's liver initiates a massive glycogenolysis cascade, breaking down stored glycogen into glucose and flooding the bloodstream with sugar at concentrations that would be lethal to most vertebrates. In Alaskan populations of *Rana sylvatica*, tissue glucose concentrations during freezing can reach levels roughly tenfold higher than those observed in more southern populations of the same species (Costanzo et al., 2013). Urea concentrations are similarly elevated, approximately twofold higher than in Ohio populations.

Jon Costanzo of the Department of Zoology at Miami University in Ohio—one of the leading researchers in vertebrate cryobiology—explained the protective mechanism to Snopes: "The solutes tend to depress the freezing point [of tissue]. It limits the amount of ice that actually forms in the body at any part. The more of that cryoprotective solute you can accumulate, the less ice will form and therefore the less stress there is on cells and tissues" (quoted in Mikkelson, 2015). Without these cryoprotectants, prolonged exposure to subzero temperatures causes ice crystals to form between cells, drawing water out of the cells by osmosis and causing lethal cellular shrinkage and membrane damage—the same mechanism that makes frostbite destructive in non-adapted organisms.

The freeze tolerance of Alaskan wood frogs is notably more extreme than that of their southern conspecifics. Costanzo et al. (2013) documented that Alaskan populations can survive cooling to approximately −16°C for extended periods and endure multiple freeze-thaw cycles within a single winter—a capacity that would be lethal to wood frogs from populations in Ohio or the northeastern United States. This latitudinal gradient in freeze tolerance correlates directly with the higher cryoprotectant concentrations in northern populations, consistent with strong selection pressure for enhanced cold hardiness at higher latitudes.

The thawing process is equally remarkable. When ambient temperatures rise above freezing, the wood frog thaws from the inside out: organs in the body core resume function before peripheral tissues, with the heart typically resuming beating within approximately one hour of thawing onset. Full locomotor recovery—the ability to hop, as the viral meme colorfully describes—requires several hours (Layne & First, 1991, *Cryo-Letters*). The animal does indeed "merrily hop off" in the colloquial sense, though the physiological recovery process is considerably more complex than that phrasing suggests.

## §5 Freeze Tolerance Across the Tree of Life: Comparative Cryobiology

The wood frog's freeze tolerance, while perhaps the most publicly famous example, exists within a broader biological context. Freeze tolerance—the ability to survive the formation of ice within body tissues—has evolved independently multiple times across the tree of life, and understanding this comparative context enriches the appreciation of what the wood frog achieves and why it matters for fields ranging from organ preservation to astrobiology.

Among vertebrates, freeze tolerance is relatively rare. Besides *Rana sylvatica*, several other North American frogs exhibit freeze tolerance to varying degrees, including the gray tree frog (*Hyla versicolor*), the spring peeper (*Pseudacris crucifer*), and the chorus frog (*Pseudacris triseriata*). Storey and Storey (1986, *American Journal of Physiology*) provided early comprehensive documentation of freeze tolerance mechanisms across these anuran species, establishing that glucose-based cryoprotection is shared among freeze-tolerant ranids while glycerol-based cryoprotection is employed by some hylids—evidence that the biochemical details of freeze tolerance vary even among closely related taxa that have converged on the same gross physiological strategy.

Among reptiles, the painted turtle (*Chrysemys picta*) exhibits freeze tolerance as a hatchling, surviving overwinter in shallow nests where soil temperatures can drop below freezing. Churchill and Storey (1992, *Journal of Experimental Biology*) documented that hatchling painted turtles can survive body ice content of approximately 50%, using glucose as a cryoprotectant in a manner analogous to the wood frog system. The garter snake (*Thamnophis sirtalis*) in certain Canadian populations also demonstrates limited freeze tolerance (Costanzo et al., 1988, *Canadian Journal of Zoology*), though less extreme than that of the wood frog.

Among invertebrates, freeze tolerance is far more widespread and in many cases more extreme. The woolly bear caterpillar (*Pyrrharctia isabella*), famous in North American folklore as a weather predictor, overwinters in a frozen state across much of its range. Many terrestrial insects and other arthropods in boreal and arctic environments employ freeze tolerance as their primary cold-hardiness strategy, using trehalose, glycerol, or other polyols as cryoprotectants (Zachariassen, 1985, *Physiological Reviews*). The Antarctic midge (*Belgica antarctica*), the only insect endemic to Antarctica, survives freezing at temperatures below −15°C and represents the most extreme insect example in one of the harshest environments on Earth (Lee et al., 2006, *Annual Review of Entomology*).

At the microscopic level, the nematode *Aphelenchus avenae* and the tardigrade (water bear) represent organisms capable of surviving not merely freezing but near-complete desiccation followed by rehydration—a related but distinct form of cryptobiosis. Tardigrades have famously survived exposure to the vacuum of space, extreme radiation, and temperatures approaching absolute zero in laboratory conditions (Jönsson et al., 2008, *Current Biology*), though the ecological relevance of these extreme tolerances remains debated.

The comparative picture reveals that freeze tolerance is not a single trait but a spectrum of adaptations employing diverse biochemical strategies to solve a common physical problem: preventing or managing intracellular ice formation. The wood frog occupies a specific and remarkable position on this spectrum—it is the most freeze-tolerant frog and among the most freeze-tolerant vertebrates documented, with Alaskan populations representing the extreme end of what vertebrate physiology has achieved in this domain.

For applied science, understanding freeze tolerance mechanisms has direct relevance to cryopreservation of organs, tissues, and cells for medical transplantation. Current organ preservation techniques are limited to short cold-storage windows (typically 4–36 hours depending on the organ); the ability to freeze and thaw complex organs without damage—as the wood frog effectively does with its entire body—remains a major unsolved problem in transplant medicine. Research on wood frog cryobiology has directly informed cryopreservation research, though translating mechanisms that evolved over millions of years of natural selection into clinical protocols remains a formidable engineering challenge (Rubinsky, 2003, *Annual Review of Biomedical Engineering*).

## §6 Verdict, Knowledge Gap, and Epistemic Assessment

**Verdict: MISCAPTIONED**

The viral claim bundles three assertions: (1) a photograph shows a frozen frog, (2) the species is the "Alaskan tree frog," and (3) this species can survive freezing solid, with its heart stopped, and subsequently thaw and resume normal activity. Assertion (3) is substantially true of the wood frog (*Rana sylvatica*), a real species that inhabits Alaska and can survive weeks with up to two-thirds of its body water converted to ice, its heart stopped and breathing ceased, subsequently thawing and recovering full physiological function (Costanzo et al., 2013, *Journal of Experimental Biology*; Layne & First, 1991, *Cryo-Letters*). Assertion (2) is false: no species called the "Alaskan tree frog" exists; neither of Alaska's two native frog species is a tree frog (Alaska Department of Fish and Game, 2008). Assertion (1) is false: the photograph appears to depict a garden ornament or decorative figurine covered with frost, not a real frog (Mikkelson, 2015).

**EARC Classification: C (Conceptual — true-fact-false-image)**

The analytical gap in this case is conceptual rather than empirical. The underlying biological facts are well-established in the peer-reviewed literature. The challenge is conceptual: how should audiences, fact-checkers, and science communicators handle cases where genuine scientific phenomena are presented through fabricated evidence? The Miscaptioned rating is adequate for the individual claim, but the broader epistemological pattern—true facts laundered through false documentation—represents an undertheorized category in misinformation research that deserves systematic attention.

The frozen frog case demonstrates that the truth value of a biological claim and the evidentiary integrity of its presentation are independent variables. A true claim can be presented with fabricated evidence, and correcting the evidence does not correct the epistemological damage caused by having initially accepted it. The audience that learned about wood frog freeze tolerance from a photograph of a garden ornament has acquired real knowledge, but they acquired it through a broken epistemic process—and that broken process, not the factual content, is what propagates when the meme is shared.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**

- Origin and creator of the original frost-covered figurine photograph: its provenance prior to the 2013 viral circulation has not been established, and whether it was originally created with deceptive intent or was repurposed from an innocent context remains unknown.
- Comparative cryoprotectant concentration data across the full latitudinal range of *Rana sylvatica*, from the southern limit of its range in Georgia and Alabama to the most northern Alaskan populations, would strengthen the understanding of how freeze tolerance scales with climate severity.
- The frequency and impact of "miscaptioned true fact" misinformation relative to purely false misinformation: no systematic analysis has quantified how common this specific misinformation type is or whether audiences who encounter it develop different epistemic behaviors than those exposed to straightforwardly false content.
- Long-term population dynamics of Alaskan wood frogs under climate change, which may alter the freeze-thaw cycling regime that these populations have adapted to survive.
