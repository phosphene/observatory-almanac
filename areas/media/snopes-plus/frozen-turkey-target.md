## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

On October 16, 2021, a photograph of a frozen turkey displaying a Target price label showing a total cost of $123,199.88 was posted in a Facebook group. The image went viral within politically conservative and law-enforcement-adjacent online communities with captions including "Biden is putting out government surplus turkeys. Get them while you can," and comments by group members attributing the price tag to President Joe Biden's economic policies, referencing inflation, governmental mismanagement, and comparisons to economic conditions in Venezuela and Cuba.

Snopes Senior Reporter Jordan Liles (2021) investigated the claim and rated it **Outdated**. The photograph, and the label it depicts, originated in November 2015—approximately six years before it recirculated in October 2021. The label itself is clearly a joke or an employee error: it shows the turkey priced at $9,999.99 per pound, which produces the astronomic total. The label also displays an expiration date of April 15, 2016, further confirming that the image predates the Biden administration by approximately five years. The original Facebook post from 2015 included joke emoji and was accompanied by a humorous caption describing the photographer's friend's discovery, establishing clear humorous intent from the outset. The image's reversion to political commentary in 2021—stripped of its 2015 context—is the specific misinformation vector.

This entry examines the image's original 2015 context, the mechanism of temporal decontextualization in viral image sharing, what the 2021 recirculation reveals about the relationship between genuine economic anxiety and politically exploitable imagery, and the research literature on outdated-image misinformation as a distinct epistemic category.

## §2 The Image's Actual Origins and the $9,999.99 Per Pound Detail

The photograph shows a Target-branded price label affixed to a frozen turkey with the following key details: a total price of $123,199.88, a per-pound price of $9,999.99, and an expiration date of April 15, 2016. The per-pound price is the operative anomaly: it is obviously and dramatically impossible as a retail turkey price, indicating that the number was either entered in error (a data-entry mistake by an employee, possibly a series of nines entered accidentally into the price field) or deliberately set as a joke by an employee or person with access to the label printing system.

Liles (2021, *Snopes*) traces the image to a Facebook post from approximately November 2015, archival analysis of which is documented in the piece. The 2015 caption read: "My friend Debbie encountered this extremely overpriced turkey at Target. What's up with poultry prices in Florida? If you have $123,000 to spend on turkey, please see me. #turkeytalk #targetturkey." The caption included a smiling emoji and two turkey emoji, clearly signaling humorous intent. The Florida geographic reference and the mention of a specific friend personalize the 2015 post in ways consistent with authentic, firsthand-observation sharing rather than fabricated political content.

The item's per-pound price renders the claim self-refuting as a literal economic data point. No frozen turkey has ever sold for $9,999.99 per pound in any documented retail context. An average 15-pound Thanksgiving turkey weighs approximately fifteen pounds; at $9,999.99 per pound, such a turkey would retail for approximately $150,000—a price that exceeds the annual median household income of the United States (approximately $70,784 as of 2021, per U.S. Census Bureau, 2022, *Current Population Survey*) by more than double. The absurdity of the price is not subtle, and recognizing it as a joke or error requires only minimal numeracy.

The Facebook group where the image recirculated in 2021 was described as "for law enforcement and their supporters only," though Liles (2021) notes that its content was dominated not by law enforcement topics but by memes supportive of Donald Trump and critical of Biden, along with far-right conspiracy theories and anti-vaccine content. The juxtaposition of the $123,000 turkey with the "Biden is putting out government surplus turkeys" caption weaponized the obvious absurdity of the 2015 joke image as political commentary—leveraging the sheer scale of the fictional number as an emotional representation of inflation rather than as a literal price claim.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Outdated Images and the Political Recirculation Lifecycle

The epistemically distinctive category this case exemplifies is what might be called *temporal decontextualization misinformation*—the phenomenon in which images or content originally created in a specific temporal and intentional context are stripped of that context and recirculated years later in a new political environment where they acquire a misleading meaning their original creators neither intended nor could have anticipated.

This category is distinct from conventional fabricated misinformation (images created with the intent to deceive), from mislabeled authentic images (genuine photographs labeled with false captions), and from Mandela Effect false memories. It is a form of what Wardle (2017, *First Draft*, First Draft News) classified as "manipulated content"—where the underlying image is real and authentic but the context provided is misleading. However, Wardle's typology was primarily designed for deliberately manipulated content; the frozen turkey case involves accidental rather than deliberate context manipulation, which creates a somewhat different epistemic and ethical status for the sharers.

Johnson and Goldwasser (2016, *Misinformation and Mass Audiences*, University of Texas Press) documented the lifecycle of viral images in political media ecosystems, finding that images with high emotional salience and number-based legibility—photographs showing specific price tags, statistics, or measurements—have particularly long circulation lifespans because the visual specificity of the number produces an impression of evidentiality. A photograph of a $123,000 price tag produces a visceral response ("I can see it right there") that is structurally resistant to verbal correction because the visual specificity is real—the photograph is genuine, the label visible, the numbers readable—even though the evidentiary inference ("prices are this high today") is false.

The research on image-based misinformation has identified a particular resilience that the frozen turkey case illustrates. Levine (2019, *Truth: A History and a Guide for the Perplexed*, St. Martin's Press) observes that photographs function as particularly strong evidence for most people because photographs appear to be unmediated records of reality—the image seems to speak for itself without an intermediary. The challenge with outdated images is precisely that this evidential function is correct in one dimension (the photograph depicts something real that actually occurred) while the contextual inference is false (this thing did not occur recently, here, or in the circumstances implied by the current caption).

For the frozen turkey specifically, the corrective challenge is asymmetric: the image is genuinely striking, the price genuinely reads $123,000, and the Target logo is real. Four of these visual data points are accurate. Only the temporal context and the political attribution are false. Corrections that focus on explaining the 2015 origin and the per-pound price error must work against a visual impression that contains substantial genuine content.

The Snopes "Outdated" verdict handles this asymmetry thoughtfully. Unlike a "False" verdict—which would mischaracterize the genuinely existing photograph—"Outdated" accurately captures the specific epistemic problem: the image is real, the label was real as an event in 2015, and the humor was genuinely found by a person in Florida; but the temporal, geographic, and political context in which the image circulated in 2021 were all substituted for the original context in ways that transformed a humorous observation into apparent political evidence.

## §4 Food Price Inflation in 2021 and the Context of Political Exploitation

The political exploitation of the turkey image in October 2021 was not random: it occurred against a backdrop of real and publicly salient food price inflation that made $123,000 turkey jokes feel emotionally resonant to audiences experiencing genuine economic stress.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics Consumer Price Index documented that food-at-home prices in the United States rose approximately 5.4% year-over-year in the 12 months ending September 2021 (BLS, 2021, *CPI for All Urban Consumers: Food at Home*). While 5.4% annual food inflation is significantly above the pre-pandemic baseline (food-at-home inflation averaged approximately 1.5–2% annually from 2015 to 2019), it is far from the conditions implied by a $9,999.99 per pound turkey. What the image offered was not literal economic data but an emotionally amplified expression of a real sentiment: food is more expensive than it used to be.

The cognitive mechanism is one that Kahneman (2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Farrar, Straus and Giroux) describes as "associative coherence"—the tendency of the mind to accept information that fits within an existing narrative without scrutinizing its literal accuracy. For an audience that was genuinely experiencing higher grocery prices in fall 2021, an image of a $123,000 turkey activated the narrative "food prices are catastrophically high under Biden" regardless of the image's actual origin. The narrative coherence provided by the political attribution overrode the intrinsic implausibility of a $9,999.99 per pound price tag.

Real turkey prices in fall 2021 were indeed elevated relative to prior years. The USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service reported that wholesale frozen turkey prices in October 2021 were approximately 10–15% above comparable 2019 levels (USDA AMS, *Poultry Market News*, 2021), reflecting pandemic-era supply chain disruptions and increased production costs. This real elevation—visible in genuine grocery store receipts—provided the emotional substrate within which the joke 2015 image could function as apparent political documentation.

## §5 The Facebook Group Context and the Politics of Law Enforcement Communities Online

Liles (2021) provides a brief but significant sociological note: the group where the image circulated identified itself as a law enforcement support community but was in practice a Trump-supportive, anti-Biden political sharing community. This context is analytically important for understanding the specific demographic and ideological profile of the image's recirculation in 2021.

Research on law enforcement-associated social media communities has documented a strong political skew toward conservative and Republican-aligned content in professionally affiliated online groups. Reisig and Chandek (2001, *Police Quarterly*, Sage) examined the organizational culture dimensions of law enforcement work that correlate with specific political orientations, finding that the occupational emphasis on order maintenance, rule enforcement, and institutional hierarchy predisposes many law enforcement professionals toward politically conservative frameworks—a finding subsequently replicated and extended by multiple researchers in the policing and criminal justice literature.

The specific characteristics of the Facebook group—identifying as law enforcement support while primarily sharing political memes—is consistent with what Benkler, Faris, and Roberts (2018, *Network Propaganda: Manipulation, Disinformation, and Radicalization in American Politics*, Oxford University Press) documented as the "hybrid" structure of right-wing political media sharing networks, in which ostensibly special-interest communities become vectors for broad political content sharing. The law enforcement label imparts credibility and community identity while the actual content function is political meme circulation—a combination that makes moderation and content labeling more difficult because the community's stated purpose is legitimate even when its actual content function is politicized misinformation sharing.

## §6 Verdict Assessment, the "Outdated" Category, and Temporal Provenance in Fact-Checking

The Snopes verdict of **Outdated** is well-calibrated to the specific epistemic situation. It correctly identifies that the claim being circulated in October 2021—implicitly, that Target was selling turkeys for $123,000 as a result of Biden-era inflation—is false and misleading. It simultaneously avoids mischaracterizing the underlying photograph as fabricated, acknowledges the joke or employee-error origin of the label, and grounds the correction in the specific evidentiary items (April 2016 expiration date, $9,999.99/lb per-pound price, 2015 original Facebook post) that collectively establish the image's pre-Biden-administration origin.

The explicit "Context" note accompanying the verdict—"A picture of a frozen turkey at Target with a $123,199.88 price tag was first posted in 2015, was a joke or a mistake, and had nothing to do with politics"—condenses the full evidentiary picture into a single sentence. The three claims in this sentence are precisely the three evidentiary items that the investigation establishes: the 2015 origin, the joke/error nature of the label, and the pre-political-context origin. This correspondence between the context note and the investigative evidence demonstrates efficient epistemic compression.

The broader significance of the "Outdated" verdict category is its capacity to handle the growing corpus of recirculating historical images that are technically accurate as photographs but factually misleading in their current attribution context. As social media platforms have accumulated years of user-generated content, and as politically motivated actors have discovered the power of archival image retrieval and recontextualization, the outdated-image misinformation category has grown substantially. Research by Nightingale, Wade, and Watson (2017, *Psychological Science*, Sage) on image memory and credibility found that images encountered even once produce lasting memory impressions that are resistant to correction—suggesting that recirculating images from 2015 can generate false impressions in 2021 audiences that the correct 2015 context, even when provided, may not fully remediate.

This case is thus not merely a curious artifact of the Thanksgiving 2021 news cycle but a representative example of a growing misinformation vector: the archive as source of weaponized historical content, the emotional amplification of real economic experience through demonstrably false numerical specificity, and the specific challenge that "outdated" images pose for fact-checking systems designed primarily to address contemporaneous fabrications.
