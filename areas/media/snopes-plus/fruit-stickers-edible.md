## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

Price Look-Up (PLU) code stickers—the small adhesive labels affixed to individual pieces of fresh produce such as apples, pears, mangoes, avocados, and bananas in retail grocery settings—have been the subject of a persistent claim, circulating widely since approximately 2014, that they are made from "edible paper" and use food-grade adhesive, therefore making them safe and even intended for consumption. The claim is often framed as a surprising consumer "life hack" or surprising food fact: you can eat the sticker, too.

Snopes investigator Kim LaCapria (2015) assigned the claim the verdict **Mostly False**. The nuanced finding is that: (a) PLU code stickers are manufactured using food-contact-compliant materials and food-grade adhesive, meaning that accidentally consuming one is unlikely to cause harm in healthy adults under normal circumstances; but (b) the stickers are not specifically manufactured from any material designed for intentional periodic consumption, are not tested or approved for routine consumption, provide no nutritional benefit, taste poor, present a choking hazard particularly to young children and elderly individuals, and are explicitly not recommended for consumption by FDA guidance and produce industry standards. The distinction between "not acutely harmful if accidentally consumed" and "edible" is the central epistemic issue in this claim.

## §2 What PLU Code Stickers Are Actually Made Of

The International Federation for Produce Standards (IFPS) administers the global PLU code system, assigning four- or five-digit numerical codes to fresh produce based on commodity type, growing method (conventional, organic, or GMO), and variety. The sticker itself consists of three components: the face stock (the printed label material), the adhesive backing, and often a liner that is removed before the sticker is applied at the packing house or retail level. The face stock for most produce labels is a thin polymer film (commonly polypropylene, polyethylene, or sometimes paper), the adhesive is typically a pressure-sensitive acrylic or rubber-based formulation, and both are selected for compatibility with the FDA's framework for food contact materials.

The FDA regulates materials that contact food under 21 CFR Parts 170–178 (food additives) and 21 CFR Part 175–177 (indirect food additives for adhesives, coatings, and polymers). Under this framework, a label applied to the exterior of a fruit or vegetable with an edible peel (such as an apple or pear) is classified as an "indirect food additive" because trace amounts of the label materials may migrate through the peel into the edible portion. This classification does NOT mean the label is designed to be eaten; it means that the materials must be selected to ensure that migration into the actual food does not create a public health risk at residue levels expected under normal use.

LaCapria's investigation found that label manufacturers consistently described their produce labels as "FDA compliant" or "food contact compliant," which is accurate but not equivalent to "edible." One label manufacturer's sales copy quoted in the Snopes piece made the distinction explicit: a label on an orange or banana peel is considered an "indirect food additive" because the food itself is not directly affected by the adhesive elements on the peel—the peel serves as a barrier. The food contact substance framework applies to ensure safety of trace migration, not to certify whole-label consumption.

No major commercial produce label manufacturer identified in LaCapria's investigation specifically described their product as "edible paper" in product specifications or technical documentation. Wiki-How, cited as a source by the HLN article that popularized the claim, did not provide any manufacturer documentation or FDA citation supporting the "edible paper" characterization. The claim therefore appears to rest on a logical inference—"FDA compliance + food-grade adhesive = edible"—that does not follow from the regulatory framework it cites.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Regulatory Inference Errors and the "FDA-Approved = Safe to Eat" Heuristic

The most important epistemic feature of the fruit sticker claim is the mechanism of error: a *regulatory inference fallacy* in which a materials compliance designation is treated as a consumption endorsement. The claim derives its apparent authority from the fact that FDA regulations genuinely do apply to produce sticker materials, and the adhesives genuinely are selected for food-contact safety. The error is the inference that these regulatory facts, taken together, imply positive endorsement of consumption—when in fact they imply only the much weaker claim that accidental or incidental consumption is unlikely to cause acute harm.

This regulatory inference error is a particularly common pattern in consumer food and product safety communication. Simoneau et al. (2000, *Food Additives and Contaminants*, vol. 17, no. 10) documented systematic public misunderstanding of "food grade" and "GRAS" (Generally Recognized as Safe) designations, finding that consumers routinely interpret these technical regulatory categories as broader safety endorsements than the regulatory text supports. The GRAS designation under 21 CFR Part 182, for example, covers compounds at specific use levels in specific food types; extrapolating GRAS status from labeled use to unlabeled use does not follow from the regulatory determination.

Applied to produce labels: the FDA's indirect food additive framework certifies that a produce sticker, affixed to the outside of a piece of fruit and present in the consumer's environment during normal fruit consumption, will not transfer harmful residues to the fruit pulp under normal handling conditions. This is a very specific claim about a very specific exposure scenario (migration through peel during storage and handling), not a general endorsement of sticker ingestion. The regulatory framework does not require that the sticker be tested for what happens when it is chewed, subjected to stomach acid, or ingested in significant quantities by people with particular digestive sensitivities.

The novelty here is the *directionality of the inference failure*: the claim does not misrepresent the regulatory facts but draws an inference from correct facts that the framework was not designed to support. This is epistemically distinct from a false claim about regulatory status, harder to correct, and potentially more durable because the corrector cannot simply say "the facts you cite are wrong"—instead, the corrector must explain the limits of what a true regulatory fact actually implies. Public health communication research (Fischoff, 1994, in *Risk Communication*, Cambridge University Press) has documented that conditional, qualification-heavy corrections tend to be less sticky than simple true-or-false corrections, meaning that the "correct but overextended inference" class of misinformation may be systematically more resistant to debunking than simple factual errors.

Additionally, the "edible paper" claim introduces a specific material category—wafer paper, rice paper, and similar food-grade cellulose substrates used in professional confectionery decoration—as an implicit reference class for fruit stickers. Genuine edible paper products do exist: rice paper is a centuries-old food product; wafer paper (made from potato starch, water, and vegetable oil) is used in pharmaceutical tablets and confectionery decoration; and edible printing paper (made from starch or modified cellulose) is used in food-grade printing applications. None of these materials, however, are used in standard PLU code sticker manufacture, where cost, durability, printability, and peel adhesion characteristics favor polymer-based label stocks over edible substrates. The claim that fruit stickers are made of "edible paper" conflates a genuine product category (edible paper for confectionery use) with a different product category (polymer-based produce labels with food-contact compliance) based only on the shared word "paper."

## §4 The Choking Hazard Problem

The safety concern most directly undermined by the "fruit stickers are edible" claim is the choking hazard presented by label films in young children and, to a lesser extent, elderly adults with dysphagia. The FDA spokesperson quoted in the 1998 newspaper article cited by LaCapria reportedly noted five to six complaints about people eating produce stickers, including one choking incident, and specifically emphasized that consumer responsibility included removing "extraneous materials" before consuming produce—particularly for children. The Facebook page cited in LaCapria's piece contained additional anecdotal accounts of produce-label choking events.

Produce labels are thin, flexible, adhesive on one side, and sized to be a choking risk for any child under four years old according to the American Academy of Pediatrics' published choking hazard standards (AAP Council on Injury, Violence, and Poison Prevention, 2010, *Pediatrics*, vol. 125, no. 3). The adhesive properties mean the label can adhere to mucosal surfaces in the oropharynx, potentially increasing obstruction risk relative to a non-adhesive object of the same size. The FDA's own produce washing guidance states that consumers should "remove all stickers or wax seals" before consuming whole fruits, a directive inconsistent with a "stickers are intentionally edible" framing.

The social media lifecycle of the "fruit stickers are edible" claim has produced observable downstream effects: parenting forums and pediatric health communities have repeatedly had to counter the suggestion that giving children fruit with PLU stickers attached is acceptable because "the stickers are edible anyway." This illustrates a concrete harm pathway from the regulatory inference error: a claim that begins as a misread of food-contact compliance documentation ends as a child safety concern in pediatric consumption contexts.

## §5 The Laser Etching Alternative: Evidence That Stickers Are Not Intended as Food

A 2007 news report on produce labeling technology, cited by LaCapria, is particularly illuminating in the context of the "edible sticker" claim. A Georgia company at that time was seeking FDA approval for a laser technology that would etch indelible but edible markings directly into fruit and vegetable skin, replacing PLU code stickers. The company described the advantage as eliminating "pesky stickers" that consumers needed to peel off before eating.

The existence and commercial motivation of this alternative labeling technology is strong evidence that the produce industry does not regard current PLU code stickers as intended edible items. If the current stickers were already edible and accepted as such by both the FDA and consumers, there would be no commercial motivation to develop a laser-etching alternative on grounds of eliminating the sticker removal step. The fact that the produce and label industries have invested in developing edible-marking alternatives specifically to replace stickers that require removal demonstrates industry awareness that removal is expected and that current sticker materials do not satisfy an edibility criterion.

Laser etching technology for produce has subsequently advanced considerably. Xenon Corp. and several European research consortia have developed and commercialized laser scoring systems for citrus and avocado that ablate the outermost layer of the peel to create permanent visible product information without any added label material (López-Reyes et al., 2016, *Computers and Electronics in Agriculture*, vol. 124). These systems have received regulatory approval in multiple jurisdictions specifically as an alternative to adhesive labeling, further confirming the regulatory and industry understanding that traditional produce labels are materials to be removed before consumption, not food-grade substances incorporated into the eating experience.

## §6 Implications: The "Mostly False" Verdict and Consumer Risk Calibration

LaCapria's "Mostly False" verdict reflects a genuinely complex risk landscape. The claim is not wholly false: accidentally consuming a PLU code sticker while eating an apple is unlikely to cause any clinically significant harm in a healthy adult. The materials used are selected for food-contact compliance; the adhesive is food-grade; the sticker is not made from toxic substances. An adult who eats an apple and accidentally swallows the PLU code sticker without noticing has not been poisoned.

The claim is mostly false because: the material is not manufactured to an "edible" specification; it is not designed for routine consumption; it provides no nutritional or sensory value and tastes poor; governing regulatory standards explicitly require consumers to remove it before eating; it presents practical choking hazard risks for children and vulnerable adults; and the "edible paper" characterization is not supported by any documented produce label product specification or manufacturer claim.

The risk calibration issue is broader than this single claim. Consumer food safety communication benefits from accurate proportionality: distinguishing between "harmless if accidentally consumed once" and "intended for consumption" is a low-cost clarification with real-world benefit, particularly in contexts (child feeding, institutional food service) where the distinction matters. A produce sticker is roughly comparable in its risk profile to a coffee filter: not poisonous if accidentally consumed, not intended for routine ingestion, not tested or approved for it, and a possible choking risk in vulnerable populations. No one would claim coffee filters are "edible" on the grounds that they are made from food-contact-compliant paper.

The appropriate summary: wash your produce, remove the sticker, and do not worry if you accidentally eat one—but do remove it, especially when feeding young children, and do not accept "FDA-compliant materials" as equivalent to "approved for eating."
