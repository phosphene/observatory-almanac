# Are California Fruits Irrigated with Toxic Fracking Wastewater?

## §1 — Claim & Verdict Summary

In early February 2016, social media users began circulating posts alerting consumers that specific brands of California-grown produce — notably tangerines and citrus products marketed under brands such as Halos and Bee Sweet Citrus — were "grown in toxic wastewater from oil company fracking activities" and therefore posed health risks to consumers, including children. The claims circulated alongside calls to boycott the implicated brands and to investigate the agribusinesses and oil companies involved. The posts drew on EcoWatch coverage of a documentary series, *Spotlight California*, as well as on prior reporting from Bloomberg and Mother Jones.

**Snopes verdict: MIXTURE.** This is one of the more nuanced Snopes verdicts in the food-contamination category because the underlying factual record is genuinely complex and contested. The following claims are substantially true: (1) some California crops in Kern County are irrigated with treated wastewater produced by Chevron's oil operations; (2) this practice has continued for more than 20 years under permits from the Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board; (3) in 2014, 11 waste-injection wells in Kern County were shut down as a precautionary measure due to aquifer management failures involving disputed jurisdictional lines between state and federal regulators; and (4) some testing of area water resources found elevated arsenic and nitrate levels, though attributing these to oil operations versus natural geological sources was contested.

The following claims are materially false or unsupported: (1) that the irrigation water has been "proved" to contain toxic pollutants from fracking specifically; (2) that the wells in Kern Front Field or Kern River Field supplying water to the Cawelo Water District used fracking operations (they did not); (3) that independent testing confirmed the irrigation water was "laced with carcinogens" at levels that contaminate the fruit; and (4) that any regulatory standard for irrigation water or finished fruit had been violated.

## §2 — Origin & Spread of the Claim

The immediate trigger was EcoWatch's 10 February 2016 summary of *Spotlight California*, Episode 2, in which filmmaker Nadia Unsal Deol documented the Cawelo Water District's use of treated oil field water. The episode featured Tom Frantz, a retired almond farmer and environmental activist who had long opposed water practices in the Kern County region, who argued that the irrigation water was "toxic" and untested. A separate segment featured environmental scientist Scott Smith covertly testing water near the Cawelo treatment plant, reportedly finding "oil and these nasty solvents."

The EcoWatch summary was then amplified through Facebook and environmental advocacy networks, acquiring increasingly alarming framing at each stage of re-transmission. By the time the claim reached mass social media audiences, it had been reframed as: "Halos tangerines are grown in toxic fracking wastewater laced with carcinogens — your children are eating these." This framing combined three separate factual errors: that the water came from fracking (it came from conventional oil production wells), that it had been shown to be toxic (it had passed regulatory testing), and that carcinogens had been detected in the fruit itself (no published study had examined this question at all).

The claim's longevity was enabled by the genuine complexity of the underlying regulatory story, which provided audiences with enough legitimate ambiguity to sustain concern even after detailed rebuttal. This is distinct from pure hoaxes (which can be definitively falsified) and from clear-cut safety violations (which can be definitively confirmed) — it occupies an intermediate epistemic zone where reasonable scientific uncertainty is available for motivated exploitation.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Fracking vs. Produced Water, and the Science of Irrigation Risk

**3.1 — Produced Water vs. Fracking Wastewater: A Critical Distinction**

The claim's most misleading element — and the one that most directly enabled its virality — was equating "oil field produced water" with "fracking wastewater." Hydraulic fracturing (fracking) is a specific drilling technique that involves injecting high-pressure water-chemical mixtures into shale formations to fracture rock and release trapped hydrocarbons. Fracking generates distinctive wastewater containing the injected chemicals, formation water released under pressure, and naturally occurring radioactive materials (NORM) from the fractured rock.

Conventional oil production — including the operations in Kern County's Kern River Field and Kern Front Field that supplied the Cawelo Water District — involves vertical or directional drilling into permeable rock formations, without the pressure-fracturing step. The resulting produced water is co-extracted crude oil and water from the formation and contains different chemical profiles than fracking-specific flowback fluid. Lois Henry, a reporter covering the Bakersfield area who was cited by KCET in September 2015, explicitly noted: "Wells in those fields are not fracked, by the way." This point was confirmed by general geological characteristics of the Kern River field, which produces heavy crude from a relatively shallow, permeable sand formation that does not require hydraulic fracturing.

The conflation of "fracking wastewater" with "produced water from conventional oil wells" was not merely imprecise — it materially distorted the risk profile. The chemical additives used in hydraulic fracturing (biocides, scale inhibitors, friction reducers, surfactants) are the primary source of concern about fracking-specific environmental contamination. Produced water from conventional wells does not contain these additives, though it may contain naturally occurring oil, brine, trace metals, and other compounds from the formation.

**3.2 — Phytotransfer Risk: Can Irrigation Water Contaminants Enter Fruit?**

The underlying public-health question — whether contaminants in irrigation water can enter the edible portions of crops — is a scientifically active area of study. The mechanisms by which plants take up compounds from irrigation water include root absorption (for water-soluble compounds), foliar deposition (for spray irrigation), and soil accumulation over time. The risk profile depends critically on the chemical species, its soil-binding characteristics, and the plant's uptake dynamics.

Shonkoff, Seth B. C., Morello-Frosch, Rachel, Pastor, Manuel, and Sadd, James (2011, *Environmental Health Disparities and the Oil and Gas Industry*, Environmental Health Perspectives, 119(12), 1665–1670) documented elevated health risk in communities adjacent to oil and gas operations, based predominantly on air-quality exposure pathways rather than food exposure. No study in the peer-reviewed literature had, as of 2016 (and Snopes's review), examined the specific question of whether produced water from Kern County's Cawelo irrigation program led to detectable contaminant levels in citrus or nut crops.

Newsweek's April 2015 coverage quoted Seth Shonkoff, then at the University of California, Berkeley: "There might not be a single risk out there with this practice. But the biggest risk that we have right now is that we just don't know ... to Shonkoff's knowledge, no scientist has ever published a study on what compounds from the oil development process might be in oilfield wastewater used on crops." This epistemic admission — honest and important — was widely extracted from context and used to support strong claims of proven hazard rather than acknowledged uncertainty.

**3.3 — The Arsenic Detection Controversy and Its Resolution**

Water Defense, an advocacy organization led by actor and environmental activist Mark Ruffalo, conducted independent water testing in 2014 and 2015 that reported elevated concentrations of methylene chloride and other solvents in water samples from the region. These results were widely cited in advocacy coverage. However, subsequent testing by the Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board found no methylene chloride at detectable levels, and the LA Times — which had originally reported Water Defense's findings — issued corrections noting that earlier concentration figures had been overstated.

Chevron's 2015 statement indicated that testing "showed no heavy metals or chemical toxins were present in the water above maximum allowable levels," with the exception of arsenic, which appeared at elevated concentrations but had been "fully addressed in the process of obtaining the permit from the Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board." The Board's permit process, in turn, acknowledged that arsenic in this portion of the Central Valley may reflect natural geological leaching rather than oil field contamination.

This multi-party testing dispute — in which a regulatory testing program produced different results from advocacy testing that had already been widely reported — is itself a microcosm of science communication failure. When advocacy testing and regulatory testing produce conflicting results, the public and media must evaluate methodological differences, potential conflicts of interest on both sides, and the relative authority of different institutional actors. This is genuinely difficult, and the media coverage largely abdicated the challenge by defaulting to the more alarming of the two result sets.

## §4 — Regulatory & Scientific Context

**4.1 — Cawelo Water District Operations**

The Cawelo Water District operates under permits from the Central Valley Regional Water Quality Control Board, which sets allowable concentrations for irrigation water contaminants. The District combines Chevron produced water with fresh water at varying ratios and distributes the blended product to approximately 90 farmers across roughly 34,000 acres growing almonds, citrus, grapes, and other crops. The program has operated for more than two decades.

California's State Water Resources Control Board conducts periodic oversight of the Regional Boards' irrigation water permitting programs. The 2014–2016 controversy prompted enhanced scrutiny, additional testing, and a legislative proposal (SB 1281, California Senate) that would have required labeling of food grown with produced water. The labeling bill failed to advance in part because the evidentiary basis for a health risk — the foundational requirement for a food labeling mandate — was never established.

**4.2 — The Aquifer Injection Controversy**

Separately from the Cawelo irrigation issue, the broader North American oil industry was simultaneously embroiled in controversy over waste-injection wells contaminating protected aquifers. California's Division of Oil, Gas and Geothermal Resources (DOGGR) acknowledged in 2014 that a decades-long jurisdictional ambiguity between state and federal (EPA) aquifer designation had resulted in injection wells operating in protected aquifers. The 11-well shutdown in Kern County was a direct response to this discovery. Tests of nearby drinking-water wells showed no contamination attributable to the injection operations, though officials noted that contamination "can migrate over time" and the situation warranted ongoing monitoring.

This regulatory failure — real, documented, and substantive — was systematically conflated with the separate Cawelo irrigation issue in media coverage. The injection-well story and the irrigation-water story involve different operations, different chemicals, different exposure pathways, and different regulatory frameworks. Their conflation created a composite narrative of pervasive oil-industry contamination of California water resources that was substantially more alarming than either individual story warranted.

## §5 — Comparative Misinformation Ecology

This case differs structurally from most food-contamination rumors because it arises from a region of genuine scientific and regulatory uncertainty rather than from a premise that is simply false. The Cawelo irrigation water story is a legitimate environmental justice concern deserving serious investigation. Kern County has some of the worst air quality and highest industrial pollution levels of any agricultural region in the United States. Farmworker communities in the area face disproportionate health burdens from multiple exposure pathways. A fair public health investigation of produced-water irrigation is warranted and, after 2016, was increasingly pursued by academic researchers at UC system institutions.

The epistemic failure was not in raising the concern but in treating uncertainty as evidence of harm. The standard epistemological principle — in the absence of evidence, suspend judgment — was replaced in much of the media coverage with a precautionary conflation: "nobody has tested this yet" was systematically translated into "we know it's bad." This is a violation of basic inferential standards that is nonetheless extremely common in environmental health journalism, where the precautionary principle is sometimes applied not as a policy tool (appropriate) but as an epistemic standard (inappropriate — it does not substitute for evidentiary inference).

Renn, Ortwin, and Levine, David (1991, *Credibility and Trust in Risk Communication*, in R. E. Kasperson & P. J. M. Stallen (Eds.), *Communicating Risks to the Public*, Kluwer Academic) note that regulatory credibility is systematically eroded when public communication about risk fails to distinguish between "we are investigating a potential problem" and "we have confirmed a problem." The Cawelo case is a textbook example of this failure mode, with multiple institutional actors (advocacy groups, journalism outlets, social media amplifiers) collapsing that distinction in the direction of maximum alarm.

## §6 — Verdict, Epistemic Gap Assessment & Reader Guidance

**Snopes verdict: MIXTURE.** The factual foundation is mixed: real regulatory failures (aquifer injection), real ongoing practices (produced-water irrigation), and real scientific uncertainty (no published studies on crop uptake of produced-water contaminants) coexist with demonstrably false claims (fracking connection, proven toxicity, carcinogens in fruit).

**EARC Rating: C (Contested — Nuanced Truth)** — The claim is neither simply true nor simply false. Both legitimate public-interest concerns and demonstrably inaccurate specifics are present in the evidence record.

**Knowledge Gap (High):**
1. No peer-reviewed study had, as of the Snopes publication, examined whether produced-water irrigation in the Cawelo District produces detectable contaminants in edible crop portions. This is the most significant remaining factual gap.
2. Long-term soil accumulation effects of produced-water irrigation on heavy metals and trace organics remain an open scientific question.
3. The resolution of the multi-party testing dispute (Water Defense vs. Regional Board findings) was never independently adjudicated by a disinterested laboratory.

**Reader guidance:**
- The legitimate concern here — whether California agribusiness's use of oil field produced water to irrigate food crops is safe for consumers and for the long-term health of the region's soil and groundwater — deserves serious ongoing scientific attention. Consumer awareness of this practice is appropriate.
- However, claims that Halos tangerines or other specific products are "grown in toxic fracking wastewater" conflate unrelated operations and overstate the evidentiary record. Boycotts predicated on the fracking-specific claim are based on a factual error.
- For up-to-date information on produced-water irrigation in California, the State Water Resources Control Board publishes inspection records and permit conditions online.
