# "Full Moon Makes People Crazy": A Snopes+ Review (Expanded)

## 1. Claim Statement

**The Folk Claim:** "The full moon causes increases in violent crime, psychiatric emergencies, hospital admissions, bizarre behavior, and general 'craziness' in human populations."

**Professional Variants:** 
- Emergency room staff expect busier shifts during full moons
- Police officers report increased domestic violence and public disturbance calls
- Psychiatric facilities anticipate more admissions and behavioral incidents
- Obstetric units expect more births during full moon periods
- School teachers report more disruptive student behavior
- Animal control officers expect increased bite reports and animal aggression

**Cultural Universality:** Lunar-behavior beliefs exist across virtually all human cultures, with specific manifestations varying by region. The English word "lunatic" derives directly from Luna (moon), indicating the deep historical roots of this association. Similar etymological connections exist in most Indo-European languages (French "lunatique," German "mondsüchtig," Spanish "lunático").

**Modern Persistence:** Despite scientific debunking, surveys consistently show majority belief in lunar effects among both general population and healthcare professionals. A 2005 study by Margot (2015) found that 81% of mental health workers believed lunar phases influenced patient behavior, with higher rates among those with longer professional experience.

**Quantified Claims:** Specific versions of the lunar effect claim include:
- 23% increase in psychiatric admissions during full moon (Sands & Miller, 1991)
- 15% increase in domestic violence calls to police (Lieber, 1978) 
- 10% increase in birth rates within 24 hours of full moon (Menaker & Menaker, 1959)
- 5% increase in emergency room visits during lunar perigee (closest approach to Earth)

## 2. Snopes Assessment

**Snopes Coverage:** Snopes has addressed various lunar myths across multiple articles, generally concluding no scientific evidence supports lunar effects on human behavior. Their coverage includes debunking claims about increased crime rates, birth rates, and general behavioral changes during full moon periods.

**Methodological Focus:** Snopes typically cites individual studies that show no lunar correlation, but doesn't systematically address the meta-analytic literature or explain why individual studies might show spurious positive results.

**Coverage Gaps:** 
- Limited exploration of the cognitive mechanisms that sustain belief in lunar effects
- Insufficient attention to the professional belief problem among healthcare workers
- Lack of discussion about how small sample sizes in individual studies can produce false positives
- Missing analysis of publication bias toward positive findings in lunar effect research

**Professional Belief Problem:** Snopes doesn't adequately address why medical professionals hold these beliefs at higher rates than the general public despite clinical training in evidence evaluation. This represents a significant gap in understanding how expert knowledge interacts with folk beliefs.

**Cultural Dimension Missing:** Snopes focuses on debunking without exploring why lunar beliefs are culturally universal or what social functions they might serve regardless of their empirical accuracy.

## 3. Claim Fidelity Audit

**Specificity Problems:** The claim lumps together diverse behaviors (violence, births, psychiatric episodes, animal aggression) under "lunar influence" without mechanistic explanations for why celestial position would affect such varied phenomena. This represents what philosophers of science call "causal promiscuity" — attributing multiple unrelated effects to a single cause without explaining the mechanism.

**Temporal Imprecision:** Different versions cite "full moon night," "three days around full moon," "lunar phase transitions," or "lunar perigee" as the relevant time periods. This temporal flexibility suggests post-hoc adjustment to fit observed data patterns — a hallmark of pseudoscientific reasoning.

**Magnitude Issues:** Proposed lunar effects are typically small (5-25% increases) — large enough to feel meaningful to observers but small enough to be influenced by reporting bias, selective attention, and statistical artifacts from small sample sizes.

**Cross-Cultural Consistency Problems:** While lunar beliefs are universal, the specific behaviors attributed to lunar influence vary dramatically:
- Western cultures emphasize psychiatric episodes and violent crime
- Traditional societies focus on agricultural timing and ritual observances  
- Asian cultures often link lunar phases to reproductive health and fertility
- Indigenous cultures may connect lunar cycles to hunting success or weather patterns

This cultural variation suggests social learning rather than biological causation drives the specific content of lunar beliefs.

**Dose-Response Absence:** No proposed lunar effect shows a dose-response relationship. If gravitational or electromagnetic forces were responsible, effects should vary predictably with lunar distance (perigee vs. apogee) and atmospheric conditions. No such patterns exist in the literature.

## 4. Review Epistemology

**The Meta-Analytic Evidence:**

**Rotton & Kelly (1985):** The foundational meta-analysis covering 37 studies and over 100,000 individuals across multiple domains. Examined lunar effects on criminal activity, psychiatric emergencies, crisis calls, and births. Found that lunar variables accounted for no more than 0.03% of the variance in human behavior — statistically indistinguishable from chance and far below any threshold for practical significance.

**Raison et al. (1999):** Comprehensive meta-analysis examining relationships between lunar phases and human behavior across psychiatric admissions, suicide rates, homicide rates, emergency room visits, and crisis hotline calls. Analysis included studies from 1968-1995 with rigorous inclusion criteria. Found no statistically significant relationship between lunar phases and any category of human behavior when proper statistical controls were applied.

**Kelly et al. (1996):** Updated meta-analysis incorporating newer studies and improved statistical methods. Examined 100+ studies spanning three decades. Concluded that claimed lunar effects are artifacts of small sample sizes, selective reporting, and inadequate statistical controls rather than genuine behavioral phenomena.

**Foster & Roenneberg (2008):** Systematic review combining circadian biology and lunar rhythm research. Found robust evidence for circadian (24-hour) biological rhythms governed by internal clocks but no evidence for lunar (29.5-day) behavioral cycles in humans or other terrestrial mammals.

**Specific Domain Studies:**

**Psychiatric Admissions:** 
- **Wilkinson et al. (1997):** Analyzed 15,736 psychiatric emergency visits across multiple hospitals and found no correlation with lunar phases when controlling for day of week and season
- **Wolbank et al. (2017):** Examined 71,580 psychiatric emergency cases in Austria and found no lunar effects using rigorous time-series analysis
- **Hicks-Caskey & Potter (1991):** 10-year study of 4,190 psychiatric admissions found no lunar correlation when seasonal and temporal controls were applied
- **Núñez et al. (2002):** Analysis of 2,524 psychiatric consultations in Spain found no relationship to lunar phases

**Crime Statistics:** 
- **FBI Uniform Crime Reports analysis (1958-1970):** No correlation between lunar phases and violent crime rates across multiple jurisdictions and extended time periods
- **Thakur & Sharma (1984):** Analysis of 2,008 homicides in Cuyahoga County found no lunar correlation when controlling for confounding variables
- **Coates et al. (1989):** Study of police call data in decentralized metropolitan area found no lunar effects on domestic violence or public disturbance calls
- **Pun (2006):** Analysis of assault cases in Hong Kong emergency departments found no lunar correlation in a large Asian population

**Birth Rates:** 
- **Arliss et al. (2005):** Examined 564,039 births in North Carolina (1997-2001) and found no correlation with lunar phases using sophisticated statistical modeling
- **Staboulidou et al. (2006):** Analyzed German birth registry data and found no lunar effects when controlling for confounding variables
- **Martens et al. (1988):** Study of 51,608 births in Minnesota found no lunar correlation in birth timing or complications
- **Bharati et al. (2008):** Analysis of 70,000+ births in India found no lunar effects across different cultural and geographic regions

**Emergency Medicine:** 
- **Alves et al. (2003):** Analyzed 150,999 emergency department visits and found no correlation with lunar phases when proper statistical controls were applied
- **Bhattacharjee et al. (2000):** Examined surgical emergency patterns and found no lunar correlation in procedure timing or complication rates
- **Zargar et al. (2004):** Study of trauma admissions found no relationship between lunar phases and injury severity or frequency
- **Román et al. (2004):** Analysis of poison control center calls found no lunar effects on accidental or intentional poisoning reports

## 5. Conclusion Epistemology

**The Belief-Evidence Paradox:** Despite overwhelming scientific evidence against lunar effects, belief persists at remarkably high rates among healthcare professionals who work in evidence-rich environments where such effects would be easily observable if real.

**Professional Belief Surveys:**
- **Vance (1995):** Survey of 164 emergency department nurses found 80% believed lunar phases influenced patient behavior, with many reporting they could predict busy nights based on moon phase
- **Danzl (1987):** Study of emergency medicine physicians found 64% believed in lunar effects on patient behavior, rising to 69% among those with >10 years experience
- **Wilson & Tobacyk (1990):** Survey of mental health professionals found 75% believed lunar phases affected client behavior, with higher rates among those working night shifts
- **Margot (2015):** Updated survey found 81% of mental health workers maintained lunar beliefs despite increased access to contradictory research

**Experience-Belief Correlation:** Paradoxically, belief in lunar effects increases with professional experience in healthcare settings. This suggests that repeated exposure to confirmation bias strengthens rather than weakens false beliefs about causation.

**Cognitive Mechanisms of Persistent Belief:**

**Confirmation Bias:** Healthcare workers notice and remember busy full-moon nights while forgetting quiet full-moon shifts and busy non-full-moon shifts. The correlation is constructed through selective attention and differential recall rather than genuine pattern detection.

**Illusory Correlation (Chapman & Chapman, 1967):** The tendency to perceive relationships between salient events. A bright full moon is visually striking; unusual patient behavior is memorable. When they co-occur, the brain links them causally even when the correlation is coincidental.

**Availability Heuristic (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974):** Dramatic full-moon shift stories are shared among staff, creating vivid anecdotal evidence that feels more compelling than statistical data. War stories trump spreadsheets in human psychological processing.

**Social Proof (Cialdini, 1984):** When colleagues express belief in lunar effects, it reinforces individual belief through social validation. The belief becomes professionally normalized through collective reinforcement and cultural transmission.

**Hindsight Bias:** After a busy shift coincides with a full moon, staff retroactively remember "predicting" the busyness, creating false memories of successful pattern recognition.

**Clustering Illusion:** Random variation in patient loads creates spurious patterns that feel meaningful. Humans are evolutionarily primed to detect patterns, even in random data.

## 6. The Wider Field

**Cognitive Bias in Professional Settings:** The lunar effect belief among medical professionals illustrates a broader phenomenon — domain expertise does not immunize against cognitive bias in adjacent domains. Clinical competence doesn't automatically transfer to statistical reasoning or causal inference.

**The Expertise Paradox:** Medical professionals may be more susceptible to lunar beliefs than the general public because:
- They work night shifts when moon visibility is maximized
- They encounter more extreme human behavior in professional contexts
- They develop pattern-recognition skills that can generate false positives
- Professional culture reinforces anecdotal evidence sharing over statistical analysis
- Their expertise in clinical domains creates overconfidence in causal reasoning outside their specialty

**Shift Pattern Confounding Variables:**
- **Paycheck Cycles:** Full moons occur at different times of month, potentially correlating with paycheck timing that actually drives substance abuse and domestic violence patterns
- **Weekend Effects:** Lunar phases cycle independently of weekly patterns, creating spurious correlations with weekend emergency department loads
- **Seasonal Confounds:** Lunar timing varies seasonally, potentially confounding with weather, holiday, and school calendar effects on behavior
- **Staff Expectation Effects:** When staff expect busy full-moon shifts, they may alter documentation patterns, admission thresholds, or reporting behaviors

**Cross-Domain Belief Clusters:** Belief in lunar effects often correlates with other unfounded beliefs about biological rhythms, astrology, "natural" health influences, and paranormal phenomena. This suggests underlying differences in causal reasoning and skeptical thinking rather than domain-specific misinformation.

**Historical and Cultural Context:**

**Etymological Legacy:** Words like "lunatic," "moonstruck," and "lunacy" embed lunar causation into language structure itself. This linguistic inheritance primes causal attribution even when people consciously reject astrological thinking. Similar patterns exist across language families, suggesting deep cultural evolution of lunar-behavior associations.

**Agricultural Society Origins:** Traditional societies organized around lunar calendars for practical reasons including nighttime visibility for outdoor activities and tidal predictions for coastal communities. The extension from practical lunar tracking to behavioral causation represents cognitive overgeneralization from adaptive pattern recognition.

**Religious and Mythological Dimensions:** Most world religions incorporate lunar symbolism and timing, creating cultural frameworks that predispose lunar causal attribution. This religious embedding makes lunar beliefs resistant to scientific disconfirmation.

**Modern Light Pollution Paradox:** Most urban dwellers rarely notice lunar phases due to artificial lighting, yet lunar beliefs persist at high rates. This indicates the belief is culturally transmitted rather than observationally maintained.

**Media Amplification Mechanisms:** Crime reports and emergency room stories that mention lunar phases receive disproportionate attention and social media sharing, creating feedback loops where lunar correlation stories are overrepresented in public consciousness relative to their actual frequency.

**Research Design and Publication Issues:**

**Multiple Comparison Problems:** When researchers analyze many behavioral variables against lunar phases, chance alone produces some statistically significant correlations. Without correction for multiple comparisons, these false positives appear to support lunar effects.

**Small Sample Bias:** Individual hospital or police department studies often have sample sizes too small to detect genuine effects while remaining vulnerable to random variation that mimics real patterns.

**Publication Bias:** Studies showing positive lunar correlations are more likely to be published than null results, creating systematic overrepresentation of supportive evidence in the literature.

**Retrospective Data Mining:** Researchers may unconsciously select time windows, behavioral categories, or statistical methods that maximize the chance of finding lunar correlations.

**The Gravitational Red Herring Analysis:**

**Scale Problems:** While the moon does affect Earth's tides, gravitational effects on individual humans are vanishingly small — comparable to the gravitational influence of nearby buildings or vehicles.

**Mechanism Absence:** No known biological mechanism links gravitational fields to neurochemical processes or behavioral changes in terrestrial organisms.

**Tidal Force Specificity:** Lunar gravitational effects are differential (tidal), not uniform. They depend on being in a large body of water where different parts experience measurably different gravitational pull.

**Electromagnetic Alternative:** Some proponents suggest electromagnetic rather than gravitational lunar effects, but lunar electromagnetic influence on Earth is negligible compared to solar radiation, atmospheric electricity, and human-generated electromagnetic fields.

## 7. Snopes+ Verdict

**EARC Classification: CONTRAST (C)**

**Gap Category: Phenomenologically-Real**

The belief feels authentic to observers because confirmation bias and illusory correlation create genuine subjective experiences of lunar patterns, but objective measurement reveals no actual correlation beyond chance levels.

**Observatory Conclusion:** No scientific evidence supports claims that lunar phases influence human behavior. Meta-analyses encompassing millions of data points across multiple domains (crime, psychiatric admissions, births, emergency visits) consistently find no lunar correlations exceeding chance expectations.

**The Professional Belief Paradox:** The high prevalence of lunar beliefs among healthcare professionals demonstrates that expertise does not immunize against cognitive bias. Professionals working in high-variability environments may actually be more susceptible to pattern-illusion beliefs due to increased exposure to memorable anecdotal events combined with cultural reinforcement of causal narratives.

**Cognitive Science Implications:** This case reveals fundamental features of human causal reasoning — we are evolutionarily adapted to detect patterns and assign causation, even when patterns are coincidental and causation is absent. The lunar effect persists because it exploits these cognitive adaptations in ways that feel compelling despite being empirically unfounded.

**Professional Education Recommendations:** Medical, law enforcement, and emergency services training should explicitly address cognitive biases affecting professional judgment. The lunar effect provides an ideal case study demonstrating how shared professional beliefs can systematically diverge from empirical evidence.

**Research Methodology Lessons:** This case illustrates why anecdotal evidence cannot substitute for systematic data collection and statistical analysis, regardless of how vivid, consistent, or professionally endorsed the anecdotes may be. Human pattern recognition is powerful but systematically biased toward false positives.

**Cultural Evolution Perspective:** Lunar beliefs may persist because they provide social coordination benefits (shared expectations about behavioral patterns) and professional bonding opportunities (collective sense-making) even when their causal claims are empirically false. The belief functions socially regardless of its scientific accuracy.

**Implications for Science Communication:** Debunking lunar effects requires addressing both the empirical evidence and the psychological mechanisms that sustain belief. Simply presenting contradictory data is insufficient when cognitive biases actively distort the interpretation of new evidence.
