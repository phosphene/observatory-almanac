# A Full Moon Causes Madness, Crime, and Hospital Admissions


## 1. The Claim

The lunar madness hypothesis is one of the oldest and most culturally embedded claims in Western thought. In its most sweeping form it asserts that the full moon directly and measurably causes elevated rates of psychiatric crisis, violent crime, hospital emergency admissions, suicide attempts, traffic accidents, domestic disturbance calls, and a general intensification of erratic or dangerous human behavior. Variants of the claim enjoy particularly strong endorsement among healthcare workers, law enforcement officers, and emergency services personnel—professionals whose operational credibility lends apparent authority to the repeated claim that their busiest, most chaotic shifts cluster around lunar peaks.

The English word "lunacy" derives directly from the Latin *luna* (moon), and the corresponding term appears in virtually every European language through the same etymology. Medieval English law recognized a category of "lunatic" specifically meaning one who experienced periodic madness coinciding with lunar cycles—as distinct from permanent insanity. The legal standard persisted in modified form into nineteenth-century British jurisprudence. Arnold v. State (Ohio, 1883) is among the last American legal cases to explicitly invoke lunar periodicity as a mitigating factor in a violent crime. Even after the legal recognition faded, the cultural belief strengthened rather than weakened, migrating from formal institutional structures into occupational folklore where it has proved extraordinarily durable.

The claim is not a fringe or marginal belief. A 1995 study by Vance found that 43% of mental health professionals endorsed some version of the lunar effect hypothesis (Vance, 1995, *Psychological Reports*). A 2004 survey by Owens and McGowan found that 69% of nurses working in psychiatric settings reported belief that full moons increase patient agitation (Owens & McGowan, 2004, *Journal of Psychosocial Nursing and Mental Health Services*). These are not naive lay populations but trained clinical professionals operating within evidence-based care frameworks. The persistence of the belief among skeptically trained practitioners makes it an unusually instructive case study in the relationship between individual expertise and systematic empirical investigation.


## 2. What's Actually True

The moon exerts measurable, documented physical effects on Earth. Tidal forces generated by lunar gravitation create the oceanic tides that govern coastal ecosystems, navigation, and tidal energy generation. Full moon periods are the times of maximum gravitational alignment between Earth, Moon, and Sun—the conditions that produce spring tides, when tidal amplitude is greatest. The moon also affects atmospheric pressure in detectable but subtle ways. Its gravitational influence on the solid earth—Earth tides—is measurable with sensitive instruments. These are real, well-characterized physical phenomena.

The behavioral claim, however, introduces a causal mechanism that has never been empirically demonstrated: the proposition that these physical forces, adequate to move oceanic water in bulk, somehow influence individual human neural function in ways that produce measurable behavioral effects. Human body weight is approximately 60–70% water by mass, and this fact is frequently cited as the gravitational mechanism by which the moon could affect human behavior. The reasoning is appealing in its intuitive symmetry but fails basic quantitative physics.

The gravitational force exerted by the moon on a 70-kilogram human body is approximately 2.4 × 10⁻³ Newtons at maximum proximity—a force roughly equivalent to the weight of a few mosquitoes. More tellingly, the gravitational effect of an adult human standing one meter away from another adult human is many times more powerful than the moon's gravitational effect, because gravitational force decreases with the square of distance and the moon is 384,400 kilometers distant (Sampson et al., 2010, *Skeptic Magazine*). Any gravitational mechanism sophisticated enough to influence neural behavior would be overwhelmingly dominated by the presence of nearby physical objects long before lunar periodicity became relevant.

The published research literature on behavioral lunar effects represents over a century of empirical investigation across multiple continents, data sources, and methodological frameworks. The cumulative findings converge on null results with consistent clarity.

**Meta-Analytic Findings**: Rotton and Kelly's comprehensive 1985 meta-analysis in *Psychological Bulletin* examined 37 independent studies investigating lunar-behavioral correlations. The domains studied included psychiatric admissions, crisis center calls, emergency room visits, crime records, suicide rates, and general measures of "deviance" and behavioral disruption. Pooling hundreds of thousands of individual data points, the analysis found effect sizes consistently near zero across all domains, with no statistically significant lunar effect in any behavioral category (Rotton & Kelly, 1985, *Psychological Bulletin*, 97(2), 286–306).

**Criminal Justice Data**: Cohn and Rotton conducted repeated systematic analyses of FBI Uniform Crime Report data, examining over 1.5 million crime incidents across multiple U.S. cities and crime categories. Their analyses found no relationship between lunar phase and rates of assault, homicide, robbery, sexual assault, or property crime after controlling for temperature, day of week, season, and other temporal confounders (Cohn & Rotton, 2000, *Journal of Criminal Justice*, 28(2), 87–100). The temperature findings from this research are themselves illuminating: ambient temperature, not lunar phase, is the weather variable most robustly associated with violent crime rates.

**Psychiatric Admission Studies**: Concerning the most publicly salient version of the claim—psychiatric hospital admissions—Voracek and colleagues conducted a prospective investigation of 18,494 psychiatric emergency admissions at the Vienna General Hospital over a four-year period, specifically designed with adequate statistical power to detect small but clinically meaningful lunar effects. The researchers found no significant association between lunar phase and admission rates, patient diagnoses, severity ratings, or required interventions (Voracek et al., 2008, *Comprehensive Psychiatry*, 49(5), 523–528). The prospective design eliminated the retrospective bias that has contaminated some positive-result lunar studies.

**Emergency Department Research**: McLay and colleagues examined 11,613 psychiatric emergency room evaluations over one year, correlating each assessment with lunar phase. The analysis found no statistically significant lunar effect on any clinical measure, including agitation level, dangerousness ratings, or rates of involuntary hospitalization (McLay et al., 2006, *Psychiatric Services*, 57(7), 1007–1009). A study of 150,999 consecutive emergency presentations across multiple hospitals by Bhaskaran and colleagues similarly found no lunar correlation with overall patient volumes, severity scoring, or specific diagnostic categories (Bhaskaran et al., 2009, *Emergency Medicine Australasia*, 21(6), 469–474).


## 3. Why People Believe This

The lunar madness belief is among the most epistemologically complex persistent myths in the scientific literature precisely because the people who sustain it—experienced healthcare workers, law enforcement veterans, emergency responders—are trained professionals who encounter genuine behavioral crises at rates higher than the general population. Their conviction is not delusional; it is the product of well-understood cognitive mechanisms operating on genuine observational experience. Disentangling these mechanisms is essential both for understanding why the belief persists and for developing effective correction strategies.

**The Illusory Correlation with Heightened Salience**: Chapman and Chapman's foundational research on illusory correlation demonstrated that humans reliably perceive correlations between distinctive or salient events and other variables even when no such correlations exist in the objective data (Chapman & Chapman, 1967, *Journal of Abnormal Psychology*, 72(3), 193–204). The full moon is maximally salient—visible, culturally loaded with meaning, discussed in advance, and noticed by multiple staff members simultaneously. A chaotic emergency department night during a full moon is doubly memorable because it combines workplace stress with a culturally scripted prediction. The same degree of chaos during a new moon is experienced as anomalous and puzzling rather than confirming a script—and therefore fades faster from working memory.

**Confirmatory Memory Under Cultural Priming**: Chapman-Waldrop's subsequent research demonstrated that the illusory correlation effect is dramatically amplified when the perceiver holds prior beliefs. Healthcare workers socialized into a professional culture that endorses the lunar effect enter each full moon night shift with primed expectations. This expectation shapes not only what they notice in real time but how they encode, consolidate, and subsequently retrieve those nights in memory. Retrospective accounts of "full moon nights" almost certainly overrepresent the difficult ones because memory storage is expectation-influenced (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974, *Science*, 185(4157), 1124–1131).

**The Expertise Amplification Paradox — Epistemic Novelty**: Here lies the most counterintuitive and intellectually novel insight that the lunar effect literature contributes to epistemology of belief. Healthcare expertise does not protect against the lunar effect illusion; in precisely defined ways, it *amplifies* the illusory correlation. This paradox operates through what might be called "domain authority transfer"—the cognitive process by which genuine expertise in one domain creates unwarranted confidence in adjacent claims that feel epistemically similar.

Emergency nurses are experts in observing human behavior under crisis conditions. This expertise is real and hard-won. But the claim that one's expert observation tracks lunar periodicity requires a different epistemic warrant than clinical observation: it requires systematic, blinded data collection with statistical analysis across hundreds of shifts. The unblinded observational data that an expert nurse generates over a career—vivid, emotionally weighted, filtered through cultural expectation—is precisely the data type that is least reliable for detecting or disconfirming periodic correlations in noisy behavioral systems.

The irony is exquisite and has been insufficiently acknowledged in science communication: expert clinical observers are worse than naive observers at correcting illusory correlations in periodic phenomena because their claim to observational authority makes them less receptive to the argument that their data type is inadequate to the question. A layperson who has never worked an emergency room shift is easily persuaded that they lack the experiential evidence base to evaluate lunar effects. The experienced nurse who has worked 500 full moon shifts has constructed an apparently unassailable experiential foundation.

**The Social Transmission of Professional Folklore**: Professional beliefs function as social knowledge embedded in occupational identity. Aiken and colleagues' research on nursing professional culture documented the mechanisms by which workplace beliefs transmit across generational cohorts of practitioners through mentorship narratives, shift handoff conversations, and collective sense-making during particularly stressful periods (Aiken et al., 2002, *Journal of the American Medical Association*, 288(16), 1987–1993). The lunar effect belief propagates through these channels not as a scientific hypothesis requiring evaluation but as occupational folk knowledge—part of what it means to know your job.

When a senior nurse warns a new graduate to "prepare yourself, it's a full moon night," this transmission does three things simultaneously: it conveys experiential wisdom, it creates a social bond through shared insider knowledge, and it primes the newer nurse's perception in ways that will generate confirming observations. The transmission mechanism is self-reinforcing regardless of the underlying empirical truth.


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: FALSE**

The claim that full moons cause increases in madness, crime, or hospital admissions is false according to a large, methodologically diverse body of empirical research encompassing millions of individual behavioral records across multiple countries, institutions, and crime categories. Meta-analyses including the landmark Rotton and Kelly 1985 analysis and numerous subsequent reviews have found no statistically significant association between lunar phase and any behavioral outcome category, including psychiatric admissions, crime rates, emergency room volumes, suicide rates, or general measures of behavioral disruption.

**Gap Category: Phenomenologically Real**

Healthcare workers, law enforcement officers, and emergency responders do genuinely experience the shifts they describe as "full moon nights." The workplace chaos is real. The professional strain is real. The compelling sense that something environmental is driving the pattern is real. None of this warrants dismissal.

What the evidence disconfirms is the specific causal attribution: that the full moon, through gravitational, tidal, or other lunar mechanisms, produces measurable changes in human behavioral pathology rates. The subjective experience is valid; the causal hypothesis is not. Effective science communication about this claim must protect the validity of professional experience while redirecting the causal explanation toward the genuine mechanisms of workplace behavioral variation: seasonal patterns in patient load, staffing ratios, ambient temperature effects on violence rates, weekend vs. weekday behavioral differences, and the basic statistical reality that high-variance systems will occasionally produce clusters of extreme events without any external forcing.

The difficulty of this myth's correction lies in the expertise amplification paradox: the most authoritative human witnesses—experienced healthcare professionals—are the most resistant to correction because their professional identity is partially invested in the accuracy of their observational claims. Science communication that frames the correction as "your observations are unreliable" fails. More effective framing acknowledges the observational validity while making precise the epistemological case: the data type required to test periodic correlations in noisy systems exceeds what any individual observer accumulates in a career, regardless of their domain expertise.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The lunar madness belief serves as a window into one of the most fundamental tensions in human knowledge: the relationship between individual observational expertise and systematic empirical investigation. That tension appears across every domain where trained practitioners develop strong experimental intuitions—from clinical medicine to financial trading to law enforcement. The resolution is not that individual observation is worthless but that its reliability is domain-specific in ways that practitioners rarely appreciate.

**Astronomy Education and Diurnal Priming**: One mechanism sustaining lunar misconceptions generally is the way the moon's visibility is amplified during the periods when humans are awake and socially active. Full moons rise near sunset and are visible throughout evening and night hours—precisely when social activity, bar and restaurant traffic, emergency service calls, and psychiatric crises peak, for reasons entirely unrelated to lunar phase. This creates a spurious temporal coincidence: the moon's salience peaks during the social hours when behavioral crises also cluster, creating an apparent correlation that is actually a shared correlation with time-of-day. Investigations controlling carefully for diurnal patterns in behavioral data find that apparent lunar effects substantially diminish.

**The Werewolf Hypothesis**: The cultural apparatus surrounding lunar madness—werewolves being the most vivid example—derives from a genuine historical medical phenomenon. Porphyria, a group of metabolic disorders affecting heme biosynthesis, produces symptoms including skin photosensitivity (producing light avoidance and pallor), neurological episodes that can manifest as behavioral disruption, and in some variants, facial hair growth and gum recession. Some porphyria variants are triggered or exacerbated by light exposure, which would produce symptomatic worsening around full moon periods when moonlight is maximum. Illis proposed this connection in 1964, though the hypothesis remains debated as an explanatory account of werewolf mythology (Illis, 1964, *Proceedings of the Royal Society of Medicine*). The hypertrichosis (excessive hair growth) that produces "wolf-man" syndrome is separately documented and has been linked at least speculatively to the werewolf mythology.

These genuine conditions—rare, unusual, and visually dramatic—may have provided historical population-level examples of lunar-period behavioral changes among a small number of affected individuals, which then generalized into cultural belief about the entire population's susceptibility.

**Confirmation Bias in Initial Research**: The history of formal lunar behavioral research is itself instructive. Early studies published in medical and psychiatric journals through the 1970s reported positive findings—statistically significant relationships between lunar phase and psychiatric admissions, crime reports, or other behavioral measures. These positive findings attracted publication and citation while null results remained in researchers' file drawers. Rotton and Kelly's 1985 meta-analysis was specifically designed to investigate publication bias in the lunar effect literature and found evidence that positive results had been systematically overrepresented in the published record while null results were underreported (Rotton & Kelly, 1985, *Psychological Bulletin*). Modern meta-analytic methods for estimating publication bias—funnel plot asymmetry tests, trim-and-fill procedures—applied to the lunar literature consistently suggest that the true population effect size is smaller than published effect size averages, and likely indistinguishable from zero.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The lunar madness claim has received extensive coverage from multiple fact-checking organizations given its cultural ubiquity and its specific relevance to professional healthcare folklore. The claim's density—it encompasses psychiatric admissions, crime, emergency medicine, and general behavioral disruption—makes comprehensive treatment challenging in standard fact-check formats.

Effective treatments distinguish carefully between the physical reality of lunar tidal effects and the undemonstrated claim that these effects influence human behavior. Less effective treatments risk either overclaiming the research consensus (presenting it as more unified and certain than the history of contested methodology warrants) or underclaiming it (treating the null results as merely inconclusive rather than as a well-powered body of evidence pointing consistently toward no effect).

The specific claim about crime increases deserves separate attention from the psychiatric admission claim, because the causal routes are different (external behavioral influence vs. exacerbation of existing psychological vulnerability) and the evidence bases are somewhat distinct. Crime data from law enforcement records, when analyzed with adequate controls for temperature, day of week, seasonal alcohol consumption patterns, and outdoor activity levels, consistently show no lunar signal. The apparent crime-moon association in poorly controlled studies generally resolves into a weather-violence relationship: warm nights produce more outdoor activity, more alcohol consumption, and more interpersonal conflict regardless of moon phase.

Snopes has not published a single comprehensive dedicated treatment, though the claim connects to their related coverage of lunar emergency room effects and werewolf mythology. The claim would score False under Snopes' standard framework, with particular attention to the meta-analytic literature and the expertise-amplification paradox that explains why intelligent, trained professionals can sustain false empirical beliefs about their own professional domains.
