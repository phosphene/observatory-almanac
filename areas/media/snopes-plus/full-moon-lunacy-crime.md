# "Full Moon Causes Madness / Increases Crime and Hospital Visits"


## 1. The Claim

The belief that full moons cause increased rates of madness, criminal activity, and hospital admissions represents one of the most enduring folk beliefs in human culture. This "lunar effect" or "lunar lunacy hypothesis" manifests in multiple specific claims: that psychiatric emergencies spike during full moons, that violent crime increases, that hospital emergency departments become busier, that surgical complications rise, that births occur more frequently, and that general human irrationality peaks during the lunar maximum.

The claim's persistence is remarkable given its ancient origins and modern scientific scrutiny. Emergency room staff, police officers, psychiatric professionals, and even some healthcare administrators often report subjective experiences that seem to confirm lunar influences on human behavior. These anecdotal reports create a compelling narrative: the moon, which demonstrably affects ocean tides through gravitational force, must also influence human behavior through similar mechanisms, given that human bodies are approximately 60% water.

The lunar effect belief extends beyond casual superstition into operational decision-making. Some healthcare facilities have reportedly adjusted staffing levels around full moons, police departments have allocated additional resources during lunar peaks, and investment strategies have been developed based on presumed lunar influences on market behavior. The claim thus represents more than folklore—it influences resource allocation and professional practice in contexts where evidence-based decision-making should prevail.

Contemporary versions of the claim often incorporate pseudo-scientific explanations involving gravitational forces, electromagnetic fields, circadian rhythm disruption, or evolutionary programming. These modern iterations attempt to provide mechanistic explanations for lunar effects while maintaining the core belief that moon phases meaningfully influence human behavior patterns.


## 2. What's Actually True

The lunar lunacy hypothesis fails empirical testing across multiple domains and research methodologies. The most comprehensive examination comes from James Rotton and Ivan W. Kelly's landmark 1985 meta-analysis published in Psychological Bulletin, which analyzed 37 studies examining relationships between lunar phases and human behavior. Their conclusion was definitive: "The lunar hypothesis is not supported by the existing evidence."

**Crime and Violence**: Multiple large-scale studies have examined lunar effects on criminal activity with consistently negative results. Arnold Lieber's widely-cited 1978 book "The Lunar Effect" claimed to demonstrate lunar influences on homicide rates, but subsequent researchers found his methodology flawed and his results non-replicable. A 2005 study analyzing over 13,000 homicides in Cuyahoga County, Ohio, found no relationship between lunar phase and violent crime. Similarly, studies of domestic violence calls, assault rates, and property crimes consistently show random distribution across lunar cycles.

**Hospital Admissions and Medical Emergencies**: Systematic analyses of emergency department visits, psychiatric admissions, and medical emergencies show no lunar patterns. A 2004 study published in the American Journal of Emergency Medicine examined over 150,000 emergency department visits and found no correlation with lunar phase. Research on psychiatric emergency admissions, suicide attempts, and acute psychiatric episodes consistently demonstrates random distribution across lunar cycles.

**Birth Rates**: Despite persistent folk belief that births increase during full moons, large-scale analyses of birth records consistently contradict this claim. A 2005 study examining over 564,000 births in North Carolina found no lunar correlation. Multiple international studies analyzing millions of births across different cultures and healthcare systems reach identical conclusions.

**Surgical Complications and Medical Outcomes**: Studies examining surgical complication rates, wound healing, bleeding patterns, and various medical outcomes during different lunar phases consistently find no meaningful relationships. The precision of modern medical record-keeping allows researchers to analyze these variables across large populations with high statistical power.

**Sleep and Behavioral Measures**: While some studies have reported minor effects on sleep patterns during full moons—such as Christian Cajochen's 2013 study suggesting approximately 3.5 minutes less sleep during full moon periods—these effects are small, inconsistent across populations, and disputed by other researchers. Even if real, such minor sleep variations cannot account for the dramatic behavioral changes claimed by lunar effect proponents.

The methodological quality of research disconfirming lunar effects is generally high, involving large sample sizes, appropriate statistical controls, and replication across multiple independent research groups. In contrast, studies claiming to support lunar effects often suffer from small sample sizes, inadequate controls for confounding variables, selective reporting, and statistical artifacts.


The scientific consensus against meaningful lunar effects on human behavior represents one of the clearest examples of how systematic empirical research can definitively resolve questions about claimed natural phenomena.

**Statistical Power**: The null findings for lunar effects are not due to insufficient data or inadequate methodology. Researchers have analyzed millions of events across multiple decades, cultures, and institutional contexts. The statistical power of this research is sufficient to detect even small effect sizes, yet consistently fails to identify meaningful lunar correlations.

**Replication Across Contexts**: The absence of lunar effects has been demonstrated across diverse populations, geographic regions, cultural contexts, and measurement approaches. This cross-cultural replication is particularly important because it rules out explanations based on specific social or environmental factors that might obscure genuine lunar influences in particular settings.

**Methodological Sophistication**: Modern research on lunar effects employs sophisticated statistical techniques to control for confounding variables such as seasonal patterns, day-of-week effects, holiday influences, weather conditions, and social events that might create spurious correlations. Despite these rigorous methodological approaches, lunar effects remain undetectable.

**Mechanistic Implausibility**: While the moon's gravitational force demonstrably affects ocean tides, the proposed mechanisms for lunar effects on human behavior lack scientific credibility. The gravitational difference between full and new moons is minuscule compared to gravitational forces from nearby objects like buildings, vehicles, or other people. Furthermore, human bodies are not analogous to oceans—they are complex biological systems with internal regulatory mechanisms that maintain homeostasis against external perturbations.

**Prospective Prediction Failure**: If lunar effects were real, they should enable prospective prediction of behavioral patterns. Healthcare systems, law enforcement agencies, and other institutions that have attempted to use lunar phases for predictive purposes have found no practical benefit, suggesting that any effects—if they exist—are too small to be operationally meaningful.

**Alternative Explanations**: Researchers have identified more plausible explanations for perceived lunar effects, including seasonal patterns that coincidentally correlate with certain lunar phases in specific time periods, social expectations that create self-fulfilling prophecies, and cognitive biases that distort perception and memory of event frequencies.

The strength of the scientific consensus reflects not just the absence of evidence for lunar effects, but the positive evidence for alternative explanations and the robust theoretical framework that explains why such effects should not be expected based on our understanding of human biology and behavior.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of lunar effect beliefs despite overwhelming contradictory evidence illustrates several important cognitive and social phenomena that extend far beyond astronomical folklore.

**Illusory Correlation**: The human tendency to perceive relationships between unrelated events lies at the heart of lunar effect beliefs. Loren J. Chapman and Jean P. Chapman's foundational research on illusory correlation demonstrates how people consistently overestimate the frequency of co-occurring salient events. When a dramatic incident occurs during a visible full moon, it creates a memorable association that becomes disproportionately weighted in subsequent belief formation.

**Confirmation Bias and Selective Attention**: Once individuals believe in lunar effects, they become more likely to notice and remember incidents that occur during full moons while overlooking or forgetting counter-examples. Emergency room staff who believe in lunar effects may notice busy nights that coincide with full moons while failing to recognize equally busy nights during other lunar phases, or quiet nights during full moons.

**Availability Heuristic**: The mental accessibility of dramatic full moon incidents influences probability estimates in ways that contradict actual statistical frequencies. Media reporting, cultural narratives, and workplace folklore all contribute to making full moon incidents more cognitively available than non-full moon incidents, skewing subjective probability assessments.

**Base Rate Neglect**: Hospital emergency departments are busy most nights, psychiatric crises occur regularly regardless of lunar phase, and crime rates fluctuate based on numerous social and environmental factors. The lunar effect belief often reflects failure to account for these base rates when evaluating whether full moon incidents represent genuine increases above normal frequencies.

**Social Proof and Institutional Reinforcement**: When respected colleagues, supervisors, or institutional policies appear to endorse lunar effect beliefs, social proof mechanisms can override individual critical evaluation. Emergency departments that adjust staffing for full moons, or police departments that increase patrols during lunar peaks, provide institutional validation for beliefs that may lack empirical foundation.

**Anthropomorphic and Teleological Thinking**: Humans naturally seek purposeful explanations for patterns in their environment. The visible drama of a full moon provides an intuitively satisfying causal explanation for unusual or dramatic events, even when no causal relationship exists.

**Historical and Cultural Transmission**: The depth of lunar effect beliefs in human culture—reflected in language (lunacy, lunatic), legal traditions (lunar defense concepts), and folklore—provides powerful social validation that can override scientific evidence for individuals who view traditional knowledge as more trustworthy than modern research.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE**

The claim that full moons cause increased rates of madness, crime, or hospital visits is not supported by scientific evidence. Rigorous research involving millions of events across multiple decades consistently shows no meaningful relationship between lunar phases and human behavior patterns.

**Key Evidence**:
- Meta-analysis of 37 studies found no support for lunar effects on human behavior (Rotton & Kelly, 1985)
- Large-scale analyses of crime statistics, hospital admissions, and birth records consistently show random distribution across lunar cycles
- Proposed mechanisms (gravitational effects, electromagnetic influences) lack scientific credibility
- Apparent lunar effects result from cognitive biases, particularly illusory correlation and confirmation bias

**Why This Matters**: Belief in lunar effects can influence resource allocation decisions in healthcare, law enforcement, and other institutions, potentially compromising service quality and efficiency. More broadly, lunar effect beliefs illustrate how cognitive biases can override scientific evidence even among educated professionals, highlighting the importance of evidence-based decision-making and scientific literacy.

**The Bottom Line**: While the full moon has inspired art, poetry, and folklore throughout human history, it does not influence human behavior in measurable ways. Emergency departments are not busier during full moons, crime rates do not spike, and psychiatric crises do not increase. The apparent connection exists in perception and memory, not in reality.

**Phenomenological Reality**: The lunar effect represents a particularly interesting case because the subjective experience of healthcare workers, police officers, and others can be compelling and consistent, even when objective data contradicts their perceptions. This illustrates the power of cognitive biases to create coherent but false beliefs about patterns in complex environments.

This claim exemplifies how ancient beliefs can persist despite modern scientific scrutiny, particularly when they involve phenomena that feel intuitively plausible and are supported by cultural narratives. The lunar effect serves as an important case study in the psychology of belief formation and the challenges of translating scientific evidence into practical decision-making in institutional contexts.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The lunar effect belief operates within broader ecosystems of pseudoscientific thinking and institutional decision-making that extend its impact beyond individual superstition.

**Healthcare Resource Allocation**: Emergency departments, psychiatric facilities, and other healthcare institutions that adjust staffing or resource allocation based on lunar phases engage in evidence-free decision-making that may compromise patient care quality. Resources dedicated to addressing non-existent lunar effects represent opportunity costs that could be invested in interventions with demonstrated efficacy.

**Criminal Justice and Policing**: Law enforcement agencies that increase patrols or modify deployment patterns based on lunar phases engage in resource allocation strategies unsupported by evidence. This can create both opportunity costs and potential civil liberties concerns if increased enforcement during lunar phases leads to disproportionate attention to certain communities or activities.

**Media and Cultural Reinforcement**: News media regularly report on lunar effect claims without appropriate scientific context, contributing to public misconceptions that influence individual and institutional behavior. Entertainment media perpetuate lunar effect myths through storylines that treat these beliefs as factual rather than fictional.

**Academic and Professional Education**: The persistence of lunar effect beliefs among some healthcare professionals, law enforcement personnel, and other educated professionals suggests gaps in scientific literacy education and critical thinking training within professional preparation programs.

**Broader Pseudoscience Ecosystem**: Lunar effect beliefs often cluster with other forms of pseudoscientific thinking, including astrology, alternative medicine practices, and conspiracy theories. Understanding why educated professionals maintain beliefs in lunar effects provides insights into how pseudoscientific thinking persists despite scientific education.

**Investment and Economic Decisions**: Some investment strategies and economic forecasting models incorporate lunar cycles, despite the lack of empirical support for lunar effects on market behavior. This represents a form of systematic irrationality in economic decision-making with potential financial consequences for individuals and institutions.

**Research Priority and Funding**: Continued investigation of lunar effects represents an opportunity cost in scientific research, diverting resources from more promising research directions. However, meta-analytical research that definitively resolves questions about lunar effects serves an important function by providing authoritative scientific conclusions that can guide policy and practice.

**International and Cross-Cultural Variations**: Lunar effect beliefs vary across cultures, with some societies showing stronger adherence to these concepts than others. Understanding these variations provides insights into how cultural factors interact with scientific education to influence belief formation and maintenance.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not produced a comprehensive fact-check specifically addressing lunar effects on human behavior, despite the claim's widespread circulation and operational impact on various institutions. This gap is notable given the platform's extensive coverage of scientific misconceptions and their typical approach to addressing beliefs with measurable, testable components.

The absence of direct Snopes coverage may reflect several factors: the claim's status as "soft" pseudoscience rather than politically charged misinformation, the technical nature of the statistical analysis required to address it properly, or the perception that lunar effects represent harmless folk belief rather than dangerous misinformation requiring urgent debunking.

However, Snopes has addressed related astronomical claims, including various "supermoon" effects, astrological predictions, and conspiracy theories involving lunar phenomena. Their approach to astronomical claims typically emphasizes scientific consensus, references peer-reviewed research, and provides accessible explanations of relevant physical principles.

The platform has also covered broader categories of correlation-versus-causation fallacies and confirmation bias, topics directly relevant to lunar effect beliefs. Their treatment of similar claims suggests they would likely approach lunar effects through the lens of cognitive psychology and statistical analysis rather than focusing primarily on astronomical or gravitational mechanisms.
