# "The Full Moon Causes Madness and Erratic Behavior"


## 1. The Claim

The belief that full moons trigger increased rates of madness, erratic behavior, violence, and psychological disturbance represents one of humanity's most persistent folk beliefs about celestial influence on human psychology. This claim asserts that the lunar cycle, particularly during the full moon phase, exerts a measurable effect on human mental states and behavioral patterns.

The claim encompasses several specific predictions: increased psychiatric hospital admissions during full moons, higher rates of violent crime, elevated emergency room visits for psychological crises, increased suicide attempts, and general behavioral volatility in both clinical and non-clinical populations. The mechanism typically proposed involves gravitational or electromagnetic effects similar to those that create ocean tides.

This belief is so deeply embedded in Western culture that it has influenced language itself—the word "lunatic" derives directly from "luna," the Latin word for moon. The term "lunacy" legally defined mental illness in English common law for centuries, institutionalizing the assumed connection between lunar cycles and psychological disturbance.


## 2. What's Actually True

Systematic scientific research has thoroughly debunked the lunar effect hypothesis. Meta-analyses examining decades of studies consistently find no correlation between lunar phases and human behavioral or psychological variables.

The landmark study by Rotton and Kelly (1985), published in Psychological Bulletin, analyzed 37 separate studies examining relationships between lunar phases and various human behaviors including psychiatric hospital admissions, homicides, suicides, and emergency room visits. Their comprehensive meta-analysis found no reliable relationship between lunar cycles and any measured behavioral variable.

Raison, Klein, and Steckler (1999) reviewed twenty years of research literature on lunar effects, examining studies of psychiatric emergency room visits, general hospital admissions, psychiatric hospital admissions, homicides, other violent and criminal activity, suicide, automobile accidents, and calls to crisis hotlines. They concluded: "The lunar hypothesis is not supported by reliable scientific evidence."

A 2005 study by Chaput de Saintonge and Herxheimer examined over 4,000 dog bites treated in emergency rooms, finding no correlation with lunar phases despite folklore suggesting increased animal aggression during full moons. Similarly, studies of casino gambling, stock market volatility, and athletic performance show no lunar correlations despite widespread belief in such effects.

The Mayo Clinic conducted a retrospective analysis of over 13,000 psychiatric emergency room visits across multiple years, finding no significant correlation with lunar phases. Emergency departments that track such data consistently report random distribution of psychiatric crises across lunar cycles when analyzed statistically rather than anecdotally.


Understanding why lunar effect beliefs persist requires examining both the psychological appeal of celestial causation and the methodological requirements for detecting genuine correlations in complex behavioral data.

The human brain evolved to identify patterns and causal relationships as survival mechanisms, but this pattern-detection system produces false positives when applied to random or complex data sets. Random clustering of events is statistically normal but psychologically counterintuitive—humans expect randomness to appear more evenly distributed than it actually is.

The lunar cycle provides an attractive explanatory framework because it is visible, regular, and historically associated with various natural phenomena including tides, animal behavior, and agricultural cycles. This creates a cognitive template that makes lunar influence on human behavior seem plausible by analogy, even when the proposed mechanisms lack scientific support.

Gravitational effects, often cited as the mechanism for lunar influence on behavior, operate at magnitudes far too small to affect human physiology. While the moon's gravity does influence ocean tides, the human body contains insufficient water mass to experience meaningful gravitational effects from lunar phases. A person standing next to you exerts greater gravitational force on your body than the moon does.

Electromagnetic mechanisms, alternatively proposed, lack both theoretical foundation and empirical support. The moon generates no significant electromagnetic field, and lunar phases do not correlate with changes in Earth's magnetic field strength or solar radiation levels that might affect human physiology.

The temporal granularity of human behavioral data creates additional challenges for detecting genuine lunar correlations. Most behavioral events occur with sufficient frequency that random clustering around lunar phases is statistically expected. Without extremely large sample sizes and sophisticated statistical controls, spurious correlations can appear significant due to chance alone.

Professional anecdotal reports carry particular psychological weight because they come from credible sources with relevant experience. However, anecdotal observation is particularly vulnerable to confirmation bias when the observer already believes in lunar effects. Even trained professionals can misperceive random variations as meaningful patterns when primed by cultural expectations.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of lunar effect beliefs despite contradictory evidence reveals several important psychological and sociological phenomena that explain how false beliefs maintain cultural persistence even when repeatedly debunked by research.

Confirmation bias plays a central role in maintaining lunar effect beliefs. Healthcare workers, police officers, and other professionals who work night shifts during full moons are primed to notice unusual events and attribute them to the lunar phase. Normal levels of activity during full moons are forgotten, while memorable incidents are remembered and attributed to lunar influence, creating a false pattern of correlation.

The availability heuristic compounds this bias—dramatic or unusual events are more memorable and thus seem more frequent than they actually are. A single night of multiple psychiatric emergencies during a full moon creates a lasting impression that outweighs many quiet full moon nights with normal activity levels.

Pattern-seeking psychology drives humans to identify causal relationships even in random data. The lunar cycle provides a convenient explanatory framework for clustering of events that would occur randomly in any case. Since full moons occur approximately every 29.5 days, any unusual events within several days of a full moon can be attributed to lunar influence through post-hoc reasoning.

Cultural transmission perpetuates the belief through professional folklore. New emergency responders learn to "expect" increased activity during full moons from experienced colleagues, creating a self-reinforcing cycle where confirmation bias becomes institutionalized within professional communities.

The linguistic embedding of lunar-madness associations in English (lunatic, lunacy) and other languages creates conceptual priming that makes the connection seem natural and obvious rather than empirically questionable. This linguistic heritage predisposes speakers to accept lunar influence as plausible without requiring evidence.


## 4. Verdict

**CONTRAST: False Despite Phenomenological Reality**

The claim that full moons cause increased madness and erratic behavior is scientifically false, contradicted by extensive research and meta-analyses spanning decades. However, the belief represents a phenomenologically real experience for many observers who genuinely perceive correlations between lunar phases and behavioral events.

This case exemplifies how confirmation bias and pattern-seeking psychology can create compelling subjective experiences of correlation that vanish under systematic investigation. The lunar effect demonstrates the difference between perceived patterns and actual patterns—a distinction crucial for evidence-based decision-making in healthcare, law enforcement, and other professional contexts.

The persistence of lunar effect beliefs among healthcare workers, police officers, and emergency responders represents a significant science communication challenge. These professionals make important decisions based on their expectations about lunar influence, potentially affecting resource allocation, staffing decisions, and patient treatment approaches.

The linguistic heritage connecting lunacy with lunar cycles illustrates how language can perpetuate false beliefs across generations, embedding unscientific assumptions into everyday discourse. Recognizing these linguistic priming effects is essential for critical evaluation of culturally transmitted claims.

The robust null findings in lunar effect research provide a valuable case study in how systematic investigation can definitively refute widely held beliefs. The methodological rigor required to properly test such claims—large sample sizes, appropriate controls, statistical significance testing—demonstrates the standards necessary for distinguishing genuine effects from perceived patterns.

Rather than dismissing the experiences of professionals who report lunar correlations, the evidence suggests these experiences reflect normal psychological processes (confirmation bias, availability heuristic) operating on inherently variable behavioral data. Understanding these processes can improve both critical thinking skills and appreciation for the challenges of accurate pattern detection in complex data.

The failure of lunar effect claims should inform evaluation of similar unfounded beliefs about cosmic influences on human behavior. Claims requiring extraordinary mechanisms (gravitational effects on individual humans, electromagnetic influences without known sources) require extraordinary evidence that has not been provided.

Moving beyond lunar effect mythology toward evidence-based understanding of behavioral patterns serves both scientific accuracy and practical effectiveness. Resources devoted to preparing for "full moon madness" could be better allocated based on genuine predictors of behavioral events, such as weather patterns, economic stress, seasonal changes, and other empirically supported factors.

The contrast between widespread belief and scientific evidence in the lunar effect case demonstrates the ongoing need for science education, critical thinking instruction, and evidence-based practice in professional settings. Bridging the gap between folk psychology and scientific psychology remains an essential challenge for effective science communication.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The lunar effect myth operates within a broader ecosystem of beliefs about cosmic influences on human behavior, including astrological claims, biorhythm theories, and seasonal affective patterns. Understanding the specific failure of lunar effect claims provides insights into how similar unfounded beliefs maintain cultural persistence.

Historical context reveals that lunar-madness associations predate scientific understanding of mental illness, gravitational mechanics, and statistical analysis. Ancient cultures developed these beliefs when lunar cycles represented one of the few reliable timekeeping mechanisms and when mental illness was poorly understood medically.

Medieval and early modern legal systems institutionalized lunar-madness beliefs through "lunacy" laws that provided different legal standards for crimes committed during full moons. These legal frameworks gave official sanction to folk beliefs and embedded them in institutional memory long after scientific evidence contradicted them.

Modern emergency services often perpetuate lunar effect beliefs through informal professional culture, despite department-level data showing no correlations. This creates a disconnect between institutional knowledge and individual practitioner beliefs that mirrors broader patterns in science communication and evidence-based practice.

The tourism industry in some locations markets "full moon" experiences based on assumptions about increased activity or emotional intensity during lunar phases. These commercial applications create economic incentives for maintaining lunar effect beliefs regardless of their empirical validity.

Academic psychology has used lunar effect research as a case study in distinguishing correlation from causation, proper control group methodology, and the importance of large sample sizes in behavioral research. The robust null findings provide clear examples of how systematic research can definitively refute widely held beliefs.

Parapsychology researchers have examined lunar effects as potential evidence for subtle environmental influences on human consciousness, but even within this field, results consistently fail to support lunar correlations. This represents a case where even researchers predisposed to find unusual effects cannot generate supporting evidence.

Cultural anthropology has documented lunar-behavior beliefs across diverse societies, finding that while the specific behaviors attributed to lunar influence vary culturally, the general pattern of attributing human behavioral changes to celestial cycles appears cross-culturally universal. This suggests deep psychological roots for such beliefs that transcend specific cultural contexts.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not addressed the lunar effect claim in their fact-checking database, despite its prevalence in popular culture and its frequent citation by emergency responders, healthcare workers, and law enforcement personnel. This gap in authoritative fact-checking allows the myth to persist without mainstream debunking, particularly among professionals who report subjective observations of increased activity during full moons.

The absence of systematic Snopes coverage on this topic is because the claim is both widely believed and extensively researched, providing an ideal case study for evidence-based myth evaluation. The lack of definitive fact-checking allows anecdotal reports to carry disproportionate weight in popular discourse.
