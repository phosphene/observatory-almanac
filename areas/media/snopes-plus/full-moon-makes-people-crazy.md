# "The Full Moon Makes People Act Crazy"


## 1. The Claim

**The Claim:** The full moon causes increased rates of mental health crises, violent behavior, emergency room visits, psychiatric admissions, criminal activity, and general "crazy" behavior in human populations. This belief manifests in several related assertions: that hospital emergency departments become busier during full moons, that police encounter more domestic violence and public disturbances, that psychiatric facilities see increased admissions, and that suicide rates spike during lunar maxima.

The claim appears ubiquitously across emergency services culture. Nurses, paramedics, police officers, and emergency physicians frequently report anecdotal observations of increased chaos, violence, and psychiatric emergencies during full moon periods. The belief has spawned the term "lunar effect" in behavioral psychology and maintains strong cultural presence through the linguistic connection between "lunacy" and "lunar."

Beyond emergency services, the belief extends into educational settings (teachers reporting increased behavioral problems), retail and service industries (customer service representatives noting more difficult interactions), and general public consciousness (people attributing unusual behavior to lunar phases). This broad cultural penetration demonstrates how the lunar effect claim transcends professional boundaries to become general folk wisdom about human behavior patterns.

The claim often includes specific mechanisms through which lunar influence supposedly operates: gravitational effects on body water content (given the moon's influence on ocean tides), disruption of sleep patterns through increased brightness, or various astrological and mystical explanations involving lunar energy affecting human consciousness and behavior.


## 2. What's Actually True

Comprehensive research over four decades has consistently failed to demonstrate any correlation between lunar phases and human behavior patterns:

**Rotton & Kelly (1985) Meta-Analysis:** The most authoritative examination appeared in *Psychological Bulletin*, analyzing 37 studies covering psychiatric admissions, crisis calls, homicides, and other behavioral measures. The meta-analysis found no statistically significant relationship between lunar phases and any measured behavioral outcomes. Effect sizes were essentially zero, and what small correlations appeared were within expected random variation.

This meta-analysis is because it combines data from multiple independent studies, providing enormous statistical power to detect even small effects. The authors concluded that lunar effects, if they exist at all, are so weak as to be clinically and practically meaningless.

**Raison, Klein & Steckler (1999) Review:** Published in *Journal of Affective Disorders*, this comprehensive review examined lunar effect studies from 1978-1997. The authors concluded: "The lunar hypothesis is groundless. Any correlation that may exist between lunar phases and human behavior is so weak as to be imperceptible and of no practical significance."

This review is notable for its systematic approach to evaluating study quality and methodology. Studies with better experimental controls showed smaller effect sizes, suggesting that apparent lunar effects in earlier research reflected methodological artifacts rather than genuine phenomena.

**Emergency Department Studies:** Multiple controlled studies of emergency department visits have found no correlation with moon phases. Alves et al. (2003) examined 150,999 emergency visits over four years and found no significant lunar associations. Similar null results emerged from studies in Miami (Buckley et al. 2006), Germany (Wolbank et al. 2003), and Australia (Staboulis et al. 2006).

These studies are particularly compelling because they examine exactly the populations where lunar effects are most commonly reported. The large sample sizes and careful methodological controls eliminate most potential sources of bias and confounding.

**Psychiatric Admission Research:** Controlled studies of psychiatric hospital admissions consistently show no lunar effects. Templer et al. (1982) examined 4,190 admissions and found no correlation. Wilkinson et al. (1997) analyzed 2,956 admissions across 12 months with identical null results.

Martin et al. (1992) conducted a particularly rigorous study of psychiatric emergency consultations, examining 1,857 cases over 70 consecutive lunar cycles. They found no correlation between lunar phases and psychiatric crises, suicide attempts, or involuntary commitments.

**Criminal Activity Analysis:** Multiple studies of violent crime rates find no lunar correlations. Lieber & Sherin (1972) initially reported correlations between moon phases and homicides, but subsequent replication attempts by multiple researchers failed to confirm these findings. The original correlations appear to have resulted from statistical artifacts and selective reporting.

More recent studies with larger datasets and better controls consistently find no lunar effects on crime rates. Rotton & Kelly's analysis included multiple studies of violent crime, property crime, and various forms of antisocial behavior — all showing null results.

**Suicide Rate Studies:** Comprehensive analyses of suicide timing show no lunar patterns. Jones & Jones (1977) examined 2,017 suicides across multiple years and found no significant correlations with moon phases. Similar null results appear consistently across different populations and geographic regions.

Mathew et al. (1991) analyzed 2,068 suicide attempts and found no lunar correlations. The study's large sample size and careful temporal analysis eliminate most potential sources of bias in examining suicide timing patterns.

**Sleep and Circadian Research:** Studies examining sleep quality and duration during different lunar phases find minimal or no effects. Cajochen et al. (2013) found small changes in sleep architecture during full moons, but these effects were subtle and didn't translate into measurable behavioral changes.

Recent research using wearable sleep monitors and objective sleep measurement techniques confirms that any lunar effects on sleep are too small to account for the dramatic behavioral changes claimed by lunar effect believers.

**Birth Rate and Reproduction Studies:** Despite folk beliefs about lunar effects on birth timing, comprehensive analyses of birth records show no correlation with lunar phases. Martens et al. (1988) examined over 4 million births and found no lunar patterns. Similar null results appear across different populations and healthcare systems.


The scientific consensus regarding lunar-behavior correlations is unambiguous and based on methodologically rigorous research spanning multiple decades and geographic regions:

**Methodological Standards:** Modern studies examining lunar effects employ appropriate statistical controls, adequate sample sizes, and proper temporal analysis techniques. Early positive findings (like Lieber & Sherin 1972) failed replication when subjected to more rigorous methodological standards.

High-quality lunar effect studies control for day-of-week effects, seasonal variations, holidays, weather conditions, and other temporal factors that might confound analyses. When these controls are applied, apparent lunar effects typically disappear.

**Cross-Cultural Consistency:** Null findings appear consistently across different cultures, healthcare systems, and geographic regions. If lunar effects existed, they should manifest universally rather than varying by cultural context or research methodology.

Studies from North America, Europe, Australia, and other regions consistently find no lunar effects, suggesting that the absence of correlation is not culturally specific or methodologically artifact.

**Biological Implausibility:** While lunar gravitational effects influence ocean tides, the human body's water content experiences no measurable lunar gravitational influence. The gravitational differential between moon phases affects terrestrial objects far too minimally to influence neurochemistry or behavior.

The tidal force from the moon on a human body is approximately 0.00000003% of Earth's gravitational force — far too small to influence any physiological processes. Claims about lunar effects on body water content lack any plausible physical mechanism.

**Statistical Power:** The cumulative research examining lunar effects has enormous statistical power — sample sizes in the hundreds of thousands across multiple independent studies. If meaningful correlations existed, this research volume would definitively detect them.

Meta-analyses combining multiple studies achieve statistical power sufficient to detect even tiny effects. The consistent null results across these high-powered analyses provide strong evidence against lunar-behavior correlations.

**Control for Confounding Variables:** Modern research controls for numerous factors that might create spurious lunar correlations: weather patterns, seasonal effects, social events, staffing changes, and reporting biases. When these controls are applied, lunar effects disappear.

Studies that initially appeared to show lunar effects often failed to control for weekend versus weekday patterns, holiday effects, or seasonal variations. More sophisticated analyses eliminate these confounding influences.

The overwhelming weight of evidence supports the null hypothesis: lunar phases do not influence human behavior in any detectable or practically significant way. The persistence of contrary beliefs reflects cognitive and social factors rather than astronomical influences.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of lunar effect beliefs despite contradictory evidence reflects well-documented cognitive biases and social transmission mechanisms:

**Confirmation Bias:** Healthcare workers who believe in lunar effects disproportionately notice and remember busy or chaotic shifts during full moons while forgetting unremarkable full moon periods. This selective attention creates subjective "evidence" that feels compelling despite being systematically biased.

Research on confirmation bias in healthcare settings shows that professionals who hold prior beliefs about lunar effects demonstrate selective recall for events that confirm their expectations while showing poor recall for disconfirming evidence.

**Apophenia and Pattern Detection:** Human cognition evolved to detect meaningful patterns, sometimes creating false correlations where none exist. The combination of a highly salient celestial event (full moon visibility) with the inherently variable nature of emergency services creates conditions conducive to illusory correlation.

The full moon provides a highly memorable temporal marker that people can easily associate with unusual events. This memorability makes lunar correlations seem more plausible than equally arbitrary temporal associations that lack such salient markers.

**Linguistic Priming:** The historical linguistic connection between "lunacy" and "lunar" phenomena provides conceptual scaffolding that makes the correlation seem intuitively plausible. This linguistic inheritance from pre-scientific medical traditions continues influencing contemporary intuitions about moon-behavior relationships.

The term "lunatic" derives from the Latin "luna" (moon), reflecting historical beliefs about lunar influences on mental health. This linguistic legacy creates cultural predisposition to accept lunar-behavior correlations as plausible even without supporting evidence.

**Social Transmission in Professional Communities:** Within emergency services, lunar effect beliefs function as cultural knowledge transmitted through storytelling, shift handovers, and professional folklore. New workers receive these beliefs as experiential wisdom from senior colleagues, creating institutional perpetuation despite lack of empirical support.

Research on medical folklore shows that professional beliefs often persist through social transmission even when contradicted by research evidence. The collaborative, high-stress nature of emergency work creates particularly strong conditions for folklore transmission.

**Expectancy Effects and Self-Fulfilling Prophecies:** Some research suggests that belief in lunar effects may create self-fulfilling prophecies. If emergency workers expect increased chaos during full moons, they may exhibit heightened alertness, lower tolerance thresholds, or altered interaction patterns that actually influence the situations they encounter.

Studies of expectancy effects in healthcare show that professional expectations can influence patient interactions, clinical decision-making, and even objective health outcomes through various psychological and social mechanisms.

**Availability Heuristic:** People judge the likelihood of events based on how easily they can recall similar instances. Full moon nights that coincide with busy or unusual shifts become highly memorable, making lunar correlations seem more common than they actually are.

The availability heuristic is particularly powerful when dealing with dramatic or emotionally significant events, exactly the type of situations (psychiatric emergencies, violent crimes) that are most commonly attributed to lunar influences.

**Base Rate Neglect:** People often fail to consider the baseline frequency of events when evaluating correlations. Emergency departments are inherently unpredictable, with significant night-to-night variation regardless of lunar phases. This natural variability provides ample opportunity for false pattern detection.

**Media Amplification:** Popular media frequently reports on lunar effect claims while giving less attention to contradictory research. This asymmetric coverage reinforces public beliefs about lunar influences while failing to communicate scientific consensus.

News stories about "full moon chaos" in emergency departments or police stations receive widespread attention, while academic studies showing null results rarely achieve similar public visibility.


## 4. Verdict

**REFUTED:** Claims that lunar phases influence human behavior patterns have been debunked by decades of methodologically rigorous research across multiple populations, geographic regions, and behavioral domains.

**Evidence Grade: A+** — Meta-analyses encompassing tens of thousands of cases consistently find no statistically significant correlations between moon phases and psychiatric admissions, emergency calls, violent crime, or other behavioral measures.

**Historical Context: Significant** — The belief stems from pre-scientific medical traditions that attributed mental illness to lunar influences, perpetuated through linguistic inheritance ("lunacy") and occupational folklore in emergency services communities.

**Contemporary Relevance: Moderate** — While scientifically groundless, the belief remains prevalent among healthcare workers and continues influencing professional culture and resource allocation decisions in some emergency services contexts.

**Epistemological Classification: R (Refuted)** — The lunar-behavior hypothesis has been definitively falsified through comprehensive empirical investigation. No credible scientific evidence supports correlations between moon phases and human behavioral patterns.

The persistence of lunar effect beliefs despite contradictory evidence demonstrates the psychological and social factors that maintain occupational folklore within professional communities. Emergency workers' subjective experiences of "full moon chaos" reflect confirmation bias, selective memory, and pattern-seeking behaviors rather than astronomical influences.

Contemporary behavioral psychology recognizes lunar effect beliefs as a classic example of illusory correlation — the tendency to perceive meaningful relationships between unrelated variables when cognitive biases and social transmission create compelling but false experiential evidence. Understanding why these beliefs persist provides valuable insights into professional culture, cognitive bias, and the challenges of translating scientific knowledge into practice communities.

The case illustrates how beliefs can serve important social and psychological functions within professional communities even when they lack empirical foundation. Lunar effect beliefs provide explanatory narratives, group solidarity, and predictive frameworks that help emergency workers cope with inherently unpredictable, high-stress environments.

Effective professional education should address both the empirical evidence against lunar effects and the psychological and social needs that make such beliefs appealing. This dual approach respects the legitimate functions that occupational folklore serves while promoting evidence-based practice and critical thinking about pattern recognition in complex, variable work environments.

Understanding the persistence of lunar effect beliefs offers broader lessons about how scientific evidence interacts with professional culture, cognitive bias, and the human need for predictive understanding in uncertain environments. These insights extend far beyond the specific lunar hypothesis to illuminate general challenges in promoting evidence-based practice in complex professional settings.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The lunar effect myth illuminates broader patterns in how professional communities construct and maintain beliefs about their work environments:

**Occupational Folklore:** Emergency services develop rich traditional knowledge systems that help workers manage unpredictable, high-stress environments. Lunar effect beliefs function within this folklore as explanatory narratives that provide cognitive structure for inherently chaotic work conditions.

Professional folklore serves important functions in high-stress occupations: it provides shared explanatory frameworks, creates group solidarity, and offers psychological comfort through predictive narratives. Lunar beliefs fulfill these functions even when empirically unfounded.

**Pattern-Seeking in Random Systems:** Emergency services inherently involve highly variable workloads driven by complex, unpredictable factors. Human psychology strongly resists attributing events to randomness, creating cognitive pressure to identify causal patterns even where none exist.

The need to find patterns in chaos is particularly strong in life-and-death professions where understanding and predicting events feels essential for effective performance. Lunar beliefs satisfy this need for predictive control even when they lack empirical basis.

**Professional Identity and Expertise:** Healthcare workers' lunar effect beliefs may partially derive from professional identity needs. Claiming sensitivity to subtle environmental patterns (like lunar influences) can reinforce occupational expertise and specialization claims.

Beliefs about special sensitivity to lunar influences can function as markers of professional experience and intuitive understanding that distinguish seasoned practitioners from novices or outsiders.

**Shift Culture and Narrative Tradition:** Emergency services operate through shift work that creates distinct temporal communities. Lunar effect stories function as shared narratives that build group cohesion and transmit occupational knowledge across different personnel and time periods.

Storytelling about unusual full moon experiences serves important social functions in shift-based work environments: it creates shared experiences across temporal boundaries and reinforces professional identity through collective narrative.

**Stress and Attribution Patterns:** High-stress work environments create psychological needs for explanatory frameworks that help workers understand and cope with challenging situations. Lunar beliefs provide external attribution for difficult shifts that might otherwise require more psychologically demanding explanations.

Attributing unusually challenging work periods to lunar influences allows workers to externalize stress and maintain sense of control over their professional environment. This attribution pattern serves psychological protective functions even when empirically incorrect.

**Research Translation Challenges:** The disconnect between research findings and professional beliefs highlights persistent challenges in translating scientific knowledge into practice communities. Despite clear research conclusions, institutional beliefs prove remarkably resistant to empirical correction.

The persistence of lunar beliefs despite contradictory evidence demonstrates how professional cultures can maintain beliefs that serve social and psychological functions independent of their empirical accuracy.

**Quality Assurance and Evidence-Based Practice:** The lunar effect case raises questions about how healthcare organizations should respond to professionally held beliefs that lack empirical support. Balancing respect for professional experience with evidence-based practice creates ongoing challenges.

Some healthcare facilities have discontinued lunar-based staffing adjustments based on research evidence, while others continue practices based on professional folklore. This variation reflects broader tensions between empirical evidence and experiential knowledge in healthcare.

Contemporary research increasingly examines why lunar effect beliefs persist rather than whether lunar effects exist. This meta-level inquiry addresses questions about professional culture, cognitive bias, and knowledge translation that extend far beyond the specific lunar hypothesis.

**Educational Implications:** The lunar effect case demonstrates challenges in professional education about distinguishing between meaningful patterns and random variation. Training healthcare workers to recognize cognitive biases and evaluate evidence critically could improve professional decision-making.

**Public Health Communication:** Understanding why lunar beliefs persist despite contradictory evidence can inform broader public health communication strategies about evaluating health claims and understanding scientific evidence.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes.com has not conducted a comprehensive fact-check specifically addressing the "full moon madness" claim as a discrete entry. While the site has addressed various lunar myths, the systematic examination of correlations between moon phases and human behavior — particularly emergency services anecdotes — has not received their dedicated treatment. This represents a notable gap given the claim's prevalence in healthcare and emergency services communities.

The absence of dedicated Snopes coverage is because the lunar effect belief influences professional decision-making, staffing patterns, and resource allocation in some healthcare facilities. Emergency departments occasionally adjust staffing based on lunar phase expectations, making this a practically consequential belief system that merits systematic fact-checking analysis.
