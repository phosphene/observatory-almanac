# Could Fungus 'Full of Psychedelic Drugs' Cause Brood X Cicadas Butts to Fall Off?

## 1. The Claim

The claim that arrived in public discourse as Brood X cicadas prepared to emerge across 15 eastern U.S. states in May 2021 is among the most inherently spectacular nature facts that a fact-checker can be asked to verify: that a parasitic fungus called *Massospora cicadina* infects periodical cicadas, eats away and replaces their abdomens (including reproductive organs) with a fungal mass, fills the affected insects with psychoactive substances including psilocybin—the active compound in "magic mushrooms"—and manipulates their behavior to maximize disease transmission, all while the insects continue to feed, move, and attempt to mate.

The "zombie fungus" framing that circulated in media coverage of the 2021 Brood X emergence presented the claim in its most sensational form: a fungus "full of psychedelic drugs" causes cicada "butts to fall off" and turns the insects into "fungal puppets" that spread spores while their bodies disintegrate. This framing was picked up by USA Today, IndyStar, ScienceNews, and dozens of additional outlets following an interview with cicada expert John Lill, Chair of Biology at George Washington University.

The claim involves multiple verifiable sub-assertions: (a) *Massospora cicadina* is a real organism that exists and infects periodical cicadas; (b) it destroys the abdomen and reproductive organs, replacing them with a functional spore mass; (c) it causes behavioral manipulation that compels continued mating activity despite organ loss; (d) it contains psilocybin (and potentially other psychoactive alkaloids); and (e) the behavioral manipulation may be mediated by the psychoactive compounds present in the fungal tissue. The Snopes article confirms these as accurate while noting that the phenomenon was not new—it has been documented in the scientific community across multiple Brood X emergence cycles.

This claim is epistemically interesting precisely because it combines spectacular-sounding assertions with substantive—and in some cases counterintuitive—scientific findings. The natural tendency to treat sensational claims as exaggerated or false creates a systematic bias against accepting such claims even when substantial evidence supports them. The Snopes fact-check serves a genuine function here: it helps readers overcome the skepticism that would be naturally triggered by a headline-level summary ("fungus full of psychedelics causes cicada butts to fall off") that sounds more like tabloid invention than natural history documentation.

## 2. What's Actually True

*Massospora cicadina* is a genuine entomopathogenic fungal parasite documented in the scientific literature since the early 20th century. It belongs to the order Entomophthorales within the phylum Zoopagomycota (although taxonomic placement has been revised; Entomophthorales was traditionally placed in the Zygomycota before molecular systematics reorganized the fungal kingdom). The fungus is an obligate pathogen of periodical cicadas (genus *Magicicada*), meaning it cannot complete its life cycle without a living cicada host—a specialization that has co-evolved over the extraordinary timescale of periodical cicada emergence cycles, which occur on 13- and 17-year schedules.

The life cycle of *M. cicadina* involves two spore types with distinct timing. Conidia (asexual spores) are produced in early stages of infection and are primarily responsible for horizontal transmission between individuals during mating aggregations. Later, resting spores are produced that can persist in soil for the full 13 or 17 years between cicada emergence events, reinfecting newly emerging nymphs as they surface. This remarkable dormancy represents one of the most extreme examples of parasitic patience in mycology: the fungus essentially waits underground for over a decade for its hosts to reappear (Soper, 1963, *American Journal of Botany*).

The behavioral manipulation is real and scientifically documented. Infected male cicadas produce a wing-flick display typically used by females to signal receptivity to mating. This manipulation causes other males to attempt mating with infected males, dramatically increasing spore transmission during the brief period when cicada populations are densely aggregated (Cooley et al., 2018, *PLOS ONE*). The fungal plug that replaces the abdomen—still functional for spore dispersal—is visible to the naked eye and has been photographed extensively. Infected cicadas continue to fly and feed despite loss of their reproductive organs, which represents a striking example of the parasite's ability to override normal host physiology.

The psychedelic compound discovery is documented in a 2018 paper in *Scientific Reports* by Boyce et al., which used mass spectrometry and genomic analysis to identify psilocybin and its chemical precursor psilocin in *M. cicadina*—as well as cathinone, a psychostimulant alkaloid (also known by the street name "bath salts") not previously known from fungal sources (Boyce et al., 2018, *Scientific Reports*). This finding was notable for two reasons: first, psilocybin was previously thought to be produced only by specific mushroom-forming fungi in the genera *Psilocybe*, *Panaeolus*, *Gymnopilus*, and a few others—not by entomopathogenic fungi in an entirely separate fungal lineage; second, the identification of cathinone (a Schedule I controlled substance derived from the khat plant, usually considered a plant product) in a fungal source was unprecedented.

The implication that these compounds mediate behavioral manipulation—that the cicadas are, in effect, kept in a state of psychedelic drive compelling continued activity—is a hypothesis advanced by the Boyce et al. study authors and discussed in subsequent popular and scientific coverage. It is a plausible hypothesis, but it is important to note that it remains unverified at the level of neurochemical mechanism: the specific receptor targets, the concentrations achieved in cicada neural tissue, and the dose-response relationships required to link psilocybin/cathinone presence to observed behavioral changes have not been directly demonstrated in controlled studies.

## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Framing Obscures

The Snopes article correctly confirms the sensational claim as accurate, which is its primary epistemic function. However, several layers of scientific complexity are absent from the popular framing that would substantially enrich a reader's understanding.

**The evolutionary origin of psilocybin in a non-mushroom-forming fungus requires explanation.** The discovery of psilocybin in *M. cicadina* raised a significant evolutionary question: is the presence of psychoactive alkaloids in cicada-parasitizing fungi an ancient feature inherited from a common ancestor with psilocybin-producing mushrooms, or has the biosynthetic pathway evolved independently (convergent evolution) in the Entomophthorales lineage? The Boyce et al. study found genetic evidence suggesting horizontal gene transfer—the movement of genes between organisms outside of normal vertical inheritance—as a possible pathway for the psilocybin biosynthesis cluster to arrive in *M. cicadina*'s genome from a distantly related fungal lineage (Boyce et al., 2018, *Scientific Reports*). This finding has significant implications for understanding the evolution of secondary metabolite biosynthesis in fungi and the repeated independent evolution of psychedelic chemistry across the fungal kingdom (Reynolds et al., 2018, *Evolution Letters*).

**The classification and evolutionary placement of *Massospora* within Entomophthorales is more complex than the popular account suggests.** Following major revisions to fungal systematics based on molecular phylogenetics (Voigt & Wöstemeyer, 2001, *Gene*; Spatafora et al., 2016, *Mycologia*), the entomopathogenic fungi that were formerly grouped in a broadly defined Zygomycota are now distributed across multiple phyla. The precise placement of *Massospora* and its relationship to other entomopathogenic genera including *Entomophaga*, *Entomophthora*, and *Pandora*—which collectively represent a functionally critical but taxonomically complex group of insect-pathogenic fungi—continues to be refined as molecular sequencing data accumulate. This taxonomic dynamism is relevant because relationships within this group bear on questions about the coevolution of host-parasite behavioral manipulation systems and the evolutionary origins of the behavioral modification mechanisms.

**The behavioral manipulation mechanism is a research frontier, not a settled question.** The claim that psilocybin and cathinone actively mediate the described behavioral modification is an attractive hypothesis with obvious logic: psilocybin acts on serotonergic receptors (5-HT2A receptors) in vertebrate neural systems producing alterations in mood, motivation, and behavior; cathinone releases dopamine and inhibits dopamine reuptake, producing stimulant effects. If cicada neural circuits are subject to comparable pharmacological influence, then the presence of these compounds in fungal tissue replacing the cicada's abdomen could plausibly maintain elevated activity and mating drive despite radical physiological damage.

However, insect neurochemistry is substantially different from mammalian neurochemistry. Insects possess serotonin and dopamine as neurotransmitters, but the receptor pharmacology, the blood-brain barrier equivalents (the blood-brain barrier in insects is the blood-brain junction formed by gap junctions in perineurial and sub-perineurial glia), and the sensitivity of specific insect neural circuits to exogenous psychoactive substrates at the doses that might be achieved through fungal colonization are poorly characterized for cicadas specifically (Bhagya & Bhagya, 2010, *Current Zoology*; Nässel & Winther, 2010, *Progress in Neurobiology*). Demonstrating that psilocybin in *M. cicadina* tissue causes specific behavioral effects in *Magicicada* requires direct experimental intervention—controlled experiments comparing infected cicada behavior with and without specific pharmacological blockade of serotonergic or dopaminergic pathways—that has not been reported in the published literature.

This gap is significant because alternative mechanisms for behavioral manipulation exist and are documented in other host-parasite systems. The behavior-manipulating fungi *Ophiocordyceps unilateralis* (the "zombie ant fungus," famous for compelling infected ants to climb vegetation before killing them) alters ant behavior through mechanical infiltration of muscle tissue rather than exclusively through neurochemical manipulation, as demonstrated by Mangold et al. (2019, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*). It is possible that *M. cicadina* manipulates cicada behavior through analogous mechanical or structural mechanisms that do not require pharmacological action, or through a combination of mechanical and chemical pathways that has yet to be characterized.

**The cathinone finding deserves separate attention as an unprecedented discovery in fungal biochemistry.** Cathinone is an amphetamine-like alkaloid most commonly associated with plant biosynthesis in the khat shrub (*Catha edulis*), which is grown and consumed as a mild stimulant in East Africa and the Arabian Peninsula. Its identification in a fungal source by Boyce et al. was genuinely novel—not merely a rediscovery of a known fungal metabolite—and represents a finding with potential implications for the understanding of psychoactive alkaloid biosynthesis across kingdoms. The biosynthetic pathway for cathinone in *Catha edulis* has been partially characterized (Aerts & De Luca, 2005, *Plant Physiology*), but how or whether a structurally and evolutionarily unrelated organism (a fungus) achieves the same biochemical outcome via an independently evolved or horizontally transferred pathway represents an open research question of genuine scientific interest.

**Public health and regulatory considerations** arising from the psilocybin finding are also underexplored. If *M. cicadina* represents an accessible natural source of psilocybin—from insects that emerge in billions at predictable geographic locations—the discovery potentially has implications for both research access to psilocybin and for regulatory discussions about natural vs. synthetic sourcing. These questions were not legally or practically relevant in 2021, but the ongoing evolution of psychedelic drug policy and research programs (particularly in the context of FDA breakthrough therapy designations for psilocybin-assisted psychotherapy) means that novel natural sources of psilocybin are not merely of academic interest.

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** True (core claims confirmed; mechanistic details partially unresolved)

*Massospora cicadina* is a genuine entomopathogenic fungal parasite that infects periodical cicadas including Brood X, destroys and replaces their abdomens with a spore-producing mass, manipulates their mating behavior to facilitate spore transmission, and contains psilocybin, psilocin, and cathinone. These claims are well-supported by published, peer-reviewed scientific literature. The Snopes verdict of True is appropriate.

**EARC Classification: A (Accurate but mechanistically incomplete)**

The verdict confirms the remarkable fact but does not communicate the scientific uncertainty surrounding the mechanism of behavioral manipulation, the evolutionary novelty of the psilocybin discovery, the taxonomic complexity of the organism's placement, or the implications of the cathinone finding.

**Gap Category: Mechanism Gap**

The primary knowledge gap is the unverified causal link between the presence of psilocybin/cathinone in fungal tissue and the specific behavioral modifications observed in infected cicadas. Popular science framing has treated this causal link as established when it is in fact a plausible hypothesis awaiting direct experimental confirmation. A complete account would also address the evolutionary origin of psilocybin biosynthesis capability in a non-mushroom-forming fungal lineage, and the comparative neuropharmacology required to assess whether psychoactive alkaloid concentrations in insect bodies produce behaviorally relevant effects via mechanisms analogous to those in vertebrates.

## 5. Cross-Connections

The *Massospora cicadina* story connects to a rich cluster of Truth Vault themes centered on host-parasite behavioral manipulation—one of the most scientifically productive and publicly captivating areas of contemporary parasitology.

The most direct connection is to *Ophiocordyceps unilateralis* (the zombie ant fungus), which became a cultural touchstone through David Attenborough's BBC series *Planet Earth* and subsequently through the video game *The Last of Us*. The ant-fungus system has been studied in far greater mechanistic detail than the cicada-*Massospora* system, and findings from the ant model—particularly the debate between neurochemical and mechanical manipulation hypotheses (Andersen et al., 2009, *PLOS ONE*; Mangold et al., 2019, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*)—provide a relevant comparative framework for evaluating claims about *M. cicadina*.

The psilocybin identification connects to broader Truth Vault entries on the biochemistry of psychedelic compounds, the pharmacology of serotonergic systems, and the evolving scientific and clinical landscape around psilocybin-assisted therapy. The recognition that psilocybin biosynthesis has evolved (or been horizontally transferred) into multiple independent fungal lineages has been taken as evidence that the psychoactive effect the compound produces in animal nervous systems may confer some evolutionary advantage to fungi that produce it—possibly through deterring fungal grazing by insects (Slot & Hibbett, 2007, *PLOS ONE*; Reynolds et al., 2018, *Evolution Letters*)—a hypothesis that carries ironic resonance when the fungus in question is producing psilocybin inside insects that it has parasitized and is consuming.

The Brood X emergence itself connects to entries on periodical cicadas' extraordinary life history—the 17-year underground development, the climate of emergence timing, the saturating predator defense strategy (predator satiation), and the ecological function of massive nutrient pulses delivered to forest ecosystems when billions of protein-rich insects emerge, die, and decompose.
