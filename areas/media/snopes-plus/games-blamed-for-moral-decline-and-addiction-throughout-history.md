# Games Have Been Blamed for Moral Decline and Addiction Throughout History


## 1. The Claim

The assertion runs roughly as follows: throughout recorded history, across diverse cultures, games—board games, gambling games, sports, card games, and in the modern era videogames—have been repeatedy condemned as threats to moral order, economic productivity, social discipline, and the character of the young. Every generation, the argument continues, produces its own version of the game panic, convinced that their particular recreational menace is uniquely dangerous, only to be replaced by the next generation's version of the same alarm. Modern videogame moral panic thus belongs to a long and somewhat comic tradition of identical anxieties in historical fancy dress.

This claim appears in gaming advocacy writing, in academic media studies as a point of methodological orientation, and in popular history content designed to place contemporary controversies in long-term perspective. It is cited in arguments about videogame regulation, parental control legislation, and "gaming disorder" classification debates. Its rhetorical function is usually to suggest that moral panic about games is a recurrent social phenomenon whose repetition should induce skepticism about each iteration.

The claim has both historical-descriptive and normative dimensions. The descriptive dimension holds that game panics are genuinely recurrent. The normative implication, often left implicit, is that recurrence suggests these panics are structurally unwarranted—that each generation's game panic was mistaken, so the current one probably is too.


## 2. What's Actually True

The historical record strongly supports the descriptive claim. Game panics are extensively documented across multiple cultures and eras.

**Ancient Rome.** Roman law regulated gambling games (*aleae ludi*) under the Lex Titia and later legislation, prohibiting dice games except during the Saturnalia festival. The *Digest* of Justinian (Justinian I, *Digesta*, 533 CE, Book XI, Chapter 5) records legal provisions allowing those who lost money gambling to recover their losses through legal action—an unusual exception to normal contract law justified by the state's characterization of gambling as corrupting. Emperors from Augustus onward gambled openly as a mark of elite privilege while policing gambling among the lower orders. Cicero's *De Officiis* (44 BCE) classifies excessive gaming among the activities unbecoming a Roman gentleman, linking it to loss of dignitas.

**Medieval Europe.** Church councils from the 3rd century onward issued prohibitions on dice games for clergy. The Council of Elvira (c. 306 CE) prohibited clergy from playing at dice. The 9th-century Carolingian capitularies included restrictions on gambling by clerics and soldiers. By the high medieval period, concerns extended to chess: the Dominican friar John of Salisbury's *Policraticus* (1159) condemned chess as a frivolous waste of time that distracted men from serious study, while Thomas Aquinas addressed chess only cautiously, permitting it as recreation while condemning excessive attachment. Medieval English royal proclamations intermittently banned tennis, football, and other games among commoners on the grounds that they distracted from archery practice essential to national defense—a utilitarian rather than purely moral framing that recurs in every era of game anxiety.

**Imperial China.** The game of mahjong faced periodic condemnation under both imperial and Republican administrations. The anti-mahjong campaigns of the Nationalist government in the 1930s, analyzed by Lo (2004, "Mahjong and Cinema in Republican Shanghai," *Journal of Chinese Cinemas*, 1:1), were explicitly framed as part of national-strengthening programs: mahjong was condemned as a waste of productive time that had contributed to China's national weakness by distracting the population from productive labor and civic engagement. Similar anxieties surrounded weiqí (Go), which was periodically characterized as dangerously absorbing despite its status as one of the four classical arts (*qínqí shūhuà*) that educated men were expected to master. The tension between Go as cultivated accomplishment and Go as addictive time-sink was a genuine feature of Chinese intellectual discourse across the Tang, Song, and Ming dynasties, as documented by Boorman (1969, "Go and Weiqi: New Thoughts on an Old Game," *American Scholar*, 38:4).

**Early modern Europe and playing cards.** Playing cards, introduced to Europe in the late 14th century from Islamic and East Asian sources, generated immediate ecclesiastical alarm. Synods in Florence, Regensburg, and Nuremberg in the 1370s-1380s condemned cards as a diabolical invention. John Northbrooke's *A Treatise Against Dicing, Dancing, Plays, and Interludes* (1577) argued that card games, dice, and theatrical performances collectively eroded Protestant moral seriousness. Puritan literature across the 16th and 17th centuries regularly grouped games with theater and dancing as corruptions of time that should be devoted to scripture and labor—a specifically Protestant theological argument that games were a form of idleness, and idleness was theologically dangerous.

**19th-century pool halls and billiards.** The billiard hall emerged as a site of moral anxiety in 19th-century American reform literature. The Young Men's Christian Association (YMCA), founded in 1844, was partly oriented toward providing alternative recreational spaces to billiard halls and saloons, which reformers characterized as gateways to gambling, alcohol, and sexual vice. The cultural imagery of the pool hall as a site of juvenile delinquency, familiar from Meredith Willson's *The Music Man* (1957), has roots in genuine 19th-century reform discourse rather than being a Broadway invention.

**Pinball.** New York City banned pinball machines from 1940 to 1976 on the grounds that they were gambling devices that preyed on youth. Similar bans existed in Los Angeles, Chicago, and other American cities. The official justification involved gambling, but the reform literature also included arguments about time wasting, truancy, and the distraction of young people from productive activities. The ban was not lifted until pinball was demonstrated in court to require skill rather than pure chance—an interesting standard that treated skill-based games as morally acceptable in ways that purely random games were not (Kocurek, 2012, "The Agony and the Exidy: A History of Video Game Violence and the Legacy of Death Race," *Game Studies*, 12:1).

**Arcade games and the moral panic of the 1970s-80s.** Before the videogame moral panic reached living rooms, the arcade was its first target. Death Race (1976), a driving game in which players ran over stick figures, generated newspaper coverage comparing arcades to gambling dens and attacking the game as a desensitization vehicle. Pac-Man and similar games were blamed for "addictive" machine design intended to extract quarters from youth. Surgeon General C. Everett Koop made public statements in 1982 characterizing videogames as addictive and harmful to children, statements he later acknowledged were made without scientific grounding (Koop, C.E., in *Public Health Reports*, quoted in retrospective by Markey & Ferguson, 2017, *Moral Combat: Why the War on Violent Video Games Is Wrong*, BenBella Books).

**Tabletop role-playing games.** *Dungeons & Dragons* (first published 1974) became the subject of organized moral panic beginning in 1979, when the disappearance of university student James Dallas Egbert III was incorrectly attributed to a LARP session in steam tunnels. The resulting media coverage, and subsequent campaigns by organizations including Bothered About Dungeons and Dragons (BADD), characterized tabletop RPGs as gateways to Satanism, suicide, and psychotic breaks. Pulling et al. (1983) claimed a correlation between D&D playing and youth suicide; these claims were analyzed and rejected by multiple scholars, including Gross (1993, "Understanding Juvenile Violence," *University of Chicago Press*) and Armitage (1988, in *Pediatrics*, questioning the causal framework entirely).

**Modern videogame panic.** The contemporary cycle—from Congressional hearings on Mortal Kombat (1993), through Jack Thompson's campaigns against Grand Theft Auto, to ongoing debates about loot boxes, "gaming disorder" classification by the World Health Organization in ICD-11 (2018), and concerns about social media gamification—is the most recent instance of the pattern. Ferguson & Kilburn (2010, "Much Ado About Nothing: The Misestimation and Overinterpretation of Violent Video Game Effects in Eastern and Western Nations," *Psychological Bulletin*, 136:2) reviewed 136 studies and found publication bias had significantly inflated apparent effect sizes; corrected analyses showed minimal associations between violent game exposure and aggressive behavior.

The claim is, therefore, historically accurate to a high degree. Cross-cultural, multi-era documentation is abundant.


## 3. Why People Believe This

People believe the underlying recurring game panics, not merely the meta-claim about them. The specific mechanism driving each generation's game alarm is **displacement anxiety projection**: moral and social anxieties whose actual sources are diffuse, structural, and politically difficult to address get displaced onto a concrete, specific, behaviorally visible leisure technology. Games are ideal displacement targets because they are publicly visible, associated with idleness, consume time that supervisory authorities (parents, clergy, employers, states) believe should be spent on productive activity, and attract voluntary participation by precisely the populations most subject to moral anxiety—youth.

Each new game technology triggers a fresh iteration because the displacement mechanism is structurally stable across historical contexts. The content changes (dice → cards → pinball → videogames → social media gamification), but the rhetorical structure is constant: the new technology is uniquely dangerous in ways that distinguish it from harmless predecessors; it particularly targets or corrupts the young; it produces addictive response patterns that remove the agent from productive life; its commercial producers are cynically aware of its harm and prioritize profit over welfare. This rhetorical structure was fully elaborated for dice games in Cicero and is fully present in contemporary gaming disorder discourse.

The meta-claim (that game panics are recurrent) is believed by gaming advocates partly because it is accurate, and partly because it offers a debunking resource: if every prior game panic was wrong about its specific target, the structural argument predicts the current one is also wrong. But this inference is not logically guaranteed. The fact that glass-bead moral panics were misfires does not prove that all moral panics misfire, nor that videogame concerns are unfounded in toto—only that the structural pattern should induce careful evidential demands rather than blanket credulity.


## 4. Verdict

**True, with an important normative caveat.** The historical record unambiguously supports the descriptive claim: games have been blamed for moral decline, addiction, and wasted time across virtually every era and culture with documented leisure practices. The pattern is genuine and robust. The normative inference that this pattern proves current concerns are misplaced is weaker than the descriptive claim and requires independent evidential evaluation. Some historical game panics may have identified real harms—gambling addiction is a genuine clinical phenomenon with documented social costs, as reviewed by Petry (2005, *Pathological Gambling: Etiology, Comorbidity, and Treatment*, American Psychological Association)—while others were straightforwardly unfounded.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The recurrence of game panic reflects several durable features of moral economy in organized societies. First, leisure is always a site of contested authority: who controls how nonproductive time is spent is a political question involving family, church, state, and commercial entertainment providers, and each shift in the balance of that authority generates moral anxiety among those losing control. Second, children and youth are persistently categorized as requiring protective supervision, meaning that technologies attracting youth are inherently suspect regardless of their specific content. Third, new communication and entertainment technologies consistently trigger concern during adoption periods when their social effects are genuinely uncertain—a reasonable uncertainty that often calcifies into unfounded certainty in the hands of moral entrepreneurs.

Stanley Cohen's *Folk Devils and Moral Panics* (1972, MacGibbon and Kee) provided the analytic framework that contemporary game panic scholarship inherits, having analyzed the British media's response to Mods and Rockers in the 1960s. Cohen identified the key roles of media amplification, moral entrepreneurs, experts recruited to validate alarms, and the cyclical nature of panics that burn intensely and then subside without policy resolution. Subsequent applications of the folk devil framework to games include Barker & Petley (eds., 2001, *Ill Effects: The Media/Violence Debate*, second edition, Routledge), which collected critical analyses of videogame violence research.

The gambling subset of game anxiety deserves separate treatment because it has partial empirical grounding. Compulsive gambling disorder is recognized as a behavioral addiction in DSM-5, classified under Substance-Related and Addictive Disorders on the basis of neurological similarities to substance addiction (American Psychiatric Association, 2013, *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition*, APA Publishing). Reilly & Smith (2013, "The Evolving Definition of Pathological Gambling in the DSM-5," *National Center for Responsible Gaming White Paper,* NCRG) document the evidence base for the DSM-5 reclassification. The Roman concern about dice addiction and the medieval Church's concern about gambling among clergy therefore had a partially valid target even if the social mechanism (demonic temptation) was wrong. This is a case where the historical alarmism identified a real phenomenon while badly mischaracterizing its mechanism.

The pattern also illustrates how commercial game industries have repeatedly shaped the terms of moral debate. Pinball manufacturers successfully argued in court that pinball required skill to secure its legalization. Video game publishers established the Entertainment Software Rating Board (ESRB) in 1994 as a self-regulatory alternative to government censorship. The legal, regulatory, and rhetorical strategies of game industries facing moral panic have been analyzed in detail by Kocurek (2015, *Coin-Operated Americans: Rebooting Boyhood at the Video Game Arcade*, University of Minnesota Press).

Understanding the pattern of game panics does not require concluding that all games are harmless. It requires maintaining consistent evidential standards: the same skepticism appropriate toward allegations that chess causes moral deterioration should be applied to allegations that violent videogames cause violence, and the same careful clinical attention that identified gambling disorder as genuine should be applied to claims about gaming disorder.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim does not typically reach fact-checkers because it is not falsifiable in the direct sense—it is a historical generalization rather than a specific factual assertion. Snopes, PolitiFact, and similar outlets are designed to evaluate discrete specific claims ("did person X say Y," "did event Z happen"), not historical patterns. The claim has no Snopes entry.

Scholarly treatment is extensive. The media effects research tradition has been critically analyzed from within by researchers including Ferguson (2015, "Do Angry Birds Make for Angry Children? A Meta-Analysis of Video Game Influences on Children's and Adolescents' Aggression, Mental Health, Wellbeing, and Academic Performance," *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 10:5) and Buckley & Anderson (2006, "A Theoretical Model of the Effects and Consequences of Playing Video Games," in Vorderer & Bryant [eds.], *Playing Video Games: Motives, Responses, and Consequences*, Lawrence Erlbaum Associates). The disagreement within psychology about video game harm assessment reflects a genuine methodological dispute about effect size estimation, publication bias, and ecological validity of laboratory aggression measures.

The appropriate response when encountering the historical-pattern claim in popular discourse is to accept the descriptive component, which is well-supported, while resisting the normative shortcut that says "therefore current concerns are unfounded." Each generation's game concerns deserve independent empirical evaluation on their merits. Some will prove groundless; some may identify real harms with wrong mechanisms; a few may be largely correct. The history of game panics tells us to calibrate carefully and demand evidence, not to dismiss concerns categorically because their predecessors were wrong.
