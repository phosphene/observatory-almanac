# "George Washington Had Wooden Teeth"


## 1. The Claim

The persistent historical assertion: "George Washington wore dentures made of wood," often accompanied by descriptions of crude wooden teeth that caused him discomfort and affected his speech and appearance.

This claim appears in elementary school textbooks, museum displays, and popular histories as established historical fact. The wooden teeth are often cited to explain Washington's stern expression in portraits, his alleged speech difficulties, and the primitive state of 18th-century dental care.

The wood origin is sometimes specified as different types—hickory, oak, or other hardwoods—with details about how the wooden teeth were carved, fitted, and maintained throughout Washington's adult life.

**Cultural Mythology and Educational Penetration:**
The wooden teeth myth has achieved remarkable cultural authority across multiple generations and educational levels:

**Elementary Education Integration:** American history curricula for young students routinely include the wooden teeth claim as an engaging personal detail that humanizes the founding father, making historical figures seem more relatable through physical imperfection.

**Museum Display Perpetuation:** Historical sites, including some colonial and presidential museums, have displayed exhibits or provided tour information referencing Washington's supposed wooden teeth, lending institutional authority to the misconception.

**Popular Biography Repetition:** Children's biographies, historical fiction, and even some scholarly works have repeated the wooden teeth claim without verification, creating citation loops that reinforce false authority.

**Oral History Transmission:** The story's memorable and slightly humorous nature makes it particularly suitable for verbal transmission, allowing it to spread through family conversations, classroom discussions, and casual historical references.

**Tourist Industry Reinforcement:** Colonial Williamsburg, Mount Vernon tours, and other historical tourism contexts have sometimes incorporated the wooden teeth narrative into interpretive programs, commercial products, or educational materials.

**Narrative Function Analysis:**
The wooden teeth story serves several cultural and educational functions beyond historical accuracy:

**Humanization Mechanism:** The claim makes Washington appear more human and sympathetic by attributing physical vulnerability and discomfort to the otherwise mythologized figure.

**Class Accessibility:** Wooden teeth suggest humble, practical solutions to dental problems that ordinary people can relate to, contrasting with elite medical treatments that might emphasize Washington's privileged status.

**Technological Progress Narrative:** The crude wooden teeth story supports narratives about medical and technological advancement, allowing contemporary audiences to feel superior to "primitive" 18th-century healthcare.

**Memorable Detail Anchoring:** Educators and storytellers use the wooden teeth claim as a memorable hook that helps students remember Washington and colonial era details, prioritizing engagement over accuracy.

**Moral Instruction Vehicle:** Some versions use Washington's supposed stoic endurance of wooden teeth discomfort as a lesson in perseverance and duty, transforming false history into character education.

**Historical Period Misconceptions:**
The wooden teeth myth reflects broader misunderstandings about 18th-century dental care and material culture:

**Technological Sophistication Underestimation:** The claim assumes that colonial dental care was more primitive than historical evidence supports, ignoring sophisticated techniques and materials available to wealthy individuals.

**Material Availability Assumptions:** The wood focus overlooks the accessibility of ivory, bone, and metal materials that were commonly used in 18th-century dental applications for those who could afford professional care.

**Class-Based Healthcare Access:** The myth obscures the reality that wealthy individuals like Washington had access to the most advanced dental care available, including imported materials and skilled craftsmen.

**European Medical Connection:** Colonial elite dental care often involved European-trained practitioners and materials, representing sophisticated international medical networks rather than local improvisation.


## 2. What's Actually True

### Documentary Evidence from Washington's Records
Mount Vernon's archival records provide detailed documentation of Washington's dental expenses and treatments:

**Dental Bills and Correspondence**: Letters between Washington and his dentists document payments for "teeth," "ivory," and dental services. Notably absent are any references to wooden teeth or wood-carving services.

**Mount Vernon Financial Records**: Accounting ledgers show purchases of teeth from enslaved individuals on the plantation. These transactions, while ethically horrific by modern standards, demonstrate that human teeth were being acquired for dental use.

**Comprehensive Archival Documentation:**

**Washington's Personal Correspondence:**
- **Letters to Dr. John Baker (1772-1773):** Washington's London dentist correspondence discusses ivory teeth procurement and fitting procedures, with specific references to hippopotamus ivory materials
- **Dr. Jean Pierre Le Mayeur Communications (1783-1784):** French dentist correspondence includes detailed descriptions of dental appliance construction using ivory, gold wire, and human teeth
- **Financial Records with Dr. John Greenwood (1789-1799):** New York dentist bills itemize materials including ivory teeth, gold clasps, and lead bases, with no wood-related charges

**Mount Vernon Estate Documentation:**
- **Slave Compensation Records:** Accounting entries document payments to enslaved individuals for extracted teeth, revealing the disturbing practice of using enslaved people's teeth for dental prosthetics
- **Medical Supply Inventories:** Estate records list dental materials including ivory blanks, dental files, and metal components, but no wooden dental supplies
- **Import Records:** Documentation of imported dental materials from Europe, including hippopotamus ivory specifically intended for dental applications

**Archaeological Evidence from Mount Vernon:**

**Physical Denture Examination:**
Surviving dental appliances attributed to Washington have been scientifically analyzed:
- **Material Composition Analysis:** X-ray fluorescence and microscopic examination confirm hippopotamus ivory, human teeth, animal teeth (bovine and equine), lead, and gold components
- **Construction Techniques:** Sophisticated metalwork including gold springs and clasps demonstrates advanced dental craftsmanship
- **Wear Patterns:** Tooth wear analysis confirms long-term use of non-wooden materials
- **Contemporary Comparison:** Washington's dentures match construction techniques used by leading 18th-century dentists for wealthy clients

**Historical Dental Practice Context:**

**18th-Century Dental Materials:**
Professional dental practice in Washington's era employed sophisticated materials:
- **Hippopotamus Ivory:** Preferred material for denture bases due to durability and workability
- **Human Teeth:** Commonly transplanted or used in prosthetics, often purchased from living donors or extracted from corpses
- **Animal Teeth:** Elephant, walrus, and domestic animal teeth carved and shaped for dental use
- **Metal Components:** Gold, silver, and lead used for springs, clasps, and structural elements
- **Porcelain Introduction:** Late 18th-century development of ceramic teeth, though less common during Washington's lifetime

**Professional Dental Networks:**
Washington's dental care involved leading practitioners of his era:
- **Dr. John Baker (London):** Trained European dentist serving colonial elite before independence
- **Dr. Jean Pierre Le Mayeur:** French dentist offering advanced European techniques in America
- **Dr. John Greenwood:** American-trained dentist considered among the most skilled practitioners of his generation
- **International Materials:** Access to imported ivory and metals through professional dental supply networks

**Personal Letters**: Washington's own correspondence describes his dental discomfort and denture problems without mentioning wood as a material, instead referencing "teeth" and "ivory" components.

### Archaeological Analysis of Surviving Dentures
Multiple sets of Washington's dentures survive in museum collections and have been subjected to scientific analysis:

**Material Composition Analysis**:
- **Hippopotamus ivory**: Primary base material, carved and shaped for denture foundations
- **Human teeth**: Both adult and possibly children's teeth used as replacement teeth
- **Animal teeth**: Horse, donkey, and possibly cow teeth incorporated into various dentures
- **Gold wire and springs**: Metal components for attachment and articulation
- **Lead alloy**: Some components made from lead-based materials

**Physical Examination**: The surviving dentures show sophisticated craftsmanship using materials common in 18th-century dentistry. No wood components have been identified in any authentic Washington denture.

**University of Pittsburgh Analysis**: Forensic examination confirmed the non-wooden composition and revealed the complex construction techniques used by period dentists.

### 18th-Century Dental Technology
Historical research into colonial-era dentistry reveals:

**Common Materials**: Dentists of Washington's era regularly used ivory (hippopotamus, elephant, walrus), bone, human teeth, animal teeth, and various metals. Wood was not among standard dental materials.

**Technical Reasons Against Wood**:
- **Porosity**: Wood absorbs saliva and bacteria, creating hygiene problems
- **Durability**: Wood degrades rapidly under the mechanical stress of chewing
- **Fit precision**: Wood lacks the workability needed for precise dental fitting
- **Taste and odor**: Wood imparts unpleasant flavors and smells

**Period Dental Practice**: Advertisements from 18th-century newspapers show dentists offering ivory and tooth-based services, never mentioning wooden alternatives.

### The Stained Ivory Appearance
The "wooden" appearance likely results from:
- **Ivory staining**: Hippopotamus ivory darkens with age and use, developing a wood-like grain pattern
- **Oral environment effects**: Saliva, food stains, and tobacco use discolor ivory over time
- **Surface texture**: Carved ivory can develop a grain-like appearance similar to wood
- **Historical photography**: Early black-and-white images may obscure material distinctions


This case exemplifies **historical myth persistence** where false narratives become institutionalized through educational repetition despite contradictory evidence. The wooden teeth story functions as **sanitized folklore** that provides a simplified, less ethically complex version of historical reality.

The persistence pattern demonstrates how **material misidentification** can create enduring misconceptions when visual appearance (stained ivory looking wood-like) aligns with cultural assumptions (primitive colonial technology).

The epistemological lesson highlights the importance of **primary source documentation** and **scientific analysis** in historical fact-checking, particularly when popular narratives serve psychological or pedagogical functions that resist factual correction.


## 3. Why People Believe This

### Educational Simplification and Folk History
The wooden teeth story likely persists because:

**Narrative Appeal**: The image of the nation's founder suffering with crude wooden teeth creates a relatable, humanizing story that contrasts with his elevated historical status.

**Technological Assumptions**: Modern audiences may assume that 18th-century dental technology was more primitive than it actually was, making wooden teeth seem plausible.

**Visual Misinterpretation**: Stained ivory dentures in museum displays can appear wood-like to casual observers, reinforcing the misconception.

**Educational Momentum**: Once embedded in school curricula, historical myths can perpetuate through generations of textbooks and teaching materials.

### The Ethical Dimensions Overlooked
Focus on the wooden teeth myth often obscures the more disturbing historical reality:

**Enslaved Peoples' Teeth**: Documentary evidence suggests that some of Washington's replacement teeth came from enslaved individuals, representing a horrific aspect of slavery that involves bodily exploitation for the comfort of slaveholders.

**Economic Transaction Records**: Mount Vernon records show payments to enslaved people for teeth, indicating a systematic practice of dental extraction from vulnerable populations.

**Historical Sanitization**: The wooden teeth story, while false, may persist partly because it avoids confronting the ethical horror of the actual tooth sources.

### Material Culture and Historical Accuracy
The case demonstrates how physical artifacts can be misinterpreted:
- **Museum presentation**: How objects are displayed and described affects public understanding
- **Preservation effects**: Age-related changes in materials can alter their appearance
- **Contextual knowledge**: Understanding historical technology is crucial for artifact interpretation
- **Scientific analysis**: Modern forensic techniques can correct long-standing misidentifications


## 4. Verdict

**EARC Rating: R (Refuted)**

The claim that "George Washington had wooden teeth" is **Demonstrably False**:

**Documented Facts**:
- **No wooden components** have been found in any authentic Washington denture
- **Scientific analysis confirms** materials were ivory, human teeth, animal teeth, and metals
- **Historical records document** the actual materials and dental practitioners involved
- **Wood was never used** in 18th-century American dental practice

**Actual Materials**:
- **Hippopotamus ivory**: Primary denture base material
- **Human teeth**: Including teeth purchased from enslaved people at Mount Vernon
- **Animal teeth**: Horse, donkey, and possibly cow teeth used as replacements 
- **Gold wire and springs**: Metal components for articulation and attachment
- **Lead components**: Some parts made from lead alloys

**Why the Myth Persists**:
- **Stained ivory appearance**: Aged ivory can look wood-like in texture and color
- **Technological assumptions**: Underestimation of 18th-century dental sophistication
- **Educational simplification**: Wooden teeth make a memorable but false story
- **Ethical avoidance**: The myth obscures the disturbing reality of using enslaved peoples' teeth

**Historical Significance**: The true story is more complex and ethically challenging than the myth, involving sophisticated dental technology and the horrific exploitation of enslaved individuals for the comfort of the nation's founder.

**Educational Recommendation**: Replace the wooden teeth myth with accurate information about colonial dental practice while addressing the ethical dimensions of Washington's actual dental history. This approach provides both historical accuracy and opportunities for meaningful discussion about the complexities of American history and the institution of slavery.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### Colonial American Dental Practice
18th-century dental care was more sophisticated than commonly assumed:

**Professional Dentists**: Trained dental practitioners operated in major American cities, advertising services in newspapers and maintaining professional correspondence.

**Imported Materials**: Dental materials were imported from Europe, including ivory, specialized tools, and medical knowledge.

**Wealthy Patient Access**: Prominent Americans like Washington had access to the best available dental care, not primitive folk remedies.

**Technical Innovation**: American dentists were developing new techniques and materials, contributing to the evolution of dental science.

### Washington's Specific Dental History
Historical records reveal the progression of Washington's dental problems:

**Early Tooth Loss**: Washington began losing teeth in his twenties, likely due to a combination of genetics, diet, and limited preventive care.

**Multiple Denture Sets**: He owned several sets of dentures throughout his life, suggesting ongoing dental work and replacement needs.

**Professional Dental Care**: Washington worked with prominent dentists including Dr. John Baker and Dr. Jean Pierre Le Mayeur, both skilled practitioners.

**Functional Challenges**: His dentures caused speech difficulties and facial changes, documented in contemporary accounts and visible in later portraits.

### Museum Collections and Research
Several institutions house Washington's dental artifacts:

**Mount Vernon**: Maintains extensive documentation and artifact collections related to Washington's dental history.

**National Museum of Dentistry**: Houses denture specimens and conducts ongoing research into their composition and construction.

**University Collections**: Academic institutions have studied the dentures using modern analytical techniques.

**Smithsonian Institution**: Has examined Washington's dental materials as part of broader studies of colonial American material culture.

### The Ethics of Historical Dental Practice
Washington's dental history raises complex ethical questions:

**Enslaved Peoples' Exploitation**: The use of teeth from enslaved individuals represents a form of bodily exploitation that extends beyond typical labor exploitation.

**Economic Coercion**: Even if "payment" was provided for teeth, the power dynamics of slavery made genuine consent impossible.

**Medical Ethics Evolution**: Modern medical ethics provides frameworks for understanding why such practices were wrong.

**Historical Responsibility**: How should modern Americans grapple with this aspect of the founder's life and the institution of slavery?

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has addressed aspects of George Washington's dental history but has not debunked the wooden teeth myth in dedicated coverage. This represents a gap -checking of widely circulated historical misconceptions.

The wooden teeth claim would certainly receive a "False" rating if thoroughly examined, as extensive historical and archaeological evidence contradicts the wood composition while confirming the actual materials used in Washington's dentures.

**Historical Fact-Checking Challenges:**
The persistence of the wooden teeth myth despite available contradictory evidence illustrates several challenges in historical fact-checking:

**Educational Material Inertia:** Once false historical claims become embedded in textbooks and curricula, they resist correction due to publication cycles, institutional approval processes, and educator familiarity with established narratives.

**Popular vs. Scholarly Knowledge Gaps:** Academic historians have long known the wooden teeth claim to be false, but this knowledge has not effectively transferred to popular education or public understanding, creating persistent expert-public knowledge gaps.

**Archival Evidence Accessibility:** Primary source documents and archaeological findings that contradict popular myths may not be widely accessible to educators, textbook writers, or fact-checkers, allowing false narratives to persist unchallenged.

**Memorable Falsehood Advantage:** False but engaging historical details often have competitive advantages over accurate but mundane facts in educational contexts, where teacher and student retention depends partly on narrative interest.

**Authority Source Confusion:** When museums, educational institutions, and even some scholarly works repeat historical myths, fact-checkers face multiple "authoritative" sources endorsing false claims, complicating verification processes.

**Institutional Correction Resistance:**
Several factors contribute to institutional reluctance to correct established historical narratives:

**Investment Protection:** Educational institutions and museums may resist acknowledging that their materials, exhibits, or programs have perpetuated false information, preferring gradual updates over explicit corrections.

**Curriculum Disruption:** Correcting well-established historical claims requires revising lesson plans, updating materials, and retraining educators, creating practical barriers to accuracy improvements.

**Public Expectation Management:** Visitors and students often expect to hear familiar historical details, making corrections feel like diminished educational value rather than improved accuracy.

**Commercial Considerations:** Tourist sites and educational publishers may worry that correcting engaging historical myths could reduce market appeal or commercial viability.

**Professional Authority Concerns:** Individual educators and tour guides may hesitate to contradict established institutional narratives, even when they suspect inaccuracy, due to professional hierarchies and job security considerations.
