## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In mid-July 2019, photographs and video footage of a diver swimming alongside an extremely large jellyfish off the coast of Falmouth, Cornwall, England, went viral across multiple social media platforms and achieved extensive mainstream media coverage globally. The images showed wildlife biologist Lizzie Daly swimming in close proximity to a barrel jellyfish (*Rhizostoma pulmo*) that she estimated at approximately 1.5 meters (roughly 5 feet) in length—approximately the length of her own body. The footage was captured by underwater photographer and cinematographer Dan Abbott as part of Daly's "Wild Ocean Week" fundraising project for the Marine Conservation Society.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2019) assigned this claim the verdict **True**. The footage is entirely authentic; no forced perspective, digital manipulation, or other form of visual distortion is present. The jellyfish is as large as it appears, and barrel jellyfish of this scale are known to occur in UK waters, particularly in summer months when warm water conditions favor jellyfish blooms. However, the framing of this as a "giant jellyfish" requires substantial contextual qualification: the barrel jellyfish, despite its impressive size in these photographs, is not the largest jellyfish in the ocean, is not classified as dangerous to humans (it produces only a mild sting often imperceptible to human skin), and is in fact considerably smaller than the lion's mane jellyfish (*Cyanea capillata*), which can achieve body lengths exceeding 36 meters (approximately 120 feet) when tentacle extension is included.

This entry examines the biology of *Rhizostoma pulmo* and the broader class of megajellyfish, the ecology of barrel jellyfish blooms in UK and European waters (including the role of ocean warming trends), the visual and psychological dimensions of how humans respond to large invertebrates encountered at close range, and the broader scientific context of jellyfish population dynamics in the Anthropocene.

## §2 The Incident, the Footage, and the Animal

The encounter took place in late July 2019 off the coast of Falmouth, a coastal town in Cornwall on England's southwestern peninsula. Falmouth and the surrounding Cornish coast constitute a documented corridor for barrel jellyfish sightings in UK waters, and the Marine Conservation Society's annual "Great British Jellyfish Survey" consistently records *Rhizostoma pulmo* sightings along the southwestern English and Welsh coasts during summer months.

Lizzie Daly, the swimmer in the footage, is a wildlife biologist and science communicator who was conducting a week of underwater filming and diving encounters—"Wild Ocean Week"—for the purpose of raising public awareness of UK marine biodiversity and fundraising for the Marine Conservation Society. Abbott, her diving partner, posted multiple photographs of the encounter to his Instagram account from various angles, providing the visual multiplicity from different perspectives and distances that allows confident assessment that the jellyfish's apparent size is not an artifact of a single carefully composed image.

The barrel jellyfish (*Rhizostoma pulmo*) is a member of the class Scyphozoa (the "true jellyfish") and the order Rhizostomeae. It is the largest extant jellyfish species regularly found in UK and European Atlantic waters. The Snopes investigation corrected a potential misunderstanding in some reporting by noting that while the Daly jellyfish appeared "the size of a human," this is a substantial size for the species but is actually not close to the record for the largest jellyfish overall—a distinction that several news stories elided or failed to make clearly.

Daly told the Guardian: "I've never seen one that big... We had seen a few smaller jellyfish at a beautiful reef nearby, and then out of the murk came this huge, beautiful jellyfish. You just take a double look and ask yourself if it's actually a metre and a half long." The encounter's viral quality derived precisely from this genuine element of surprise: a species known to UK divers primarily as an occasional summer visitor appeared at unusual scale, in conditions that allowed extended close observation and high-quality photography.

Abbott's professional underwater cinematographer background is relevant to the footage quality: the images were sharp, well-exposed, and taken from multiple angles with Daly present as a human-scale reference in most frames, making independent assessment of the jellyfish's size relatively straightforward. Daly's estimate of approximately 1.5 meters bell diameter is consistent with the visual record.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Biometric Verification of Large Animal Claims and Jellyfish Size Biology

The epistemically notable feature of this case is that it represents the inverse of the usual large-animal-claim verification challenge: instead of footage being manipulated to make an animal appear larger than it is (cf. the 10-meter anaconda case), here the footage is authentic and the animal is genuinely large—but the authentic size requires contextual biological knowledge to interpret correctly. The primary epistemic task is not detecting manipulation but providing the context that transforms a striking visual into a scientifically meaningful observation.

The barrel jellyfish size record is an interesting case in animal measurement biology. *Rhizostoma pulmo* has a documented range of bell diameters spanning roughly 10 centimeters (juvenile) to approximately 90 centimeters (typical large adult) in UK waters, with occasional specimens exceeding one meter—making Daly's 1.5-meter estimate either at the absolute maximum for the species or slightly above typical documented maximums. Hay et al. (2011), writing in *Journal of the Marine Biological Association of the United Kingdom* (Cambridge University Press), documented UK barrel jellyfish sightings and reported bell diameters primarily in the 40–80 centimeter range in systematic survey data, with large individuals forming the tail of a right-skewed distribution. This context means the observed animal represents a genuinely unusual specimen—not merely a typical barrel jellyfish filmed at close range—even before considering any filming conditions.

The size of the lion's mane jellyfish (*Cyanea capillata*)—the animal Evon correctly identifies as considerably larger than *R. pulmo*—merits scientific context. The frequently cited maximum size for lion's mane jellyfish derives from a specimen measured by Alexander Agassiz in Massachusetts Bay in 1870, which had a bell diameter of approximately 2.3 meters and tentacles extending more than 36 meters (about 120 feet) (Moore, 2012, citing historical records in *The Scientific Monthly*, American Association for the Advancement of Science). Subsequent measurement of comparably sized individuals has been limited by the obvious logistical difficulty of measuring unconstrained live animals in the open ocean; contemporary size records in systematically collected literature data rarely document individuals exceeding 1.5–2 meters bell diameter (Marsh, 2019, *Marine Biology*, Springer Nature), though encounters with very large individuals are periodically reported by commercial fishers and divers.

The epistemic novelty lies in what might be called the **authentic marvel problem**: some viral wildlife content is authentic and the animal is genuinely large, but misinterpretation can still occur in two directions. Some viewers questioned whether the footage was manipulated because the jellyfish appeared too large to be "real"—a false skepticism that reflects the same size-prior calibration problem as size manipulation cases, but in reverse. Other viewers, seeing the coverage label this a "giant jellyfish," assumed it was the largest jellyfish possible, missing the biological context that places *R. pulmo* and *C. capillata* on very different scales. Both misreadings require scientific context to correct, even though neither involves any deception: the footage is authentic and the coverage was largely though not uniformly accurate.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Evon's (2019) investigation confirmed the footage's authenticity, identified its subjects and context, and provided appropriate scale comparison to the lion's mane jellyfish. Several threads merit supplementary attention:

**The species identification precision**: The investigation refers consistently to "barrel jellyfish" and attributes a size of approximately 5 feet (1.5 meters). *Rhizostoma pulmo* is a well-attested member of the order Rhizostomeae, identifiable by its opaque bell (lacking the visible oral arms of some related species) and robust body form without trailing tentacles of the kind that characterize Semaeostomeae jellyfish like *Cyanea capillata*. However, species identification from video footage and its confirmation was not independently documented through expert biological assessment in the Snopes write-up.

**Jellyfish bloom frequency context**: The investigation treats this as an individual exceptional encounter without situating it within the context of whether barrel jellyfish bloom events have become more frequent in UK waters. This context is scientifically important: if blooms are increasingly frequent, the probability of future similar encounters increases, and the conservation and ecological significance of the images differs depending on whether they document a rarity or a new baseline.

**The Marine Conservation Society fundraising context**: The Wild Ocean Week project was explicitly a fundraising campaign for the Marine Conservation Society. This context—the encounter was sought as part of a deliberate marine wildlife documentation effort during a conservation fundraiser—is relevant to understanding why such high-quality footage exists and was widely disseminated. The conservation communication dimensions of the viral encounter (what it achieved for public engagement with UK marine biodiversity) are not addressed.

**Record documentation status**: Whether this specific individual, if it could be confirmed at 1.5 meters bell diameter, would constitute a UK or European record for the species is not established in the investigation. Systematic records of maximum *R. pulmo* size in UK waters are maintained by the Marine Conservation Society's survey program; comparison to those records was not performed.

## §5 Jellyfish in the Changing Ocean: Bioindicators of Environmental State

The barrel jellyfish encounter off Cornwall is not merely an isolated viral moment; it sits within a broader scientific debate about whether jellyfish populations globally are increasing, and if so, what that increase signals about ocean ecological state.

Jellyfish blooms—mass aggregations of individual medusae often encompassing billions of animals—are episodic events that have been documented for decades throughout the world's major ocean basins. Their relationship to fisheries, aquaculture, and ocean ecosystem health is complex. Purcell (2012), writing in *Hydrobiologia* (Springer Nature), reviewed evidence for and against global jellyfish population increases and documented genuine uncertainty: bloom frequency is difficult to track because historical records are inconsistent and jellyfish populations naturally fluctuate over decadal cycles. However, certain structural factors—ocean warming, overfishing of jellyfish predators and competitors, coastal eutrophication, and the proliferation of artificial hard substrates (piers, docks, offshore platforms) that provide settlement habitat for jellyfish polyp stages—consistently favor jellyfish over competing taxa.

Brotz et al. (2012), writing in *Hydrobiologia* (Springer Nature), conducted a meta-analysis of jellyfish population trends across 45 large marine ecosystems and found that 62% showed increasing trends while only 15% showed decreasing trends, with the remainder showing no significant trend or insufficient data. This pattern is consistent with a global-scale shift in favor of gelatinous zooplankton in ocean ecosystems restructured by human activity.

In UK waters specifically, including the Celtic Sea and English Channel where barrel jellyfish are regularly surveyed, citizen science data from the Marine Conservation Society indicates year-to-year variability in barrel jellyfish abundance with occasional exceptional years—such as 2019—that produced large numbers of large individuals. Whether this represents a long-term increase or normal inter-annual variability is not established from existing data.

The conservation significance of this ecological context extends beyond jellyfish themselves: barrel jellyfish (*R. pulmo*) in UK waters serve as important prey items for leatherback sea turtles (*Dermochelys coriacea*), which make extensive seasonal migrations to UK and Irish waters to feed on jellyfish aggregations (Fossette et al., 2010, *Marine Ecology Progress Series*, Inter-Research Science Center). The whale shark (*Rhincodon typus*) and numerous seabird species also consume jellyfish. Understanding jellyfish abundance dynamics is therefore directly relevant to the conservation of multiple charismatic megafauna that the marine conservation community actively monitors.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: TRUE**

The photographs and video do show a genuinely large barrel jellyfish (*Rhizostoma pulmo*) off the coast of Falmouth, Cornwall, filmed by Dan Abbott and featuring wildlife biologist Lizzie Daly in July 2019. The footage is authentic, unmanipulated, and represented accurately: Daly estimated the jellyfish at approximately 1.5 meters (5 feet) in bell diameter, consistent with the visual record. This is a large specimen of its species, though the barrel jellyfish is not the largest jellyfish in the ocean—that distinction belongs to the lion's mane jellyfish (*Cyanea capillata*), whose total body length (including tentacles) can exceed 36 meters (120 feet). The encounter is genuine; the "giant jellyfish" framing is accurate for the species context but should not suggest this is the largest jellyfish possible.

**EARC Classification: R (Representational — authentic marvel requiring biological context)**

The analytical gap is representational: the footage is authentic and needs no debunking, but correct interpretation requires biological context (species identification, comparison across jellyfish species, UK barrel jellyfish size records) that is not present in the original viral framing. This is a "true but incomplete" case where the Snopes investigation adds genuine epistemic value through contextual enrichment rather than false-claim refutation.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Whether the specific individual observed constitutes a documented size record for *Rhizostoma pulmo* in UK or European waters
- Systematic trend data on barrel jellyfish bloom frequency and individual maximum sizes in UK Celtic Sea and English Channel waters from Marine Conservation Society survey records
- Species phylogenetics and anatomical details relevant to confident remote species identification from the footage
- Broader ecological drivers of the 2019 barrel jellyfish abundance year in UK waters, and how it compared to surrounding years in long-term survey data
