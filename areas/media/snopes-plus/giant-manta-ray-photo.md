## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A photograph depicting an enormous flat-bodied sea creature displayed on or near a pier has circulated widely online, typically accompanied by captions asserting that it shows a genuine giant manta ray—sometimes called a "devil fish"—caught in 1933 off the coast of New Jersey. The claim is substantially accurate in its core historical elements, but the specific viral photograph has been misunderstood by many viewers.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2018) rated this claim **Mostly True**. The underlying fishing adventure is historically documented: in 1933, a New York silk manufacturer named Captain A.L. Kahn inadvertently snared a massive manta ray when the animal became entangled in his anchor line during a boating trip off the New Jersey coast. After a harrowing multi-hour battle—one that required the assistance of a U.S. Coast Guard cutter and approximately twenty bullets to subdue the animal—Kahn brought the creature ashore at Brielle, New Jersey. The ray was found to weigh between 5,000 and 6,000 pounds and measured 20 feet and 5 inches in width.

What is false, or more precisely misleading, is the implied nature of the most-shared photograph. The viral image does not show the freshly-caught manta ray as it was hauled ashore; instead, it shows the mounted, taxidermied version of the animal, which Kahn had prepared so that he could exhibit it at public events across the eastern United States. The preserved specimen is real, the story is real, but the photograph is of a taxidermist's reconstruction rather than the living (or freshly dead) animal. This is a meaningful distinction because taxidermy of an animal of this scale in the 1930s was a significant and somewhat unusual undertaking, and the preserved form may not precisely replicate the animal's original dimensions or skin texture.

## §2 The Documented Historical Event

The 1933 manta ray catch is not a piece of mere folklore. It was documented in contemporaneous newspaper reporting, including a substantial account published in the *St. Louis Post-Dispatch* Sunday Magazine on 10 December 1933 (Strawn, 1933). The account described Kahn—a successful New York silk manufacturer who kept a cabin cruiser, the *Miss Pensacola II*, for leisure fishing—as being on a routine outing off the New Jersey coast when he discovered his anchor line had become snagged on something enormous. The subsequent struggle lasted approximately three hours. The creature, described as a "giant devilfish," dragged Kahn's boat against the wind and tide before the arrival of a U.S. Coast Guard cutter provided enough additional horsepower and firepower (approximately twenty bullets were discharged into the animal) to subdue it.

The manta ray was brought ashore at Brielle, New Jersey, where it became an immediate local sensation. Kahn took advantage of the public curiosity and began charging ten cents per head for admission to view the creature. The Post-Dispatch account noted that within the first three days, enough admission money had been collected to donate a new $3,000 fire truck to the local fire department—a substantial sum in Depression-era America (Strawn, 1933).

The Post-Dispatch noted that the manta's dimensions—20 feet and 5 inches in width, and between 5,000 and 6,000 pounds—were remarkable. Whether or not these measurements were precise (field measurements of extremely large, recently killed marine animals are notoriously difficult), they were broadly plausible. The Florida Museum of Natural History documents the oceanic manta ray (*Mobula birostris*, formerly *Manta birostris*) as having an average wingspan of approximately 22 feet (6.7 meters), with weights typically up to about 3,000 pounds, though exceptional specimens have been reported at higher weights (Florida Museum, 2018). A second contemporaneous newspaper account—cited by Evon (2018) from a Florida newspaper of the same era—described authorities hauling in a separate "devil fish" estimated at more than 5,000 pounds, lending contextual support to Kahn's weight claim.

After the initial rush of paying visitors, Kahn had the manta sent to a taxidermist to be preserved for exhibition. The *St. Louis Post-Dispatch* noted that the taxidermied specimen was placed on exhibit in New York City, with the profits from exhibition intended for charity, and Kahn expressed a desire ultimately to donate it to a natural history museum (Strawn, 1933). A second confirming photograph—published in the *Honolulu Star-Bulletin* (Evon, 2018)—identified Kahn standing alongside the mounted specimen and specified it had been mounted for exhibition, with a caption noting that "Captain Kahn is seen holding a baby devil fish born after the parent's death," indicating the female ray was pregnant when captured.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Taxidermy, Preservation, and the Distortion of Historical Animal Records

The "Mostly True" rating applied to this claim is a useful illustration of a recurring epistemic problem in historical biology and zoology: the mediation between living specimens and visual record through the transformative process of taxidermy. When the viral photo circulates with captions like "giant manta ray caught in 1933," virtually everything in that caption is accurate—but the photograph itself introduces a layer of representational uncertainty that is rarely acknowledged.

Professional taxidermy of marine animals, particularly large elasmobranchs (rays and sharks), in the early twentieth century was technically problematic. Unlike mammalian mounts, which use the tanned hide over an artificial form, marine animal skins present preservation challenges: cartilaginous skeletons (manta rays, like all batoid rays, have no true bone), large volumes of water-retention tissue, and rapid decomposition in warm temperatures all complicate the taxidermist's task. The resulting mounted specimen frequently represented a combination of preserved original material with substantial reconstructive work—artificial forms, reshaped fins, strategic padding—that reflected aesthetic judgment as much as biological accuracy (Fisher, 2013, *Taxidermy and Natural History Collections*, Museum Management and Curatorship). This means the mounted specimen that appears in the viral photograph may differ from the original animal in ways that are not obvious to viewers: the wings may be extended to their maximum span rather than their resting position, the body may appear bulkier or differently proportioned, and skin color or texture will have been altered by preservation.

The 1930s were a period of considerable public fascination with large marine animals, partially driven by sensationalized press. The term "devil fish"—applied to manta rays since at least the mid-nineteenth century due to their horn-like cephalic fins and occasional perceived tendency to engulf divers—served as a ready-made narrative of monster-discovery for newspaper editors (Homma et al., 1999, *Rays of the World*, Food and Agriculture Organization). This narrative context shaped how Kahn presented his catch, how newspapers covered it, and how the taxidermied trophy was subsequently exhibited. The result is that the object in the viral photograph is not merely a scientific specimen but a cultural artifact constructed jointly by a taxidermist, a showman-fisherman, and a Depression-era media environment predisposed to celebrate monster stories.

For epistemic purposes, this creates what might be called the *historical specimen problem*: when a photograph of a preserved or mounted animal is recirculated as evidence of an extreme-size claim—without identification of the mediating taxidermist's work—viewers cannot know whether they are seeing biological reality or theatrical reconstruction. The Kahn manta case is particularly instructive because the claim is genuinely "Mostly True"—the core event is documented, the size is plausible per the biology—yet the specific visual evidence (the photograph) represents a third-order document: a photograph of a taxidermied reconstruction of an animal caught in 1933.

The persistence and virality of this particular image also illuminates the broader asymmetry between visual and textual persuasion in internet-era fact-checking. The newspaper accounts that confirm the catch are available, but they require reading and source-tracing. The photograph is immediate and viscerally compelling. Research on dual-process cognition and risk perception (Fischhoff et al., 1978, *Policy Sciences*; Slovic, 1987, *Science*) consistently finds that vivid visual information activates System 1 (fast, intuitive) processing more powerfully than textual descriptions, producing stronger and more durable beliefs. A dramatic photograph of an enormous flat creature is processed as biological evidence of an enormous flat creature, regardless of whether the observer consciously registers that it was taken after a taxidermist's intervention.

## §4 The Biology of Manta Rays and the "Devil Fish" Tradition

The oceanic manta ray, *Mobula birostris*, is the largest ray species currently recognized. It belongs to the family Mobulidae, order Myliobatiformes, within the superorder Batoidea (rays and skates). Unlike most stingrays, manta rays bear no tail spine and are harmless; the "devil fish" epithet, while historically colorful, is ornithologically inaccurate and reflects the cephalic fins (used to funnel plankton-rich water into the feeding animal's open mouth) which can appear horn- or wing-like when viewed from above.

Adult *Mobula birostris* typically reach a disc width—measured across the "wingspan" between wing tips—of 4.5 to 5 meters (approximately 15 to 16.5 feet), with exceptional individuals recorded up to approximately 7 meters (about 23 feet) (White et al., 2006, *Zootaxa*—the paper reclassifying devil rays into the genus *Mobula*). Weight records are less reliable than length records because weighing a multi-meter-wide elasmobranch is mechanistically difficult; the Florida Museum of Natural History (2018) notes typical weights of up to approximately 1,350 kilograms (about 2,980 pounds), consistent with the upper range of scientifically documented specimens.

Kahn's reported dimensions—5,000 to 6,000 pounds, 20 feet and 5 inches across—fall somewhat above typical maxima but are not scientifically impossible, particularly for the early twentieth century when oceanic populations had been subject to less fishing pressure. It is worth noting, however, that weight estimates for large marine animals handled on docks in 1933 were not performed with calibrated equipment; a rough slung-scale estimate or straightforward visual assessment could carry substantial error. Evon (2018) notes mild skepticism about the weight figures but concedes they are within the "realm of possibility" given the species biology. A separate newspaper account from Fort Lauderdale (Evon, 2018) describing a different 5,000-pound devil fish caught around the same period and requiring a city-owned crane for extraction provides additional contextual evidence that such animals were occasionally caught in Atlantic coastal waters in the early twentieth century.

The species now enjoys protections in international waters: *Mobula birostris* is listed on Appendix II of CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species) and classified as "Endangered" on the IUCN Red List (Marshall et al., 2011, *IUCN Red List of Threatened Species*). Threats include direct harvesting—primarily for gill-raker plates used in traditional medicine markets—and bycatch in pelagic fisheries (Couturier et al., 2012, *Biological Conservation*). By the early 2010s reef manta ray populations (*Mobula alfredi*) declined up to 86% in some regions over three reef manta generation lengths (Croll et al., 2016, *Science of the Total Environment*, cited in Marshall et al., 2011).

## §5 Viral Spread and the Emotional Architecture of Animal Awe

The manta ray photograph plugs into a recognized psychological structure: awe at the scale of non-human life. Research on awe as an emotion—notably the foundational theoretical paper by Keltner and Haidt (2003, *Cognition and Emotion*)—identifies two key appraisals: perceived vastness (the stimulus is perceived as much greater in scale than the self or the ordinary) and a need for accommodation (the stimulus challenges existing mental schemas). Both conditions are met when a viewer encounters what appears to be a photographic document of an animal larger than a car.

Bridget et al. (2017, *Psychological Science*) found that awe-inducing content has unusually high social sharing rates; experimentally induced awe elevated participants' willingness to share content compared to neutral or amusing stimuli. This helps explain the particular virality of animal-scale images regardless of their accuracy. The social function of sharing such an image is not merely transmitting information but inviting the viewer's community to co-experience awe—a socially bonding function that operates independently of concerns about the photograph's precise epistemic status.

The Kahn catch was itself designed as a spectacle, and Kahn was a sophisticated showman. The progression from natural wonder (the anchor-line catch) to public exhibition (ten-cent admission at Brielle) to taxidermied touring display replays a classic American tradition of natural curiosity display that dates to P.T. Barnum. The resulting photograph is therefore not merely an accidental document but a product of a deliberate display strategy—and this cultural genealogy is largely invisible to contemporary social media users who encounter the image divorced from its production context.

## §6 Contemporary Relevance and Knowledge Gaps

This case illustrates several durable tensions in viral fact-checking:

**Temporal dislocation of visual evidence.** The photograph dates from no earlier than late 1933 (after the taxidermy was completed) and was taken in a media context entirely different from that of contemporary social media. Its recirculation in the 2010s and beyond strips away the original contextual cues (contemporaneous newspaper captions identifying it as a mounted specimen) that would have provided some disambiguation to original viewers.

**The EARC gap assigned here is R (Recency).** The core claim is well-resolved—the history is documented, the biology is plausible, the photograph's nature as a taxidermy mount is clearly identified. However, the scientific record of pre-industrial Atlantic manta ray population size and maximum individual dimensions is incomplete. The extraordinary specimens recorded in the 1930s era literature may reflect a now-lost population that typically grew larger than contemporary individuals (potentially due to lifespan truncation from fishing pressure), or they may reflect measurement exaggeration; no modern study has rigorously addressed this historical question. The literature on elasmobranch population baseline biology (historical size-at-age reconstruction from pre-exploitation populations) is a recognized research gap in marine conservation science (Dulvy et al., 2014, *eLife*).

**Knowledge gaps:** What were baseline maximum sizes of *Mobula birostris* in pre-industrial Atlantic populations? How accurately can taxidermied specimens from the 1930s be used to reconstruct original animal dimensions? At what point did the viral photograph detach from its original captioning as a "mounted specimen" and begin circulating as evidence of a living or freshly-caught animal?

**Practical implication for consumers of viral animal photographs:** any dramatic photograph purporting to document an extremely large animal—particularly one linked to a historical event—warrants examination of whether the image shows the living animal, a dead but unpreserved animal, or a taxidermied mount. All three categories are epistemically different, and the distinction is rarely legible from the image alone.
