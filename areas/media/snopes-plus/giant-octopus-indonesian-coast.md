## The Claim

In early June 2024, an image began circulating across social media — most heavily on the platform X (formerly Twitter) — purporting to show a real, monstrous octopus washed up on the coast of Indonesia. Multiple posts claimed the photograph was taken in Bali, and the image spread rapidly through nature enthusiast communities, cryptozoology circles, and general viral-content feeds. The octopus depicted in the image was gigantic by any measure: its bloated, glistening body sprawled across a sandy beach, five tentacles visible and reaching outward, with a small crowd of beachgoers gathered at a respectful distance as though stunned by the creature before them. The scale of the animal compared to the human bystanders was breathtaking. If real, it would have been one of the most extraordinary marine discoveries of the modern era.

Posts accompanying the image used language designed to amplify credibility. Some framed it as a breaking environmental event. Others invoked concerns about ocean health, climate disruption, or deep-sea disturbances. The image resonated because it tapped into two simultaneous currents of cultural anxiety: fear of what the deep ocean contains, and a growing, generalised dread that ecosystems are behaving in unprecedented ways. Neither of these anxieties is unfounded in the abstract — real marine science regularly produces genuinely astonishing finds — but in this case, the image itself was a fabrication.

The claim, stated plainly: an authentic photograph shows the body of a massive octopus washed up on the Indonesian coast.

---

## What's Actually True

The image is fake. It was generated by artificial intelligence.

Snopes fact-checker Nur Nasreen Ibrahim traced the image through a reverse-image search and found no authentic origin — no news outlet reported the event, no photographer claimed credit, and no environmental or marine biology organisation commented on it. The earliest known appearance of the image was on an Instagram account operated by a self-described "Digital Creator" who experiments with various AI image generation engines. The account, @best_of_ai_, had shared the image as part of a creative video presentation, with the caption clearly labelling the content as fictional. The image was titled "The Mourning of the Abyssal Titans" and was accompanied by an elaborate piece of narrative fiction describing the creature's emergence from the deep.

The caption contained this direct statement: *"This is a fictional story."*

Four days after that Instagram post, the image had gone viral across X, stripped of its fictional framing, caption, and creative origin entirely. This stripping-of-context is a signature pattern of AI-image misinformation. The original content creator had embedded sufficient signals of artificiality — the lyrical prose, the explicit fiction disclaimer, the clearly artistic context of the account — but the image was extracted and re-shared as though it were a documentary photograph.

A range of visual cues also point to AI generation when the image is examined carefully. The tentacle structure is anatomically unusual — real octopuses have eight arms, and the image prominently shows only five, a telltale sign of AI hallucination of anatomy. The beach crowd is rendered in the blurred, slightly-uncanny style recognisable in modern diffusion model outputs: faces that don't quite resolve, postures that feel like composites, lighting that is consistent but too uniform. The wet sand texture and the creature's skin have an uncanny sheen that sits slightly outside the range of real beach photography.

More fundamentally, if an octopus of this scale had actually washed ashore in Bali — a densely-visited tourist destination — the event would have been covered immediately and extensively by Indonesian media, international nature organisations, and the global scientific press. Marine biologists would have been dispatched. Photographs from multiple sources would have flooded social media within hours. None of that happened, because the organism depicted does not exist.

The factual baseline worth acknowledging: giant octopuses are real animals. The Giant Pacific Octopus (*Enteroctopus dofleini*) is the world's largest known octopus species and can reach arm spans of up to 4.3 metres, with some unverified historical accounts suggesting even larger individuals. They are not beach-washing animals of the scale depicted in the viral image, however — the creature in the AI image would need to be many times larger than any known specimen. The image exploits genuine public knowledge that large cephalopods exist to anchor a fundamentally false claim in biological plausibility.

## Why People Believe This

The spread of this image followed patterns that are well-documented in the study of viral misinformation, but it also reveals something particular about how AI-generated content operates in the current media ecosystem.

**The plausibility bridge.** Giant cephalopods — octopuses, squids, and their relatives — have been a fixture of maritime mythology for centuries. The Kraken, the tentacled monster that haunted European sailors' imaginations, was not entirely invented: giant squid (*Architeuthis dux*) are real organisms and were cryptids themselves for much of modern history, with documented scientific confirmation of their existence only arriving in the early 2000s. The deep ocean genuinely does contain organisms so alien and large that photographs of them, when authentic, look improbable. This historical record creates what might be called a plausibility bridge: because some stunning marine discoveries turn out to be real, the schema for evaluating a new claim involves some genuine uncertainty. "Could this be real?" is not an irrational question when the ocean is involved.

**The emotional resonance of scale.** Viral images that juxtapose the monumental with the human — an enormous creature, a tiny crowd of observers — trigger a consistent psychological response. There is something deeply arresting about the image regardless of its truth value. The visual composition activates awe, which is an emotion associated with cognitive openness. Research in the psychology of belief suggests that awe states reduce analytical scepticism, making viewers more susceptible to accepting extraordinary claims presented through emotionally resonant imagery. The image was, by design or accident, extraordinarily well-engineered for virality.

**Stripped context.** The Instagram account that originally posted the image was an AI art account. Any reasonably attentive viewer of that account would have understood they were looking at creative generative art, not documentary photography. But the image was lifted from that context and placed into a context of assertions — X posts framed as news, retweets treated as corroboration. This recontextualisation is not always malicious; sometimes it is simply thoughtless. A user sees something striking, reposts with a "Is this real?" caption, and hundreds of downstream users interpret the question as answered affirmatively because the post exists.

**Indonesia as an exotic location.** The framing of the claim in Bali, Indonesia is not arbitrary. For many Western consumers of viral content, Indonesia functions as a distant, exotic, vaguely mysterious place — somewhere rich in wildlife, adventure tourism, and cultural difference. Claims that originate in vivid geo-specific framing benefit from what researchers call "geographic othering": the implausibility threshold is lower for events said to occur in places the audience has limited direct experience with. If the image had claimed the octopus washed up on a beach in New Jersey or Brighton, the scepticism would have been arguably higher.

**AI quality has crossed a threshold.** Perhaps most significantly, this incident arrived at a moment when AI image generation had reached a quality level sufficient to fool casual observers under normal viewing conditions. This was not obviously fake in the way that early AI images were. The rendering was sophisticated enough to require deliberate scrutiny to identify as synthetic. The public was — and in many cases still is — not adequately calibrated for this quality level. Detection intuitions developed on lower-quality fakes do not generalise forward.

---

## Verdict

**The image is AI-generated and the claim is false.**

There is no evidence of any giant octopus washing ashore in Indonesia in June 2024. The image was created by a Digital Artist who explicitly labelled it as fictional. It was extracted from that context and spread as though authentic. The scale of the depicted creature substantially exceeds that of any known octopus species. No credible media organisations, marine biology institutions, or environmental NGOs reported the event. The image fails reverse-image search verification. Multiple visual artefacts consistent with AI generation are present.

The biological premise — that very large octopuses exist — is accurate. The specific claim — that this photograph shows one — is not. This is a case of a fabricated image exploiting genuine biological knowledge to achieve plausibility.

## The Wider Picture

This image belongs to an emerging category of misinformation that fact-checkers and media literacy researchers are still developing frameworks for: **AI-generated wildlife imagery circulated as documentary evidence.**

Wildlife and nature content has historically been among the most trusted categories of visual media. Wildlife photography has a long tradition of peer review, professional attribution, and institutional embeddedness — the BBC Natural History Unit, National Geographic, and similar organisations have built enormous credibility through decades of difficult, rigorous documentation. The existence of that institutional credibility means that nature images, when presented without institutional attribution but in the visual register of nature photography, tend to inherit some of the genre's trust by association.

AI image generation can now produce content within that visual register. The result is a trust asymmetry: audiences apply credibility assumptions appropriate to professionally verified documentary photography to images that were generated in seconds by a free or low-cost AI tool.

This asymmetry has consequences beyond individual viral images. When enough AI-generated nature images circulate — some of which may incidentally resemble real phenomena — they degrade the epistemic reliability of the category. A genuine photograph of an anomalous deep-sea creature washed ashore now faces a higher burden of proof than it would have before this era. This is not entirely bad — erring toward scepticism about extraordinary visual claims is rational — but it creates a situation in which genuine scientific discoveries may be reflexively dismissed as AI fakes by audiences who have learned the wrong lesson from cases like this.

The specific issue of undersea mega-fauna is particularly charged. The oceans remain vastly understudied. We have better maps of the surface of Mars than of the deep ocean floor. Every few years, a species previously known only from historical records or incomplete specimens is photographed alive for the first time. The giant squid, the coelacanth, the megamouth shark, and numerous other organisms were effectively cryptids before they were confirmed. The cultural template for "extraordinary ocean creature revealed to be real" is well-populated. AI-generated content exploiting this template is therefore working with a particularly well-primed audience.

There is also a dimension to this story relating to Indonesia's actual marine environment. Indonesian waters are among the most biodiverse on Earth — the Coral Triangle, of which Indonesia is a central node, contains roughly 75% of all known coral species. The oceans around Bali and across the Indonesian archipelago are genuinely extraordinary habitats. The use of "Indonesia" in viral wildlife fakes is therefore not random — it activates true background knowledge (Indonesian waters are remarkable) in service of a false specific claim (this creature washed up there). This manipulation of genuine environmental knowledge is a feature of sophisticated misinformation.

Finally, this incident participates in a broader pattern of deep-ocean mythology being reprocessed through AI aesthetics. The prompt-engineering community has produced an enormous corpus of deep-sea horror imagery — vast creatures, abyssal landscapes, biological forms that exceed normal categorisation. Some of this work circulates clearly as art. Some circulates as deliberate misinformation. And some — like "The Mourning of the Abyssal Titans" — occupies an ambiguous middle zone, created as art, weaponised as fact without the creator's apparent intent or involvement.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check on this image illustrates a methodologically clean approach to a relatively straightforward AI-image debunk, and is worth examining in some detail as a model of the genre.

**Step 1: Reverse-image search.** The primary tool used was Google's reverse-image search, which allows investigators to find other instances of an image online and trace its earliest appearance. This technique is standard in fact-checking and is particularly effective for AI-generated images that have a single source of origin. The search confirmed no documentary origin — no photographer, no media outlet, no corroborating source — which is itself strong negative evidence for authenticity. Real photographs of significant natural events leave traces: news stories, social media posts from people physically present, EXIF metadata sometimes visible in shared originals, etc.

**Step 2: Source tracing.** Finding the @best_of_ai_ Instagram account as the earliest known source of the image was critical. The Instagram account is unambiguous about its nature — it is an AI art exploration project, not a documentary photography account. The fictional framing of the post, including the explicit statement "This is a fictional story," constituted direct creator intent evidence.

**Step 3: Absence of corroboration.** The fact-checker noted that if the event depicted had genuinely occurred, it would have been covered by environmental news sources. This is an application of the journalistic silence test: extraordinary events in accessible locations generate coverage. The absence of coverage is meaningful, though it is not by itself definitive proof of fabrication — coverage gaps do occur. When combined with the reverse-image search result and the discovered AI origin, the absence of corroboration completes a convincing debunking argument.

**Step 4: Biological contextualisation.** The fact-check implicitly acknowledged the plausibility bridge by presenting the finding straightforwardly without needing to argue that giant octopuses don't exist — just that this image doesn't show one. This is good technique: confirming the underlying biology while demolishing the specific claim avoids giving unnecessary ammunition to the "well you can't know for sure" counter-argument that often follows blanket dismissals.

**What the fact-check did not do** — and what a deeper analysis could include — is a detailed AI provenance analysis: identification of the specific model or generation tool likely used, spectral analysis of AI artefacts, examination of known anatomical hallucination patterns in octopus imagery from particular generative models. These techniques exist, but are outside the standard scope of mainstream fact-checking operations, which appropriately prioritise speed and accessibility of communication.

The broader challenge for fact-checkers in the AI image era is one of throughput. Snopes and similar organisations are staffed teams producing a finite number of fact-checks per day. The volume of AI-generated misinformation circulating online is essentially unlimited and growing. The model of individual fact-check articles responding to specific viral claims is structurally mismatched to the volume problem, and a range of proposals exist — AI-assisted detection, platform-level labelling, improved digital literacy education — to address this. None has yet emerged as a definitive solution.

What fact-checkers like this one can do — and do well — is provide a public record: a documented, citable analysis of a specific image's origin and falsity, which remains accessible long after the viral moment has passed. For the record: this octopus was never on an Indonesian beach. It emerged from a model, floated through social media stripped of its fictional clothes, and eventually grounded itself on the shores of the fact-checking apparatus. It is art that was briefly mistaken for reality — which, in the age of generative AI, is likely to become an increasingly routine species of hazard.
