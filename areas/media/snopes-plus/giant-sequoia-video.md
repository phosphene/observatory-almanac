# Is this video of snowy giant sequoia tree real?


## 1. The Claim

On April 25, 2026, a Facebook page called Ethereal Earth published a short video clip showing what appeared to be a solitary person standing at the base of a colossal giant sequoia tree in a deeply snow-covered landscape. The tree in the video was rendered at a scale that dwarfed the human figure several times over even at the point where the trunk met the snow. The camera panned slowly upward from the base of the tree while the person stood small at its foot, the visual effect communicating an overwhelming sense of biological scale. The clip received tens of millions of views.

The caption read in part: "These giant sequoias in Sequoia National Park are the tallest trees on Earth. A single tree can weigh over 2 million pounds and live for more than 3,000 years. The person at the base gives you a scale no view ever truly captures."

The embedded claims were multiple. First, an implicit visual claim: that this footage was authentically captured in Sequoia National Park and shows a real tree and a real person. Second, several factual claims embedded in the caption about the biological characteristics of giant sequoias: that they are the tallest trees on Earth; that individual trees can weigh over two million pounds; that they can live more than three thousand years.

The visual claim is false. The video is AI-generated. The factual claims are partly true and partly false: the weight and age claims are approximately correct or understated, but the tallness claim is a significant factual error. Giant sequoias (*Sequoiadendron giganteum*) are the most massive trees on Earth by volume but are not the tallest; that distinction belongs to the coast redwood (*Sequoia sempervirens*).

Snopes (Liles, 2026) rated the video as **Fake**.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The video is AI-generated.** The Ethereal Earth Facebook page itself disclosed that it publishes AI content: the page bio read "Some images may be AI depictions with sole purpose of sparking interest in Earth and especially nature." This self-disclosure does not appear to have been prominently noticed by most viewers before the video achieved viral reach, and the framing of the caption did not foreground the AI provenance.

Jordan Liles (Snopes, 2026) conducted SynthID scanning — using Google's SynthID Detector, a tool that identifies an invisible watermark embedded in content generated by Google AI platforms — on multiple pieces of content from the Ethereal Earth page. The SynthID scan of the giant sequoia video did not return a Google AI watermark, meaning the video was not generated using Google's AI tools; its specific AI provenance remains unidentified. However, SynthID confirmed Google AI watermarks in other images on the same page — including images of a cloud formation over Mount Rainier and a raw steak resembling a public figure — establishing that the page does use Google AI tools for at least some of its content.

The clip also showed specific technical characteristics associated with AI-generated video. It lasted exactly fifteen seconds — PCMag (Circelli, 2026) has documented the prevalence of fifteen-second duration as a common AI-generation output constraint. The camera movement during the upward pan was unusually smooth — a movement quality that is either associated with AI generation or with professional steadicam equipment, both scenarios inconsistent with the casual documentation framing implied by the content. The human figure at the base of the tree did not move or interact with the environment in any way visible during the clip. These technical artefacts are consistent with AI generation but individually insufficient for definitive attribution without tool-specific watermarking or other provenance data.

**The giant sequoia facts in the caption.** The caption contained genuinely interesting factual statements about giant sequoias, some accurate, one significantly wrong.

*Tallest trees on Earth — FALSE.* Giant sequoias are not the tallest trees on Earth. The U.S. National Park Service explicitly addresses this distinction: the tallest tree in the world is Hyperion, a coast redwood (*Sequoia sempervirens*) located in Redwood National Park in northern California, measuring approximately 115.9 metres (380.3 feet) in height (Van Pelt et al., various; National Park Service, Redwood National and State Parks). Giant sequoias in Sequoia National Park are significantly shorter — the tallest giant sequoias reach approximately 90–95 metres (295–311 feet), well below the tallest coast redwoods. The confusion between "giant sequoia" and "coast redwood" is common and persistent; both species are sometimes colloquially called "redwoods," and the two do occupy different but sometimes overlapping ecological and cultural niches. Giant sequoias have the largest volume; coast redwoods have the greatest height. These are different superlatives.

*Weight claims — APPROXIMATELY TRUE.* The General Sherman Tree in Sequoia National Park, the largest tree in the world by volume, is estimated by the National Park Service to weigh approximately 2.7 million pounds (approximately 1.2 million kilograms). The caption's claim that "a single tree can weigh over 2 million pounds" is thus accurate as a statement about the General Sherman tree specifically and as a statement about the maximum size of the species. Representative individual giant sequoias weigh considerably less; the two-million-pound figure applies to the very largest individuals.

*Age claims — TRUE AND UNDERSTATED.* Giant sequoias are known to reach ages of up to approximately 3,400 years based on ring counts from felled specimens. The caption's claim of "more than 3,000 years" is correct; the species' maximum documented longevity somewhat exceeds 3,000 years. The oldest verified age is approximately 3,266 years for a logged individual (National Park Service, Sequoia and Kings Canyon; Stephenson, 1994, "Long-Term Dynamics of Giant Sequoia Populations: Implications for Managing a Relict Species," *USDA Forest Service General Technical Report PSW-151*).


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

Snopes' assessment correctly identifies the video as fake and accurately addresses the factual error regarding tallness. What the fact-check does not develop is either the biology of giant sequoias at the depth warranted by the claims, or the evolving epistemology of AI video detection and why the techniques applied to this video represent an important but still-developing set of tools.

**AI video detection: the current state.** The SynthID approach that Liles used is significant and worth explaining fully. SynthID, developed by Google DeepMind and first announced in August 2023 (DeepMind, 2023, blog post), embeds an imperceptible watermark into AI-generated images and video at the point of generation by Google AI tools. The watermark is designed to survive common post-processing operations — compression, cropping, minor colour adjustments — while remaining undetectable to human vision. A detector tool then scans content for the watermark signature.

The limitation is fundamental and significant: SynthID only detects watermarks in content generated by Google's own AI platforms (currently including Imagen, VideoPoet, and Gemini-based generation tools). Content generated by competitor platforms — OpenAI's Sora or DALL-E, Stability AI's products, Meta's AI generation tools, Runway, Pika, or any of the dozens of other AI video generation platforms now commercially available — will not carry a Google SynthID watermark and will return a negative scan result. A negative SynthID result therefore does not suggest the content is authentic; it means only that it was not generated by the specific Google tools covered by SynthID.

This is why Liles' (2026) accurate description of the result — "A SynthID scan of the fake giant sequoia clip confirmed users did not create the video with Google's AI tools. It's unclear which AI platform users prompted to generate the clip" — is epistemically precise. The authenticity determination in this case rests not primarily on SynthID results but on the self-disclosure by the content creator and the presence of visible AI artefacts. The SynthID confirmation on other page content establishes that the page uses Google AI tools for some content, but cannot be used to attribute the sequoia video specifically.

The broader AI video detection landscape in 2026 involves multiple competing approaches. Model-based classifiers — systems trained specifically to distinguish AI-generated video from authentic footage — are commercially available from providers including Content Authenticity Initiative (CAI) members and third-party forensics companies. However, these classifiers suffer from a fundamental limitation: they must be trained on examples of AI-generated content, and they cannot reliably detect artefacts from generation models whose outputs they have not been trained to recognise (Corvi et al., 2023, "On the Detection of Synthetic Images Generated by Diffusion Models," *IEEE International Conference on Acoustics, Speech and Signal Processing*). As new generation models are released, existing classifiers must be retrained or they become progressively less effective against the most current generation tools.

C2PA (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) content credentials — metadata standards that aim to establish a chain of custody from capture to publication — offer an alternative approach that does not depend on detecting artefacts. Under C2PA standards, content generated by participating platforms carries embedded metadata identifying its origin; content lacking such credentials is flagged as either unverified or as potentially AI-generated. However, adoption is uneven across platforms, content can be re-uploaded in ways that strip metadata, and the absence of C2PA credentials may simply indicate non-participating origin rather than AI generation.

The giant sequoia video case is therefore a useful case study in the limits of AI detection: the video is fake, and multiple pieces of evidence converge on this conclusion, but no single detection method provides definitive provenance attribution. The reliable evidence is circumstantial — self-disclosure, known page behaviour, technical artefacts, implausible content scale — rather than cryptographic proof.

**Giant sequoia biology: what the scale actually means.** The irony of the viral fake video is that authentic giant sequoias are genuinely extraordinary enough that fabrication is unnecessary — the real trees are among the most visually imposing organisms on Earth. Understanding why requires some biological context.

Giant sequoias (*Sequoiadendron giganteum*) are endemic to a narrow band of the western slopes of the Sierra Nevada mountains in California, occurring at elevations of roughly 1,400–2,150 metres in approximately 73 discrete groves (Stephenson, 1994, cited above). Their restricted range reflects a combination of specific precipitation requirements, soil conditions, and fire regime dependence. The species is obligately dependent on periodic fire for reproduction: sequoia cones can remain on the tree for up to twenty years, persisting in a dry state until the heat of a passing wildfire dehydrates the cone scales and causes them to open, releasing seeds onto the freshly cleared and mineralised soil surface exposed by the fire (Harvey et al., 1980, "Fire in the Giant Sequoia-Mixed Conifer Forest: Effects on Fuels, Trees and Related Vegetation," *USDA Forest Service General Technical Report PSW-52*). Crown fires — which other conifers cannot survive — may kill other trees in a stand but leave mature giant sequoias that have developed a thick, fire-resistant bark layer intact while simultaneously triggering mass seed release from accumulated cone crops.

The volume metric used to define the General Sherman Tree as the world's largest tree — 52,508 cubic feet (1,487 cubic metres) — is not a measure of height, weight, circumference, or trunk diameter alone, but of total wood volume integrated across the entire trunk and major limbs. This is a somewhat abstract measure for an organism whose visual impact is primarily communicated through trunk diameter at ground level (approximately 11 metres / 36 feet for General Sherman) and height. At ground level, a mature giant sequoia presents a trunk diameter that exceeds the height of a person by roughly five to six times, and the trunk base in an old-growth individual has significant taper over the first several metres, giving it a characteristic visual silhouette — massive, buttressed, deeply furrowed — that is distinct from the tall, columnar silhouette of the tallest coast redwoods.

The General Sherman Tree's height (84 metres / 274.9 feet) is genuine and imposing, but it is significantly shorter than the tallest individual giant sequoias (approximately 94 metres), which are themselves shorter than the tallest coast redwoods (approximately 115.9 metres). The common conflation of these species in popular media is therefore consequential: the real superlatives are extraordinary and different from each other.


## 4. Why People Believe This

The engagement dynamics of the Ethereal Earth video — tens of millions of views — reflect several converging factors that operate largely independently of content authenticity. Giant sequoias are a subject of genuine and widespread wonder; the emotional payoff of "scale revelation" content (a human figure placed near an extraordinarily large object to communicate its size) is well-established in nature and science communication. The snowy winter setting amplified the visual drama. The caption provided educational framing that positioned the video as informative rather than purely entertainment.

The Ethereal Earth page biography note — "Some images may be AI depictions" — was present but not foregrounded. Most viewers encountering the video through shares and recommended content would engage with the video and caption without ever visiting the originating page's about section. The distinction between "AI depiction" and "authentic footage" was therefore not salient for most viewers.

This represents a structural feature of how AI nature content spreads: the disclosure is technically present but effectively invisible to the majority of viewers, the content is formatted to evoke the codes of authentic documentary footage, and the emotional resonance of the subject matter (extraordinary natural objects) creates engagement that does not require authenticity verification. Engagement metrics reward the content identically regardless of its AI status.

The factual error in the caption — calling giant sequoias the tallest trees — illustrates a related but distinct mechanism: AI generation of photorealistic video does not require or entail factual accuracy in accompanying text, and the plausibility of adjacent accurate facts (weight, age) may confer credibility on adjacent inaccurate facts (height superlative). The caption was correct on two of three factual points, and the specific error (conflating tallest with most massive) is one that many humans also make — reducing the probability that a reader would flag it as evidence of AI-generated or otherwise unreliable content.

Snopes has noted this is part of a consistent pattern: multiple prior viral posts have falsely identified Hyperion, the world's tallest tree, with photographs of other trees. The association of superlative claims and large-tree imagery has a long and consistent viral history, apparently independent of whether the content is AI-generated or authentic.


## 5. The Broader Viral AI Nature Content Problem

The giant sequoia video is a representative example of a broader content category that has expanded dramatically since the public availability of high-quality AI video generation tools. Nature and wildlife content — a category that is visually compelling, generally non-political, and emotionally accessible to broad audiences — has become one of the primary genres for AI-generated viral content because it is difficult for general audiences to detect and tends to generate strong positive engagement signals.

The Ethereal Earth page is notable because it explicitly disclosed its AI content policy rather than suppressing this information. Many similar pages do not provide equivalent disclosure. The disclosure itself was formatted as a minor biographical note rather than as prominent content labelling, and the gap between this disclosure's prominence and the content's reach — tens of millions of views — reflects the structural disconnect between technically present transparency and effectively communicated transparency.

Platform-level responses to AI nature content in 2026 remain inconsistent. Meta's platforms (Facebook and Instagram) have implemented AI content labelling policies requiring disclosure on AI-generated posts, but enforcement is uneven and self-disclosure mechanisms depend on creators voluntarily identifying AI-generated content. Third-party detection remains technically limited for the reasons described in section 3 above.

The appeal of giant sequoia-related content is also worth noting as a feature of the specific subject matter. Real giant sequoias are objects of significant cultural and emotional investment in United States nature culture; they are protected species in a protected park; they are used as symbols of ecological longevity and natural heritage. The video exploits this investment — the viewer's genuine appreciation for real giant sequoias is activated by artificial footage — in a way that is different from, and arguably more ethically complex than, viral content about less culturally loaded subjects.


## 6. Bottom Line

The April 2026 Ethereal Earth video of a snow-covered giant sequoia is AI-generated, not authentic footage. This conclusion is supported by the page's own disclosure of AI content, the absence of SynthID evidence linking the video to Google AI generation tools (though other page content does carry Google AI watermarks), fifteen-second clip duration, and unnatural smoothness of camera movement. The Snopes rating of **Fake** is correct.

The caption accompanying the video contained one significant factual error: giant sequoias are not the tallest trees on Earth. The world's tallest trees are coast redwoods (*Sequoia sempervirens*); the tallest individual, Hyperion, reached approximately 115.9 metres. Giant sequoias are the most massive trees on Earth by volume, a distinct superlative that the caption did not use. The caption's claims about maximum weight (two million pounds — accurate relative to the General Sherman Tree's 2.7 million pounds) and maximum age (more than 3,000 years — accurate and slightly understated relative to the approximately 3,400-year documented maximum) were essentially correct.

The epistemological value of this case lies primarily in what it reveals about AI video detection: detection methods in 2026 are platform-specific, watermark-dependent, and unable to provide definitive provenance attribution for content from the majority of generation platforms. Reliable detection depends on a combination of self-disclosure, technical artefact analysis, and content plausibility rather than any single reliable forensic method.
