# Was a Giant Squid Found in a Harbor in Japan?


## 1. The Claim

In late January 2021, social media users in Japan and internationally encountered video footage of an enormous cephalopod moored to a dock in a small fishing port. The claim circulating alongside that footage was explicit and anatomically significant: a living giant squid — *Architeuthis dux*, the largest known invertebrate on Earth — had been found in a Japanese harbor.

The claim arrived with two embedded layers of assertion. The first was factual and verifiable: that the event occurred, that the animal was genuine, and that a credentialed institution had recorded and confirmed the sighting. The second layer was epistemically unstated but culturally weighted: the surprise implicit in the footage. A giant squid does not belong in a harbour — it belongs in the lightless deep, at pressures and depths no recreational diver would reach. The animal in the video was alive, though apparently weakened. It had been moored with rope by a fisherman. That strangeness was itself the content of the claim.

The claim also implicitly referenced a precedent. Japanese coastal waters had previously produced a celebrated live squid sighting in December 2015, when a specimen estimated at approximately 3.7 metres entered the shallow waters of Toyama Bay on Christmas Eve and was guided back out to sea by diver Akinobu Kimura (The Guardian, 2015). The 2021 event reprised that earlier episode, intensifying questions about whether these appearances were coincidental anomalies or part of a pattern with scientific structure underlying it.

For readers unfamiliar with cephalopod biology, the claim triggered a more generalised curiosity: how large do giant squid actually get? How deep do they live? Why would an animal of this kind ever surface? These questions rarely appeared in the viral framing of the footage but were scientifically essential for evaluating what the sighting meant.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rated the claim **True**. Journalist Bethania Palma investigated the footage and confirmed its provenance: the Aquas Aquarium (Shimane Aquarium Aquas) in Shimane Prefecture, coastal southern Japan, had posted the original video on Twitter in late January 2021. The aquarium confirmed that the animal had been found at the Inome fishing port in Izumo City. A fisherman had located it around noon on January 26, moored it to the quay with rope, and notified the aquarium.

Palma consulted Dr. Michael Vecchione, Curator of Cephalopods at the Smithsonian's National Museum of Natural History and Senior Zoologist at the NOAA/National Marine Fisheries Service National Systematics Laboratory — a leading expert in deep-sea cephalopod taxonomy and biology. Vecchione confirmed that the animal appeared, in a second video posted by the aquarium, to be breathing — but that it was probably near death. His assessment was based on direct examination of the footage, not secondary reporting.

Subsequent independent reporting established the physical dimensions of the specimen: mantle (body) length of 167 centimetres, weight of approximately 170 kilograms, and total arm reach of roughly 410 centimetres, although the animal was missing its longest feeding tentacles. The aquarium ultimately determined that the squid's chances of survival if released were too low, and it was preserved as a frozen specimen — the first confirmed living giant squid the aquarium had recorded.

Snopes further noted that the Sea of Japan has previously hosted giant squid appearances — in particular the 2015 Toyama Bay event, which received widespread international coverage — and quoted Vecchione to the effect that the Sea of Japan can act as a "natural trap" for the species, particularly in winter. This detail was cited with reference to a scientific paper in the journal *Marine Biodiversity* (Springer Link, 2016), though Snopes did not name the study's authors or describe its findings in technical detail.

On the central factual question — was a living giant squid found in a Japanese harbor in January 2021? — the verdict is unambiguous: yes. The claim is accurate.


## 3. Why This Case Rewards Closer Inspection

The epistemically interesting dimension of this fact-check is not the verdict, which is correct and clearly reached, but the unexplored infrastructure underneath it. Snopes closes the loop at factual confirmation. What remains unaddressed is why giant squid appear in Japanese harbors at all — a question with a rigorous, peer-reviewed scientific answer that substantially changes how the event should be understood.

The dominant lay reading of a giant squid in a harbor is that something extraordinary has happened: the deep sea has briefly made itself visible, an ambassador of a dark world having wandered accidentally into ours. This framing, while romantically satisfying, treats the event as anomalous. The scientific record suggests something different: these appearances are semi-predictable, seasonally structured, and driven by a specific physiological constraint that the Sea of Japan geography happens to amplify.

That constraint is the thermal sensitivity of hemocyanin — the copper-based, blue-blooded oxygen transport protein that *Architeuthis* uses in place of haemoglobin. Hemocyanin in giant squid exhibits a marked decline in oxygen-binding affinity at elevated temperatures. Research on the hemocyanin physiology of related deep-water cephalopods indicates that arterial oxygen saturation decreases substantially when water temperatures exceed the cold baseline of the squid's natural habitat (Pörtner, 2002, *Journal of Experimental Biology*). For a squid adapted to the 4–8 °C range of sub-thermocline depths between 400 and 1,000 metres, even modest warming can compromise respiratory function.

The Sea of Japan creates a precise environmentally-realised version of this physiological trap. The Tsushima Warm Current enters the southern Sea of Japan through the Korea Strait, driving surface temperatures that can exceed 15 °C in winter months — well above the hemocyanin threshold. Giant squid that migrate northward through the Tsushima Strait enter an enclosed body of water where escape routes back to cold deep ocean are limited. The basin geometry does not allow easy egress to the Pacific deepwater. Animals that become physiologically stressed by the warmer surface layers cannot simply retreat to depth through the lateral margins; they are, in physical terms, trapped.

A systematic review of *Architeuthis* global distribution, with particular focus on the Sea of Japan as a biological hotspot, documented 57 confirmed giant squid in Japanese coastal waters of the Sea of Japan between January 2014 and March 2015 — an extraordinary concentration compared to the historical rate of strandings globally (Guerreiro-Novo et al., 2023, *Marine Biodiversity*). This is not a sample of one: the Sea of Japan routinely produces more confirmed giant squid records than almost any other region on Earth, and the temporal clustering in winter months aligns precisely with the thermal trap mechanism.

The 2021 Izumo specimen fits this pattern perfectly. Found in late January, at the coldest surface-water period when Tsushima Warm Current intrusions are at their most physiologically stressful for deep-water migrants, the animal was almost certainly a victim of thermic oxygen depletion — not random misadventure. Its condition (weakened, near death, missing its longest tentacles, likely having already been struggling for some time before the fisherman found it) is consistent with the progressive physiological failure that hemocyanin degradation would produce.

This matters epistemically because it transforms the narrative frame. The 2021 sighting was not a once-in-a-lifetime encounter with a creature from a lost world. It was an instance of a documented, physiologically explicable event category that the Sea of Japan produces with some regularity. The appropriate public science story is not merely "it's real" but "here's why this particular body of water serves as a seasonal collection point for one of the ocean's most elusive organisms." That story is scientifically richer, more epistemically instructive, and more useful for building genuine understanding of deep-sea biology.

The fact-check's elision of this context is understandable — Snopes operates within a brevity constraint and serves a broad audience — but it does mean that the wider significance of the event goes unstated where it could be most usefully conveyed.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** True  
**Confidence:** Very High  
**Epistemic Basis:** Direct confirmation from primary source (Aquas Aquarium) + expert interview (Vecchione, Smithsonian)  
**EARC Assessment:** Accurate — Snopes reaches the correct verdict by appropriate methodology  
**Narrative Gap:** Real — physiological and oceanographic context go unaddressed  
**Phenomenological Status:** Well-documented, repeated  
**Mechanistic Status:** Established (thermal hemocyanin trap)  
**Folk Wonder Value:** Justified

The claim is true. The giant squid appeared in Izumo City's Inome fishing port on January 26, 2021, was confirmed by the Aquas Aquarium, measured approximately 167 centimetres in mantle length and 170 kilograms, and was almost certainly near death when found. Snopes's sourcing is sound: the Aquas Aquarium's own video documentation, corroborated by expert commentary from one of the world's foremost cephalopod specialists, establishes the facts unambiguously.

The epistemological limitation is one of depth rather than accuracy. Snopes confirms that the event happened; it does not explain the systematic processes that made it happen, nor does it situate 2021 within the longer scientific history of *Architeuthis* observations in Japanese waters. A reader who encounters only the Snopes fact-check knows the squid was real but may walk away with a reinforced sense that giant squid appear in harbors for mysterious and unknowable reasons — which is precisely the opposite of what the scientific literature supports.

For readership whose interest is calibration — understanding not just whether a claim is true but what kind of true it is and why — the Snopes treatment leaves meaningful explanatory territory unexplored.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The January 2021 Izumo sighting belongs to a history of *Architeuthis* encounters with humans that spans centuries of folklore, false rumor, maritime legend, and ultimately some of the most dramatic scientific discoveries of the modern era.

**The Biology of *Architeuthis dux***: Giant squid are cephalopod mollusks of the family Architeuthidae, and genetic analysis has confirmed that despite wide geographic spread across the North and South Atlantic, North Pacific, and waters off Japan, there is likely only a single global species — *Architeuthis dux* (Winkelmann et al., 2013, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*). Females routinely exceed 10 metres in total length (including feeding tentacles) and can reach 13 to 15 metres in exceptional cases; males tend to be smaller. The Smithsonian Institution's Ocean Portal records the largest scientifically measured specimen at approximately 43 feet (13 metres), with weight estimates reaching 220 kilograms or more.

Their eyes are among the most remarkable structures in the animal kingdom: at diameters of 25–30 centimetres, they are the largest eyes of any living animal, an evolutionary response to detecting bioluminescence in the dim mesopelagic zone. Research by Nilsson et al. (2012, *Current Biology*) proposed that these extraordinarily large eyes are specifically adapted to detect the movement of large predators — particularly sperm whales — that stimulate bioluminescence in the surrounding water column as they navigate at depth. This counter-intuitive finding reframes the giant eye not as a passive light-collector but as a large-predator early-warning system.

Despite their size, *Architeuthis* appear to live briefly. Beak microstructure analysis — examining growth increment rings analogous to tree rings — suggests lifespans of approximately two to five years for most specimens (Gauldie et al., see Vecchione's expert commentary, 2021; beak aging methodology described by Perales-Raya et al., 2014, *Journal of the Marine Biological Association of the United Kingdom*). Vecchione confirmed to Snopes that typical giant squid live one to two years, with maximums of three to four years. For the largest invertebrate on Earth, this is a strikingly short existence, suggesting extremely rapid growth rates fueled by high metabolic throughput in cold, resource-rich deep-water environments.

**The Science of Observation**: For most of human history, *Architeuthis* was known primarily through fragments: beaks found in sperm whale stomachs, arms and tentacles occasionally washing ashore, decomposed mantle sections netted by trawlers. The animal was real enough — it inspired the *kraken* mythology of Scandinavian sailors and appeared in the scientific literature as early as the 1870s from specimens collected by Danish naturalist Japetus Steenstrup — but it had never been photographed alive in its natural habitat.

This changed on September 30, 2004, when researchers Tsunemi Kubodera (National Science Museum, Tokyo) and Kyoichi Mori (Ogasawara Whale Watching Association) deployed a remote-triggered baited camera system at approximately 900 metres' depth off the Ogasawara Islands, 600 miles south of Tokyo. The camera captured over 500 images of an approximately eight-metre giant squid attacking the bait — a predatory, active animal, not the passive drift-feeder some had envisioned. The squid severed a 5.5-metre feeding tentacle in its struggles; that tentacle was recovered and DNA-confirmed as *Architeuthis*. Kubodera and Mori published the first-ever live photographic documentation of a giant squid in the wild in *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* (Kubodera & Mori, 2005, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences*). It was one of the most significant marine biology discoveries of the 2000s.

**The Sea of Japan Hotspot and the 2014–2015 Mass Occurrence**: The 2021 Izumo sighting was not the first indication that Japanese coastal waters disproportionately produce giant squid encounters. Between January 2014 and March 2015, an extraordinary 57 confirmed *Architeuthis dux* specimens were recorded in the Japanese coastal waters of the Sea of Japan (Guerreiro-Novo et al., 2023, *Marine Biodiversity*). This mass occurrence, unprecedented in the scientific record, concentrated primarily around Sado Island and in Toyama Bay — the same bay where the celebrated Christmas 2015 live encounter would subsequently occur.

The scientific explanation for this concentration invokes the Sea of Japan's unusual oceanographic structure. Unlike the open Pacific, which offers unconstrained depth access in all directions, the Sea of Japan is a semi-enclosed marginal sea bounded by the Japanese archipelago to the east and the Korean and Siberian coasts to the west. The only significant southern ingress is the Tsushima Strait, through which the Tsushima Warm Current drives warm surface waters northward in a pattern that peaks in winter — precisely the season when giant squid strandings cluster. Animals moving northward through the strait enter a water body from which Pacific-depth escape is topographically difficult, and whose surface layers, warmed by the Tsushima Current, impair their hemocyanin-based respiration.

**From Myth to Carcass to Living Animal**: The 2021 event is also the culmination of a three-stage epistemic progression in humanity's knowledge of giant squid. The first stage was mythological: legendary sea monsters, sailors' tales, the *kraken* growing in the imaginations of seafarers who glimpsed only fragments of enormous tentacled animals. The second was material: beaks in whale stomachs, desiccated specimens, nets recovering parts, the gradual scientific establishment that *Architeuthis* was biologically real but functionally inaccessible. The third — which only began seriously in 2004 with Kubodera and Mori's photographs — is the stage of living observation, where we can see the animal in motion, and begin to understand its behaviour.

Each harbour appearance, including the 2015 Toyama Bay juvenile and the 2021 Izumo specimen, is data in that third stage. The animals are dying, not thriving, in these shallow-water encounters; but their appearance generates photographic records, physical measurements, genetic samples, and direct physiological observations that supplement the necessarily sparse deep-sea record. The 2021 specimen was frozen and preserved — adding to a museum collection that future researchers will use to constrain population genetics, growth rate models, and geographic range.

**Public Understanding and the Visibility of the Invisible**: One of the structurally interesting features of giant squid as a scientific subject is that public interest vastly outstrips scientific access. Sperm whales and giant squid are known to engage in sustained predatory battles — sperm whale beaks found in *Architeuthis* stomach contents, and squid sucker scars preserved in whale skin, testify to these deep encounters that no human has ever directly observed. We know it happens; we have never seen it. Giant squid eat, grow to the size of a school bus, and die at depths where light does not penetrate — and we know almost none of this from direct observation.

This asymmetry — extreme public fascination meeting extreme scientific inaccessibility — is exactly the epistemic terrain that fact-checkers enter when they verify harbour sightings. The correct verdict is easy. The harder interpretive task is situating that verdict within the actual scientific understanding of the species: why these appearances happen, what they reveal and don't reveal about the animal's normal existence, and how the observable sightings in shallow water relate to an entire ecological life that remains almost entirely hidden from view.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim **True** and does so correctly. The methodology is straightforward: identify the source video (Aquas Aquarium's Instagram/Twitter), contact a named expert (Vecchione, Smithsonian), confirm the event's provenance, note relevant contextual history (the 2015 Toyama Bay sighting), and report accurately. Palma's piece is clean, well-sourced, and does not overstate. The verdict is right.

The fact-check's limitation is not error but frame-narrowness, a structural feature of the Snopes format rather than a failure of the particular piece. Snopes is built around binary proposition evaluation: is the claim true, false, or somewhere in between? This works superbly for claims that are straightforwardly false (the 10% brain myth), partially false (many distorted-but-grounded claims), or hotly contested (the chemical imbalance model in psychiatry). It works less well for claims that are simply true but whose affirmation is least interesting part of the story.

The July 2021 Izumo sighting is exactly this kind of case. Yes, a giant squid was found in a Japanese harbor. That is correct. But the scientifically richer account begins where Snopes stops: the thermal hemocyanin impairment hypothesis (Pörtner, 2002, *Journal of Experimental Biology*), the Sea of Japan hotspot documentation (Guerreiro-Novo et al., 2023, *Marine Biodiversity*), the history of the species' scientific emergence from myth to specimen to live observation (Kubodera & Mori, 2005, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*), and the evolutionary implications of an organism the size of a pickup truck that lives for two years in total darkness and has almost never been seen alive in its natural environment.

Responsible fact-checking confirms what happened. Responsible science communication explains the world in which it happened. For *Architeuthis dux* and the Sea of Japan, those are substantially different stories, and both are worth telling — ideally together.
