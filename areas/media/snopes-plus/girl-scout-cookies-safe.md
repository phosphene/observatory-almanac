# Girl Scout cookies are still safe to eat despite reports of heavy metals and pesticides


## 1. The Claim

In early 2025, as the annual Girl Scout cookie fundraising season got underway, the self-described consumer advocacy groups Moms Across America (MAA) and GMOScience jointly published a set of laboratory reports alleging that samples of Girl Scout cookies tested positive for the herbicide glyphosate and five heavy metals: arsenic, cadmium, lead, mercury, and aluminum. The reports, published on the GMOScience website with associated certificates of analysis from accredited laboratories, attracted widespread social media engagement on Instagram, X (formerly Twitter), and TikTok, and reached the Snopes editorial inbox through reader inquiries.

The specific claims made in MAA and GMOScience's published materials included: 100% of the 25 tested cookie samples were positive for glyphosate; 100% were positive for toxic metals; 88% were positive for all five named heavy metals; 76% showed cadmium levels exceeding EPA limits; and 96% were positive for lead. The materials also included calculations suggesting that cadmium levels exceeded EPA standards, a claim that was widely reproduced in social media posts warning parents that Girl Scout cookies were toxic to children.

These alarming statistics spread through networks associated with organic food advocacy, vaccine-skeptical communities, and health-focused influencer ecosystems. The claim effectively weaponized the trusted cultural status of Girl Scout cookies—a beloved institution generating approximately $1 billion in annual sales and serving as the organizational backbone of a major youth development program—against the organization's credibility, framing the cookies as a health hazard actively being suppressed from public view.


## 2. What's Actually True

The laboratory testing was real and was performed by accredited laboratories: Health Research Institute (HRI), a nonprofit research lab, conducted glyphosate testing, while separate accredited facilities conducted heavy metals analysis. The test results are documented on publicly available certificates of analysis, and the chemicals detected are genuinely present in the tested samples. All of these factual elements are accurate.

The critical context that the MAA and GMOScience materials systematically omitted or misrepresented is dose and reference standard. Every measured concentration of every detected substance fell within or below the safety tolerances established by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) for food consumed by humans.

**Glyphosate**: Glyphosate is a broad-spectrum herbicide registered for use in the United States since 1974 (EPA registration). The FDA has established tolerances for glyphosate residues in food crops ranging from 0.1 to 400 parts per million (ppm) depending on the crop and use pattern (FDA, 2024, "Questions and Answers: Glyphosate"). The HRI laboratory report shows the highest detected glyphosate level in any tested cookie sample at 111.07 nanograms per gram (ng/g). Converting this to ppm: 111.07 ng/g = 0.1111 ppm. This places the highest detected value at the low end of the FDA's tolerance range for grains. A 66-pound child would need to consume approximately 9,000 of these cookies in a single day to approach the EPA's chronic reference dose for glyphosate, according to Dr. Jessica B. Steier, CEO of Vital Statistics Counseling and host of "The Unbiased Science" podcast.

**Heavy Metals**: The heavy metals detected—arsenic, cadmium, lead, mercury, and aluminum—are naturally occurring elements present in soil at trace concentrations globally. They enter crops through root uptake from soil and are therefore present in essentially all grain-based foods at some measurable level. The MAA/GMOScience materials compared detected heavy metal concentrations against EPA safety limits for drinking water. This comparison is methodologically incoherent: drinking water limits are designed for a substance consumed at approximately two liters per day, every day, for a lifetime, by the entire exposed population including sensitive groups (infants, immunocompromised individuals). Food safety thresholds for the same substances are established under entirely different exposure modeling assumptions—accounting for the intermittent, varied, and quantity-limited nature of food consumption rather than continuous uniform daily intake. Applying drinking water standards to food concentration data systematically overstates risk by factors that can range from tens to thousands, depending on realistic consumption patterns.

When the detected heavy metal concentrations are compared against applicable food safety standards—the FDA's action levels, the Joint FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives (JECFA) provisional tolerable weekly intakes, and the FDA's total diet survey benchmarks—the measured values fall within normal ranges for commercial baked goods made with conventional wheat, grain, and sugar ingredients. The Girl Scouts of the USA (GSUSA) responded to Snopes that their products comply with all FDA and relevant authority requirements, and that their licensed bakers "are leaders in their industry and adhere to rigorous food safety standards." GSUSA stated that all cookies are produced by trusted licensed bakers operating under FDA standards.

John Fagan, chief scientist and CEO of HRI, confirmed to Snopes that his laboratory's glyphosate testing was accurate as reported, but notably did not endorse the interpretation that the detected levels represent a health hazard. Fagan's comments to Snopes were narrowly scoped to validating the numerical accuracy of the glyphosate measurements, which is consistent with a laboratory scientist's appropriate professional scope—reporting what was measured rather than interpreting safety implications.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The Girl Scout cookies contamination claim achieved rapid, wide distribution because it successfully exploited a convergent set of cognitive vulnerabilities and social motivations that deserve careful analysis.

**The Dose Is the Toxicologist's Most Inconvenient Truth**: The foundational principle of toxicology—articulated most famously by Paracelsus in the sixteenth century as "sola dosis facit venenum" (the dose alone makes the poison)—remains the single most systematically ignored concept in public risk discourse. Population surveys consistently show that large majorities of lay audiences believe the relevant question for assessing chemical hazards is presence or absence, not concentration. When told that a substance associated with harm (lead, arsenic, glyphosate) is "found in" or "detected in" a food product, the typical lay audience processes this as evidence of exposure to the harmful substance itself, without applying any quantitative dose threshold. This binary presence/absence framing is exploited systematically by advocacy organizations that design communications to trigger contamination concern through detection announcements unsupported by dose context.

Research on risk perception demonstrates that lay audiences systematically overweight the "naturalness" of a risk—with synthetic chemicals like glyphosate rated as more dangerous than naturally occurring substances at equivalent doses—and that the availability of vivid harm narratives strongly amplifies contamination concern independent of quantitative evidence (Slovic, 1987, *Science*; Finucane et al., 2000, *Journal of Behavioral Decision Making*). The heavy metals in Girl Scout cookies are primarily naturally occurring elements present in agricultural soils globally, but the label "toxic heavy metals" activates availability of high-dose industrial poisoning narratives that are entirely disconnected from trace dietary exposure.

**The Reference Standard Manipulation**: The specific methodological choice to compare food contamination levels against drinking water standards rather than food safety standards is not an inadvertent error. The EPA's maximum contaminant levels (MCLs) for heavy metals in drinking water are set at extremely conservative levels because they target lifetime chronic daily exposure in the entire population, including the most sensitive individuals. Cadmium's EPA MCL in drinking water is 5 micrograms per liter (5 ppb). A regular household chocolate chip cookie contains cadmium naturally; virtually every grain-based food would exceed the cadmium drinking water MCL if the standard were applied to all foods. The EPA explicitly does not apply these standards to food, and doing so is methodologically unjustifiable to any food scientist or toxicologist.

**Epistemic Novelty—The Institutional Trust Inversion and Advocacy Science Genre**: This case exhibits a distinctive and increasingly common epistemic pattern in contemporary health misinformation: what might be termed *advocacy science mimicry*. MAA and GMOScience produced materials that superficially resemble legitimate scientific communications: they engaged accredited laboratories, published certificates of analysis with specific numerical results, included statistical summaries, and released the materials alongside a formal report document. The mimicry of scientific genre conventions—quantitative data, laboratory credentials, specific detection limits, percentage-positive statistics—lends the claims an authority that pure advocacy rhetoric would not carry.

The epistemic novelty in this case lies in how the mimicry exploits a specific gap in public scientific literacy: most consumers cannot distinguish between a legitimately designed and interpreted study and one that uses legitimate measurement while applying misleading interpretive frameworks. The testing methodology was sound; the interpretation was not. This is a more sophisticated manipulation than simply fabricating data, because it creates a situation where fact-checks that note "the testing was real" appear to validate the underlying concern while the interpretive correction ("but the standard used was wrong") requires a level of regulatory and toxicological expertise that most audiences do not hold.

The organizational pattern also merits examination. MAA has been involved in previous advocacy science campaigns that Snopes has investigated, including a 2024 claim about Chick-fil-A. GMOScience's CEO is listed as an adviser to MAA according to MAA's own website, creating an organizational relationship between the two groups that calling them independent is difficult to justify. The combination of organizational entanglement with the advocacy-science communication style constitutes a type of credibility laundering: the appearance of independent laboratory verification is constructed by organizations with shared advocacy agendas. This pattern is not unique to these organizations; it has become a recognizable structural feature of food safety advocacy communications.

**Cultural Capitalizing on Girl Scout Brand Trust**: The specific targeting of Girl Scout cookies is analytically interesting as a rhetorical choice. The Girl Scout cookie brand carries unusually high cultural capital—it is associated with childhood, community fundraising, volunteerism, and institutional trust. Alarming claims about Girl Scout cookies are more shareable and emotionally activating than equivalent claims about a generic biscuit brand precisely because they involve the betrayal of trusted cultural innocence. The alarm is amplified by the organization's association with children: any framing around harm to children—particularly young girls participating in an ostensibly educational program—triggers powerful protective motivations that override analytical engagement.


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: TRUE**

Girl Scout cookies are safe to eat. The laboratory testing conducted by MAA and GMOScience is real and the numerical results reported are accurate as measurements. However, every detected substance—glyphosate and all five named heavy metals—was present at concentrations well within the safety thresholds established by the FDA, EPA, JECFA, and CDC for human food consumption. The claim that detected levels exceed safety standards is false and was produced by applying inapplicable drinking water standards to food concentration data. The cookies comply with applicable food safety regulations. The GSUSA, the licensed bakers producing the cookies, and independent scientists including Dr. Jessica Steier, a public health researcher at Vital Statistics Counseling and host of The Unbiased Science Podcast, have all confirmed that the results show regulatory compliance rather than violation.

The specific statistic that "76% of samples exceeded EPA limits for cadmium" is constructed by applying EPA drinking water maximum contaminant levels to food samples—a comparison that is not scientifically valid and would, if applied consistently, require alarm about virtually every grain-based food on the market including organic products.

**Gap Category: Misleading Statistics**

This claim belongs to a category of misinformation driven not by fabricated data but by deliberately misleading statistical framing. The misrepresentation lies in the choice of reference standard, not in the measurement values themselves. This type of statistical manipulation is particularly resistant to correction because the underlying numbers are real and the organizations producing the materials can accurately claim that their laboratory results are genuine. Effective fact-check treatment must go beyond verifying measurement accuracy to scrutinize the interpretive framework applied to the measurements.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Girl Scout cookies claim is best understood within the broader ecosystem of food safety advocacy science, a domain that has expanded significantly over the past decade in part because of the declining cost and increasing accessibility of environmental contaminant testing. The technology that allows detection of nanogram-per-gram concentrations of pesticide residues and parts-per-trillion levels of heavy metals in food is genuinely impressive—it was developed through serious analytical chemistry research and represents a real advance in food safety monitoring capacity. The same technology is, however, straightforwardly available for advocacy use, and the gap between detection and harm is substantial and not self-evident.

The regulatory science of food safety thresholds is complex and reflects decades of epidemiological and toxicological evidence. Heavy metal safety thresholds in food are set through processes that include estimates of background dietary exposure, bioavailability (the fraction of ingested substance that actually enters the bloodstream), margin-of-exposure calculations, and differential susceptibility across population groups. The FDA's approach to lead in food, for instance, encompasses not just action levels for specific foods but a dietary exposure model that aggregates exposure across all food sources to ensure total dietary lead intake remains below levels associated with measurable neurological effects in children (FDA Action Plan for Lead in Food, 2022). Glyphosate tolerance levels in grain-based food are set at levels calibrated against the chronic reference dose—the estimated daily exposure that would cause no adverse effect over a lifetime—with safety factors typically of 100-fold or more applied to actual detected agricultural residues.

Broader regulatory context also matters: glyphosate's safety status has been contested, with the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classifying it as "probably carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2A) in 2015, while the EPA, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA), and the WHO/FAO Joint Meeting on Pesticide Residues have all concluded that glyphosate is unlikely to be carcinogenic at realistic dietary exposure levels (IARC, 2015, *Monographs Vol. 112*; EPA Glyphosate Issue Paper, 2016; EFSA, 2015, *EFSA Journal*). The IARC classification is categorically different from a finding that glyphosate causes cancer at detected food residue levels; IARC's mandate concerns hazard identification (can this substance cause cancer under any conditions?) rather than risk assessment (does it cause cancer at typical human exposure levels?). This distinction is systematically erased in advocacy communications that cite IARC classifications as though they established harm at dietary exposure levels, and understanding it requires specific literacy in regulatory risk assessment frameworks that the general public is rarely equipped with.

Guo and colleagues conducted a systematic review of dietary glyphosate exposure across populations in multiple countries and found that estimated dietary intakes consistently fall far below acceptable daily intake levels established by regulatory agencies, typically by one to three orders of magnitude in non-occupationally-exposed populations (Guo et al., 2022, *Environmental Health Perspectives*). This review was not available at the time of the initial MAA/GMOScience report but is consistent with the FDA's characterization of detected residue levels.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published a detailed fact-check of the Girl Scout cookies contamination claim in February 2025 (updated March 18, 2025), authored by Joey Esposito, ultimately rating the claim True—meaning the cookies are safe to eat—with extensive contextual work explaining why the MAA/GMOScience framing is misleading despite the accuracy of the underlying measurement data. The treatment effectively explains the reference standard problem: that the MAA claim was constructed by applying EPA drinking water standards to food samples, a methodologically indefensible comparison.

Notably, Snopes contacted all relevant parties: MAA and GMOScience maintained their position and claimed the Girl Scouts of America never responded to their outreach. GSUSA confirmed to Snopes that all products comply with applicable standards. HRI chief scientist John Fagan confirmed the accuracy of his lab's glyphosate measurements in the limited technical scope of that confirmation. Dr. Steier provided the most accessible summary of the safety picture: "The certified lab results show all tested parameters fall well within established food safety guidelines... This is total fear mongering."

MAA and GMOScience's response to the fact-checks was consistent with advocacy organizations operating in bad faith on public evidence: they maintained their findings while reframing them as calling for improved standards and organic ingredient sourcing rather than addressing the interpretive framing criticism directly. This evasive pivot—from "the cookies exceed safety limits" to "we're calling for stricter limits and organic ingredients"—is a common defensive move that preserves the brand damage accomplished by the original claim while making the claim technically more difficult to refute, since advocating for higher standards is not a falsifiable factual claim.

The case demonstrates the importance of reference standard scrutiny as a core fact-check methodology. When an advocacy claim is built on laboratory data, the three essential questions are: (1) Was the measurement methodology appropriate and accurately conducted? (2) Is the chosen reference standard applicable to the measured substance and exposure pathway? (3) Does the interpretive framing accurately represent the safety implication of the data under the applicable standard? In the Girl Scout cookies case, question 1 answers yes; questions 2 and 3 answer no. A fact-check that stops at question 1 would validate rather than correct the misinformation.

The broader methodological lesson for consumers is one of skeptical engagement with alarming detection claims: the question to ask about any "X found in Y food" claim is not whether X was detected but whether the detected concentration exceeds applicable food safety thresholds at realistic consumption levels. That question requires reference to regulatory frameworks that are publicly available but rarely consulted, and that advocacy materials are structurally motivated to obscure.
