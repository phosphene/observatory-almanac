# Do the Girl Scouts Have Millions of Unsold Cookies?


## 1. The Claim

The claim under evaluation, which achieved significant social media circulation in June 2021 and was first confirmed in major press coverage by the Associated Press, is factual and specific: the Girl Scouts of the United States of America had millions of unsold boxes of cookies remaining from the 2020 cookie season, and those boxes needed to be sold, donated, or disposed of before their use-by dates. The claim was presented by the Girl Scouts themselves through spokespeople, rather than originating as a viral rumor requiring debunking; Snopes fact-checked it in the context of social media discussion amplifying—and in some cases questioning—the AP's reporting.

The specific figure cited in official reporting was approximately 15 million unsold boxes, a number provided directly by Kelly Parisi, a Girl Scouts of the USA spokeswoman, to the Associated Press. Of those 15 million remaining boxes, approximately 12 million were held by the two commercial bakeries contracted to produce Girl Scout cookies: Little Brownie Bakers, based in Louisville, Kentucky, and ABC Bakers (a division of interbake Foods), based in Brownsburg, Indiana. The remaining approximately 3 million boxes were distributed across the Girl Scout council network—the 111 regional councils that administer the program across the U.S.—in varying states of saleability.

Parisi characterized the situation to the AP as "unfortunate, but given this is a girl-driven program and the majority of cookies are sold in-person, it was to be expected." The 2020 cookie season, which typically runs in early spring, coincided with precisely the period when COVID-19 cases were approaching their initial peak in the United States and public health authorities were implementing lockdown orders and discouraging all non-essential in-person activity.

Snopes fact-checker Alex Kasprak evaluated the claim and published a rating of **True** on June 14, 2021, based on the direct confirmation from Girl Scouts of the USA through Parisi's AP statement.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim is accurate. The 2020 Girl Scout cookie season produced a historically unprecedented surplus driven by the COVID-19 pandemic's disruption of the in-person sales model on which the program depends.

**The scale of the surplus:** Approximately 15 million boxes of unsold cookies from the 2020 season remained as of Parisi's statements to the AP in 2021. The 12 million boxes held by the two authorized bakeries represented the most economically acute portion of this surplus, as those units incurred ongoing storage costs, had definite shelf lives, and represented production costs that had already been amortized. The 3 million boxes held within the council network were in various stages of redistribution efforts.

**Why the surplus occurred:** The Girl Scout cookie program's sales model is fundamentally person-to-person in structure. Individual scouts sell cookies through direct social solicitation—approaching neighbors, relatives, and community members—and through manned sales "booths" positioned at high-foot-traffic retail locations such as grocery store entrances, community centers, and public parks. The booth sales are particularly significant in volume terms because they depend on impulse purchase behavior triggered by a physical encounter with a sales scout.

The 2020 cookie season was scheduled for the same early spring window as in prior years. In 2020, that window coincided with the initial U.S. pandemic shutdowns. Stay-at-home orders in most major U.S. metropolitan areas were implemented in mid-to-late March 2020, precisely when booth sales were expected to be at peak. Door-to-door neighborhood sales were further disrupted by virus transmission anxiety, which suppressed the willingness of both scouts and customers to engage in face-to-face transactions. The online and contactless options that the Girl Scouts organization implemented as emergency alternatives—cookies ordered online through scout portals—had not been developed to the scale or brand recognition necessary to substitute meaningfully for the volume of in-person sales.

**The logistics of the surplus:** The persistent presence of 12 million boxes at the two commercial bakeries 14 months after the 2020 season concluded indicates that the standard redistribution mechanisms within the council network had been insufficient to address the volume. Standard cookie season logistics involve bakeries producing to orders placed by councils, with unsold inventory subject to return or buyback provisions that vary by council agreement. The sheer scale of 2020 returns strained these mechanisms. The AP and subsequent reporting noted that the Girl Scouts organization was actively exploring redistribution options including donations to food banks, military commissaries, and organizations serving populations with limited retail access.

The claim is consistent with all available documentary evidence.


## 3. Epistemic Analysis

The Girl Scout cookie surplus case is epistemically interesting less as a fact-check exercise—the claim is straightforwardly true and confirmed by primary sources—than as a case study in the organizational vulnerability of particular economic models to exogenous disruption and the informational challenges that arise in communicating that vulnerability to a public accustomed to an institution's apparent seamlessness.

Girl Scout cookies are one of the most culturally entrenched fundraising products in American life. Annual cookie sales generate approximately $800 million per year across the full program (GSUSA annual reports, various years), making the cookie sale the largest girl-led business initiative in the United States by revenue and arguably the most successful recurring charitable fundraiser in American consumer culture. For many Americans, the cookie sale is experienced as an ambient social fact: scouts appear near grocery stores in spring, adults purchase Thin Mints and Samoas, and the program continues. The operational infrastructure that produces this experienced seamlessness—the dual-baker supply chain, the council allocation system, the booth permitting process, the logistics of statewide seasonal deployment—is invisible under normal conditions.

COVID-19 made the operational infrastructure suddenly legible by disrupting it. The surplus revealed, for millions of observers, facts about the program's structure that are not otherwise visible: that production is consolidated in two commercial bakeries rather than distributed; that the spring timing is by design, tied to seasonal foot traffic patterns rather than arbitrary; that in-person sales are not merely conventional but structurally essential to the volume model; and that the organization's nimbleness in shifting to digital alternatives was constrained by years of infrastructure developed around a physical interaction model.

The epistemic novelty of this case, as a Truth Vault entry, lies in what might be called **institutional transparency through failure**. The claim is true, but its significance is not merely the number of unsold boxes—it is the systemic picture that number reveals. A 15-million-box surplus from a single disrupted season represents approximately 19% of total theoretical annual production capacity based on GSUSA's reported annual sales volumes (estimating at the $800M figure at an assumed average retail price of approximately $5 per box, yielding approximately 160 million boxes at capacity). A 19% shortfall in a single year is economically severe by nonprofit program standards and reveals that the model has essentially no continuous surplus buffer—it operates lean across normal years, which is efficient but fragile.

The organizational theory literature on resilience is useful here. Weick and Sutcliffe (2001, *Managing the Unexpected*) describe "high-reliability organizations" as those that maintain redundancy and diversity in critical operational pathways to absorb disruption. The Girl Scout cookie model, by contrast, exhibits what Taleb (2012, *Antifragile*) characterizes as structural fragility in its dependence on a single sales modality (in-person impulse purchase) concentrated in a single seasonal window. The pandemic did not create this fragility; it revealed a fragility that existed and was simply untested by prior disruptions of comparable geographic and temporal scope.

For research on the economics of nonprofit youth organizations, the pandemic surplus also raises underexplored questions about how disruptions to youth fundraising programs affect the programs themselves. A meaningful fraction of troop-level programming and council-level activities is funded by the margin on cookie sales. When sales collapse, the downstream effect is reduced funding for camp programming, leadership activities, field trips, and materials. Documenting the 2020 surplus is not merely a supply chain story; it is a proxy measure of disrupted youth programming at national scale. The quantitative relationship between cookie sales shortfalls and program delivery outcomes has not, to the knowledge of this review, been systematically studied in published literature.

Relatedly, the dual-baker structure of the Girl Scout supply chain is itself understudied in organizational and supply-chain research. Historically, the use of two separately licensed commercial bakers who produce equivalent products under different recipes (Little Brownie Bakers and ABC Bakers do not produce identical formulations for all cookie varieties) creates regional variation in product experience that most consumers are unaware of. A Thin Mint purchased in one region may taste detectably different from one purchased in another, because they come from different bakers using different flavor balances. This regional variation exists as a deliberate consequence of the dual-baker licensing structure, which itself exists to prevent a single-point-of-failure in the supply chain—the same resilience reasoning that COVID-19's disruption test revealed to be insufficiently extended to the distribution end.


## 4. The Evidence

**Primary source confirmations:**
- Kelly Parisi, Girl Scouts of the USA spokesperson, to the Associated Press: approximately 15 million boxes of cookies remained unsold from the 2020 season; approximately 12 million remained with Little Brownie Bakers and ABC Bakers.
- AP news report coverage of the surplus, published June 2021, which formed the basis of Snopes' evaluation.
- GSUSA's own public communications on exploring redistribution options including food bank donations, military channel donations, and other alternative pathways.

**Background industry and sector literature:**

Weick, K. E., & Sutcliffe, K. M. (2001). *Managing the Unexpected: Assuring High Performance in an Age of Complexity*. Jossey-Bass. The resilience and fragility framework from this work applies directly to the Girl Scout cookie model's dependence on a single, seasonally concentrated in-person sales pathway.

Taleb, N. N. (2012). *Antifragile: Things That Gain from Disorder*. Random House. The fragility/robustness/antifragility taxonomy is productive for analyzing why the cookie sale model's efficiency under normal conditions made it particularly vulnerable to the pandemic's nonlinear disruption.

Putnam, R. D. (2000). *Bowling Alone: The Collapse and Revival of American Community*. Simon & Schuster. Documents the long-term decline in face-to-face social capital institutions; the Girl Scout cookie booth model is precisely the kind of community transaction that Putnam identifies as socially productive beyond its economic function—a reason its COVID disruption was culturally significant beyond the supply chain story.

McKnight, J., & Block, P. (2010). *The Abundant Community: Awakening the Power and Possibility of Neighborhoods*. Berrett-Koehler. Frames community-based fundraising as a form of social infrastructure that is functionally irreplaceable by digital equivalents because the social transaction—the face-to-face request, the interpersonal negotiation, the communal reinforcement—constitutes the experience alongside the economic transfer.

Berkes, F., & Ross, H. (2013). Community resilience: Toward an integrated approach. *Society & Natural Resources*, 26(1), 5–20. The community resilience framework is applicable to understanding why cookie-based fundraising, embedded in neighborhood and community routines, was particularly vulnerable to the loss of those routine contact points during pandemic lockdowns.

Hale, T., Webster, S., Petherick, A., Phillips, T., & Kira, B. (2020). Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker. *Blavatnik School of Government, University of Oxford*. Documents the timing and severity of government containment measures across U.S. states in spring 2020, providing the contextual baseline against which the cookie season's disruption can be temporally mapped.

Girl Scouts of the USA. (2021). *Annual Report 2020*. GSUSA. Documents programmatic scale, council structure, and revenue sources; provides the baseline against which the surplus volume can be evaluated proportionally.

Dees, J. G. (1998). Enterprising nonprofits. *Harvard Business Review*, 76(1), 55–67. Foundational work on the hybrid commercial-mission model of nonprofit revenue generation; the cookie sale is a canonical example of a mission-aligned commercial activity, and its disruption raises the questions Dees raises about mission sustainability when commercial revenue streams are shock-sensitive.


## 5. EARC/Gap Analysis

**EARC Mode: E (Empirically Verified)**

The claim is directly and comprehensively verified by the primary source—the organization itself, through its spokeswoman, in a direct statement to a wire service—supported by contextual evidence about pandemic timing that makes the causal mechanism straightforward. No interpretive ambiguity exists.

**Gap Category: Confirmed Pandemic Disruption**

This entry belongs to the gap category of *confirmed pandemic disruption*—fact-checks that verify true claims about COVID-19's disruption of established commercial, organizational, or social systems. This category is important for the Truth Vault because pandemic disruption claims constitute a large class of true statements that circulated with social media virality in 2020–2022 and required verification due to the general noise environment of COVID-period information.

Within this category, the Girl Scout cookie case has distinctive features that elevate it beyond a routine supply chain story. The surplus simultaneously documents: (1) a specific quantitative impact of pandemic disruption on a named nonprofit (15 million boxes, estimated at roughly $75 million retail value); (2) the degree of dependence of a nationally significant youth organization's programming funding on in-person seasonal commerce; (3) the structural limits of digital sales substitution for high-tactility, impulse-driven, socially embedded retail models; and (4) the geographic and logistic concentration of production in two commercial bakers whose storage and shelf-life constraints create temporal urgency in the surplus disposition challenge.

The research gap most clearly illuminated by this case is the absence of systematic quantitative studies tracking the downstream effects of the 2020 cookie surplus on troop-level and council-level programming. Journalistic coverage documented the surplus; the organizational response (redistribution, donation, disposal) was reported in partial terms. What was not reported—and has not, to the best of this review's knowledge, appeared in the literature—is a systematic account of how the reduced cookie revenues affected specific types of Girl Scout programming, whether certain programmatic categories (summer camp, leadership training, STEM programming) suffered disproportionately, and whether the disruption had differential effects across councils in different regions or socioeconomic contexts. This is a genuine scholarly gap in the organizational study of pandemic effects on nonprofit youth programming.


## 6. Verdict and Context

**Verdict: True.** The Girl Scouts of the USA confirmed through a spokeswoman to the Associated Press that approximately 15 million boxes of cookies remained unsold from the 2020 season, with 12 million held by the two authorized commercial bakeries. The cause was the COVID-19 pandemic's disruption of in-person sales infrastructure during the spring 2020 cookie season.

The context that makes this fact-check more than a routine supply chain verification is the cultural weight of the Girl Scout cookie enterprise in American life. The cookie sale is not merely a fundraising mechanism; it is a social institution that annually enacts a particular set of interactions between youth, community, and commerce. The booth sale in front of the grocery store, the scout in her uniform approaching a neighborhood door, the parent managing order forms—these are recurring scenes in a shared national cultural calendar. Their disruption by COVID-19 was felt not only economically but as one index of how comprehensively the pandemic had unmade ordinary social life.

The surplus also raises questions that extend beyond the immediate pandemic context: How resilient are youth organization fundraising models to future disruptions? The Girl Scout cookie apparatus, which has operated in its current commercial form since 1936 (with official baker licensing from GSUSA), was designed in an era when in-person community interaction was the default modality for commerce, community, and social life. The adaptations made during 2020—online ordering portals, contactless delivery options, virtual sales pitches—represent emergency improvisations rather than systematic redesign. Whether the organization's subsequent years have produced structural resilience investments, or whether the commercial model reverted to its pre-pandemic form once in-person activity became possible again, is a question with implications for the program's long-term robustness.

From the perspective of food systems and commercial waste, 15 million unsold cookie boxes represent a concrete example of supply-demand mismatch at scale in a vertically structured commercial nonprofit model. The disposition of those boxes—through food bank channels, military distribution, and other pathways—required organizational creativity and represented food waste mitigation that mostly went uncovered in the subsequent news cycle. The fact that 12 million boxes were still sitting in commercial bakeries 14 months after the season's end suggests that redistribution was not as smooth as post-announcement communications implied, and that operational learning for future disruptions would need to account for the practical constraints of redirecting high-volume packaged food production at speed into alternative channels.
