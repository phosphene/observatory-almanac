# Glass Is a Slow-Moving Liquid — That's Why Old Windows Are Thicker at the Bottom


## 1. The Claim

"Glass is a very slow-moving liquid — that's why old cathedral windows are thicker at the bottom. The glass has been flowing downward for centuries." This claim circulates in science classrooms, in popular science articles, and in casual conversation as an elegant example of how everyday intuitions about solid and liquid can be wrong. It has been repeated in textbooks. It has been cited as a charming illustration of geological timescales in everyday life. It has been used to make glass seem mysterious and ancient in a pleasing way.

The claim has two interlocking components: first, that glass is technically a liquid rather than a solid; second, that the visible thickness gradient in old window glass is evidence of this liquid nature at work. Both components are wrong, though in instructive ways — one is a genuine misapplication of materials science terminology, and the other rests on an incorrect understanding of historical glassmaking.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Glass is an amorphous solid, not a liquid.** The technical distinction matters. Crystalline solids — like table salt or quartz — have atoms arranged in repeating periodic structures. Liquids have no long-range atomic order and flow under applied stress. Glass occupies an intermediate category: it lacks long-range crystalline order (it's amorphous), but it does not flow under gravity at room temperature. This distinction between "lacking crystal structure" and "behaving like a liquid" is where the myth arises.

Glass has a glass transition temperature (Tg) — the temperature above which it softens and begins to flow. For common soda-lime glass (window glass), Tg is approximately 520°C. Below this temperature, glass is mechanically a solid: it has a well-defined elastic modulus, it stores stress rather than relaxing it, and it shatters rather than deforming when stressed beyond its limit. None of these are behaviors of liquids.

The confusion arises from a misapplication of an older classification in which glass was described as a "supercooled liquid" — meaning it had cooled below the crystallization temperature without crystallizing, remaining in a metastable amorphous state. This terminology, once common in materials science, implied continuous liquid behavior that was never actually present at room temperature. Modern materials science uses "amorphous solid" precisely to avoid this confusion.

**Old window glass is thicker at the bottom because of manufacturing, not flow.** Medieval and early modern glassmakers used the **crown glass** process. Molten glass was gathered on a blowpipe, blown into a spherical bubble, then transferred to a pontil rod and spun rapidly. Centrifugal force flattened the bubble into a disk — the "crown" or "bull's-eye" pane — with a characteristic pattern: thicker at the edges, thinner toward the center, with a distinctive bull's-eye mark where the pontil rod was attached.

These disks were cut into panes for window installation. Glaziers faced a practical choice: the irregular, thicker edges of the disk were more structurally stable and better at resisting wind pressure. Installing the heavier, thicker edge at the bottom made mechanical sense and produced more durable installations. The result was window panes systematically thicker at the bottom — not because glass flowed there over centuries, but because craftsmen put the thicker end at the bottom when they installed the pane.

**The calculation that settles the question:**

In 1998, **Edgar Zanotto**, a materials scientist at the Federal University of São Carlos in Brazil, published a calculation in the *American Journal of Physics* that directly addressed whether room-temperature glass could flow on any humanly relevant timescale. Using established viscosity data for silicate glasses at room temperature and classical fluid dynamics, Zanotto calculated the time required for a pane of glass to show measurable deformation at 20°C.

His result: approximately **10^23 years** — many orders of magnitude longer than the current age of the universe (approximately 1.4 × 10^10 years). The viscosity of glass at room temperature is so extraordinarily high (roughly 10^21 Pascal-seconds, compared to water's 10^-3 Pa·s) that gravitational flow is not merely slow — it is, for all practical purposes, physically absent. Ancient Roman glass from two millennia ago shows no measurable flow, consistent with Zanotto's calculation.

**The evidence from ancient glass:**

If glass were flowing on century timescales, Roman glass artifacts — bottles, beads, window fragments — that have survived 2,000 years should show measurable thickness gradients caused by gravitational flow. They do not. Systematic measurements of ancient glass show no evidence of deformation consistent with viscous flow under gravity. This empirical observation corroborates the theoretical calculation: glass does not flow at room temperature.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The "supercooled liquid" terminology:**

The myth has a genuine academic ancestor. For much of the 20th century, glass was described in materials science textbooks as a "supercooled liquid" — a substance that had cooled below its equilibrium crystallization temperature while remaining amorphous. This terminology was technically defensible within a specific thermodynamic framework and appeared in respected scientific literature.

The problem was the word "liquid." In everyday language, liquids flow. If glass is a liquid, it must flow. If it flows, and old windows are thicker at the bottom, the myth assembles itself from available parts. The terminology gave the myth scientific-sounding legitimacy even though the viscosity data had always shown that room-temperature flow was impossibly slow.

**Zanotto & Gupta's 1999 paper** in the *American Journal of Physics* directly addressed the terminology problem, arguing that "supercooled liquid" was misleading and that "amorphous solid" more accurately described glass's mechanical behavior below Tg. The paper explicitly examined whether glass should be considered a liquid for practical purposes and concluded that it should not — the distinction between disordered atomic structure and fluid behavior was being systematically conflated.

**The observational evidence that seems to confirm it:**

Old windows in European cathedrals often do show thickness variation. Visitors who observe this variation and are told "glass is a liquid" find the explanation intuitively satisfying. The observation is real; the explanation is wrong. This is the classic structure of a compelling false explanation: it accounts for something you can actually see.

The thickness variation in old glass is, in fact, genuine evidence — but of the crown glass manufacturing process, not of viscous flow. A careful analysis reveals that the gradient is not consistent with gravitational flow (which would produce smoothly graded thickness with the very bottom most displaced), but rather with the radial pattern of the crown glass disk (irregular variation from center to edge). The shape of the variation is a fingerprint of the manufacturing method, not of slow drainage.

**Why the glass flow story is so appealing:**

The claim has narrative virtues that make it memorable and worth repeating. It recasts a mundane material as something strange and timeful. It suggests that if you watch long enough, you'll see glass doing something surprising. It connects a familiar object to geological and cosmological timescales in a way that makes the universe feel vast and the material world alive. These are exactly the qualities that make a piece of science communication go viral — whether or not it is accurate.

**Textbook proliferation:**

The myth appeared in credentialed sources. It was reproduced in chemistry and physics textbooks that cited the "supercooled liquid" classification. When a claim appears in textbooks used in formal education, it acquires a kind of institutional authority that makes it very difficult to dislodge — even after the underlying claim has been addressed in the primary literature.

**The confirmation mechanism:**

People who hold the "glass flows" belief and visit old churches or museums find apparent confirmation everywhere. Any variation in old glass — and crown glass is inherently variable — can be interpreted as evidence of flow. Once the explanatory framework is in place, the evidence seems to accumulate effortlessly. The interpretation is wrong, but the experience of finding confirmation is genuine.

**The scale of misunderstanding:**

A 2013 informal survey of physics instructors in the UK found that a substantial fraction believed the "glass is a liquid" claim and had taught it as fact. The claim had penetrated teaching practice deeply enough that correcting it required not just reaching students but reaching instructors who would otherwise continue transmitting the myth.

**Historical glassmaking and the glazier's logic:**

The crown glass manufacturing process was the dominant method for window glass production from medieval times through the 18th century, when cylinder (or broad) glass methods gradually replaced it. Crown glass disks were expensive and somewhat irregular; glaziers developed practical expertise in extracting the most usable panes from each disk and installing them in ways that maximized durability.

The decision to put the thicker edge at the bottom was sensible craft knowledge: heavier material at the base of a pane-in-frame reduces the tendency to sag in the glazing (the soft lead or putty that holds glass in its frame), improves resistance to wind pressure, and matches the intuitive logic of building construction (heavier things go lower). There was no mystery and no need for a "glass flows" explanation — craftsmen were simply doing what worked.

**Modern glass manufacturing:**

Modern float glass — the standard for contemporary windows — is produced by floating molten glass on a bed of molten tin, producing sheets of near-perfect uniform thickness. You won't find thickness gradients in modern windows, which removes even the observational basis for the myth in everyday contemporary experience. The "evidence" (uneven old glass) is invisible in modern contexts; only visitors to old buildings or museums encounter it.


## 4. Verdict

**DISTORTED BUT GROUNDED.** Both components of the claim contain a grain of genuine science that has been misapplied or over-extended.

**The grain of truth about glass:**
- Glass does lack long-range crystalline order — it is amorphous.
- The "supercooled liquid" classification was historically used in materials science.
- Glass does flow — at temperatures above its glass transition temperature (~520°C for window glass).

**The distortion about glass:**
- Below Tg, glass is mechanically a solid. It does not flow under gravity at room temperature.
- Zanotto's 1998 calculation: measurable flow at room temperature would require ~10^23 years.
- "Amorphous solid" is the current and more accurate classification; "supercooled liquid" has been abandoned for practical purposes.

**The grain of truth about old windows:**
- Old window glass is often genuinely thicker at the bottom.
- The thickness variation is observable and real.

**The distortion about old windows:**
- The thickness gradient results from crown glass manufacturing and deliberate installation choices by glaziers.
- The gradient shape is inconsistent with gravitational flow; it is consistent with radial variation in crown glass disks.
- Roman glass artifacts 2,000+ years old show no measurable flow — empirically ruling out the mechanism.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The physics of viscosity and amorphous solids:**

Viscosity is resistance to flow — the measure of how much a material resists deformation under shear stress. It is not a binary property (liquid vs. solid) but a continuous one ranging across many orders of magnitude. At one extreme: air (viscosity ~10^-5 Pa·s). Water (~10^-3 Pa·s). Honey (~10 Pa·s). Glass at 600°C (~10^6 Pa·s). Glass at room temperature (~10^21 Pa·s). Earth's mantle (~10^21 Pa·s over geological timescales).

The mantle comparison is instructive. Earth's mantle rocks flow — but on timescales of millions of years, under enormous pressures, at temperatures far above room temperature. Even then, this is barely "flowing" in any everyday sense. Glass at room temperature has roughly the same viscosity as the upper mantle, but without the temperature and pressure that enable mantle flow. The comparison illustrates just how meaningless "flow" becomes at these viscosity values.

**Other amorphous solids and the classification problem:**

Glass is not the only amorphous solid that has attracted classification confusion. Pitch — a tar-like substance derived from petroleum or coal — is genuinely amorphous and does flow at room temperature, though extremely slowly. The **Pitch Drop Experiment** at the University of Queensland, running continuously since 1927, has documented approximately nine drops of pitch falling over nearly a century. Pitch at room temperature has a viscosity of about 10^8 Pa·s — still very high, but enormously lower than glass.

If anything, pitch demonstrates that if glass were flowing at room temperature, the flow would be detectable — pitch's much lower viscosity already produces drops only once per decade. Glass's viscosity is 13 orders of magnitude higher than pitch. If a century is barely enough for pitch to produce nine drops, glass would require unimaginably longer timescales to produce any measurable deformation.

**The history of glass and ancient technology:**

Glassmaking has roots stretching back to at least 3500 BCE in Mesopotamia and Egypt, initially for beads and small vessels before window glass was developed. The crown glass process that produced the irregular old window panes central to this myth was developed in Syria and spread through the Roman Empire, reaching medieval Europe and persisting until the 18th century.

The development of the cylinder (broad) glass process in the 17th-18th centuries allowed larger, more uniform sheets. The Siemens float glass process of the 20th century (later improved as the Pilkington process) produced the near-perfect sheet glass that is now universal. The history of window glass is a history of technological improvement toward uniformity — the irregularities visible in old cathedral windows are the imperfections of early technology, not the flow signatures of a liquid material.

**Science communication and the memorable wrong claim:**

The glass-liquid myth illustrates a persistent challenge in science communication: the accurate version of a story is sometimes less memorable than an inaccurate one. "Glass is a liquid that flows so slowly you need centuries to see it" is a memorable, evocative claim. "Glass is an amorphous solid manufactured by a historical process that produced thickness gradients that craftsmen installed with the heavy end down" is accurate and complete — and considerably less compelling as dinner-party conversation.

This asymmetry in memorability creates a systematic pressure toward the propagation of simplified or inaccurate explanations. Correcting the myth requires not just providing accurate information but doing so in a way that is at least as interesting as the wrong version. The calculation that room-temperature flow would take 10^23 years — an expanse of time so vast it dwarfs the age of the universe — is actually a fascinating piece of information in its own right. Zanotto's calculation is more interesting than the myth it refutes, once you appreciate what it means: that the apparent solidity of glass is not just practical but almost incomprehensibly deep.

**Materials science and the glass transition:**

The glass transition temperature is one of the most interesting concepts in condensed matter physics precisely because it resists clean classification. Above Tg, glass is a viscous liquid. Below Tg, it is an amorphous solid. But the transition is not a phase transition in the thermodynamic sense — there is no discontinuous change in entropy or volume at Tg, unlike melting or boiling. The glass transition is a kinetic phenomenon: as a material cools through the transition temperature, molecular rearrangements become slower than the cooling rate, and the material becomes kinetically trapped in a disordered configuration.

This is genuinely strange and interesting physics. It means that the same material can be either liquid or solid depending on the timescale of observation — at very long timescales, even glass slightly above Tg behaves like a solid. The "glass is a liquid" myth fumbles toward this genuine strangeness and gets it catastrophically wrong, applying the long-timescale behavior relevant above Tg to conditions far below Tg where it has no relevance.

The real story — that the solid/liquid distinction is not absolute, that it depends on temperature and observation timescale, that an amorphous material can exhibit elastic solidity while lacking crystal structure — is more profound and more accurate than the myth. It just requires more careful explanation.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not published a direct entry on the claim that glass flows and that old windows are thicker at the bottom as evidence of this flow. This is a meaningful gap: the claim appears regularly in science classrooms, popular science writing, and online educational content. It has had sufficient circulation to appear in peer-reviewed physics education literature as an example of a persistent misconception.

Several other scientific education resources have addressed the claim — notably the work of Zanotto himself, who has written accessible explainers on the topic, and *Physics Today* and the *American Journal of Physics*, which have published articles specifically aimed at correcting the textbook misconception. Robert Brill at the Corning Museum of Glass has also published empirical measurements of ancient glass confirming the absence of flow, providing the observational counterpart to Zanotto's theoretical calculation.

The convergence of a precise theoretical calculation (Zanotto, 1998) and empirical measurements of ancient artifacts (Brill) that together rule out glass flow by entirely independent methods represents exactly the kind of settled, multiply-confirmed scientific question that benefits from systematic fact-checking to reach general audiences who would otherwise continue to encounter the claim in educational contexts without a clear, accessible rebuttal.
