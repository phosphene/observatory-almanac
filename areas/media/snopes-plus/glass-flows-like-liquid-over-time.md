# Glass is a slow-moving liquid, which is why old windows are thicker at the bottom

## 1. The Claim

The story circulates widely in popular science contexts, dinner-table conversation, and secondary school physics classes: glass is technically a liquid, not a solid. This means that glass flows very, very slowly over human timescales, and the evidence is visible in old European cathedrals and historic buildings. Walk into a medieval church, the story goes, and look at the windows. The glass will be noticeably thicker at the bottom than at the top, because over centuries of gradual flow, the material has accumulated at the bottom under the influence of gravity—just as a very viscous liquid would.

The claim often comes paired with a genuine physics concept: glass is an amorphous solid, meaning it lacks the regular crystalline atomic structure of materials like ice, quartz, or table salt. This is true. And from this true premise, the popular account makes an inferential leap: if it's not crystalline, it must be liquid in some sense, and if it's liquid, it must flow on long enough timescales.

The observation seems to confirm the theory. Old windows in medieval buildings genuinely do have glass that is thicker at the bottom. If you visit Chartres Cathedral, Cologne Cathedral, or the historic churches of England and Germany, many of the original windows show this pattern. The theory appears to explain a real, visible phenomenon.

This makes the claim harder to dismiss than most folk physics. It isn't just a story—it appears to be borne out by observation. The challenge is that both the theory and the observation are real, but the causal link between them is wrong.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

**Glass does not flow at room temperature on any human timescale, or indeed on any geologically meaningful timescale.**

The authoritative calculation comes from Edgar Zanotto and Arun Gupta's 1999 paper in the *American Journal of Physics*, "Do cathedral glasses flow?" Using established viscosity data for silicate glass at room temperature and applying standard fluid mechanics calculations, they showed that a pane of glass 1 millimeter thick would take on the order of 10^32 years to flow by even 1 nanometer under its own weight at room temperature. The current age of the universe is approximately 1.4 × 10^10 years. The flow time for glass at room temperature exceeds the age of the universe by more than twenty orders of magnitude.

This is not a close call. The viscosity of glass at room temperature is approximately 10^21 Pascal-seconds. For comparison, water has a viscosity of 10^-3 Pa·s, honey approximately 10 Pa·s, and even very slow geological materials like the Earth's mantle have viscosities in the range of 10^19–10^21 Pa·s—and the mantle moves centimeters per year. Glass at room temperature is immeasurably more rigid than ordinary intuitions about "very slow liquids" can capture.

**The actual cause of thicker-bottom medieval glass is manufacturing.**

Pre-industrial European window glass was made using one of two techniques. The dominant technique for large windows was the "crown glass" or "bull's-eye" method: glassblowers would spin a gather of molten glass on the end of an iron rod until centrifugal force spread it into a large, roughly circular disc. This disc was thicker at the edges (which were farthest from the center of rotation and received the most accumulated material) and thinner toward the center. When the disc was cut into panes, pieces cut near the edges of the disc were thicker than pieces cut near the center. The famous "bull's-eye" panes that appear in the centers of some historic windows are the center disks of these crown glass pieces.

The alternative, the "cylinder" or "muff" method, involved blowing glass into a cylindrical shape, cutting the cylinder open lengthwise, and flattening it in a kiln. This produced rectangular sheets, but they were not perfectly uniform in thickness.

Both methods produced irregular panes. When glaziers installed these panes, there is evidence—though not universal—that they sometimes preferred to install thicker, heavier portions at the bottom of the frame for stability. Even when they did not, the random non-uniformities in the glass meant that some panes were thicker at one end than the other, and by chance some would be thicker at the bottom.

Additionally, medieval glass contains high concentrations of impurities—iron oxides giving it color, bubbles, striae (internal flow lines from manufacturing)—that affect its apparent thickness optically as well as physically. The appearance of "thicker at the bottom" is partly an optical effect of impurities distributed unevenly through the manufacturing process.

**The grounded part: glass is genuinely an amorphous solid.**

Zanotto and Gupta's paper does not dispute that glass lacks crystalline structure. It does. Silicon dioxide, the primary component of most window glass, can exist as crystalline quartz or as amorphous silica glass, depending on how it cools from the melt. Slow cooling allows atoms to arrange into regular crystal lattices. Rapid quenching freezes the atoms in a disordered arrangement before they can crystallize, producing an amorphous solid.

This is a real physical distinction, and the question of what exactly amorphous solids *are*—whether they are true solids in the thermodynamic sense, or extremely high-viscosity liquids that are kinetically trapped—is a legitimate area of ongoing scientific discussion. Glass is in a "metastable" state: its thermodynamic minimum energy configuration would be crystalline, but the energy barrier to crystallization is so high at room temperature that the disordered arrangement persists essentially indefinitely. At high temperatures, silica glass will crystallize ("devitrification"), which is why glassmakers have to manage thermal cycles carefully.

So the underlying scientific concept—glass is not a crystalline solid; it occupies an amorphous, metastable state; its properties are intermediate between ideal solid and ideal liquid in certain technical senses—is real and interesting. The error is in assuming this means glass flows on human timescales.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The claim has unusual staying power because it is constructed from three ingredients that each seem credible individually: a real physical phenomenon (amorphous solid structure), a real observation (thicker-bottom old windows), and a logically plausible (but wrong) causal mechanism connecting them.

**The "liquid glass" idea is genuinely taught in watered-down form in science education.** Many physics and chemistry curricula introduce amorphous solids by contrasting them with crystalline solids, sometimes using the language that glass is "in some ways more like a liquid." This is a simplification intended to convey a real physical concept, but it often lodges in memory as "glass is basically a slow liquid," detached from the quantitative context that makes clear the flow timescales are astronomical.

**The observation is real.** People who have visited medieval cathedrals and noticed the bottom-thick glass are accurately reporting what they saw. The observation is not a myth; the causal explanation is. This makes the claim feel empirically grounded in a way that pure fabrications are not.

**The mechanism is intuitively plausible.** Even professional scientists, when first encountering this claim without having done the viscosity calculation, often find it initially plausible. Intuition does not have a good native feeling for viscosities of 10^21 Pa·s. The human experience of viscous materials—honey, tar, pitch—tops out around the range of 10^4–10^6 Pa·s, which is many orders of magnitude below glass. Extrapolating human viscosity intuition to glass at room temperature systematically underestimates how rigid it is.

**The pitch drop experiment lurks in the background.** Pitch (bitumen), a material superficially similar to glass in its brittle room-temperature behavior, does flow measurably. The University of Queensland's famous pitch drop experiment, begun in 1927, has produced about nine drops in roughly 90 years—visible, documented slow flow. People who know about pitch drop sometimes conflate pitch and glass as both belonging to the "seems solid but flows slowly" category. They are not the same material. Pitch has a viscosity many orders of magnitude lower than glass.

**Authoritative-sounding sources have spread the claim uncritically.** Popular science writers, museum exhibit designers, and science communicators have repeated the glass-flows claim for decades, often without checking the viscosity math. Its appearance in respected popular science outlets gave it the veneer of well-established fact rather than widespread misconception.

## 4. Verdict

**EARC: E — Explained (Distorted but Grounded)**

The claim as stated—that glass flows slowly at room temperature and this explains thicker-bottom medieval windows—is false. The flow timescale at room temperature exceeds the age of the universe by more than twenty orders of magnitude. The thicker-bottom pattern in medieval windows is a manufacturing artifact, not evidence of viscous flow.

However, the claim is not pure invention. It rests on a real physical observation (amorphous solid structure), a real historical pattern (thicker-bottom medieval glass), and a real scientific concept (glass is in a metastable thermodynamic state distinct from true crystalline solids). The distortion lies in the inferential step that connects these real things: the assumption that "not crystalline" implies "flows on human timescales."

This is distorted but grounded: the grounds are real, the distortion is the causal mechanism.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The glass-as-liquid claim is an excellent specimen of a specific type of popular science error: the category-error extrapolation. It takes a real distinction (crystalline vs. amorphous solids), maps it onto a simplified human-scale category (solid vs. liquid), and then draws real-world predictions from the simplified category that do not follow from the actual distinction.

This type of error is common in popular science communication because science educators and writers face genuine pedagogical pressure to make difficult concepts accessible. Saying "glass is not a crystalline solid in the way quartz is—it lacks long-range atomic order and is technically in a metastable state" is accurate but abstract. Saying "glass is technically a very slow liquid" is simple and memorable and generates immediate intuitive understanding of the key distinction. The simplification sacrifices accuracy for accessibility, and then travels far past the contexts where the teacher would have corrected the extrapolation.

The materials science of amorphous solids is, separately, a genuinely rich area of active research. Understanding the glass transition—the temperature range over which amorphous materials shift from rigid to viscous behavior—has practical implications for everything from smartphone screens to fiber optic cables to volcanic geology. Volcanic glasses (obsidian, pumice) are geologically common and scientifically important. Metallic glasses, amorphous metals produced by ultra-rapid quenching, are an active research frontier with potential applications in transformer cores, medical devices, and structural materials. The science that the popular glass-as-liquid claim garbles is interesting in its own right.

The medieval glass manufacturing story also illuminates a non-obvious aspect of material culture history. Pre-industrial glassmaking was an extraordinarily skilled craft industry with distinctive regional traditions. The distinctive appearance of historic European stained glass—the slight irregularities, the bubbles, the color striations, the thickness variations—is not a defect but a record of the techniques that produced it. When conservators restore or replace damaged panes in historic buildings, replicating the appearance of historic glass requires not just matching the chemical composition but matching the manufacturing technique, because the visual character is inseparable from the process.

The question of how glass transitions thermodynamically is also more open than the simple "it's a solid" dismissal suggests. As Zanotto himself has noted, the status of the glassy state as either a true thermodynamic phase or a kinetically arrested metastable form of a liquid remains a topic of active theoretical discussion in condensed matter physics. This is not because glass flows on human timescales—it demonstrably does not—but because the thermodynamic status of the amorphous solid state involves questions about what "solid" means at the level of equilibrium statistical mechanics. The popular claim has the right question and the wrong answer.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The glass-as-liquid claim is among the more commonly debunked physics myths in fact-checking and popular science circles. Snopes, various scientific debunking blogs, and professional physics communicators have addressed it multiple times, generally reaching the same conclusions: the proposed mechanism (room-temperature flow) is physically impossible on human timescales, and the evidence cited (thicker-bottom medieval windows) has a manufacturing explanation.

Zanotto and Gupta's 1999 *American Journal of Physics* paper is the standard reference for the viscosity calculation and is widely cited in debunking accounts. The paper is admirably clear and accessible, performing the calculation step by step and noting how long the commonly cited "millions of years" estimates would still understate the actual flow time.

Where fact-checkers sometimes fall short is in engaging with the underlying physical concept that the myth garbles. Many debunking accounts focus heavily on "glass is a solid, not a liquid," which—as Zanotto himself has pointed out—is a slightly overcorrected simplification of the thermodynamic situation. Glass is not a liquid in any practical or human-timescale sense. But the amorphous solid state does occupy a theoretically interesting position in condensed matter physics, and characterizing it as simply "a solid" without further qualification leaves out the part of the underlying science that makes the original misconception understandable as a distortion rather than a fabrication.

The best fact-checking responses to this claim handle three distinct components: (1) the viscosity physics that rules out room-temperature flow on observable timescales, (2) the manufacturing history that explains thicker-bottom medieval glass, and (3) a brief description of what the amorphous solid state actually means—rescuing the interesting underlying science from the wreckage of the popular myth. Claims that only address (1) and (2) leave readers with a less scientifically accurate picture than they could have, and may inadvertently reinforce the overcorrection that glass is simply, unremarkably solid.

This is also a case where scientific correction has been available for a long time—the Zanotto/Gupta paper appeared in 1999—but the myth continues to circulate because it is memorable, intuitive, and frequently repeated by credible-seeming sources who have not done the calculation themselves. Debunking alone does not remove a well-embedded misconception; it creates a correction layer that must be encountered more than the original claim, which is unlikely in typical information environments. The myth will continue to circulate alongside the correction for the foreseeable future.

The glass transition temperature is a concept worth explaining briefly, because it clarifies what the myth gets partially right. Every amorphous material has a glass transition temperature (Tg) above which it behaves increasingly like a viscous liquid and below which it behaves like a rigid solid. For common soda-lime silicate glass (the type in most windows), Tg is approximately 550–600°C. Far below Tg—say, at room temperature—the material is far into the rigid solid regime. The viscosity is astronomically high. Near Tg, the material becomes soft and workable, which is how glassblowers shape it. Above Tg, it flows freely. The myth effectively treats glass at room temperature as though it were near its Tg, which would require room temperature to be orders of magnitude hotter than it is.

This also clarifies why the pitch drop experiment, which some people cite as analogous, is not analogous. Pitch (bitumen) has a Tg well below room temperature—roughly -20°C to 0°C depending on composition. At room temperature, pitch is above its glass transition temperature and thus in a highly viscous but flowable regime. The University of Queensland pitch drop experiment does show real measurable flow. But pitch and window glass are not the same type of material or in the same thermal regime relative to their respective Tg values. The comparison fails at the first quantitative step.

The medieval glass manufacturing context also has a broader cultural history worth noting. Crown glass production required extraordinary skill developed over generations. Glassblowing guilds in Venice, the Rhineland, and England maintained closely guarded trade secrets about composition and technique. The distinctive appearance of medieval stained glass—including its thickness irregularities, color variations from iron and manganese impurities, and visible manufacturing striae—has become aesthetically valued precisely because it is unreproducible by modern float glass manufacturing. When conservators and craftspeople attempt to reproduce historic glass for restoration projects, they must deliberately return to pre-industrial techniques to match not just the chemistry but the visual character of the original material. The 'defects' of the manufacturing process are part of the cultural artifact. The glass-flows myth inadvertently aestheticizes what is actually a manufacturing constraint, attributing the character of the glass to geological time rather than human craft.
