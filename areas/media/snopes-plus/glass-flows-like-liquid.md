# Glass is a slow-moving liquid, which is why old windows are thicker at the bottom

## The Claim

Walk through an old European church, a colonial-era building, or any structure with genuine antique glazing, and you will likely notice something: the window panes are not uniform. They taper. The glass tends to be noticeably thicker toward the bottom of the pane and thinner at the top. For many visitors, a guide or a helpful companion will explain this apparent anomaly with what sounds like a compelling piece of physics: glass is actually a very slow-moving liquid, and over centuries, it has flowed downward under gravity, thickening at the bottom the way honey would if you let it sit for a very long time.

The claim has the feel of surprising but well-established science—the kind of counterintuitive fact that inverts an everyday assumption. We think of glass as a solid because we interact with it as one, but at a deeper physical level, the story goes, it is actually a liquid in slow motion, and the old windows are its fossil record.

This is a satisfying narrative. It connects an observable physical fact (the variation in glass thickness) to an underlying physical mechanism (the viscous flow of an amorphous material), invokes the deep time of medieval buildings, and overturns a naive assumption. It is also, in its essentials, incorrect—though the reasons why are more interesting than the simple debunking.

---

## What's Actually True

To understand why this claim fails, you need to understand three things: the physics of glass, the geology of time and viscosity, and the history of how medieval glass was made.

**What glass actually is**

Glass is an amorphous solid. The word "amorphous" is key: it means the atoms in glass are not arranged in a regular crystalline lattice the way they are in metals or minerals like quartz. Instead, the atomic structure of glass is disordered—the silicon dioxide (in the case of silicate glass) is networked in a random, irregular pattern. This is structurally similar to the arrangement of atoms in a liquid.

This similarity is the grain of truth embedded in the myth. In terms of atomic-level structure, glass does resemble a liquid more than it resembles a crystal. It lacks the long-range order that defines a crystalline solid. This has led some teachers and popular science writers to describe glass as a "supercooled liquid"—a liquid that has been cooled so quickly that it solidified before its atoms could arrange into a crystal structure.

However, the structural similarity to liquids does not mean glass *behaves* like a liquid at the temperatures and timescales relevant to human experience. The critical property here is viscosity—resistance to flow. Liquids flow because their molecules can move past each other. The viscosity of a substance determines how quickly it flows under a given stress.

**The viscosity argument, quantified**

Silicate glass at room temperature has an extraordinarily high viscosity—on the order of 10^21 Pascal-seconds, or higher. To give that number scale: water has a viscosity of about 0.001 Pa·s. Honey is roughly 10 Pa·s. Asphalt is around 10^8 Pa·s. Room-temperature glass is roughly ten trillion times more viscous than asphalt.

Edgar Zanotto, a materials scientist at the Federal University of São Carlos in Brazil, addressed this question rigorously in a 1998 paper published in the *American Journal of Physics*. He calculated that for glass at ambient temperature to show detectable flow—a shift at the atomic scale of even a single atomic diameter—the required time would exceed the current age of the universe (approximately 13.8 billion years). The flow timescale is not merely long; it is geophysically irrelevant. Medieval glass is, at most, a thousand years old. Window glass has not flowed.

This is not a matter of scientific uncertainty or ongoing debate in materials science. It is a settled calculation. Glass does not flow at room temperature on any timescale relevant to the existence of buildings, civilizations, or even geological epochs.

**Why old windows are actually thicker at the bottom**

The explanation for the tapered thickness of old glass panes is not viscous flow; it is manufacturing and installation practice.

Before the industrial age and the development of plate glass production in the nineteenth century, window glass was made primarily by two methods: crown glass and cylinder glass (also called muff glass or broad glass).

In the crown glass method, a glassblower would gather molten glass on a blowpipe, blow it into a bubble, then transfer it to an iron rod (the "punty") and spin it rapidly. The centrifugal force would cause the bubble to flatten into a disk, sometimes over a meter in diameter. The center of the disk—where the punty attached—was thicker and left a characteristic "bull's eye" bullion knot. The outer rim of the disk was the thinnest part. When the disk was cut into panes for window installation, the thickness was necessarily variable. The cutting process could not produce uniform sheets.

In the cylinder glass method, the glassblower would shape the molten glass into a long cylinder, which was then cut longitudinally and flattened on a heated surface. This method also produced glass of uneven thickness.

Both methods yielded panes that were thicker in some parts than others. Here is where the installation practice becomes decisive: medieval and early modern glaziers, exercising professional judgment, consistently installed panes with the thicker edge toward the bottom of the window frame. The reasons were practical and structural. A pane with its heavier mass lower in the frame sits more stably in the lead calms (the grooved strips of lead that hold individual panes in a leaded window). A pane installed thin-side-down was more liable to shift, work loose, or fracture under its own weight. Glaziers knew this. The orientation was intentional.

This means the pattern that observers notice—thicker glass at the bottom—reflects a deliberate craft decision made at installation, not centuries of gravitational flow. The glass was already thicker at the bottom on the day it was installed, precisely because someone put it that way.

## Why People Believe This

The myth is compelling for several overlapping reasons, and its persistence reflects genuine complexity in the underlying science.

**The amorphous solid problem**

Glass genuinely does occupy a strange position in the taxonomy of matter. The distinction between a very-high-viscosity liquid and an amorphous solid is not intuitively obvious, and the scientific language around it has historically been imprecise. Some physics and chemistry textbooks have described glass as a supercooled liquid or as a state intermediate between liquid and solid. This imprecision, even when technically defensible in a narrow structural sense, licenses the popular misconception that glass is "really" a liquid in meaningful practical terms.

When people hear a credentialed source describe glass as a supercooled liquid, they understandably update toward "glass flows." The scientific nuance—that structural amorphousness at the atomic scale says nothing about macroscopic flow rates at ambient temperatures—is not typically communicated in these brief descriptions.

**Observable physical evidence**

Unlike many myths that float free of any observable foundation, this one comes with what appears to be physical evidence. Old windows *are* thicker at the bottom. This is not imagined. The visitor to a medieval church does observe genuine variation in glass thickness. Having this observable fact presented as confirming evidence for the flow theory makes the claim feel empirically anchored. A debunker must not only explain why the mechanism is wrong but also provide an alternative explanation for the real phenomenon—which requires engaging the manufacturing history.

**Counterintuitive satisfaction**

Claims that upend common-sense categories—things that seem solid are really liquid; objects that appear stationary are actually moving—have a particular rhetorical power. They signal insider scientific knowledge, the kind of thing that separates people who have looked beneath appearances from those who trust naive perception. Sharing the glass-liquid claim feels like transmitting genuine sophistication. Its social function in conversation reinforces its circulation independent of its accuracy.

**Deep time intuition failure**

Human intuitions about time are calibrated to human lifespans, not to geological or cosmological scales. It is genuinely difficult to feel, as opposed to understand intellectually, that "a thousand years is not a long time" for a substance with viscosity on the order of 10^21 Pa·s. When someone says "glass flows slowly," the mental image generated is of something like cold syrup—slow but perceptible. The actual numbers, which put detectable flow at timescales longer than the universe's age, are outside the range of effective intuition.

## Verdict

**False.** Glass is an amorphous solid that does not flow at room temperature on any human-relevant timescale. Calculations by Zanotto (1998) establish that room-temperature glass would require longer than the age of the universe to show measurable flow. The thickness variation in old window panes is explained entirely by pre-industrial manufacturing methods (crown glass, cylinder glass) and by the deliberate glazier practice of installing panes with their thicker edge at the bottom for structural stability.

The claim contains a grain of genuine scientific observation—glass is structurally amorphous in a way that superficially resembles liquids—but this structural fact does not entail the viscous flow behavior the myth describes.

## The Wider Picture

The glass-liquid myth is a case study in what might be called the *analogy problem* in popular science communication: the technique of explaining unfamiliar scientific concepts through comparisons to familiar things can produce durable misconceptions when the analogy is partial or imprecise.

Describing glass as a supercooled liquid is a legitimate pedagogical shortcut for explaining why glass lacks a crystalline structure. But the analogy imports the implication that glass shares other properties of liquids—specifically, that it flows. The partial analogy, once lodged, generates predictions (glass flows; old windows should show evidence of this flow) that feel like natural inferences from the established science but are in fact extrapolations beyond the analogy's valid domain.

This pattern recurs throughout science communication. Electrons are described as "orbiting" atomic nuclei like planets orbiting a sun, which is a useful starting point but generates catastrophically wrong predictions about electron behavior. DNA is described as a "blueprint" for organisms, which licenses the misconception that genetic information is deterministic and context-independent. In each case, a useful simplification gets extended beyond its valid range, and the extension becomes more culturally durable than the original, carefully bounded claim.

The glass case also illustrates the evidential problem with "it must be" reasoning—the form of inference that goes from "the mechanism should produce this effect" to "this effect is therefore evidence of the mechanism." Observers see thick-bottomed panes and reason that flow must have occurred. But inference from effect to mechanism requires excluding alternative explanations. The manufacturing history was available to anyone who inquired; the glazier practice of thick-side-down installation was documented in the historical record. The myth persisted not because the evidence was unavailable but because the myth was already believed and the evidence was not sought.

For materials science specifically, the glass case has been used as a teaching example about the distinction between structural classification (amorphous vs. crystalline) and dynamic behavior (viscous flow rate, glass transition temperature, elastic modulus). A material's category membership—whether it is "really" a solid or "really" a liquid—matters less than its measurable properties under specific conditions. Glass behaves as a solid for every practical and engineering purpose because its measurable flow rate at ambient conditions is zero for all practical purposes.

The medieval manufacturing history is also worth dwelling on for its own sake. Pre-industrial glass production was a skilled craft requiring significant technical knowledge about silica sources, flux materials (potash or soda), firing temperatures, and forming techniques. Crown glass production in particular required precise coordination of blowing, thermal management, and spinning to produce disks of usable quality. The "imperfection" that the myth tries to explain by viscous flow is, from another angle, evidence of the technical constraints and practical wisdom of a pre-industrial craft tradition.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The glass-liquid claim is a relatively well-documented entry in the fact-check ecosystem, and its treatment illustrates some general principles about how to handle claims that mix genuine scientific concepts with popular overextensions.

**The two-part refutation**

Rigorous fact-check treatments of this claim characteristically have two components: refuting the mechanism and explaining the observable phenomenon. A treatment that only says "glass doesn't flow" without explaining why old windows are thicker at the bottom leaves a genuine mystery unaddressed and may actually reinforce the original myth by leaving the evidence base intact. The manufacturing history—crown glass, cylinder glass, glazier installation practice—is an essential part of the complete debunk.

**Primary source for the viscosity calculation**

Zanotto's 1998 *American Journal of Physics* paper is the standard citation for the quantitative claim about flow timescales. It is accessible to educated non-specialists and has been cited in popular fact-check treatments across multiple outlets including *Scientific American*, the *Corning Museum of Glass*, and physics education resources. Fact-checkers working this topic should engage with it rather than relying on secondary popularizations.

**Expert sourcing**

The claim sits at the intersection of materials science (glass viscosity, amorphous solid classification) and history of technology (pre-industrial glass manufacturing). Rigorous treatments source from both domains. Materials scientists can speak to the physics; historians of technology and glassmaking historians can document the manufacturing practices. Treating this as purely a physics question misses the manufacturing explanation; treating it as purely historical misses the need to rebut the physical mechanism.

**Handling the "amorphous solid" nuance**

Careful fact-checkers acknowledge the legitimate complexity: glass is genuinely unusual in its atomic structure, the liquid/solid boundary is not as sharp as everyday language implies, and the supercooled-liquid description is not without basis. Acknowledging this complexity while still being clear about what it does not imply—macroscopic flow at room temperature—models the kind of honest engagement with scientific nuance that distinguishes good fact-checking from simple debunking. The goal is not to make the reader feel foolish for having heard a claim; it is to give them a more accurate and complete picture than the myth provided.

**The glass transition temperature**

A complete treatment of the glass-liquid boundary also introduces the glass transition temperature (Tg)—the temperature above which glass behaves more like a viscous liquid and below which it behaves as a rigid amorphous solid. For silicate window glass, this transition occurs at approximately 550–600°C (1020–1110°F). At temperatures above Tg, glass can indeed be shaped, blown, and manipulated as a viscous material—this is the working regime of all glassblowing. Below Tg, the material is in every practical sense a solid.

The glass transition concept is important for rigorous fact-check treatments because it explains why the "glass is a liquid" formulation has *some* physical basis at high temperatures: above Tg, glass flows readily, which is why glassblowers can work it. The myth takes this high-temperature behavior and incorrectly extrapolates it to room temperature, where glass is orders of magnitude stiffer and where any flow is physically negligible.

Understanding the glass transition also explains why the viscosity figures cited above are so extreme at room temperature. Viscosity in amorphous materials changes exponentially with temperature. Moving from the glassblowing range (~1200°C) to room temperature (~20°C) spans a viscosity range of roughly twenty orders of magnitude—from molten glass that flows as easily as honey to a material so rigid that detectable flow requires timescales that exceed the age of the universe.

**Sourcing the manufacturing history**

The manufacturing history—crown glass, cylinder glass, and glazier installation practice—is documented in the history of technology literature. Fact-checkers who wish to source this dimension of the debunk should consult the historical literature on pre-industrial glassmaking rather than relying solely on physics sources. The two lines of evidence—the materials science establishing that flow did not occur, and the manufacturing history explaining what actually caused the thickness variation—are both necessary and independently sourced. The claim is fully resolved only when both are addressed.
