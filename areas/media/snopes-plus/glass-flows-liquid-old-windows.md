# Glass Is a Slow-Flowing Liquid, Proven by Thicker Bottoms in Old Windows

## §1. The Claim

Walk into any medieval cathedral in Europe — Chartres, Canterbury, Cologne — and run your fingers along the edge of an original window. You will find that many of the oldest panes are measurably thicker at the bottom than at the top. The glass is not uniform. It tapers. And for generations, science teachers, tour guides, and dinner-party raconteurs have invoked this observation as proof of an extraordinary proposition: glass is not really a solid. It is a liquid — an incredibly viscous one, to be sure — but a liquid nonetheless, and over the centuries it has been slowly, imperceptibly flowing downward under the pull of gravity, pooling at the bottom of the pane like honey sliding down the inner wall of a jar.

The claim is seductive because it begins with something you can verify with your own hands. The uneven thickness of old windowpanes is not imaginary. It is not a trick of the light. It is a real, repeatable, physical observation. And from that undeniable foundation, the myth constructs an elegant explanatory bridge: the glass *was* uniform when it was installed; it *became* thicker at the bottom because it flowed. The passage of centuries — five hundred years, seven hundred years, a thousand — supplies the missing timescale. The everyday intuition that glass is rigid gets reframed as a failure of human temporal perception: we simply cannot observe a process that unfolds over geological patience.

This is what makes the "old windows" variant of the glass-liquid myth so much more persistent and persuasive than the naked claim that "glass is a liquid." The abstract version can be debated on definitional grounds — what do we even mean by "liquid"? — and dismissed as a semantic curiosity. But the old-windows version comes armed with *evidence*. It converts a theoretical oddity into an empirical narrative. It says: here is the proof, right in front of you, embedded in the walls of Europe's greatest buildings. Touch it. Measure it. The evidence is five centuries old, and it has been patiently accumulating all that time.

This is the epistemological heart of the matter. The claim does not merely assert that glass is a liquid. It asserts that the visual record of medieval architecture *proves* it. And it is this evidentiary architecture — the way a genuine observation is recruited as the load-bearing pillar of a false causal story — that makes the myth worth examining in detail. The observation is real. The explanation is completely wrong.

## §2. What's Actually True

The thicker bottoms of medieval windowpanes have nothing to do with gravitational flow. They are artifacts of pre-industrial glassmaking techniques — specifically the *crown glass* and *cylinder glass* (also called *broad sheet*) methods that dominated European glass production from the medieval period through the early nineteenth century.

**Crown glass** was the dominant technique in England and France from roughly the fourteenth through the eighteenth century. A glassblower gathered a mass of molten glass on the end of a blowpipe, inflated it into a hollow sphere, then transferred it to a solid iron rod called a *pontil*. With the sphere attached to the pontil, the blower rapidly spun the assembly, and centrifugal force flattened the sphere into a large, roughly circular disc — the "crown" or "table." The centre of the disc, where the pontil was attached, retained a thick lump of glass called the *bullseye* or *bull's-eye*. The rest of the disc was thinnest at the outer rim and gradually thicker toward the centre. Panes were cut from this disc, and because the disc had a natural gradient of thickness from centre to edge, no two panes were perfectly uniform (Davison, S., 2003, *Conservation and Restoration of Glass*, 2nd ed., Butterworth-Heinemann).

**Cylinder glass** (or **broad sheet glass**), which was more prevalent in continental Europe and eventually superseded crown glass in England, involved blowing the molten glass into an elongated cylinder, cutting off the ends, slitting the cylinder lengthwise, and then reheating and flattening it into a sheet. This method produced somewhat more uniform panes than crown glass, but the flattening process still left variations in thickness, particularly near the edges of the original cylinder where the glass was naturally thicker (Cable, M., 1999, "Mechanization of Glass Manufacture," *Journal of the American Ceramic Society*, 82(5), 1093–1112).

In both methods, the resulting panes had one edge noticeably thicker than the other. When a glazier set these panes into leading (the lead came strips that hold stained-glass panels together) or into wooden frames, practical wisdom dictated placing the thicker, heavier edge at the bottom for structural stability. The thicker edge made a more secure seat in the leading, and gravity would work with the mounting rather than against it. This was a sensible craft decision — not a law of physics unfolding over centuries.

Critically, not all old windowpanes are thicker at the bottom. Surveys of medieval windows have found panes installed with the thicker edge at the top, at the sides, or oriented apparently at random (Zanotto, E.D., 1998, "Do Cathedral Glasses Flow?", *American Journal of Physics*, 66(5), 392–395). If gravitational flow were the explanation, *every* old pane should be thicker at the bottom, and the degree of thickening should correlate with the age of the window and the temperature history of the building. Neither correlation holds. The thickness variations are distributed in patterns consistent with random or craft-directed installation, not with a uniform physical process operating over time.

Snopes is correct: the claim is false. The evidence — properly interrogated — actually refutes the gravitational-flow hypothesis rather than supporting it.

## §3. Why People Believe This

### The Anchor of Visible Evidence

The persistence of this myth illuminates a specific cognitive phenomenon that deserves a name more precise than "confirmation bias," though confirmation bias is certainly part of the machinery. The phenomenon is **observational anchoring in causal inference**: a genuine, verifiable observation becomes the epistemic anchor for an explanation, and the vividness and tangibility of the observation insulates the explanation from scrutiny.

The psychologist Daniel Kahneman described a related process in the context of *System 1* thinking — the fast, intuitive, pattern-matching mode of cognition that generates causal stories almost involuntarily from perceptual inputs (Kahneman, D., 2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Farrar, Straus and Giroux). When you see a windowpane that is thick at the bottom and thin at the top, System 1 immediately volunteers a narrative: something heavy settled downward. The narrative is so natural, so consistent with everyday experience of viscous fluids, that it arrives feeling like an insight rather than a hypothesis. And once it has arrived, the observation — the undeniable, touchable, measurable observation — serves as its anchor. To question the explanation feels like questioning the evidence, which would be absurd: you can *see* the thickness gradient. You can *feel* it.

This is the epistemic trap. The observation is real. The causal story is wrong. But the cognitive architecture that links them makes the story feel as real as the observation.

### Confirmation Bias in Folk Physics

Confirmation bias amplifies the effect. Once a person has heard the glass-is-a-liquid explanation, they begin to *look for* thick-bottomed windows and *find* them — because, as we have established, many old windows genuinely are thicker at the bottom, due to glazier installation practice. Each confirming instance reinforces the causal story. Disconfirming instances — windows that are thicker at the top, or old glass objects (bottles, lenses, ornaments) that show no flow at all — are either unnoticed or explained away as exceptions.

The physicist Robert Doremus noted this phenomenon with some exasperation in his textbook on glass science: "The notion that glass flows at room temperature is widely repeated despite a complete absence of supporting evidence. The viscosity of glass at room temperature is so high that measurable flow would require timescales many orders of magnitude longer than the age of the universe" (Doremus, R.H., 1994, *Glass Science*, 2nd ed., Wiley, p. 101). Doremus was pointing not just at the physics but at the sociology — the way a vivid, intuitive claim propagates through classrooms and popular science writing despite being flatly contradicted by quantitative analysis.

### The Legitimate Ambiguity: What *Is* Glass?

Part of the myth's durability comes from a grain of genuine scientific nuance. Glass is technically an *amorphous solid* — a material that lacks the long-range crystalline order characteristic of crystalline solids like quartz, diamond, or table salt. When molten glass cools, it does not undergo a sharp phase transition at a well-defined melting point. Instead, it passes through a *glass transition* region, gradually becoming more viscous until it behaves as a rigid solid, without ever developing the periodic atomic lattice that defines a crystal.

This genuinely unusual material state — sometimes described as a "frozen liquid" or "supercooled liquid" in older textbooks — invites the inference that glass retains some liquid-like properties, including the ability to flow. And indeed, at the thermodynamic level, the distinction between a glass and an extremely viscous liquid is a subject of ongoing (if largely resolved) scientific discussion. The physicist Philip Anderson quipped that the glass transition is "the deepest and most interesting unsolved problem in solid state theory" (Anderson, P.W., 1995, "Through the Glass Lightly," *Science*, 267(5204), 1615–1616).

But the critical quantitative point is this: the viscosity of soda-lime glass at room temperature is approximately 10²¹ Pa·s — that is, one *sextillion* pascal-seconds (Zanotto, E.D. & Gupta, P.K., 1999, "Do Cathedral Glasses Flow?", *American Journal of Physics*, 67(3), 260–262). For comparison, the viscosity of water at room temperature is approximately 10⁻³ Pa·s, and the viscosity of honey is roughly 2–10 Pa·s. The viscosity ratio between glass and water is therefore on the order of 10²⁴ — a trillion trillion. At this viscosity, Zanotto and Gupta calculated that it would take longer than the age of the universe — far longer, by many orders of magnitude — for a pane of cathedral glass to show any measurable change in thickness due to gravitational flow. Their paper, published in the *American Journal of Physics*, is the definitive quantitative refutation of the flow myth: the numbers are not even close. Medieval cathedrals are impressive, but they are not old enough by a factor of roughly 10²⁰.

So the legitimate ambiguity — glass is structurally disordered, like a liquid — is real. But the specific empirical claim — glass flows detectably at room temperature over centuries — is not just false; it is false by a preposterously large margin. The ambiguity in classification licenses a qualitative intuition ("glass is like a liquid") that the quantitative physics utterly destroys ("glass does not flow like a liquid under any conditions relevant to human experience").

## §4. Verdict

**Propositional status:** False.

**Confidence:** Very High.

The observational component of the claim is genuine: many medieval windowpanes are indeed thicker at the bottom than at the top. This is a real, measurable physical fact. But the causal inference drawn from this observation — that the thickness gradient results from centuries of viscous gravitational flow — is comprehensively refuted by:

1. **Manufacturing history.** Crown glass and cylinder glass techniques inherently produced non-uniform panes. The thickness variation was present from the moment of manufacture (Davison, 2003; Cable, 1999).

2. **Installation practice.** Glaziers routinely placed the thicker edge downward for structural stability. The orientation of the thickness gradient is a craft decision, not a physical process (Zanotto, 1998).

3. **Counterexamples.** Not all old panes are thicker at the bottom. Some are thicker at the top or sides, which is inconsistent with a uniform gravitational-flow mechanism.

4. **Quantitative physics.** The viscosity of glass at room temperature (~10²¹ Pa·s) is so astronomically high that measurable flow would require timescales exceeding the age of the universe by many orders of magnitude (Zanotto & Gupta, 1999; Doremus, 1994).

The claim is false. The observation is real. The explanation is wrong.

## §5. The Wider Picture

### Pre-Industrial Glassmaking in Europe

The history of European glassmaking is a history of ingenuity constrained by the limits of manual craft. Glass production in Europe descended from Roman techniques, which were themselves inherited from earlier traditions in Egypt, Mesopotamia, and the Levant. The earliest known glass objects — small beads and vessels — date to approximately 3,500 BCE in Mesopotamia and Egypt (Shortland, A.J., 2012, *Lapis Lazuli from the Kiln: Glass and Glassmaking in the Late Bronze Age*, Leuven University Press). Importantly for our purposes, ancient Egyptian and Roman glass objects that are three to four thousand years old show no measurable flow or deformation attributable to viscous creep at ambient temperatures. If glass flowed perceptibly over centuries, these objects — far older than any medieval window — should show dramatic distortion. They do not.

The crown glass method emerged in Europe during the medieval period and reached its highest refinement in England and Normandy. The technique required extraordinary skill: the glassblower had to judge the temperature of the molten mass by colour alone, control the inflation of the bubble with breath pressure calibrated by experience, and spin the disc at precisely the right speed to achieve a reasonably flat table. The resulting discs ranged from roughly 1.2 to 1.5 metres in diameter. Panes were cut from the disc in rectangular pieces, with the most uniform (and therefore most expensive) panes coming from the middle zone — neither too close to the thick centre bullseye nor too close to the thin, often irregular edge (Dungworth, D. & Clark, C., 2004, "The Conservation of Crown Glass," *Historic England Research Reports*).

The cylinder glass method, also called *muff glass* or *broad sheet*, involved a different geometry but the same fundamental limitation: manual production could not achieve the uniform thickness that modern float glass takes for granted. The blown cylinder was typically 30–45 cm in diameter and up to 1.5 metres long. After cutting and flattening, sheets exhibited thickness variations of 1–3 mm across a single pane — more than enough to be noticeable by eye and touch, and more than enough to serve as the observational anchor for the flow myth (Cable, 1999).

### The Float Glass Revolution

The story of how glass *became* uniform is itself remarkable. In 1952, Sir Alastair Pilkington of Pilkington Brothers Ltd. in St Helens, Lancashire, conceived the idea of floating molten glass on a bath of molten tin to produce perfectly flat, uniformly thick sheets. After seven years and £7 million of development (roughly £180 million in 2026 terms), the float glass process was announced in 1959 and commercialised in the early 1960s (Pilkington, L.A.B., 1969, "Review Lecture: The Float Glass Process," *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London A*, 314(1516), 1–25). Today, virtually all flat glass in the world is made by the float process. Modern float glass is so uniform that its thickness varies by less than 0.1 mm across a standard sheet.

The float glass revolution means that the observational foundation of the myth — thick-bottomed windows — is a specifically *historical* phenomenon. Modern windows do not exhibit thickness gradients, and they will not develop them over time, because the glass is uniform to begin with and the flow hypothesis is physically impossible at room temperature. In a sense, the myth is self-limiting: as pre-industrial windows continue to be replaced or supplemented with modern glass, the observational anchor will gradually disappear from everyday experience.

### How the Myth Spread Through Classrooms

Perhaps the most troubling aspect of the glass-flow myth is the role of science education in propagating it. For decades, the claim appeared in chemistry and physics textbooks as an illustration of the amorphous nature of glass. It was a vivid, counterintuitive, tangible example — exactly the kind of memorable anecdote that makes good pedagogy. The problem was that it was wrong.

The myth served a legitimate pedagogical purpose: it drew attention to the genuinely unusual material properties of glasses and the distinction between amorphous and crystalline solids. But it did so by anchoring that lesson in a false empirical claim, creating a situation where students learned the right concept (glass is an amorphous solid, structurally distinct from crystalline solids) through the wrong evidence (glass flows in windows). When the myth was eventually debunked — largely through Zanotto's papers in the 1990s and subsequent popular-science coverage — the pedagogical damage was twofold. First, students who had learned the flow story had to unlearn it. Second, the debunking risked throwing out the genuine insight (glass is structurally interesting) along with the false claim (glass flows in windows).

This pattern — a real concept taught through a false example, followed by a debunking that undermines confidence in the concept itself — is a recurring failure mode in science education. It illustrates why empirical accuracy in pedagogical examples matters, even when the conceptual point being illustrated is correct (Hammer, D., 1996, "More than Misconceptions: Multiple Perspectives on Student Knowledge and Reasoning, and an Appropriate Role for Education Research," *American Journal of Physics*, 64(10), 1316–1325).

### What Archaeological Glass Tells Us

The most powerful empirical refutation of the glass-flow myth comes not from medieval windows but from archaeological glass. The British Museum, the Louvre, the Metropolitan Museum of Art, and dozens of other institutions hold glass objects — vessels, beads, amulets, decorative panels — that are two, three, and even four thousand years old. Roman glass vessels from the first century CE are routinely recovered from archaeological sites across the former empire. Egyptian glass beads dating to the mid-second millennium BCE have been found in contexts from the eastern Mediterranean to northern Europe.

None of these objects show measurable gravitational flow. Roman glass vessels that have sat on shelves (or in the ground) for two millennia retain their original shapes, wall thicknesses, and decorative details. If glass at room temperature flowed at a rate sufficient to produce visible thickness changes in a few centuries, glass objects several times older should be dramatically deformed. They are not. The archaeological record alone is sufficient to falsify the cathedral-glass flow hypothesis — and it does so on a timescale four to eight times longer than even the oldest European cathedral windows (Brill, R.H., 1999, *Chemical Analyses of Early Glasses*, Corning Museum of Glass).

## §6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's treatment of the glass-flow claim follows the standard structure of binary fact-checking: present the claim, evaluate the evidence, issue a verdict. In this case, the verdict — False — is unambiguously correct. Snopes correctly identifies the manufacturing explanation for uneven window thickness, cites the relevant physics, and debunks the flow claim. The fact-check performs its primary function well: anyone who Googles the claim will find a clear, authoritative statement that it is false.

The limitation of the binary verdict, however, is that it does not fully capture what makes this myth epistemologically interesting. The claim is false, but it is not false in the same way as, say, "the Great Wall of China is visible from space" (a claim with no observational anchor at all). The glass-flow myth is false *despite* the presence of real supporting evidence — evidence that genuinely exists and genuinely requires explanation. The binary verdict "False" does not distinguish between claims that are fabricated whole cloth and claims that are genuine observations attached to false causal narratives.

This is not a criticism of Snopes's methodology so much as a recognition of its inherent constraints. Binary fact-checking is optimised for the question "Should I believe this?" and the answer, in this case, is clearly "No." But the more nuanced question — "What should I do with the real observation that anchors this false claim?" — falls outside the format's scope. The observation of uneven window thickness is not debunked by the verdict. It remains a genuine feature of medieval architecture that deserves a true explanation. The binary format risks leaving readers with the impression that the *observation* was wrong, when in fact only the *explanation* was wrong.

The best fact-checking on this topic — and Snopes does this reasonably well — provides the correct explanation (manufacturing techniques, glazier practice) alongside the debunking. This transforms the fact-check from a mere negation ("this is false") into an educational act ("this is false, and here is what is actually going on"). The glass-flow myth, precisely because it involves a real observation and a false causal chain, is an ideal candidate for this richer form of fact-checking — one that does not merely adjudicate truth and falsehood but illuminates the epistemic processes by which real evidence generates false beliefs.

The deeper lesson, for fact-checkers and for all of us, is that observational evidence is never self-interpreting. An observation is a datum, not an explanation. The thicker bottoms of medieval windows are a datum. The story about viscous flow is an explanation — and in this case, a spectacularly wrong one. The gap between observation and explanation is the space where myths are born, and it is the space where careful thinking, quantitative analysis, and historical knowledge do their most important work.
