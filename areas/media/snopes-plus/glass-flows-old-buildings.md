# Old Window Glass Is Thicker at the Bottom Because Glass Flows

## 1. The Claim

Walk into any medieval cathedral, any Georgian townhouse, or any antique shop in Europe, and you can see it plainly: the panes of old glass are thicker at the bottom than the top. The explanation that circulates with almost ritual confidence goes like this — glass is technically a liquid, a supercooled liquid that never fully solidifies, and over centuries it slowly flows downward under the pull of gravity, accumulating at the base of each windowpane. The thicker bottom is the accumulated glass of centuries, creeping imperceptibly downward.

This story has the feel of hidden depth. It flatters the teller with a secret they know about the nature of matter, one invisible to casual observation. It has been repeated in science classrooms, in popular-science writing, in documentaries, and in the confident asides of tour guides who have never been corrected. It is also, in its essential claims, wrong — though the correction requires some care, because the observable phenomenon it purports to explain is entirely real.

## 2. What's Actually True

The thickness variation in old window glass is real. The explanation is not glass flow. It is glass manufacturing.

Before modern float glass production, the dominant method for producing flat window glass in Europe was the crown glass process. A glassblower would gather molten glass on a blowpipe, inflate it into a hollow sphere, transfer it to a solid iron rod called a pontil, and then spin it rapidly. Centrifugal force would cause the rotating sphere to open into a flat disc, typically 90 to 150 centimeters in diameter. This disc — the crown — was not uniform in thickness. The center bore a characteristic bull's-eye thickening where the pontil had been attached, and the edges were thinner and smoother. More relevantly: the disc was not perfectly flat across its surface, and its thickness varied depending on the spinning dynamics.

When the disc was cut into rectangular panes for installation, the resulting pieces were not uniform in thickness. Glaziers working with these panes understood this perfectly well. They had a practical incentive to install them with the thicker edge at the bottom: a heavier base makes the pane more stable in its lead cames or wooden frame, and the thicker edge is also the edge less likely to crack from thermal expansion and the small but real mechanical pressures of the frame. The installation convention of thick-edge-down was a craftsman's choice, not the outcome of centuries of slow viscoelastic deformation.

This explanation was definitively clarified in the scientific literature by Edgar Zanotto, a glass scientist at the Federal University of São Carlos in Brazil, in a 1998 paper published in the *American Journal of Physics*. Zanotto's analysis addressed the viscosity of glass at ambient temperatures and the rates of flow that viscosity would permit. His finding was that at room temperature, glass is so viscous — so enormously resistant to flow — that the time required for a pane of glass to show measurable flow-driven thickness redistribution would be on the order of 10^32 years. The current age of the universe is approximately 1.38 × 10^10 years. To put this in perspective, the universe would need to be roughly a hundred billion trillion times older than it actually is before a windowpane could flow visibly under gravity at room temperature.

The "glass is a liquid" claim that undergirds the flowing-window myth is itself a simplification that requires unpacking. Glass is classified by materials scientists as an amorphous solid — sometimes called a supercooled liquid in technical contexts, but this designation refers to its structural disorder at the atomic level, not to any practical tendency to flow. Unlike crystalline solids, glass lacks the long-range periodic atomic order that characterizes materials like quartz or calcite. Instead, its atoms are frozen into a disordered arrangement, analogous to the arrangement in a liquid — hence "supercooled liquid." But this structural similarity to a liquid does not translate into liquid-like behavior at the macroscopic level. The glass transition temperature for common soda-lime glass (the kind used in windows) is approximately 520 to 600 degrees Celsius. Below that temperature, and certainly at the ambient temperatures of European buildings, the material behaves as a solid with extraordinary rigidity.

### Glass Chemistry and the Silica Network

To understand why room-temperature glass flow is so impossible, it helps to know something about the atomic structure of silicate glass. Window glass is primarily soda-lime glass, composed of roughly 70–75% silicon dioxide (SiO₂), 15% sodium oxide (Na₂O), and 10% calcium oxide (CaO), with small additions of other oxides. The sodium and calcium ions interrupt the continuous SiO₂ network and lower the melting point to a workable range, but the fundamental mechanical properties of the glass are dominated by the silica network.

In crystalline quartz (pure SiO₂), silicon and oxygen atoms are arranged in a perfect long-range periodic lattice. In glass, the same atoms form a network of SiO₄ tetrahedra that are linked at their corners — but without the long-range periodicity. The network is disordered, forming a three-dimensional tangle. This network is extremely stiff. The Si-O bond is one of the strongest bonds in common inorganic chemistry, with a bond dissociation energy of approximately 800 kJ/mol. To make glass flow, these bonds do not need to be broken — flow is a matter of bonds rearranging — but the energy barriers to rearrangement at room temperature are enormous.

Viscosity is the macroscopic expression of these energy barriers. At 1400°C (well above the melting point of soda-lime glass), the viscosity is around 10 Pa·s — roughly the viscosity of honey at room temperature. This is a workable viscosity for glass-blowing. At 600°C (near the glass transition), viscosity is around 10^12 Pa·s — the glass is a rigid solid to any practical definition. At 20°C (room temperature), viscosity is approximately 10^40 Pa·s. This is not a number that corresponds to any physical substance in everyday experience. It is a theoretical extrapolation derived from the Arrhenius equation and confirmed by various indirect measurements. It means that at room temperature, glass is not merely stiff — it is, on any human or geological timescale, absolutely immobile.

### The Crown Glass Process in Detail

The crown glass manufacturing process that produced the irregular panes in historic buildings is genuinely interesting, and understanding it makes the non-flow explanation obvious.

The process begins with a gather of molten glass on a blowpipe. The glassblower inflates a bubble, transfers it to a pontil rod (a solid iron rod attached to what will become the center of the disc), and reheats it in the furnace. The critical step is spinning: the pontil is spun rapidly, and the softened glass — driven by centrifugal force — opens outward into a flat disc. The characteristic "bull's-eye" visible at the center of some old panes is the thickened region where the pontil was attached. The disc could reach 90 to 150 cm in diameter, with thickness varying from the center out to the rim.

Because the disc formed by centrifugal dynamics rather than precision mechanical shaping, no two discs were identical, and the thickness profile varied across any given disc. When cut into panes, the results were irregular in thickness. This irregularity was a known property of crown glass — it was priced accordingly, and the better, more uniform pieces cut from the outer rim commanded higher prices than the bull's-eye center pieces, which were used in cheaper installations.

The glazier handling these variable-thickness panes had both practical and aesthetic reasons to orient them thick-edge-down. Practically, a bottom-heavy pane is more stable in its glazing frame and less prone to working loose over time. Aesthetically, the slight distortion that came with variable thickness was less visually disruptive when oriented consistently. This convention was so standard that it has been documented in historical glazing manuals and in examination of surviving medieval windows.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of the glass-flows explanation depends on several converging factors, each of which contributes to the story's stickiness.

**The observable phenomenon is real.** Old windows genuinely are thicker at the bottom. There is something there to explain, and people encounter it in settings — medieval churches, old farmhouses, heritage sites — that are already charged with a sense of historical depth and mystery. The observation is not in dispute.

**The "glass is a liquid" claim has genuine scientific resonance.** Materials scientists do sometimes describe glass as an amorphous solid or a supercooled liquid, and these terms are accurate in carefully specified senses. But when a non-specialist hears "glass is technically a liquid," the implication that it flows, slowly but inexorably, seems to follow naturally. The technical precision of the statement is lost in transmission.

**The myth flatters curiosity.** Stories that reveal a hidden truth about ordinary objects — that glass is secretly flowing, that your coffee table is very slowly collapsing, that the floor under your feet is technically moving — appeal to a particular kind of scientific curiosity. They make the everyday world strange in a satisfying way. This is a narrative pleasure, and it works against correction.

**The explanation is never visually falsifiable in real time.** You cannot watch glass failing to flow. You cannot see the manufacturing history of an old pane from outside. The absence of flow over any human timescale is itself consistent with the myth, since the story already stipulates that the flow is imperceptible and requires centuries. This makes it hard to challenge intuitively.

**The glazier's convention is less satisfying.** "Glaziers put the thick edge down for stability" is a perfectly good explanation, but it has none of the metaphysical frisson of "glass is secretly a liquid that has been slowly flowing for five hundred years." The true explanation is mundane compared to the myth, and mundane explanations often lose in popular circulation.

**Scientific education has sometimes inadvertently reinforced it.** Physics courses that introduce the concept of glass as an amorphous solid sometimes add the gloss that "old cathedral windows are thicker at the bottom because glass flows," treating it as an interesting application of the material science being taught. This pedagogical mistake has propagated through generations of students who correctly remember the teacher's example but not the underlying correction.

## 4. Verdict

**Explanation** — the claim is an overreach from a real observation. Old windows are genuinely thicker at the bottom, but the cause is manufacturing variation in crown glass, not flow. Edgar Zanotto's 1998 calculation places the timescale of room-temperature glass flow at approximately 10^32 years — many orders of magnitude beyond the age of the universe. The "glass is a liquid" framing is a technical descriptor for atomic-level structural disorder, not a prediction of macroscopic flow behavior. Glaziers installed crown glass thick-edge-down as a practical craftsman's convention. The myth is a distortion of real observations and real but misapplied scientific concepts.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The glass-flows story is an instructive case in the sociology of scientific myth. It illustrates how a genuine scientific fact — that glass has an amorphous, non-crystalline atomic structure — can be transmuted through imprecise popularization into a false empirical claim. The journey from "glass is structurally disordered at the atomic level" to "old windows are thicker at the bottom because glass flows" involves at least three acts of distortion: the atomic structural claim is translated into macroscopic behavior, the macroscopic behavior is attributed to an impossibly slow but real process, and the real cause (manufacturing) is displaced.

This pattern — accurate science, imprecise popularization, empirically false conclusion — appears across many persistent scientific myths. The brain-use myth (we only use 10% of our brains) follows a similar path from real neurological observations about energy efficiency. The tongue-map myth (taste regions are separately localized) emerged from a mistranslation of a real 1901 paper. In each case, the original science was not invented — it was distorted.

The glass example is also valuable because it demonstrates the concept of viscosity at a human scale. Glass at room temperature is not merely very viscous — it is viscous beyond any practical human reckoning. The viscosity of silica glass at 25°C is approximately 10^40 Pascal-seconds. For comparison, the viscosity of water is about 10^-3 Pascal-seconds. Room-temperature glass is approximately 43 orders of magnitude more viscous than water. At these viscosities, the Newtonian concept of slow flow under gravity becomes physically meaningless on any timescale that touches human experience, geological history, or cosmological time.

The crown glass manufacturing process itself is worth knowing independently of the myth it corrects. Crown glass produced the characteristic bull's-eye panes still visible in some heritage buildings — the central disc where the pontil was attached, left intentionally as a decorative feature in cheaper glazing. The process was supplanted in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries by the cylinder (broad glass) process, which produced more uniform sheets by blowing a cylinder, cutting it lengthwise, and flattening it in a kiln. Modern float glass, developed by Sir Alastair Pilkington in the 1950s and commercialized from 1959, produces panes of near-perfect uniform thickness by floating molten glass on a bath of molten tin. Old glass is not just old — it is glass made by a different industrial process with different thickness profiles, and its installation was governed by craftsman conventions now entirely forgotten.

There is a related question that sometimes enters the conversation: does medieval glass flow on the scale of decades, rather than millennia? The answer is no. Research on medieval glass panes measured with precision instruments has shown no systematic correlation between glass age and degree of thickness variation. Some old panes are thicker at the top; others vary horizontally. The pattern is consistent with random installation of variable-thickness crown glass, not with systematic downward flow. This empirical finding — that old glass does not uniformly concentrate thickness at the bottom — is itself strong evidence against the flow hypothesis, and it is largely what motivated Zanotto to calculate the theoretical flow rates in the first place.

The glass-flows myth also has implications for heritage conservation. If conservators believed that glass flows, they might hesitate to rotate old panes to reduce stress, or might interpret directional thickness variation as diagnostic of age. The correct understanding — that thickness variation is a manufacturing artifact, not a flow product — allows conservators to treat old glass on its actual material properties rather than on a false model. This has practical consequences for the preservation of medieval glazing in churches and civic buildings across Europe.

Finally, the myth illustrates something important about the relationship between metaphor and science. "Glass is a liquid" began as a precise technical metaphor describing atomic-level structure. It escaped its technical context, acquired a literal meaning it was never meant to carry, and produced a cascade of false inferences. This is not an unusual story. Technical terms routinely escape into popular discourse carrying meanings that their originators would not recognize. The corrective is not to avoid metaphor — science cannot communicate without it — but to police the boundary between the metaphor and the literal claim, and to correct quickly when the boundary is breached.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The glass-flows myth is unusual among popular science misconceptions in that it has been clearly and quantitatively refuted in the peer-reviewed literature. Zanotto's 1998 *American Journal of Physics* paper provides the definitive scientific correction, and several subsequent papers — including work by Zanotto and Gupta (1999) — have reinforced and extended the analysis.

Standard fact-checking sites have addressed variants of this claim with ratings ranging from "false" to "mixture" (the mixture rating accounting for the true observation that old windows are indeed thicker at the bottom, with the cause being the point of contention). The consensus among fact-checkers is that the flow explanation is incorrect and the manufacturing-plus-installation explanation is correct.

The myth is also addressed in materials science pedagogy as a teaching case for the distinction between structural amorphousness and macroscopic flow, and for the concept of viscosity at extreme values. The Zanotto paper is now a standard citation in introductory glass science courses, and it has been cited in popular-science explanations on outlets ranging from Physics Today to Corning's own public communications about glass science.

One persistent variation of the myth concedes the manufacturing point but argues that the two factors (manufacturing variation and slow flow) both contribute. This variant is also false: given the calculated flow timescales, the manufacturing variation accounts for 100% of the observed thickness distribution, with flow contributing a contribution so small it cannot in principle be measured with any instrument available to science.

The lesson is not that glass is more boring than the myth suggests. Glass is genuinely extraordinary — its amorphous atomic structure, its optical properties, its thermal behavior, its role in the development of telescopes, microscopes, and fiber optics, its central place in the development of chemistry and astronomy are all remarkable. The flowing-window story does not add to this richness. It replaces a real and interesting history of glassmaking craft with a false claim about physics. The real story — about crown glass, pontil marks, the decision of an anonymous medieval glazier to set the heavy edge down — is more interesting than the myth, once you know how to see it.
