# Glass Is a Liquid That Flows Very Slowly Over Time

## The Claim

Glass, the transparent material found in windows, vessels, and lenses throughout human history, is not actually a solid in the conventional sense. It is a liquid — specifically a supercooled liquid — that has not yet finished settling into its equilibrium crystalline state. Given enough time, glass flows under the influence of gravity, and the evidence is visible in old cathedral windows, which are measurably thicker at the bottom than the top because the glass has slowly oozed downward over centuries.

This claim is popular precisely because it sounds counterintuitive in a satisfying way. Solid-seeming glass is secretly a liquid. The ancient windows you can see in European cathedrals are themselves a slow-motion demonstration of fluid dynamics playing out across centuries. The claim invites people to see the familiar as strange and to suspect that the apparent solidity of everyday objects conceals hidden mobility.

---

## What's Actually True

Glass at room temperature does not flow on any timescale relevant to human experience, to recorded history, or to the lifespan of the universe. The claim that it is a liquid slowly flowing is false in its practical form, and the evidence usually cited for it — the thickness variation in old windows — has a completely different and well-documented explanation.

**The thermodynamic state of glass.** Glass is an amorphous solid. It lacks the long-range crystalline order of materials like ice or iron, which arrange their atoms in repeating periodic lattices. Instead, glass has a disordered, network-like atomic structure that is frozen in place. This is what makes glass technically interesting to materials scientists: it occupies a state that doesn't fit neatly into the solid/liquid binary, because it forms by cooling from a melt without undergoing the ordered crystallization that produces conventional solids.

Some descriptions of glass call it a "supercooled liquid" — meaning a material that has been cooled below the temperature at which it would normally crystallize, without actually crystallizing. This description captures something real about glass's thermodynamic status, but it is badly misleading when taken to imply that glass behaves like a liquid in any practical sense. The viscosity of glass at room temperature is astronomically high — on the order of 10²⁰ to 10²⁴ Pascal-seconds, compared to water's approximately 10⁻³ Pascal-seconds. At room temperature, glass is so viscous that it is immobile for all practical purposes.

**The Zanotto and Gupta calculation.** The most rigorous demolition of the glass-flows myth comes from a 1999 paper by Edgar Zanotto and Prabhat Gupta published in the *American Journal of Physics*. Zanotto and Gupta calculated the timescale on which different types of glass would show measurable flow at typical room temperatures. Their conclusion: window glass (soda-lime silicate glass) would take on the order of 10³² years to show any flow perceptible to measurement. The universe is approximately 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years old. By any reasonable physical standard, glass at room temperature is not flowing, and the suggestion that centuries-old windows have flowed is not supported by physics.

**Why old windows are thicker at the bottom.** The actual explanation for the uneven thickness of medieval cathedral glass has nothing to do with flow and everything to do with how glass was manufactured and installed. The dominant technique for making flat glass in medieval Europe was the crown glass process: a glassblower would gather molten glass on a blowpipe, spin it rapidly to flatten it into a disc using centrifugal force, and then allow it to cool. The resulting disc was thicker toward its outer edges (where centrifugal force had pushed more glass) and thinner toward the center. This meant that panes cut from crown glass discs were inherently uneven in thickness.

When glaziers installed these panes, they routinely placed the thicker edge downward. This was sensible practice: a thicker base is more stable and better suited to bear the weight of the pane above it. The result — thick bottoms, thin tops — is exactly what we observe in medieval windows, and it follows entirely from manufacturing and installation practices, with no flow required at any stage.

This explanation is not a hypothesis; it is the documented account given by historians of glass manufacturing and by glaziers who understand the properties of historical glass. The glass-flow interpretation arose from people who noticed the thickness variation but did not know the manufacturing history.

## Why People Believe This

The glass-flows myth is a good example of a false explanation that is more interesting than the true one. Understanding why it persists requires attending to both the appeal of the misconception and the real scientific kernel around which it grew.

**The counterintuitive appeal.** Claims that reframe the familiar as strange are cognitively sticky. "Glass is actually a liquid" violates a basic categorical assumption — that glass is a solid — in a way that feels revelatory. It promises access to a hidden layer of physical reality invisible to casual observation. This is the same cognitive pleasure that makes urban legends, paradoxes, and surprising scientific claims so shareable: they disrupt the expected and reward the person who knows the secret version of the story.

**The partial truth about glass's unusual nature.** Glass genuinely does not fit the conventional solid/liquid binary cleanly. It lacks crystalline structure. It has a glass transition temperature (Tg) rather than a sharp melting point. These properties make it scientifically anomalous in ways that support a loose description of glass as "sort of like a frozen liquid." The problem is that "sort of like" is doing enormous work in that description, and when it gets dropped, the description becomes false. The partial truth that glass is thermodynamically unusual provides a substrate on which the false claim that it flows can root.

**Misinterpretation of observable evidence.** Old windows really do have uneven thickness. When someone notices this — particularly in a cathedral with a reputation for age — it is natural to reach for an explanation. If they happen to know that glass is "a liquid," the pieces seem to fit: glass is a liquid, centuries have passed, gravity has acted, glass has settled to the bottom. The misinterpretation is plausible precisely because its premises are in the neighborhood of true things.

**Repetition in educational and popular science contexts.** The glass-flows claim has been repeated in science classrooms, popular science books, and media coverage often enough that many people encountered it from a source they considered authoritative. Once a misconception is absorbed from an authoritative source, it is resistant to correction even from subsequent authoritative sources — a well-documented phenomenon in science education research.

**The difficulty of distinguishing amorphous solid from supercooled liquid.** Professional materials scientists distinguish between these states using technical criteria — viscosity thresholds, relaxation timescales, thermodynamic stability — that are not part of general scientific literacy. In ordinary discourse, the distinction between "amorphous solid" and "supercooled liquid" is easy to collapse, especially when doing so produces a more interesting claim.

## Verdict

**Distorted but grounded.** The claim that glass flows measurably over human-relevant timescales is false. The evidence cited for it — uneven medieval windows — has a different and well-documented explanation in manufacturing and installation practices. The physics, rigorously calculated by Zanotto and Gupta (1999), rules out any meaningful flow at room temperature for timescales up to and vastly exceeding the age of the universe. However, the myth is not invented from nothing: it latches onto genuine features of glass's unusual thermodynamic state — its amorphous structure, its lack of a sharp melting point, its intermediate position between ordered solid and disordered liquid — and extends them far beyond what the evidence supports.

## The Wider Picture

The glass myth illustrates a specific pattern in popular science communication: the transformation of a technically accurate but heavily qualified claim into an unqualified statement that changes its meaning entirely.

The technically accurate version is something like: "Glass is an amorphous solid whose atomic structure resembles that of a frozen liquid more than that of a crystal, and it lacks the long-range order that characterizes conventional solids." This is true and scientifically interesting. The jump to "glass is a liquid that flows" preserves the sense of novelty while shedding the qualifications that made the original claim defensible.

This pattern shows up repeatedly in popular science. Claims about quantum mechanics ("particles can be in two places at once"), about relativity ("time slows down at high speeds"), and about neuroscience ("we only use 10% of our brain") all involve kernel truths that have been stripped of the conditions and qualifications that give them their precise meaning. The resulting popular claims are memorable and shareable precisely because they have been simplified to the point where their technical accuracy is broken.

The glass case is particularly instructive because the false version has a built-in empirical test (go look at old windows!) that seems to confirm it but actually reflects a completely different causal story. This kind of pseudo-confirmation — where real evidence appears to support a false explanation — is one of the hardest situations for scientific correction to navigate. Pointing out that the windows really are thicker at the bottom doesn't undermine the myth; it validates its premise. You have to also explain the alternative causal story, which requires more background and is more cognitively demanding than the myth it replaces.

Materials science has made significant progress in understanding the glass transition — the range of temperatures over which a glass-forming material transitions from a rigid, solid-like state to a viscous, liquid-like state — and the fundamental science of amorphous solids is an active research area. The glass transition temperature (Tg) concept allows precise characterization of when and how glassy materials become mobile, and it confirms that at temperatures well below Tg (as window glass is at room temperature), mobility is effectively zero on any practical timescale.

Understanding glass's actual properties matters beyond debunking myths. The development of glass ceramics, optical fibers, metallic glasses, and other amorphous materials depends on precise understanding of how structure and viscosity relate. The "glass is a liquid" myth, by getting this relationship badly wrong, obscures the genuinely interesting science of amorphous solids.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The glass-flows myth is one of the more thoroughly debunked popular misconceptions in circulation, and it has received treatment from multiple fact-checking and science communication venues.

**The American Journal of Physics (1999)** — the Zanotto and Gupta paper — is the most rigorous published treatment and is frequently cited in secondary discussions. It provides the quantitative foundation for the debunking: not just "glass doesn't flow" but "here is how long it would actually take, and that timescale exceeds the age of the universe by many orders of magnitude."

**Various science communication venues**, including blogs, YouTube channels, and popular science publications, have addressed the myth, typically emphasizing the manufacturing explanation for medieval window thickness. The manufacturing explanation is the most accessible corrective because it offers an alternative causal story that explains the observable evidence.

**Snopes** has not published a dedicated entry on this specific claim, though the factual basis for the debunking is well established in peer-reviewed literature.

The main challenge for fact-checkers addressing this myth is that it requires two distinct moves: (1) explaining why glass doesn't flow, which requires some thermodynamics and materials science; and (2) explaining why old windows are thicker at the bottom, which requires some history of glassmaking. Doing both is more demanding than most myth-busting communications require. Doing only (1) leaves audiences without a satisfying replacement story for the observable evidence. Doing only (2) leaves audiences without understanding why the "glass flows" explanation was wrong in principle.

The best treatments do both, and they are careful to preserve the genuinely interesting truth that glass occupies an unusual state in the landscape of materials — not to save the myth, but because understanding what is actually true about glass is more interesting than either believing the myth or simply knowing it is false.
