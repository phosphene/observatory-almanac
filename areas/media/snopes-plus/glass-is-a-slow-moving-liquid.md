# "Glass Is Actually a Slow-Moving Liquid, Which Is Why Old Windowpanes Are Thicker at the Bottom"


## 1. The Claim

Glass, this claim asserts, is not a true solid but rather an extremely viscous liquid—a supercooled fluid that has never fully "frozen" into a crystalline state, and which therefore continues to flow, imperceptibly slowly, under the influence of gravity over geological time. The visible evidence for this ongoing flow, the claim continues, can be observed in old buildings, particularly medieval churches and cathedrals: the panes of stained or clear glass in ancient windows are systematically thicker at the bottom than at the top, precisely as one would expect if the glass had been slowly flowing downward over centuries.

This claim circulates widely in popular science contexts, in STEM education environments, on social media, and in casual conversation. It frequently appears as a counterintuitive fact—the kind of surprising truth that demonstrates the inadequacy of ordinary perception and the superiority of scientific understanding. It is presented not merely as trivia but as a demonstration of a deep principle: that matter exists on a continuum between solid and liquid, that ordinary categories are imprecise, and that the universe operates on timescales our evolved intuitions are poorly equipped to comprehend. The claim thus functions as a specimen of correct scientific reasoning against naive folk categorization.

The invocation of medieval cathedrals as physical evidence grounds the claim in observable, tactile reality accessible to any traveler to Europe. The glass is visibly uneven; the unevenness is measurable; it correlates with the direction gravity would pull a viscous fluid. The claim offers not merely an abstract physical principle but a piece of evidence that viewers can examine with their own hands by running a finger across an ancient windowpane. This combination—a counterintuitive physical principle and visible empirical confirmation—gives the claim an unusually strong emotional and cognitive grip.

Despite its compelling form, the claim is false on both its material and its evidentiary legs. Glass is not flowing over human-relevant timescales, and the thickness distribution in old windowpanes has a different, better-documented cause.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Glass is an amorphous solid, not a liquid.** The material science of glass requires careful distinction between atomic structure and mechanical behavior. Glass is indeed amorphous—meaning its atoms are arranged without the long-range periodic order that defines crystalline solids. This is the technical basis for the claim that glass resembles a liquid at the atomic level. In crystalline materials like table salt or quartz, atoms or ions occupy specific lattice positions in a repeating pattern; in glass, the atomic arrangement is more disordered, resembling the arrangement of atoms in a liquid at the moment of rapid cooling.

This structural similarity to a liquid, however, does not imply that glass behaves mechanically like a liquid at room temperature. Mechanical behavior—flow, deformation, viscosity—is governed by different physical parameters than atomic arrangement. The key parameter is viscosity. All materials flow; the question is at what rate under what conditions. The viscosity of glass at room temperature (approximately 20°C) has been calculated and measured to be on the order of 10^18 to 10^21 poise (units of dynamic viscosity). For comparison: water at room temperature has a viscosity of approximately 0.01 poise; warm honey is approximately 10 poise; molten glass at forming temperature (around 700–1000°C) is 10^3 to 10^8 poise.

The implications are calculable. At its room-temperature viscosity, a glass pane under gravitational stress would require approximately 10^32 years to deform measurably. The age of the universe is approximately 1.38 × 10^10 years. Medieval glass is, at most, about 800 years old. Any gravitational flow in glass on the timescale of human civilization is not merely imperceptible; it is physically impossible to any meaningful significant figure. To speak of glass as "flowing slowly" over centuries is not a useful approximation; it is categorically incorrect.

Condensed matter physicists have formalized this distinction. Glass falls into the category of amorphous solids: materials that have the disordered atomic structure of a liquid but the mechanical properties—rigidity, elastic response under stress, resistance to flow—of a solid. This category distinction is significant. An amorphous solid does not flow at room temperature regardless of timescale. The disordering of atomic structure, which gives glass its characteristic transparency and isotropic optical properties, is irrelevant to whether the material flows.

**Medieval windows are thicker at the bottom because of how they were manufactured.** The primary pre-industrial technique for producing flat glass panels was the "crown glass" method, also known as the spun or bullion method. In this process, a glassblower gathered molten glass on the end of a blowpipe, inflated it into a globe, and then transferred it to a "pontil" rod—a solid iron rod attached to the outside of the glass bubble—and reheated it. While the glass was hot and plastic, the glassblower or an assistant used centrifugal force by spinning the pontil rapidly; the rotating action caused the glass bubble to open and flatten into a disk, sometimes up to 1.5 meters in diameter.

Because the disk was formed by centrifugal spinning, the glass was not of uniform thickness: material tended to be thicker at the outer edges and thinner toward the center. The characteristic "bull's-eye" or "rondel" at the center—frequently visible in medieval windows—is where the pontil was attached; the surrounding disk tapers from the edges inward but with substantial variation across its area. When glaziers cut windows from these disks, they obtained pieces of varying thickness from different positions on the disk. The thicker edge pieces were used or oriented according to the preferences of the glazier, available stock, and the requirements of the specific window frame.

The practice of installing glass with the thicker edge at the bottom was indeed common among glaziers and is attested in historical trade descriptions. This was done because a pane installed with its thicker edge at the base is more resistant to racking—lateral deformation—under wind pressure, has lower center of gravity for stability, and was simply easier to bed and seal into the lead came of leaded window frames. The variation in thickness has nothing to do with gravitational flow; it is manufacturing variation installed in a preferred orientation by skilled craftsmen for structural reasons.

More recent float glass production, introduced commercially by Alastair Pilkington of Pilkington Brothers around 1959, produces glass of highly uniform thickness by floating molten glass on a bed of molten tin—a process that is itself informed by the physics of liquid-surface tension and produces sheets with less than 100 microns of variation across large widths.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The glass-as-liquid myth is a compelling illustration of how a half-truth—a physical fact that is technically accurate but routinely misapplied and misinterpreted—can generate a false claim that has the apparent support of genuine scientific expertise.

**The amorphous structure fact is real.** Glass is genuinely amorphous. The statement that glass lacks the crystalline order of a true solid is correct in a technical structural sense, and this fact is widely taught in introductory materials science and chemistry courses. The error lies in inferring from this structural fact to a behavioral conclusion: amorphous → liquid-like structure → liquid-like mechanical behavior → flow. Each arrow in that chain is wrong as applied to glass at ambient temperature. But listeners who encounter the structural fact without the accompanying mechanistic qualification understand a simple, dramatic conclusion: glass is liquid. The technical qualification—that amorphous structure does not imply liquid behavior—requires knowledge of viscosity, glass transition temperature, and condensed matter phase theory that most audiences lack and that the explanatory context often omits.

**The cathedral evidence seems definitive.** Medieval glass in genuine historic buildings does exhibit variation in thickness. This is observable, measurable, and real. When visitors run fingers across the ancient glass and feel the variation, they are not imagining it. The existence of real variation in real glass creates genuine empirical support for the claim: here is the glass, here is the variation, and here is the theory that explains it. The fact that a competing theory—pre-industrial manufacturing variation—explains the thickness distribution more accurately and parsimoniously does not register for visitors who have not been told about crown glass production methods. The manufacturing explanation requires specialized knowledge; the liquid-flow explanation requires only gravity.

**Counterintuitive claims have heightened credibility in science communication contexts.** The glass-as-liquid story is precisely the type of fact that science communicators and teachers often use to engage skeptical or bored audiences: it seems wrong, it overturns an assumption, and its truth reveals how strange the physical world actually is. When a claim arrives in the frame of "surprising scientific truth," audiences are primed to accept it without the scrutiny they would apply to an obvious-feeling assertion. The cognitive mechanism here is the opposite of ordinary skepticism: counterintuitive claims in authoritative contexts get a credibility bonus because their very strangeness functions as evidence that they could only come from specialized knowledge. Ordinary folk wisdom would not produce such a result; therefore, the scientific-seeming alternative must be correct.

**Visual evidence is phenomenologically compelling.** The act of touching ancient glass and feeling thickness variation produces a sensory experience that subsequent verbal correction struggles to override. When the physical evidence and the verbal claim align—even if the physical evidence actually supports a different interpretation—the sensory experience reinforces belief in a way that is resistant to purely argumentative correction. The manufacturing explanation is not felt; the thickness variation is. This embodied phenomenology of belief formation is under-recognized in standard fact-checking frameworks that focus on the verbal and propositional level.

**Scientific popularizers have repeated it.** The claim that glass is a slow-moving liquid appeared in reputable popular science books and magazine articles throughout the late twentieth century, including in sources with significant institutional authority. When audiences encounter a claim in National Geographic, Scientific American (in historical editions), or popular science books by credentialed authors, they reasonably treat it as verified. The correction of this claim by professional materials scientists—particularly through widely circulated academic papers by Edgar Dutra Zanotto and others in the late 1990s and 2000s—occurred primarily in technical literature rather than in the popular venues where the original error propagated. The asymmetry of propagation and correction allowed the myth to continue circulating long after its scientific resolution.


## 4. Verdict

**False.** Glass is not a slow-moving liquid on any timescale relevant to human civilization or recorded history. At room temperature, glass has a viscosity so enormous that gravitational flow is physically impossible to any meaningful degree over periods shorter than many orders of magnitude greater than the age of the universe. The claim that medieval windowpanes are evidence of gravitational flow is additionally false on its evidentiary basis: variation in thickness in pre-industrial glass panes results from crown glass manufacturing methods and installation practices, not flow. The amorphous atomic structure of glass, while technically accurate, does not imply liquid mechanical behavior, and the inference from structural character to flow behavior is a category error in condensed matter physics.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The glass-as-liquid myth illustrates a specific failure mode in science communication that is worth examining carefully: the case where a technically correct fact is used to support a false conclusion because the qualification that distinguishes truth from error requires specialized knowledge that is not transmitted alongside the fact.

The underlying truth—that glass is an amorphous solid rather than a crystalline solid—is not only correct but genuinely important in materials science, optics, and manufacturing. Amorphous structure underlies glass's transparency, its isotropic optical properties, its specific thermal characteristics, and its brittleness in contrast to ductile crystalline metals. Understanding glass as amorphous is scientifically meaningful and does overturn naive categorizations of "solid" that assume crystalline order. This is legitimate counterintuitive science communication.

The error arises in the subsequent inference: amorphous → therefore liquid → therefore slow flow. This inference fails because it conflates two independent dimensions of material description: atomic-scale order (crystalline vs. amorphous) and bulk mechanical behavior (solid vs. liquid). These dimensions are correlated in many materials but they are not identical. Glass is the classic demonstration of this distinction: amorphous structure with solid mechanical behavior. The fact that it demonstrates the distinction is precisely why it is pedagogically useful; the error in the myth is invoking glass as evidence for a conflation the material specifically refutes.

The glass myth also illuminates the epistemology of "half-truth" in science communication. A half-truth carries more persuasive force than a pure fabrication because one component of it is verifiable, credentialed, and defended by genuine expertise. When a scientist correctly explains that glass lacks crystalline order, and when an interlocutor then infers (incorrectly) that glass therefore flows, the scientist's credibility attaches to the inference the scientist did not make. The expert endorsement of the true component is misappropriated to the false conclusion. Audiences who are not equipped to draw the precise boundary between what the expert said and what they inferred are not being foolish; they are navigating a legitimate epistemic problem that good science communication should anticipate and address.

The comparative study of physics myths finds this half-truth structure in several canonical cases: the claim that humans use only 10% of their brain draws on real neuroscientific data about metabolic activity patterns; the claim that the Great Wall of China is visible from space draws on the plausible scaling of large earthworks; the glass-flowing claim draws on real amorphous structure. In each case, the corrective must address not only the false conclusion but the true component appropriately, preserving the genuine insight while refusing the false extension.

Materials scientist Edgar D. Zanotto at the Federal University of São Carlos, together with colleagues John C. Mauro and others, published systematic calculations demonstrating the impossibility of glass flow at ambient temperatures in peer-reviewed literature in 1998 and in subsequent review papers. Zanotto explicitly identified the myth's misuse of amorphous structure as its core error and provided both the viscosity calculations and the manufacturing history of medieval glass as corrective. His work is frequently cited in subsequent science communication, though it remains less widely known than the myth it refuted.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The glass-as-liquid myth presents an unusual challenge for standard fact-checking operations because the claim enters public discourse primarily through legitimate science communication channels—popular science books, science teachers, museum exhibits—rather than through the politically motivated misinformation or viral hoax pathways that most fact-checking organizations are optimized to address. The claim is not partisan; it is not associated with any specific crisis or news cycle; it does not have an obvious political sponsor. It is simply a well-circulated scientific error dressed in the authority of credentialed sources.

Fact-checking methodology for physical science myths therefore requires engagement with primary scientific literature in a way that standard misinformation fact-checking does not. The verdict here rests not on documentary evidence about events—the kind of evidence available for historical or political claims—but on physical calculations and experimental measurements of material properties. A fact-checker who cannot independently evaluate viscosity calculations and glass transition physics must source expert opinion from materials scientists, which introduces reliance on testimonial authority. The best fact-checks of this claim cite peer-reviewed physical science literature directly and explain the viscosity argument in terms accessible to non-specialist readers.

The most effective presentations combine three elements: the viscosity calculation (explained accessibly, with the comparison time spans made vivid), the crown glass manufacturing history (with illustrations of the spinning process and resulting disk if available), and an explicit engagement with why the amorphous-structure fact doesn't imply flow (to pre-empt the anticipated "but glass is amorphous" counterargument from readers who have encountered that piece). Without the third element, corrections are frequently met with "but I heard glass is technically a liquid"—a response that demonstrates the half-truth structure of the original claim and shows why correcting the conclusion without addressing the premise is insufficient.

Science communication organizations including the American Physical Society, the Society of Glass Technology, and materials science departments at research universities have all addressed this myth in public-facing content. The consensus is unambiguous. The ongoing challenge is that the myth continues to circulate in informal science education contexts—science teachers passing it to students, popular science writers reproducing it from older sources—and these distribution pathways are not well-monitored by standard fact-checking infrastructure that focuses on news and social media. A claim that lives primarily in textbooks and classrooms requires correction infrastructure that integrates with educational publishers and curricula, which is a different and more institutionally complex challenge than correcting a Twitter-viral hoax.
