# Glass is a liquid that flows slowly over time

## 1. The Claim

This claim has achieved a remarkable status in popular science discourse: it is widely repeated as a fascinating and counterintuitive scientific fact, the kind of thing people deploy at dinner parties to demonstrate that the world is stranger than it appears. The claim runs roughly as follows: glass is not a solid but an extremely viscous liquid that flows imperceptibly slowly at room temperature. Evidence allegedly supporting this: old cathedral windows are often thicker at the bottom, which is cited as proof that the glass has flowed downward over centuries.

The claim appeals because it has the structure of a good counterintuitive discovery — something that challenges folk categories. If glass is "really" a liquid, then the ordinary solid/liquid distinction turns out to be more porous than we thought, and the ancient windows in Gothic cathedrals become inadvertent scientific instruments recording centuries of imperceptible flow. The narrative is elegant and memorable.

There are two distinct claims embedded here that require separate analysis:
1. Glass is a liquid (or more precisely, a supercooled liquid) that flows over time.
2. Old windows are thicker at the bottom because glass has flowed over centuries.

Both claims are false, though each false claim points toward genuine and interesting scientific complexity. The corrections are as instructive as the errors.

## 2. What's Actually True

### The State of Glass: Amorphous Solid, Not Supercooled Liquid

Glass is an amorphous solid. This requires unpacking, because the "amorphous" part is the source of much confusion.

Most solid materials are crystalline: their atoms or molecules are arranged in highly ordered, repeating lattice structures. Salt (sodium chloride) forms cubic crystals. Diamond is a face-centered cubic lattice of carbon atoms. Quartz (silicon dioxide in crystalline form) forms hexagonal crystals. In crystalline solids, there is a sharp phase transition from liquid to solid at a specific temperature (the melting point), at which the atomic structure reorganizes from the disordered liquid state into the ordered crystalline solid.

**Amorphous solids** — of which glass is the paradigmatic example — lack this long-range crystalline order. When silica glass is formed by cooling molten silicon dioxide, the cooling happens rapidly enough that the silicon and oxygen atoms do not have time to arrange themselves into the regular quartz crystal structure. Instead, they are locked into a disordered, non-crystalline arrangement that resembles, at the atomic scale, the structural randomness of a liquid.

This is the grain of truth in the "glass is a liquid" claim: glass shares structural features with liquids at the microscopic level, in that both lack crystalline long-range order. But structure is not the same as phase, and the absence of crystalline order does not make glass a liquid. Glass has the mechanical properties of a solid: it is rigid, it fractures rather than flows under stress at room temperature, it maintains its shape indefinitely. These properties are incompatible with liquid behavior.

The term "supercooled liquid" is sometimes applied to glass, and this is where the confusion deepens. A supercooled liquid is a material cooled below its melting point without crystallizing — it remains in the liquid state beyond the temperature at which it would normally solidify. This is a real phenomenon: water can be supercooled to around -40°C under carefully controlled conditions while remaining liquid. But glass is not a supercooled liquid in the ordinary thermodynamic sense, because it undergoes a glass transition rather than remaining in a thermodynamically liquid phase.

The glass transition temperature (Tg) is the temperature below which a glassy material behaves as a solid — rigid, with an elastic response to stress — and above which it behaves as a highly viscous liquid. For window glass (soda-lime silica), Tg is approximately 520-600°C. Below this temperature, glass is a solid for all practical purposes. The question of whether glass below Tg is a "real" solid or an extremely viscous liquid is partly definitional and has been a subject of genuine scientific debate — but the consensus in materials science is that glass is an amorphous solid, not a supercooled liquid.

### The Viscosity Argument: Mathematical Refutation

Even if one grants the supercooled liquid classification, the claim that glass flows at room temperature is definitively refuted by viscosity calculations. This calculation was performed rigorously by Edgar D. Zanotto and Paulo K. Gupta in their 1999 paper in the *American Journal of Physics*, "Do Cathedral Glasses Flow? — Additional Remarks," a follow-up to Zanotto's earlier 1998 paper on the same topic.

Viscosity is a measure of a fluid's resistance to flow; higher viscosity means slower flow. Water at room temperature has a viscosity of approximately 1 millipascal-second (mPa·s). Honey is approximately 2,000–10,000 mPa·s. The viscosity of window glass at its glass transition temperature (~550°C) is approximately 10¹³ mPa·s — a factor of roughly 10¹⁶ (ten quadrillion) higher than water.

At room temperature, the viscosity of silicate glass is estimated to be of the order of 10²⁰ to 10²³ Pa·s (pascal-seconds), depending on composition and temperature. Zanotto and Gupta calculated that for glass at room temperature to show a measurable thickness change of 1 nanometer (one billionth of a meter) — far below any observable scale — it would take on the order of 10³² years. The current age of the universe is approximately 1.38 × 10¹⁰ years. The time required for any measurable flow is thus roughly 10²² times the current age of the universe.

This is not a situation where flow occurs but we haven't waited long enough. It is a situation where, for any practical or meaningful definition of "flow," glass at room temperature does not flow. The viscosity is so extraordinarily high that the concept of flow is not merely imperceptible but essentially inapplicable.

### The Cathedral Windows: Manufacturing, Not Flow

The specific evidence most commonly cited — that ancient cathedral windows are thicker at the bottom, proving centuries of flow — is based on a misunderstanding of historical glassmaking technology.

Before the development of float glass manufacturing in the 1950s (the Pilkington process, which creates perfectly uniform flat glass by floating molten glass on a bath of molten tin), window glass was made by two methods that produced inherently non-uniform thicknesses.

**Crown glass** was made by blowing a glob of molten glass into a sphere, transferring it to an iron rod (punty) and spinning it rapidly while reheating, centrifugal force causing it to open into a flat disc. The result was a roughly circular pane of glass with a characteristic thickness gradient — thicker at the outer rim where the glass had been furthest from the center and experienced less centrifugal thinning, and with a thicker "bull's-eye" or "crown" knob at the center where the punty was attached. The panes were not uniform.

**Cylinder glass** (also called broad glass) was made by blowing a long cylinder of glass, cutting the ends off, slitting the cylinder lengthwise, and reheating it in a flat furnace to open flat. The resulting pane was thicker at one end (the closed end of the cylinder, where glass accumulated) than the other, and often had a pronounced bow or ripple from the process.

When glaziers installed glass from either method into window frames, they generally placed the thicker edge downward. This was practical — a thicker base provides more structural stability in a window frame — and in the case of cylinder glass, aesthetically preferable since the thicker end was also the end with more visible distortion.

The unevenness in old glass windows is thus an artifact of manufacturing, present at installation, not a result of centuries of flow. This was established definitively by Zanotto and is corroborated by the historical record: surviving medieval glassworking treatises describe the manufacturing processes that produced these thickness variations, and museum collections of medieval glass include examples where the thick end is at the top or on the sides, not only at the bottom.

### The Glass Transition and Scientific Debate

The claim is not entirely without scientific grounding in one respect: there is genuine ongoing debate in materials science and condensed matter physics about the precise thermodynamic classification of the glassy state. The question of whether glass is a distinct thermodynamic phase, a non-equilibrium arrested state, or a supercooled liquid with an impossibly long relaxation time remains an active research area. Physicists including Peter Wolynes and David Reichman have contributed to a substantial literature on the theoretical foundations of the glass transition.

The key point of genuine complexity is that glass is not in thermodynamic equilibrium. If you wait long enough — over geological or cosmological timescales — glass will relax toward either a crystalline state (devitrification) or will begin to flow. The question is whether this characteristic timescale is physically meaningful. For most materials scientists and physicists, a process requiring 10³² years to produce nanometer-scale changes is not usefully described as "flowing" in any operational sense, even if the theoretical framework permits it.

The popular claim collapses the interesting theoretical complexity — the genuine non-equilibrium nature of the glassy state, the ongoing debate about its thermodynamic classification — into a concrete, time-bounded claim (glass flows, and old windows prove it) that is simply wrong.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### The Structure of the Appealing Science Story

The "glass is a liquid" claim has all the hallmarks of a memorable science myth: it is counterintuitive, it offers a new way of seeing something familiar (windows), it comes with tangible evidence you can go look at (old church windows), and it deploys the satisfying rhetorical move of correcting naive folk categories with hidden scientific truth.

These properties make it an ideal piece of "interesting science" for informal science communication — the kind of content that gets shared because it is surprising and memorable, not because it is rigorously checked. Once a claim achieves this status in informal science communication, it is extremely resistant to correction: each person who encounters it treats the repetition as evidence of reliability, and the counter-narrative ("actually, glass is a solid") sounds like pedantry correcting something obviously true.

### Partial Truth and the Supercooled Liquid Framework

The claim also has the structural advantage of containing enough truth to be defensible. Glass does lack crystalline order. The glass transition is real. There is genuine debate about the thermodynamic classification of the glassy state. Someone with moderate scientific knowledge who repeats the "glass is a liquid" claim and gets corrected can always fall back on the real complexity: "well, technically it depends on how you define a solid," which is partly true. This retreat to the genuinely complex prevents the claim from being cleanly falsifiable in casual conversation.

### The Cathedral Window Evidence

The visual evidence cited — old windows thicker at the bottom — is genuinely observable. Anyone who visits a medieval cathedral and looks carefully at the windows may indeed notice that the glass is not perfectly uniform, and that thicker portions often occur at the bottom of the pane. This is true. It is observable. The problem is purely one of causal attribution: the thickness variation predates installation.

When visual evidence is available and the causal story seems plausible, most people do not seek alternative explanations. The manufacturing explanation — crown glass, cylinder glass, glaziers deliberately placing thicker edges downward — requires historical knowledge of pre-industrial glassmaking that most people don't have. The flow explanation requires only the (incorrect) general principle that glass is a viscous liquid. Given the evidence and the explanatory frameworks available, most people will arrive at the wrong conclusion not through irrationality but through absence of information.

### Science Education and the Appeal of Paradox

Science education that emphasizes surprising findings and counterintuitive results without sufficient mechanistic grounding can inadvertently contribute to this kind of persistent myth. "Glass is actually a liquid!" functions as a pedagogical device to make students think about states of matter differently. When this device is deployed without the qualification that at room temperature glass is effectively a solid for any timescale that matters, the lesson that sticks is the surprising conclusion, not the nuanced qualification.

## 4. Verdict

**False.** Glass is not a liquid. It is an amorphous solid that lacks crystalline structure but possesses the mechanical properties of a solid — rigidity, fracture behavior, shape maintenance — that are definitionally incompatible with liquid behavior. At room temperature, the viscosity of silicate glass is so extreme (roughly 10²⁰–10²³ Pa·s) that measurable flow would require timescales many orders of magnitude longer than the age of the universe.

The cathedral window evidence is also false: the thickness variation in medieval glass is an artifact of pre-industrial glassmaking methods (crown glass and cylinder glass), not of flow. Glaziers placed thicker edges downward at installation.

The genuinely interesting science involves the thermodynamic complexity of the amorphous state and the ongoing debates about the glass transition — complexity that the popular claim distorts rather than illuminates.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### Materials Science of the Glassy State

The physics of glass and amorphous materials is a genuine frontier in condensed matter science. The glass transition is sometimes described as one of the most important unsolved problems in materials physics — not because we lack a working description, but because the theoretical foundations remain contested. The question of whether the glass transition is a true thermodynamic phase transition (in which case there would be a genuine discontinuity in thermodynamic quantities) or a kinetic phenomenon (in which case it is merely the point at which the relaxation time exceeds the observation timescale) has been actively debated for decades.

Researchers like Andrea Liu and Sidney Nagel have studied "jamming" phenomena — the way dense systems of particles of various kinds, including emulsions, foams, and granular materials, become mechanically rigid when cooled, compressed, or otherwise constrained. The glass transition is one instance of this broader jamming phenomenology. Understanding jamming has implications not only for glass but for the physics of any system that undergoes a transition from fluid to rigid behavior.

Metallic glasses — amorphous metals formed by cooling liquid metal so rapidly that crystallization cannot occur — represent an important class of materials with applications in high-performance alloys, corrosion-resistant coatings, and medical devices. Their non-equilibrium amorphous structure gives them unusual combinations of hardness, elasticity, and corrosion resistance. The science of amorphous solids is thus not merely esoteric: it has direct technological relevance, and the popular "glass is a liquid" claim obscures rather than illuminates the interesting physics involved.

### The History of Glassmaking

The medieval and early modern history of glassmaking is itself a fascinating story that the flow myth completely obscures. The transition from crown glass to cylinder glass to modern float glass represents centuries of empirical engineering innovation. Venetian glassmakers on the island of Murano developed techniques for producing glass of extraordinary clarity and minimal defect, guarding their secrets so jealously that glassblowers were forbidden from leaving the island under pain of death.

The Lorraine glassmakers of northeastern France developed the broad glass or cylinder method, and the resulting glass — with its characteristic distortion and thickness variation — was the dominant window glass technology in Europe for centuries. Understanding the manufacturing origins of the thickness variation in old windows is not mere debunking; it opens onto this longer history of craft technology, trade secret, and material innovation.

### The Epistemology of Scientific Myths

The glass-is-a-liquid myth exemplifies a pattern worth naming: the scientifically-flavored urban legend that achieves persistence by containing just enough real science to be defensible when challenged. The real science here — the amorphous nature of glass, the glass transition, the ongoing debate about thermodynamic classification — is genuinely interesting and complex. The popular claim simplifies this complexity into a false concrete statement (glass flows, windows prove it) that is more memorable than the truth but less illuminating.

This pattern appears throughout science communication: accurate underlying complexity is collapsed into a simple surprising claim that can be easily shared. The surprising claim then circulates widely, survives casual challenges by retreating to the real complexity, and resists correction because the debunking seems like it's attacking complexity rather than defending accuracy. Zanotto and Gupta's papers are notable for meeting this myth on its own terms — providing the mathematical demolition (viscosity calculations, relaxation time estimates) that makes the claim impossible to salvage even in its most defensible form.

The lesson for science communicators is that the appeal to counterintuitive complexity is valuable — students and the public should be challenged to question their assumptions about solid and liquid — but that the mechanism of challenge matters. "Glass is a liquid" teaches the wrong lesson and the wrong mechanism. The better lesson — "the categories solid and liquid are less sharp than you think, and glass is an interesting edge case" — is both truer and more scientifically valuable, but it sacrifices the memorable punchline.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim has received more direct fact-checking attention than many science myths, partly because it is so widely circulated and partly because it has specific testable components. Snopes has addressed related claims, and various science communication outlets have explicitly debunked the cathedral window assertion.

The fact-checking treatment is generally accurate: the windows-flow claim is false, and glass is technically an amorphous solid. Where fact-checkers vary is in how they handle the genuinely complex thermodynamic questions about the glassy state. Some debunks are overconfident, dismissing any complexity with "glass is just a solid, full stop." Others acknowledge that the glassy state is thermodynamically unusual while correctly noting that the flow claim is false and the window evidence is a manufacturing artifact.

Zanotto's papers in the *American Journal of Physics* provide the definitive scientific treatment and have been widely cited in popular science debunking efforts. The combination of historical analysis (glassmaking methods), viscosity calculations, and thermodynamic clarification in Zanotto's work represents an exemplary piece of scientific myth-busting: it addresses the evidence cited for the myth, provides the correct alternative explanation, and does the quantitative work to make the counter-claim rigorous rather than merely assertive.

What the standard fact-check sometimes misses is the dimension captured by the Observatory Almanac's EARC classification: this is an E (Essentially False) claim with an epistemologically-loaded gap category. The claim is not merely wrong — it maps onto genuine scientific complexity (the amorphous nature of glass, the contested thermodynamics of the glass transition) in a way that makes it resistant to simple correction. The appropriate fact-check response is not just "False — here's why" but "False — and here's the genuinely interesting science that the false claim is obscuring." The difference matters for whether the correction actually changes understanding or merely replaces one unexamined belief with another.
