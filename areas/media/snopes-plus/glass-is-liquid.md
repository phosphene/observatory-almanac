# "Glass Is a Liquid / Old Windows Are Thicker at the Bottom Because Glass Flows": A Snopes+ Review

## 1. Claim Statement

One of the most persistent myths in popular science asserts that glass is actually a liquid rather than a solid, and that this liquid nature can be observed in old buildings where glass windows are supposedly thicker at the bottom than at the top. According to this claim, glass flows extremely slowly over centuries or millennia, causing it to gradually accumulate at the bottom of window panes due to gravity. This assertion often appears in educational contexts, sometimes even in textbooks, and has become a classic example of "common knowledge" that feels intuitively plausible but is fundamentally incorrect.

The claim typically includes several related assertions: that glass lacks a crystalline structure (true), that this makes it a liquid rather than a solid (false), that medieval cathedral windows demonstrate this flow through their uneven thickness (false attribution), and that given enough time, glass will visibly deform under its own weight (false at normal temperatures). The persistence of this myth speaks to deeper questions about how we categorize states of matter and how folk explanations for observed phenomena can become entrenched even when they contradict established science.

## 2. Snopes Assessment

Snopes has addressed this claim and rated it as "False." Their analysis correctly identifies that glass is not a liquid and that the thickness variations in old windows result from historical manufacturing techniques rather than flow over time. The Snopes verdict aligns with the scientific consensus established through decades of materials science research. However, the Snopes treatment, while accurate in its conclusion, provides limited depth regarding the fascinating physics that makes this myth superficially plausible and the historical context that gave rise to the misunderstanding.

The Snopes analysis touches on the key points: glass is an amorphous solid, not a liquid; medieval manufacturing created uneven glass; and glaziers placed thicker edges at the bottom for stability. This assessment is scientifically sound but leaves room for deeper exploration of why the myth persists and what it reveals about the complex nature of glass as a material.

## 3. Claim Fidelity Audit

The claim contains several layers of truth and falsehood that require careful parsing. Glass indeed has unusual properties that distinguish it from typical crystalline solids: it lacks long-range atomic order, exhibits a glass transition rather than a sharp melting point, and demonstrates viscoelastic behavior under extreme conditions. These properties create genuine ambiguity about how to categorize glass within traditional solid-liquid-gas frameworks, lending superficial credibility to the "glass is liquid" assertion.

Medieval cathedral windows do exhibit thickness variations, with many showing thicker sections at the bottom—this observation is accurate. The manufacturing explanation is also well-documented: medieval glass-making techniques, particularly the crown glass process, produced circular sheets of glass that were naturally thicker at the edges and thinner in the center. When these sheets were cut into panes for windows, glaziers logically placed the thicker, more stable edge at the bottom.

However, the causal connection between these observations and the liquid nature of glass represents a fundamental category error. The claim conflates the amorphous structure of glass (true) with liquid behavior (false), and attributes observed thickness patterns (real) to the wrong mechanism (flow vs. manufacturing). This represents a classic case of post hoc reasoning: observing an effect (thick bottoms) and incorrectly inferring a cause (liquid flow) without considering alternative explanations.

## 4. Review Epistemology

The epistemological foundation of this myth rests on several cognitive biases and knowledge gaps that make it particularly sticky. First, the visual similarity between glass formation (watching molten glass cool and solidify) and the imagined process of slow flow creates a powerful analogical framework. When people observe glass being heated until it flows, then cooled until it appears solid, the intermediate states suggest a continuum rather than discrete phase changes.

Second, the folk physics intuition that "if something can flow when hot, it must still flow when cold, just more slowly" reflects a reasonable but incorrect extrapolation. This linear thinking fails to account for the exponential relationship between temperature and viscosity in glass, where small temperature changes produce enormous differences in flow rates.

Third, the medieval window evidence provides what appears to be direct observational support for the theory. The fact that old windows often do show thickness variations creates a compelling "smoking gun" that seems to confirm the hypothesis. Without knowledge of historical manufacturing techniques, this evidence appears to strongly support the liquid glass theory.

Fourth, the claim benefits from scientific-sounding language about "amorphous solids" and "glass transitions" that makes it seem sophisticated and well-informed. The use of legitimate scientific terminology lends credibility even when the underlying logic is flawed.

The persistence of this myth also reflects broader epistemological issues in science education, where simplified explanations for complex phenomena can create lasting misconceptions. The difficulty of explaining exactly what an "amorphous solid" is, and why it differs from both crystalline solids and liquids, creates a pedagogical vacuum that the "glass is liquid" explanation fills, even though it's wrong.

## 5. Conclusion Epistemology

The scientific resolution of this question relies on precise definitions of material states and quantitative measurements of flow rates that demonstrate the impossibility of observable glass flow at room temperature. Edgar Zanotto and John Mauro's 2017 calculations provide the definitive refutation: they estimated that for room-temperature glass to show visible flow, it would require longer than the current age of the universe (approximately 13.8 billion years).

These calculations are based on established principles of materials science, particularly the relationship between temperature, viscosity, and flow rates in amorphous materials. At room temperature, glass has a viscosity approximately 10^17 times greater than water, making any observable flow impossible on human timescales. The mathematical precision of these estimates, combined with experimental verification through accelerated aging studies, provides epistemologically robust evidence against the flow hypothesis.

The manufacturing explanation for medieval window thickness variations is supported by historical documentation, archaeological evidence, and materials analysis of surviving glass samples. Studies of medieval glass-making techniques, particularly the crown glass process used throughout Europe, demonstrate that the thickness patterns observed in old windows match exactly what would be expected from these manufacturing methods, not from gravitational flow.

Contemporary glass manufacturing provides additional evidence: modern float glass, produced under precisely controlled conditions to uniform thickness, shows no measurable flow even in samples that have been carefully monitored for decades. If glass flowed observably at room temperature, this would be evident in modern precision glass applications, where even microscopic deformations would be detectable and problematic.

## 6. The Wider Field

The glass liquid myth connects to broader themes in materials science, particularly the ongoing research into the nature of amorphous materials and the glass transition. While the specific claim about observable flow is false, the underlying questions about how to categorize glass remain active areas of scientific investigation. The glass transition represents one of the most important unsolved problems in condensed matter physics, with implications for everything from pharmaceutical manufacturing to geological processes.

Recent research has revealed that glass is far more complex than a simple "frozen liquid." Studies using advanced techniques like X-ray scattering and nuclear magnetic resonance have identified multiple relaxation processes in glass, suggesting that the material exists in a hierarchy of metastable states rather than a single equilibrium condition. This research confirms that while glass is not a liquid, it's also not a conventional solid in the crystalline sense.

The myth also intersects with historical preservation and archaeology, where understanding the true causes of glass deterioration is crucial for conserving cultural artifacts. Medieval windows do degrade over time, but through chemical processes like alkali leaching and surface crystallization rather than mechanical flow. Proper conservation requires understanding these actual degradation mechanisms rather than focusing on mythical flow effects.

In the context of science education, the glass liquid myth serves as a cautionary tale about the importance of precision in scientific language and the dangers of oversimplification. The widespread acceptance of this myth among educated audiences demonstrates how scientific-sounding explanations can spread and persist even when they contradict established knowledge. This has implications for how we teach complex topics and combat scientific misinformation more broadly.

The myth also reveals interesting cultural patterns in how folk explanations for natural phenomena develop and spread. The appeal of the glass flow explanation lies partly in its satisfying narrative structure: it provides a simple, mechanistic explanation for an observed phenomenon that connects everyday experience (glass becoming soft when heated) with historical observation (thick window bottoms). This narrative completeness makes the explanation psychologically compelling even when it's factually incorrect.

## 7. Snopes+ Verdict

**ENHANCE**: While Snopes correctly identifies this claim as false, the full story reveals fascinating insights into materials science, medieval manufacturing, and the psychology of persistent myths that merit deeper exploration.

The definitive scientific verdict is clear: glass is an amorphous solid, not a liquid, and does not flow observably at room temperature. The thickness variations in medieval windows result from historical manufacturing processes, specifically the crown glass technique that produced naturally varying thickness patterns. Modern physics provides precise quantitative predictions that make room-temperature glass flow impossible on human timescales.

However, the persistence and appeal of this myth illuminate important questions about the nature of scientific knowledge and public understanding. The claim succeeds because it addresses real phenomena (glass's unusual properties, medieval window variations) with a superficially plausible explanation that connects familiar experiences (heated glass flowing) with observed evidence (thick window bottoms). The fact that this explanation is wrong doesn't diminish its psychological appeal or its utility as a teaching moment about the importance of rigorous scientific methodology.

The enhanced understanding reveals that while glass is definitively not a liquid, it occupies a unique position in our understanding of condensed matter that continues to challenge researchers. The glass transition remains one of the most important unsolved problems in physics, and ongoing research continues to reveal new complexities in how amorphous materials behave. This ongoing scientific frontier suggests that while the specific "flowing glass" claim is false, our understanding of glass itself continues to evolve.

For educators and science communicators, this case demonstrates the importance of addressing not just what is true, but why false explanations can seem compelling. Understanding the appeal of the glass liquid myth—its narrative coherence, its use of legitimate scientific terminology, its connection to observable phenomena—provides insights into how to craft more effective scientific explanations that satisfy both intellectual curiosity and psychological needs for coherent understanding.

The glass liquid myth ultimately serves as a perfect example of how scientific literacy requires not just knowing facts, but understanding the processes by which scientific knowledge is established, tested, and validated. While glass may not flow like a liquid, the story of this myth flows quite naturally into deeper questions about evidence, explanation, and the ongoing human quest to understand the material world.
