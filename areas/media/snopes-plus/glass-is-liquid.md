# "Glass Is a Liquid / Old Windows Are Thicker at the Bottom Because Glass Flows"


## 1. The Claim

One of the most persistent myths in materials science asserts that glass is actually a very viscous liquid rather than a solid, and that this liquid nature causes it to flow slowly over time. The primary "evidence" cited for this claim is the observation that many old windows, particularly in historical buildings and churches, appear thicker at the bottom than at the top. Proponents argue that over centuries, gravity has caused the glass to flow downward, accumulating additional thickness at the bottom edge of windowpanes.

This claim often extends to broader assertions about glass's material properties, suggesting that all glass objects will eventually deform under their own weight given sufficient time. Some versions of the myth propose that ancient glass artifacts show visible signs of flow, or that even modern windows will begin to sag if left undisturbed for decades. The most ambitious variants suggest that extremely old glass objects, such as Roman artifacts or medieval church windows, should show pronounced deformation effects if examined carefully.

The claim has gained additional credibility through its frequent repetition in educational contexts, including some textbooks and museum displays that present glass flow as an example of extremely slow physical processes. Tour guides at historical sites often cite glass flow when explaining window thickness variations to visitors, contributing to the myth's authority through association with educational institutions and cultural heritage sites.

The intuitive appeal of the explanation—that glass retains some liquid-like properties from its molten manufacturing state—has contributed to its widespread acceptance. This explanation satisfies common misconceptions about the relationship between manufacturing processes and final material properties, suggesting that materials somehow "remember" their molten state. The claim also appeals to romantic notions about historical buildings containing direct physical evidence of the passage of time, with windows serving as geological-scale records of gravitational processes.

Popular science presentations have sometimes inadvertently reinforced the myth by describing glass as an "amorphous solid" or discussing its "liquid-like structure" without clearly distinguishing between atomic arrangement and macroscopic behavior. These descriptions, while technically accurate in specific contexts, can be misinterpreted by lay audiences as supporting the flow hypothesis. The resulting confusion between microscopic structure and bulk material behavior has allowed the myth to persist even in educational environments where scientific accuracy is supposedly prioritized.


## 2. What's Actually True

The glass flow claim contains two distinct assertions that require separate evaluation: (1) the material classification of glass as a liquid, and (2) the explanation for thick-bottomed old windows. These components have different truth values and different evidential bases, requiring careful analysis to understand where the claim intersects with reality and where it diverges from scientific understanding.

**Observable Phenomena: Window Thickness Variations**
The observation that old windows are often thicker at the bottom is generally accurate—this phenomenon can be observed in medieval cathedrals, colonial American buildings, and other historical structures worldwide. Detailed measurements of medieval church windows consistently show thickness variations ranging from 2-3mm at the top to 8-10mm at the bottom in extreme cases. This pattern appears across different geographical regions and historical periods, suggesting a systematic cause rather than random variation.

However, precise documentation of these thickness patterns is often lacking. While the general observation is accurate, systematic measurements with proper controls for original installation orientation are rare. Some windows may have been rotated or reinstalled during renovation projects, complicating analysis of whether current thickness patterns reflect original manufacturing or subsequent processes.

Modern conservation efforts have provided better documentation of window thickness variations. Projects at Canterbury Cathedral, Notre-Dame, and other major historical sites have created detailed thickness maps of original medieval glass, confirming the prevalence of bottom-heavy thickness distributions. These measurements provide robust evidence for the phenomenon that glass flow proponents seek to explain.

**Material Science Fundamentals: Glass Structure and Behavior**
Glass represents a unique state of matter that challenges simple solid-liquid classification schemes. At the molecular level, glass has an amorphous atomic structure that lacks the regular crystalline patterns characteristic of most solids. This atomic arrangement resembles a "snapshot" of liquid structure frozen at the moment of solidification, leading to descriptions of glass as a "frozen liquid" or "supercooled liquid."

However, this structural similarity to liquids does not translate into liquid-like mechanical behavior at room temperature. Glass molecules are locked in position by their local energy landscape, with individual atoms vibrating around fixed equilibrium positions rather than flowing freely as in true liquids. The energy required for coordinated molecular motion that would produce bulk flow far exceeds the thermal energy available at room temperature.

Modern materials science provides precise measurements of glass viscosity at different temperatures. At room temperature, window glass has a viscosity of approximately 10^20 to 10^25 Pascal-seconds, depending on composition. For comparison, water has a viscosity of 0.001 Pascal-seconds, honey ranges from 1-10 Pascal-seconds, and even cold asphalt is only around 10^8 Pascal-seconds. These measurements demonstrate that glass viscosity at room temperature is not merely high but astronomically beyond the range where flow would be observable on human timescales.

**Historical Manufacturing: Crown Glass Production**
The causal explanation attributing thickness variations to gravitational flow represents a fundamental misunderstanding of historical glassmaking methods. Medieval and early modern window glass was produced using the crown glass technique, which inherently created uneven thickness due to manufacturing physics rather than subsequent flow.

The crown glass process involved gathering molten glass on a pontil rod, reheating it, and spinning it rapidly to create large flat discs through centrifugal force. This spinning action naturally produced thickness variations, with the center (where the pontil attached) being thicker than the edges, and uneven thickness distribution throughout the disc. Glaziers then cut rectangular panes from these discs and installed them in windows.

For structural stability and aesthetic reasons, glaziers typically installed the thicker portions at the bottom of window frames. Thicker glass provided better support for the overall window structure, and bottom-heavy installation reduced visual distortion that would occur if thick sections were placed at eye level. This installation practice created the systematic pattern of thick-bottomed windows that glass flow proponents observe in historical buildings.

Documentary evidence from medieval and Renaissance glazier manuals confirms these installation practices. Guild records and apprenticeship documents detail the crown glass manufacturing process and installation techniques, providing historical documentation for the non-flow explanation of thickness variations. These historical sources describe installation practices as deliberate choices rather than random orientation, explaining the systematic nature of thickness patterns.


Modern materials science provides definitive answers to both components of the glass flow claim through multiple lines of evidence from physics, chemistry, materials engineering, and historical analysis. The convergence of evidence from these different disciplines creates a robust foundation for rejecting the flow hypothesis while explaining the origins of the misconception.

**Glass State Classification: Definitive Physical Evidence**
Glass is definitively classified as an amorphous solid, not a liquid, based on multiple independent measurements and theoretical frameworks. At room temperature, silica glass has a viscosity of approximately 10^20 to 10^25 Pascal-seconds—a number so large that it approaches mathematical abstraction rather than physical reality. For practical purposes, this viscosity is effectively infinite, making flow impossible on any observable timescale.

Spectroscopic analysis confirms glass's solid-state behavior through measurements of molecular motion and structural relaxation. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy reveals that glass molecules vibrate around fixed positions rather than translating freely as in liquids. X-ray diffraction patterns show stable atomic arrangements that persist indefinitely at room temperature, contrasting with the continuously changing configurations characteristic of liquid states.

Thermal analysis provides additional evidence through differential scanning calorimetry measurements that detect glass transition temperatures. These measurements show that glass exhibits distinct solid-state behavior at room temperature, with liquid-like behavior only appearing at temperatures hundreds of degrees above normal environmental conditions. The glass transition represents a genuine phase boundary between solid and liquid behavior that occurs at well-defined temperatures far above ambient conditions.

**Flow Timescale Calculations: Quantitative Analysis**
Zanotto and Gupta (1999) performed detailed calculations of glass flow timescales using established viscosity measurements and mechanical stress analysis. Their calculations demonstrate that for observable glass flow to occur in medieval windows (defined as 1mm of deformation), the required timespan would exceed 10^32 years—vastly longer than the age of the universe (approximately 1.4 × 10^10 years). This calculation demonstrates that glass flow is not merely slow but effectively impossible on human or even geological timescales.

These calculations account for realistic stress conditions including gravitational loading, thermal cycling, and mechanical vibration that glass windows experience in real environments. Even under the most favorable assumptions for promoting flow—maximum gravitational stress, optimal temperature conditions, longest possible time periods—the calculations show that detectable deformation cannot occur within the age of the solar system.

Independent calculations by Shelby (2005) using different analytical approaches confirm these timescale estimates, providing convergent evidence that glass flow represents a physical impossibility rather than merely an extremely slow process. These calculations are based on well-established physical principles and have been verified through multiple analytical methods.

**Crown Glass Manufacturing: Historical Documentation**
Systematic analysis of historical glassmaking reveals that thick-bottomed windows result from medieval and colonial-era crown glass manufacturing rather than subsequent flow processes. The crown glass technique involved spinning molten glass into large discs, which naturally produced uneven thickness due to centrifugal forces and temperature gradients during the spinning process.

Archaeological evidence from medieval glassmaking sites confirms the crown glass production process through recovered pontil scars, furnace remains, and partially completed glass discs. This physical evidence corroborates written historical sources describing crown glass techniques and demonstrates the consistency of manufacturing methods across different geographical regions and time periods.

Glaziers systematically installed crown glass pieces with thicker portions at the bottom for structural and aesthetic reasons. Guild records and apprenticeship manuals document these installation practices, providing historical evidence for deliberate thickness orientation rather than random installation. This systematic installation practice explains why thick-bottomed windows appear consistently across different historical sites and time periods.

**Molecular Dynamics: Theoretical Framework**
Modern understanding of glass structure through molecular dynamics simulations reveals that while individual atoms do vibrate at room temperature, the energy required for coordinated molecular motion that would produce flow is far higher than ambient thermal energy provides. Glass molecules are effectively locked in position by their local energy landscape, with energy barriers for molecular motion that cannot be overcome by thermal fluctuations at room temperature.

Computational materials science has created detailed models of glass structure and behavior that account for atomic-level interactions and energy landscapes. These models predict glass behavior across different temperature and timescale ranges, providing theoretical frameworks that match experimental observations of glass stability and flow resistance.

The theoretical understanding of glass behavior connects to broader principles in condensed matter physics and statistical mechanics, providing conceptual frameworks for understanding why certain materials exhibit solid-state behavior despite having amorphous structure. This theoretical foundation explains glass behavior as a natural consequence of molecular interactions and energy landscapes rather than an exception requiring special explanations.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemological foundation for glass flow beliefs rests on several interconnected misconceptions about materials science, manufacturing history, and the relationship between microscopic structure and macroscopic behavior. Understanding these misconceptions illuminates broader patterns in scientific misinformation and the challenges of public science education.

**Definitional Confusion: Structure vs. Behavior**
The claim conflates "amorphous structure" (disordered atomic arrangement) with "liquid behavior" (continuous deformation under stress). While glass indeed has an amorphous structure frozen from its liquid manufacturing state, this structural property does not determine mechanical behavior at room temperature. This represents a fundamental category error where static structural properties are confused with dynamic behavioral properties.

This confusion reflects broader challenges in materials science education, where technical terminology can be misinterpreted by non-specialist audiences. Terms like "amorphous solid," "glassy state," and "supercooled liquid" have precise scientific meanings that differ from their apparent implications in everyday language. The resulting terminology barrier creates opportunities for misconceptions to develop and persist.

The structure-behavior distinction is crucial across materials science, where atomic-scale properties often have counterintuitive relationships to bulk behavior. Diamond and graphite have identical atomic composition but dramatically different properties due to structural differences. Similarly, glass structure provides important information about material properties but cannot be used to predict behavior without considering temperature, stress, and timescale factors.

**Temporal Scale Errors: Human vs. Molecular Timescales**
The claim underestimates the enormous difference between human observation timescales (decades to centuries) and the timescales required for significant molecular motion in solid glass (geological ages). This represents a fundamental misunderstanding of the relationship between molecular processes and observable phenomena.

Human intuition about time scales is poorly calibrated for molecular processes. While we can observe ice melting in minutes and see rust forming over months, the timescales for solid-state molecular reorganization are orders of magnitude longer than direct human experience. This temporal scale mismatch creates cognitive blind spots where impossible processes can seem plausible if they occur slowly enough.

Quantitative analysis reveals the magnitude of this temporal scale error. Calculations based on glass viscosity measurements indicate that observable flow in medieval windows would require timeframes exceeding 10^30 years—vastly longer than the age of the universe. This calculation demonstrates that glass flow is not merely slow but effectively impossible on any timescale relevant to human civilization.

**Historical Methodology Gaps: Manufacturing Context**
The claim lacks engagement with documentary evidence about historical glassmaking techniques, particularly the crown glass manufacturing process that inherently produced uneven thickness. This represents a failure to consider alternative explanations for observed phenomena and demonstrates poor historical methodology where physical observations are interpreted without appropriate historical context.

Proper historical analysis requires considering the technological and social contexts in which observed phenomena originated. Glass windows exist within specific historical manufacturing traditions with well-documented techniques and practices. Ignoring this historical context leads to anachronistic explanations where modern assumptions about material behavior are projected onto historical artifacts.

The crown glass manufacturing process is thoroughly documented in historical sources, guild records, and archaeological evidence. This documentation provides clear alternative explanations for window thickness variations that require no appeal to impossible physical processes. The failure to consult historical sources represents a methodological error that could be avoided through interdisciplinary collaboration between materials scientists and historians.

**Measurement Precision and Baseline Problems**
While thickness variations in old windows are real, precise measurements of whether additional flow has occurred would require baseline data from the time of installation, which is generally unavailable for historical windows. This creates an evidentiary gap where claims about flow processes cannot be definitively tested against historical measurements.

The absence of installation-era baseline measurements means that current thickness patterns could reflect original manufacturing, installation choices, or subsequent processes including thermal cycling, structural settling, or mechanical stress. Without proper controls, observed thickness variations cannot be attributed to any specific cause with confidence.

Modern conservation efforts are beginning to address this measurement gap by creating detailed documentation of historical windows before renovation. These projects provide baseline measurements that future researchers can use to detect any actual changes in glass structure. However, the timescales required for detectable glass flow mean that even these modern measurements may not provide definitive answers for centuries.
