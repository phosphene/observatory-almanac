# Glass Is a Slow-Flowing Liquid, Which Is Why Old Windows Are Thicker at the Bottom

## The Claim

Walk into any medieval European cathedral, and you can see something the ancients could not have intended: the window glass is visibly thicker toward the bottom of each pane. The explanation, so the story goes, is physics. Glass is not truly a solid — it is a supercooled liquid, a material that never finished its transition to a stable crystalline state. Over centuries, the inexorable pull of gravity has caused the glass to creep downward, accumulating mass at the base of each pane in a process too slow for any human lifetime to observe but visible in the aggregate over hundreds of years.

This claim combines two ideas that feel like they support each other: glass is unusual among materials (plausibly true), and ancient windows show measurable thickness variation (observably true). The combination produces a story that seems to explain observable evidence with a surprising physical principle, which is exactly the form that compelling popular science takes.

---

## What's Actually True

Both the causal mechanism and the implied evidence interpretation are wrong.

**Glass is an amorphous solid, not a flowing liquid.** The technical description of glass as a "supercooled liquid" appears in some materials science literature but is contested and, more importantly, is not license to conclude that glass behaves like a liquid. Glass forms when a molten silicate mixture is cooled rapidly enough that its atoms cannot arrange themselves into the periodic crystalline lattice that would normally form at that temperature. The result is a material whose atomic structure is disordered and frozen in place — resembling, at the atomic scale, the structure of a liquid, but with the mechanical properties of a solid.

The key physical property here is viscosity. At room temperature, window glass (soda-lime silicate glass) has an estimated viscosity of approximately 10²⁴ Pascal-seconds. For reference, water's viscosity is about 10⁻³ Pa·s; honey is around 10 Pa·s; pitch (another material sometimes cited for being a very slow "liquid," as in the famous pitch drop experiment) runs around 10⁸ Pa·s at room temperature. Glass is roughly 10¹⁶ times more viscous than pitch. The pitch drop experiment takes decades to show a single drip. Glass is so far beyond that range that "flow" is not a meaningful description of its behavior at room temperature.

**The quantitative case.** Edgar Zanotto and Prabhat Gupta published a precise calculation in the *American Journal of Physics* in 1999 examining exactly this question. They calculated the timescale required for window glass to show any perceptible flow at room temperature, using established viscosity data and the physics of viscous flow. Their conclusion: the required timescale exceeds 10³² years — more than twenty orders of magnitude longer than the current age of the universe (approximately 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years). No medieval cathedral, no matter how old, has been standing for anything approaching a fraction of that timescale. The glass has not flowed. It is not flowing. It will not flow within any timescale that has meaning for human purposes.

**The real reason medieval windows are thicker at the bottom.** The manufacturing and installation history of medieval flat glass completely explains the observed thickness variation without any appeal to viscous flow.

The dominant method for producing flat glass in medieval Europe was the crown glass process, also called the bull's-eye method. A glassblower gathered a large gather of molten glass on a blowpipe, blew it into a sphere, transferred it to a solid rod (pontil), and then reheated and rapidly spun the glass. Centrifugal force caused the spinning glass to flatten outward into a disc, with the center of the disc remaining thicker and the edges thinner. The disc was then cut into rectangular panes.

Because crown glass discs were not uniform in thickness — the outer portions tended to be thinner than the inner portions, and the overall profile was uneven — the resulting panes were inherently variable in thickness across their surface. Glaziers working with these panes understood this and developed a standard practice: when setting panes into window frames, they placed the heavier, thicker edge downward. The rationale was structural: a heavier base is more stable, and placing excess mass at the bottom reduces stress on the thinner glass above it. This was sensible craftsmanship, not a consequence of physical laws acting on glass after installation.

The observable result — panes thicker at the bottom — is therefore a direct legacy of manufacturing characteristics combined with deliberate installation choice. It was produced at the time of installation, not accumulated over centuries.

**Further evidence from the manufacturing history.** If glass flow were the explanation, we would expect to see several additional phenomena: glass that has been installed horizontally (such as in skylights or flat display cases) should show center sagging; glass in panes mounted at odd angles should show flow in the direction of gravity rather than always toward the bottom; and newer glass should show less bottom-weighting than older glass. None of these predictions are confirmed by observation. By contrast, if manufacturing variation combined with installation practice is the explanation, we would expect exactly what we see: bottom-weighting across panes of various ages in windows that have been installed in the conventional orientation, with the degree of unevenness reflecting the manufacturing method rather than the age of the glass.

Glass produced by modern float glass methods — in which molten glass floats on a bed of molten tin, producing extremely uniform sheets — does not show thickness variation in aged windows. This is exactly what the manufacturing explanation predicts and what the flow explanation cannot account for.

## Why People Believe This

The myth combines real observations with plausible-sounding science in a way that is structured to resist casual debunking.

**The evidence is visible and genuine.** If you visit a medieval cathedral and examine its windows, you really can see thickness variation. This is not an invented or exaggerated premise. The myth is persuasive in part because it explains something real, even if the explanation is wrong.

**The appeal of slow-motion physics.** There is a category of physical phenomena — continental drift, radioactive decay, the cosmic expansion — that operate on timescales too vast for direct human observation but are nonetheless real and consequential. The glass-flows claim fits the pattern of this category: slow processes accumulating over long timescales. Once someone knows that continental drift and radioactive decay are real, the suggestion that glass also flows slowly does not require an extraordinary leap. It asks only that you extend a familiar category of slow physics to a new material.

**"Glass is a liquid" has been repeated authoritatively.** Versions of the supercooled liquid description have appeared in textbooks, museum exhibits, and popular science media. Many people who believe the glass-flows myth encountered it from a source they considered reliable. The claim has been repeated often enough, in enough credible-seeming contexts, that questioning it feels like challenging received scientific knowledge.

**The partial truth about glass's unusual structure.** As with many persistent myths, the glass-flows claim is rooted in a real scientific oddity. Glass genuinely is unusual in its atomic structure. Describing it as a "frozen liquid" or "supercooled liquid" is not entirely without basis — it captures something about the disordered, non-crystalline arrangement of its atoms. The problem is the extension from "unusual molecular structure" to "practically behaves like a liquid on any relevant timescale." The extension is unjustified, but the kernel truth that supports it makes the full claim harder to dismiss.

**The causal story sounds complete.** Medieval windows are thicker at the bottom. Glass is a liquid. Liquids flow downward under gravity. Therefore, the glass has flowed. This syllogism appears to make the myth self-contained and explanatorily satisfying. Debunking it requires not just rejecting the conclusion but unpacking the false premise (glass flows on relevant timescales) and replacing the causal story with a better one (manufacturing and installation). That is considerably more work than affirming a simple story.

## Verdict

**Distorted but grounded.** The claim that medieval windows are thicker at the bottom because glass flows over time is false in both its proposed mechanism and its implied causal history. Glass does not flow measurably at room temperature on any timescale relevant to human existence, and the observable thickness variation in medieval windows results from crown glass manufacturing techniques and glazier installation practices, not viscous flow. The myth nonetheless latches onto real features of glass's unusual thermodynamic status and onto genuinely observable evidence, which is what gives it its persistence and apparent plausibility.

## The Wider Picture

The glass myth illuminates something important about how scientific misconceptions propagate and why the standard category of "myth vs. fact" is often inadequate for explaining them.

The crown glass manufacturing process is itself a fascinating piece of technological history. Glassmaking in medieval Europe required specialized knowledge, closely guarded trade secrets, and craft skills developed over generations. The Venetian glass tradition, particularly the guild centered on Murano, maintained dominance in European glassmaking through the medieval and early Renaissance periods by carefully controlling the techniques and knowledge of the trade. Crown glass, Normandy glass (cylinder glass), and later plate glass were produced by distinct methods, and the qualities and defects of each method left distinctive traces in the surviving material record.

This is exactly the kind of historical and material context that gets lost when a myth fills the explanatory gap. Once someone "knows" that the glass flowed, they have no need to ask about the manufacturing history. The myth closes off inquiry precisely because it provides a satisfying answer. The true story — that what you are looking at in a cathedral window is a document of medieval craft practice, of glassblowers spinning molten material and glaziers placing panes with knowledge of their material's properties — is in some ways more interesting than the myth. But it requires more background to appreciate.

The glass myth also illustrates the relationship between popular descriptions of scientific categories and the physical properties those categories are supposed to capture. "Solid" and "liquid" are folk categories as much as scientific ones, and the folk version of the distinction (solids are hard and don't flow; liquids are soft and flow) does not map perfectly onto the technical distinctions of materials science. Materials scientists use precise criteria — viscosity thresholds, crystalline order, glass transition temperatures — to characterize states of matter. Glass occupies an interesting position in that landscape, and the technical literature uses varying terminology to describe it.

The "supercooled liquid" description that underlies the myth is not wrong, exactly — it captures the fact that glass lacks the crystalline order that would characterize an equilibrium solid at its temperature. But it misleads when taken to imply that glass has the flow properties of a liquid. The technical qualification ("at room temperature glass is so viscous that flow is negligible for any practical or astronomical timescale") never makes it into popular communication. What makes it is: "glass is a liquid." The rest falls away.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The glass-flows myth is one of the more thoroughly addressed misconceptions in popular science, and several venues have published substantive corrections.

**Zanotto and Gupta (1999)** is the primary academic reference and is cited by most substantive debunking efforts. It provides the quantitative argument that removes any ambiguity: this is not a matter of philosophical interpretation about what counts as "solid" or "liquid" but a straightforward calculation showing that glass would not flow perceptibly in any timeframe relevant to the history of the universe.

**The manufacturing history** has been documented by historians of technology and glass specialists. The crown glass process, the structural logic of installing heavier glass edges downward, and the characteristic profiles of different historical manufacturing methods are all documented in the historical and technical literature on glassmaking.

**Science communication venues** have generally treated this myth well, though the quality varies. The best treatments explain both why glass doesn't flow (thermodynamics/viscosity) and why the windows look the way they do (manufacturing). Treatments that only address one side leave a gap that can allow the myth to persist.

**Snopes** has not published a standalone entry on this specific claim. The myth falls into a category — misconceptions about the physical properties of everyday materials — that is well addressed in academic science communication but less consistently covered by general fact-checking platforms.

The pedagogical challenge this myth poses is representative of a broader challenge in science communication: how to correct a false claim that is built on real observations and partially true premises without leaving the audience with no story at all. The corrective work has to be constructive — it has to replace the false causal story with a better one, not just remove the false one. The manufacturing history of medieval glass is the replacement story, and it is genuinely interesting: it connects what visitors can see in a cathedral window today to the craft practices of medieval glassmakers working without any of the materials science understanding we now have.
