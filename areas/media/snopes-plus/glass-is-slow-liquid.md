# "Glass Is a Slow-Moving Liquid"

## 1. The Claim

**The Claim**: Glass is not truly solid but rather an extremely viscous liquid that flows imperceptibly slowly over decades or centuries. As evidence, proponents point to old windows—particularly in medieval churches and historic buildings—that appear thicker at the bottom than the top, supposedly demonstrating centuries of gravitational flow.

This persistent myth appears across educational contexts, from casual conversation to surprisingly authoritative sources. The claim typically includes specific details: that glass flows so slowly it takes hundreds or thousands of years to show visible effects, that this explains why antique windows are often thicker at their base, and that this phenomenon proves glass occupies a unique state of matter between solid and liquid.

The narrative often extends to romantic notions about the continuity of matter across centuries—the idea that cathedral windows installed by medieval craftsmen are still slowly moving today, representing an unbroken physical connection to the distant past. Some versions claim that extremely old windows show visible distortion from this alleged flow, with measurements supposedly confirming gradual changes in thickness distribution over time.

Educational presentations sometimes use this claim to introduce concepts about states of matter, suggesting that glass demonstrates the arbitrary nature of the solid-liquid distinction and that all materials exist on a continuum rather than in discrete categories. The myth has become so embedded in popular understanding that even some science teachers and museum guides repeat it as established fact.

The practical implications of this belief include concerns about the long-term stability of glass structures, suggestions that ancient glass artifacts are somehow still "moving," and misconceptions about the fundamental nature of materials science. Some versions extend the timeline claim, suggesting that given enough millennia, glass windows would eventually flow like honey.

## 2. What's Actually True

**Definitive Material Science**: Glass is unequivocally an amorphous solid, not a liquid of any viscosity. At room temperature, glass molecules are locked in a rigid, disordered structure that exhibits no flow whatsoever. The confusion arises from glass's formation process—it's created by rapidly cooling molten silica, which freezes the material in a non-crystalline state, but this does not mean it retains liquid properties once cooled.

Edgar D. Zanotto of the Federal University of São Carlos, Brazil, and John C. Mauro of Pennsylvania State University conducted definitive calculations published in the American Journal of Physics in 2017. They determined that for medieval cathedral glass to flow measurably under its own weight would require a time span longer than the age of the universe—approximately 13.8 billion years. Even then, the deformation would be measured in fractions of atomic diameters.

**Medieval Window Manufacturing**: The thicker-at-the-bottom phenomenon in old windows results entirely from historical glass production methods, not gravitational flow. Medieval and early modern glass was made using the "crown glass" process: molten glass was spun into large circular sheets called "crowns," creating naturally varying thickness across the surface. The center, where the glass attached to the spinning rod (the "bull's eye"), was thickest and often discarded or used for less visible applications.

Glassmakers cut rectangular panes from these crowns, inevitably producing pieces with uneven thickness. When installing windows, craftsmen deliberately placed the thicker edge at the bottom for structural stability and weather resistance. This practice was documented in glazier manuals from the period and represents intelligent engineering, not accommodation of flowing glass.

Robert Brill of the Corning Museum of Glass analyzed hundreds of historical window samples and found that thickness variations correlate perfectly with known crown glass production patterns, showing no evidence of flow-related changes over time. Windows from the same period but different locations show identical thickness distributions, ruling out local gravitational effects.

**Molecular Structure Analysis**: Modern spectroscopic analysis of ancient glass samples reveals molecular structures identical to those of newly manufactured glass of the same composition. X-ray crystallography shows no evidence of molecular movement, stress patterns, or structural changes consistent with viscous flow. The silicon-oxygen bonds that form glass's backbone remain precisely as rigid in thousand-year-old samples as in contemporary glass.

Temperature-dependent viscosity measurements confirm that glass transitions from rigid solid to workable liquid only at temperatures above 1000°F (540°C). At room temperature, glass viscosity is effectively infinite—meaning zero flow under any realistic force or timeframe. The mathematical relationship between temperature and glass viscosity follows well-established physics laws that leave no room for room-temperature flow.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**Intuitive Appeal of the Explanation**: The glass-flow myth provides an elegantly simple explanation for a commonly observed phenomenon—old windows that appear thicker at the bottom. This observation is real and widespread, making the explanation seem credible without requiring technical knowledge about historical manufacturing processes. People naturally prefer explanations that account for visible evidence, even when those explanations are incorrect.

The myth also satisfies psychological preferences for continuity and gradual change over sudden discontinuity. The idea that matter exists on a spectrum rather than in discrete categories appeals to our intuition about the natural world, where boundaries often seem fluid and transitional. Glass flowing over centuries fits comfortable mental models about how the physical world operates over long timescales.

**Authority and Repetition**: The claim has been repeated by numerous seemingly credible sources, including some textbooks, museum displays, and educational materials. Once established in educational contexts, misconceptions become self-reinforcing through the authority of teachers and institutions. Students who learn incorrect information from trusted sources often become teachers themselves, perpetuating the cycle.

The myth's persistence demonstrates how scientific-sounding explanations can gain credibility through repetition rather than evidence. Each repetition lends apparent authority to subsequent iterations, creating an illusion of widespread expert consensus. The technical language around "amorphous solids" and "viscosity" makes the claim sound sophisticated and scientific to non-experts.

**Conceptual Confusion About States of Matter**: Many people learned oversimplified models of solid, liquid, and gas states without understanding the complexities of materials science. Glass doesn't fit neatly into elementary explanations of states of matter, creating conceptual gaps that the flow myth appears to fill. The idea that glass is "neither solid nor liquid" seems to resolve this categorization problem.

Educational systems often present states of matter as discrete categories with clear boundaries, failing to explain materials like glass that form through non-equilibrium processes. This creates cognitive dissonance when students encounter materials that don't match their simplified models, making incorrect but intuitive explanations more appealing than technically accurate ones.

**Historical Romantic Appeal**: The notion that medieval cathedral windows are still slowly moving appeals to romantic sensibilities about connections to the distant past. This emotional appeal makes the claim memorable and satisfying in ways that technical explanations about crown glass manufacturing cannot match. People enjoy believing they can observe processes spanning centuries.

The myth transforms mundane building maintenance into a profound connection with history, suggesting that physical structures embody temporal continuity across generations. This narrative satisfaction makes people resistant to technical corrections that diminish the poetry of the explanation, even when the corrections are factually superior.

## 4. Verdict

**False**: Glass does not flow at room temperature on any meaningful timescale. The thicker-bottom appearance of old windows results from historical manufacturing processes and deliberate installation practices, not gravitational flow over time.

The scientific consensus is unambiguous: glass is an amorphous solid with infinite viscosity at normal temperatures. Molecular analysis of ancient glass samples shows no evidence of structural changes consistent with flow, while historical documentation explains thickness variations through well-understood production methods.

This verdict is supported by mathematical calculations showing that measurable glass flow would require timeframes exceeding the age of the universe, experimental analysis of historical window samples revealing no flow-related changes, comprehensive documentation of medieval glass manufacturing processes, and molecular-level studies confirming the rigid structure of glass at room temperature.

**Supporting Evidence Classification**:
- **Definitive physical evidence**: Molecular analysis of ancient glass samples showing no structural changes
- **Mathematical proof**: Calculations demonstrating flow would require universe-scale timeframes  
- **Historical documentation**: Period sources describing crown glass manufacturing and installation practices
- **Experimental verification**: Laboratory measurements confirming infinite glass viscosity at room temperature

## 5. The Wider Picture

**Materials Science Education**: This myth highlights fundamental gaps in public understanding of materials science and states of matter. Glass represents one of many materials whose properties don't fit elementary classifications, requiring more sophisticated frameworks to understand properly. The persistence of the flow myth demonstrates the need for better science education that addresses common misconceptions directly.

Materials science has identified numerous materials with unusual properties that challenge simple solid-liquid-gas categories: liquid crystals, plasmas, Bose-Einstein condensates, and various amorphous solids. Public science literacy requires understanding that material properties exist on continua rather than in discrete categories, but this understanding must be based on accurate rather than mythical examples.

**Historical Preservation and Architecture**: Understanding the true nature of historical glass has practical implications for building preservation and restoration. Conservators need accurate information about how materials behave over time to make appropriate preservation decisions. Myths about glass flow could lead to unnecessary or inappropriate interventions based on false assumptions about structural changes.

The myth also illustrates how technical explanations for historical phenomena often become simplified into more appealing but incorrect narratives. This pattern appears across many aspects of historical interpretation, where complex technical realities get replaced by more romantically satisfying but factually wrong stories.

**Scientific Method and Authority**: The glass flow myth demonstrates how claims can gain credibility through repetition rather than evidence, highlighting the importance of primary sources and experimental verification in science education. The myth's persistence shows that authority and repetition cannot substitute for empirical evidence, no matter how widespread the misconception becomes.

This case study illustrates the crucial distinction between superficially plausible explanations and scientifically verified ones. It demonstrates why science education must emphasize the process of verification rather than just the communication of facts, since facts without verification processes leave students vulnerable to well-crafted misconceptions.

**Philosophical Implications About Time and Change**: The myth reflects deeper philosophical questions about the relationship between human timescales and physical processes. While glass doesn't flow over centuries, many other geological and astronomical processes do occur on timescales that dwarf human experience. The appeal of the glass myth may partly reflect genuine wonder about long-term physical processes.

Understanding which processes actually operate on century-to-millennium timescales—continental drift, star formation, evolutionary change—versus those that don't helps calibrate our sense of temporal scales in the natural world. This calibration is essential for understanding everything from climate change to geological history.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes Approach**: Snopes rates this claim as "False" and provides a comprehensive explanation of the scientific evidence against glass flow. Their fact-check includes expert quotes from materials scientists, mathematical calculations about glass viscosity, and historical information about medieval glass manufacturing processes. They explicitly address the cathedral window evidence and explain crown glass production methods.

Snopes emphasizes the distinction between the formation of glass (which involves cooling from a liquid state) and the behavior of glass at room temperature (which is definitively solid). Their analysis includes specific citations to peer-reviewed research and expert interviews with glass scientists and historians.

**Academic Fact-Checking**: Materials science textbooks and educational resources consistently debunk this myth, often using it as a case study in scientific misconceptions. The Corning Museum of Glass maintains educational materials specifically addressing this myth, including analysis of historical glass samples and documentation of manufacturing processes.

Professional materials science organizations have published educational resources specifically targeting this misconception, recognizing its widespread persistence despite clear scientific evidence against it. These resources typically combine historical analysis of glassmaking techniques with modern molecular-level analysis of glass structure.

**Physics Education Resources**: Physics textbooks increasingly address this myth directly, using it as an example of how intuitive explanations can be scientifically incorrect. Educational materials emphasize the importance of distinguishing between the process by which materials form and their properties once formed.

The myth has become a standard example in courses about amorphous materials and the physics of solids, demonstrating why understanding molecular structure is essential for predicting material behavior over time.

**Expert Consensus**: Materials scientists, physicists, historians of technology, and glass manufacturing experts universally reject the flow claim. This consensus spans multiple disciplines and includes both theoretical physicists and practical glassmakers, providing unusual breadth of expert agreement across different types of expertise.

The consensus extends internationally and across different institutional settings, from university physics departments to industrial glass manufacturers to museum conservation departments. This broad agreement across diverse expert communities strengthens confidence in the rejection of the flow myth.

**Media Coverage Patterns**: Science journalists increasingly treat the glass flow myth as a settled question, using it as an example of persistent scientific misconceptions rather than as a matter requiring ongoing investigation. Popular science publications regularly include it in lists of common myths, helping educate broader audiences about the importance of verifying scientific claims.

However, the myth's persistence demonstrates the challenge of correcting widespread misconceptions, even when expert consensus is clear and evidence is definitive. Media coverage of the debunking often fails to reach audiences who learned the original myth through educational or casual channels.

The glass flow myth serves as a case study in how scientific misconceptions persist despite clear evidence, the importance of understanding historical context for interpreting physical evidence, and the need for science education that addresses common myths directly rather than assuming they will disappear when correct information is presented.
