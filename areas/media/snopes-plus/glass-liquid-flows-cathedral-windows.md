# Glass Is a Slow-Moving Liquid, Which Is Why Old Windows Are Thicker at the Bottom

## 1. The Claim

Visit any medieval cathedral with its original glazing — Chartres, Cologne, Canterbury — and a well-meaning guide may explain the uneven thickness of the glass panes with an elegant story: glass is actually a supercooled liquid, still slowly flowing under gravity over the centuries, and the greater thickness at the bottom of old window panes is the visible result of this millennial creep. It is a wonderfully evocative idea: every old church window a kind of slow-motion waterfall, the glass imperceptibly descending over the centuries, the building itself a record of geological patience.

The story has several things going for it. It sounds scientific — "supercooled liquid" is an actual technical term. It offers a satisfying explanation for something genuinely observable (the uneven thickness of old glass). And it has a certain poetry: the idea that solid things are not quite as solid as they appear, that even stone and glass are, on a long enough timescale, moving.

None of it is true. Glass is not flowing in medieval windows, and the uneven thickness has a different, more prosaic explanation. But the claim's persistence and its appeal reveal something interesting about how we reason about materials, time, and the limits of our intuitions about physical categories.

## 2. What's Actually True

### The State of Glass: Amorphous Solid, Not Supercooled Liquid

The technical characterization of glass's physical state is a matter of ongoing discussion among materials scientists, but the practical conclusion for this myth is clear. Glass at room temperature is a solid — it does not flow on any humanly relevant timescale.

The confusion arises from glass's atomic structure. In a crystalline solid like quartz or table salt, atoms are arranged in a regular, repeating lattice. In glass, the atoms are arranged in a disordered, random network more like the structure of a liquid than a crystal. This disorder is what makes glass transparent (crystalline materials typically scatter light at grain boundaries), what gives it its characteristic fracture pattern, and what has led some to describe it as an "amorphous solid" or, in older literature, a "supercooled liquid."

The "supercooled liquid" label, while technically contested, was once used by some materials scientists because glass lacks the sharp melting point of a crystalline solid — it softens gradually over a range of temperatures rather than transitioning abruptly from solid to liquid. This gradual softening, combined with the liquid-like atomic arrangement, suggested to some early researchers that glass was a liquid that had been cooled below its freezing point without ever crystallizing.

Modern materials science has largely moved away from this characterization, preferring to describe glass as an amorphous solid or a glassy state — a distinct phase with properties intermediate between crystalline solids and true liquids, but behaviorally solid on all practically relevant timescales at temperatures far below the glass transition temperature.

### The Zanotto-Gupta Calculation

The decisive quantitative treatment of this myth is a 1999 paper by Edgar Dutra Zanotto and Paulo Murilo de Castro Gupta published in the *American Journal of Physics*: "Do Cathedral Glasses Flow?" Zanotto and Gupta applied the known viscosity of glass at room temperature to calculate how long it would take for medieval glass to flow measurably under gravity.

The result was unambiguous: at room temperature, soda-lime glass — the approximate composition of most window glass — has a viscosity on the order of 10²⁰ pascal-seconds. (For comparison, water has a viscosity of about 10⁻³ Pa·s.) The viscosity of glass at room temperature is roughly 10²³ times greater than the viscosity of water. Zanotto and Gupta calculated that detectable flow in glass panes at ambient temperature would require timescales on the order of 10³² years — vastly, incomprehensibly longer than the age of the universe (approximately 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years).

Even allowing for generous uncertainty in the estimates, the conclusion is robust: the difference between the relevant timescales is so enormous that no realistic refinement of the calculation could close the gap. Medieval glass has not moved since it was installed, and it will not move for a time that makes the present moment essentially the same as the moment of installation by any physically relevant standard.

This is an important kind of scientific result: not just "the effect is small" but "the effect is so small that the claim is physically nonsensical." The glass-flowing story is not approximately true; it is wrong by an astronomically large margin.

### How Medieval Glass Was Actually Made

The uneven thickness of medieval window glass is real. Many — though not all — old panes are indeed thicker at the bottom than at the top. But the explanation is manufacturing technique, not viscous flow.

Medieval glassmakers used two primary techniques for producing flat glass panes: the cylinder method and the crown method. Both produced glass that was inherently uneven in thickness.

**The cylinder method (muff glass):** A glassblower would inflate a globe of molten glass into a long cylinder, cut off the ends, slit the cylinder along its length, and then reheat it in a flat kiln until it spread out into a flat sheet. The resulting pane was thicker along the cut edges and where the cylinder had been non-uniform, and thinner in the middle sections. This method was widely used in Germany and England from roughly the tenth through eighteenth centuries.

**The crown method (crown glass):** A glassblower would gather molten glass on the end of a pontil, open it up into a flat disc by spinning rapidly — centrifugal force spreading the glass outward into a large circular pane with a characteristic central "bull's-eye" where the pontil was attached. The resulting disc was thinner near the edges, thicker toward the center, and deeply non-uniform overall. Crown glass was the dominant technique in France and England for much of the medieval and early modern period.

In both techniques, the finished panes were non-uniform in thickness. When glaziers installed these uneven panes, they made practical choices about orientation. Helmut Graeber Pfaender, in his authoritative 1996 reference *Schott Guide to Glass*, and other historians of glass technology, document that glaziers typically installed panes with the thicker, heavier portion at the bottom — the natural position for a piece of material that is wider at one end. This is not physics compelling the glass to the bottom; it is craftsmen making sensible choices about stability and fit.

Critically, not all old panes are thicker at the bottom. Survey work of medieval church windows has found panes that are thicker at the top, thicker at the sides, or uniformly thick. If flowing were the explanation, all panes should be thicker at the bottom, with the amount of thickening increasing with age. Neither prediction is confirmed by the actual data.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### The "Supercooled Liquid" Label Creates Plausible Scaffolding

Glass's categorization as an "amorphous solid" or — in older texts — a "supercooled liquid" provides the technical scaffolding for the myth. If you have heard that glass is technically a liquid, and if you know that liquids flow, and if you see that old glass panes are thicker at the bottom, the inference seems almost compelling. The premise, the mechanism, and the observation all slot together.

The problem is that the premise ("glass is a liquid") is at best a contested historical characterization, and the mechanism ("liquids flow") collapses vastly different viscosities into a single category. Water flows. Honey flows. Pitch flows (very slowly). Glass at room temperature might all be called "liquids" in some technical sense if we accept the amorphous-solid argument, but their behaviors at relevant timescales are not comparable. The category "liquid" conceals rather than reveals the physical reality.

### The Observable Evidence Points the Wrong Way

The myth is remarkable in that it is falsified, at least in principle, by the very evidence it purports to explain. If glass were flowing on a centuries-long timescale, we would expect a systematic gradient: panes installed longer ago should show more pronounced thickening at the bottom than more recently installed panes. We would expect glass panes to be measurably thicker at the bottom today than they were in measurements taken decades ago. We would expect to see evidence of the glass deforming under its own weight in other ways — bowing, stress fractures at the edges where the glass meets the frame.

None of these predictions are confirmed. The systematic survey of old windows shows thickness patterns that are consistent with manufacturing technique and glazier choice, not with viscous flow. The myth has persuaded many people not to look at the evidence carefully, but the evidence, when examined, does not support it.

### The Physical Intuition Gap

People are generally good at reasoning about fluids at viscosities they encounter in daily life — water, oil, honey, cement. Extending intuition to viscosities 10²⁵ times greater than honey requires conceptual tools that are not part of ordinary experience. The fact that any substance with a finite viscosity will eventually flow under gravity is technically true but practically meaningless when the relevant timescale exceeds the age of the universe by many orders of magnitude.

This intuition gap is a standard feature of reasoning about extreme physical parameters. People struggle with very large and very small numbers in the same way — the gut sense of "it's just very slow" cannot capture the difference between "it would take ten thousand years" and "it would take 10³² years." The latter is not just a very long time; it is a timescale that makes the former not even a rounding error in comparison.

### The Appeal of Hidden Motion in Solid Things

There is also an aesthetic dimension to the myth's appeal. The idea that solid-seeming objects are secretly in motion — that the apparent permanence of stone and glass is an illusion, that everything is process and flow rather than static substance — is both philosophically resonant and frequently invoked in poetry, religion, and philosophy. The Buddhist conception of impermanence, the Heraclitean observation that you cannot step in the same river twice, the geological insight that mountains are temporary features on a restless crust — these ideas are all variations on the theme that what appears solid is, at a deeper level, in motion.

The glass-flowing story fits this thematic tradition and draws energy from it. It feels true in the way that insights about impermanence often feel true, regardless of whether the specific physical mechanism is accurate.

### Propagation Through Science Education

The myth has achieved sufficient cultural penetration to appear in some educational materials and museum exhibits, which has given it a degree of apparent authority. When a story is told by a museum guide in a cathedral or mentioned in a school textbook, it acquires the patina of verified knowledge. The feedback loop between popular culture and educational material is not always regulated by careful fact-checking, and the glass-flowing story has benefited from this gap.

## 4. Verdict

**False on both counts — glass does not flow measurably at room temperature, and the uneven thickness of medieval windows is explained by manufacturing technique, not viscous flow.**

The Zanotto-Gupta calculation is decisive: at room temperature, any detectable flow in window glass would require timescales astronomically larger than the age of the universe. This is not a close call; it is a refutation by an incomprehensibly large margin.

The distribution of thickness in medieval windows is explained by the crown and cylinder glassmaking methods, which produced inherently uneven panes, combined with the glaziers' sensible practice of installing heavier ends at the bottom. Surveys of medieval windows find thickness distributions inconsistent with systematic downward flow.

The grain of truth is that glass's atomic structure resembles a liquid's, which is genuinely interesting materials science, and that glass does flow — just only at temperatures close to its glass transition temperature (above roughly 500°C for common glass), not at room temperature.

Both the mechanism (flowing glass) and the explanation (this mechanism accounts for thickness patterns in old windows) are rejected.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### Materials Science and the Liquid-Solid Boundary

The glass myth is a good vehicle for explaining why the solid-liquid distinction is less crisp than it appears in introductory physics courses. Crystalline solids have sharp melting points and well-defined atomic structures. Amorphous solids like glass, pitch, tar, and many plastics lack these features — they soften over a range of temperatures, and their atomic arrangements are disordered. This does make them physically distinct from crystalline solids in ways that are real and scientifically interesting.

The glass transition temperature — the temperature below which a glass-forming substance behaves as a solid and above which it behaves as a supercooled liquid — is a genuine phenomenon with significant industrial and scientific importance. Polymer glasses, metallic glasses, and oxide glasses (of which window glass is an example) all exhibit glass transitions. Understanding these transitions has practical applications in materials design, food science, and pharmaceutical formulation.

None of this means that glass at room temperature flows. The glass transition for common window glass is around 500-600°C. At room temperature, the glass is not near its transition; it is enormously far from it. The interesting physics of amorphous solids does not translate into flowing cathedral windows.

### The Pitch Drop Experiment

There is a real material that flows very slowly under gravity and can be observed doing so on a human timescale: pitch. The University of Queensland pitch drop experiment, begun in 1927 by Professor Thomas Parnell, involved placing pitch in a funnel and waiting for it to drip. The first drop fell in 1938 — eleven years after setup. Subsequent drops have fallen approximately every nine to thirteen years. This is genuinely slow flow, observable on a human timescale, and has been confirmed by direct measurement.

Glass is incomprehensibly more viscous than pitch. If glass behaved like pitch — dripping one drop per decade — it would be a different substance with different applications. The pitch drop experiment is the thing the glass-flowing myth imagines glass to be. Glass is not that thing.

The pitch drop experiment is also notable for its own reasons: it won the 2005 Ig Nobel Prize in Physics, and it was only in 2013 that a drop was actually observed falling in real time, after the experiment had been running for 86 years. It is an instructive example of viscous flow that is genuinely slow but still incomparably faster than anything glass does at room temperature.

### Manufacturing as a Source of Historical Evidence

The medieval glass manufacturing context raises a broader point: many features of historical artifacts that appear to require exotic physical explanations are in fact fully explained by the manufacturing techniques available at the time. Uneven pottery glazes, asymmetries in hand-forged metal, dimensional variations in early printed books — these features arise from the limits and characteristic properties of pre-industrial craft production, not from subsequent physical processes acting on the finished object.

This is worth keeping in mind more generally. The impulse to explain observable features of old objects by invoking physical processes acting over long timescales — the glass flowing, the pigments migrating, the wood slowly changing shape — is sometimes correct (wood does move with humidity cycles; metal does corrode; organic pigments do fade). But the first hypothesis should be the manufacturing hypothesis: could this feature have been present from the beginning, as a consequence of how the object was made?

In the glass case, the answer is unambiguously yes. The manufacturing techniques of medieval glassmakers produced uneven panes as a routine consequence of the process, not as an aberration. The question "why is this glass uneven?" is answered by the history of technology, not by the physics of viscous flow.

### The Value of Order-of-Magnitude Reasoning

The Zanotto-Gupta result also illustrates the value of back-of-envelope quantitative reasoning for cutting through plausible-sounding but wrong claims. The viscosity of glass at room temperature is a known quantity. The geometry of a window pane and the force of gravity are known. A calculation taking perhaps an hour to perform yielded a result — 10³² years — that definitively settled the question. No elaborate experiment was required; the math was accessible with known parameters.

This kind of order-of-magnitude reasoning is a standard tool in physics but is less commonly applied in popular discussions of claims about the physical world. The glass-flowing story could have been definitively refuted by anyone with access to a viscosity handbook and a willingness to do the arithmetic. That it persisted as long as it did in educated discourse — appearing in museum exhibits, textbooks, and casual scientific conversation — reflects not a failure of physics but a failure to apply physics to a popular claim.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

### The Standard Debunking

The glass-flowing myth is now a standard subject in science fact-checking, and the consensus verdict is clear: false. The Zanotto-Gupta paper from 1999 has become the canonical reference, and most treatments cite it or the argument it makes. The manufacturing explanation for uneven medieval windows is also standard in these treatments.

Better fact-checks distinguish between the two parts of the claim: (1) is glass a slowly-flowing liquid? and (2) is that why medieval windows are thicker at the bottom? Answering no to (1) does not technically rule out that (2) could be true for some other reason, though in practice both are wrong.

### The Materials Science Nuance

A few treatments, particularly those aimed at scientifically sophisticated audiences, engage with the amorphous solid / supercooled liquid debate and explain why the technical complexity in categorizing glass's state does not rescue the flowing-window claim. The key insight is that even if one accepts the "supercooled liquid" characterization in some technical sense, the viscosity at room temperature is so high that flow is not a physically meaningful description of the material's behavior on any humanly relevant timescale.

This distinction — between a technically accurate description of atomic structure and the behaviorally relevant properties at a given temperature — is worth making explicitly because it is where the popular myth's plausibility comes from. The error is not in saying glass has a liquid-like atomic structure; the error is in inferring from this that it flows.

### What Good Fact-Checking Adds

The best treatments of this myth add what the standard debunking often omits: the positive account of medieval glass manufacturing and why we would expect to see exactly the patterns of thickness variation we observe, independent of any viscous flow. Without this positive account, the debunking leaves a vacuum — if the glass isn't flowing, why is it thicker at the bottom? — that can be filled by the original myth again.

Good fact-checking is not just negation; it is replacement. The glass case is an example of a myth that requires a replacement explanation to be stably falsified in popular understanding.
