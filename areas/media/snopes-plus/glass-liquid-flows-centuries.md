# Glass Is a Slow-Moving Liquid, Which Is Why Old Windows Are Thicker at the Bottom

## §1. The Claim

The claim is durable, elegant, and almost irresistible: glass, despite its rigid appearance, is actually a liquid — a very, very slow-moving one. Over centuries, it flows imperceptibly downward under the influence of gravity. The proof, so the story goes, is right in front of us: examine medieval cathedral windows and you will find that the panes are consistently thicker at the bottom than at the top. This thickness gradient, the claim asserts, is the visible signature of liquid flow accumulated over hundreds of years.

The myth circulates in several recognizable variants. In its most common form, it appears as an authoritative-sounding factoid passed along by science teachers, tour guides standing in the naves of Gothic cathedrals, and authors of popular science columns. A more sophisticated version frames the claim in terms of thermodynamics: glass, it is said, never truly solidifies but instead exists as a "supercooled liquid," a substance caught in an arrested transition between liquid and solid states. Because it never crosses a true phase boundary, the argument continues, it retains some residual capacity to flow — just very, very slowly.

The claim has an unusually wide cultural reach. It appears in casual conversation, in university lecture halls, in newspaper science sections, and in internet listicles of "surprising facts." It has been attributed, without strong sourcing, to various physics professors and glass scientists. The urban-legend scholar Jan Harold Brunvand included it among the class of scientific myths that persist because they sound plausible to educated audiences (Brunvand, J.H., 2001, *Encyclopedia of Urban Legends*, ABC-CLIO). It circulates with particular vigor in educational contexts, precisely because it seems to offer a memorable, hands-on illustration of an otherwise abstract concept — the nature of the glassy state.

Why does it stick? Several factors converge. First, the claim has a visible piece of evidence attached to it: old windows really are uneven. Anyone who has visited a medieval church can confirm the observation. Second, the claim offers a surprising, counterintuitive insight — the kind of fact that makes the teller feel clever and the listener feel educated. It inverts an everyday assumption (glass is solid) with a sophisticated correction (actually, it's a liquid). Third, the underlying science is genuinely complex. The nature of the glassy state is a live topic in condensed matter physics, and the boundary between "solid" and "liquid" is less straightforward than most people assume. The myth exploits a real area of scientific nuance, which gives it a patina of legitimacy that simpler falsehoods lack.

The combination of visible evidence, counterintuitive payoff, and genuine scientific complexity makes this one of the most persistent physics myths in popular culture. It is a textbook case of what might be called an *epistemically armored falsehood* — a claim whose resistance to correction derives not from ignorance but from a superficial alignment with real knowledge.

## §2. What's Actually True

Snopes rates the claim **False**, and this verdict is correct. Glass is not a liquid, and the thickness variations in medieval windows are not evidence of gravitational flow. The rating is well-supported by the scientific consensus as it has existed since at least the late 1990s.

What Snopes gets right is the core debunking. The site correctly identifies two key facts: first, that the uneven thickness of medieval glass panes is an artifact of historical manufacturing processes, not a consequence of flow over time; and second, that glass at room temperature is, for all practical purposes, a rigid solid whose viscosity is so astronomically high that measurable flow would require timescales far exceeding the age of the universe, let alone the age of any cathedral.

These two points are sufficient to debunk the claim, and Snopes presents them clearly. The factual architecture is sound.

What the Snopes treatment characteristically omits — not through error but through the inherent constraints of the fact-checking format — is the deeper story. The format demands a binary verdict: True or False. And the claim is indeed false. But the *reason* it persists is not simple ignorance. It persists because the underlying physics is genuinely complicated, because the categorical status of glass has been a matter of serious scientific debate, and because the visual evidence (uneven medieval windows) is real even though its cause has been misidentified.

Snopes does not, for instance, engage with the thermodynamic subtlety that glass lacks a well-defined melting point, or that the glass transition itself remains one of the deepest unsolved problems in condensed matter physics. It does not discuss why the "supercooled liquid" framing, while misleading for popular audiences, has a legitimate technical meaning in certain contexts. And it does not explore the materials-science history that produced those uneven window panes in the first place — a history that is itself a fascinating story about the relationship between craft knowledge and industrial standardization.

The gap here is not a gap in accuracy. It is a gap in *explanatory depth*. And it is precisely this gap that the Observatory's Truth Vault methodology is designed to fill.

## §3. Why People Believe This

### The Category Problem

The persistence of the glass-as-liquid myth is rooted in a genuine philosophical and physical puzzle: what *is* glass, exactly?

In the standard taxonomy taught in introductory chemistry and physics, matter exists in three familiar phases — solid, liquid, and gas — distinguished by their molecular ordering. Crystalline solids have long-range periodic order: their atoms or molecules sit in repeating lattice arrangements. Liquids lack long-range order but have short-range correlations and the ability to flow under applied stress. Gases lack both long-range and short-range order.

Glass fits uncomfortably into this scheme. Structurally, glass resembles a liquid: its atoms are arranged in a disordered, non-periodic fashion, lacking the long-range translational symmetry of a crystal. But mechanically, glass behaves like a solid: it is rigid, brittle, and elastic. It fractures rather than flows under ordinary conditions. This dual character — liquid-like structure, solid-like mechanics — is the source of the confusion.

The technical term for glass is *amorphous solid*. It is a solid in the mechanical sense (it resists shear stress and does not flow on any humanly observable timescale) but it is non-crystalline in its atomic arrangement. The glass transition — the process by which a liquid becomes a glass upon cooling — is not a first-order phase transition like freezing. There is no latent heat, no discontinuity in volume or entropy, no sharp boundary between the liquid and glassy states. Instead, the liquid's viscosity increases continuously and dramatically as it cools, until molecular rearrangement becomes so slow that the material is effectively frozen on any practical timescale (Debenedetti, P.G. & Stillinger, F.H., 2001, "Supercooled Liquids and the Glass Transition," *Nature*, 410, 259–267).

This absence of a sharp phase boundary is the crux of the problem. If there is no thermodynamic discontinuity marking the transition from liquid to glass, in what sense has the material truly "become" a solid? The glass transition temperature, T_g, is not a fixed thermodynamic quantity but a kinetic one: it depends on the cooling rate. Cool the same material more slowly, and T_g shifts to a lower temperature. This rate-dependence is unlike anything in the crystallization of, say, water to ice, and it has led to genuine debate about whether glass should be classified as a solid, a liquid, or something else entirely.

### The "Supercooled Liquid" Framing

The phrase "supercooled liquid" has a precise technical meaning in thermodynamics: it refers to a liquid cooled below its equilibrium melting point without crystallizing. In this strict sense, the liquid phase of a glass-forming material that has been cooled below T_m (the melting temperature of its crystalline polymorph) but above T_g is indeed a supercooled liquid. The material is metastable — it "wants" to crystallize but is kinetically prevented from doing so.

Below T_g, however, the situation changes. The material falls out of metastable equilibrium: its structure is no longer exploring the full landscape of available configurations. It is "frozen" into a particular disordered arrangement. Whether this frozen state should still be called a "supercooled liquid" or is more properly described as a distinct state of matter — the glassy state — has been debated for decades. Anderson, P.W. (1995, *Science*, 267, 1615–1616) famously described the glass transition as "the deepest and most interesting unsolved problem in solid state theory."

The myth exploits this ambiguity. If scientists themselves sometimes refer to glasses as "supercooled liquids," then it seems a small step to say that glass can flow. But this conflates thermodynamic classification with mechanical behavior. A material can be thermodynamically metastable (in the sense that a more stable crystalline phase exists) while being mechanically rigid on any timescale relevant to human experience.

### The Visual Evidence

The myth's most powerful weapon is the visible evidence. Medieval window panes really are thicker at the bottom — sometimes. Tour guides point to them; visitors see them with their own eyes. The mechanism seems obvious: the glass flowed downward.

But the observation itself is unreliable as evidence for flow. Studies of medieval windows have found that the thickness variations are inconsistent: some panes are thicker at the bottom, some at the top, some at the sides (Zanotto, E.D., 1998, "Do Cathedral Glasses Flow?" *American Journal of Physics*, 66, 392–395). If gravitational flow were the cause, all panes would be thicker at the bottom — and the thicker edge would always be at the lowest point regardless of how the pane was oriented in the frame. The inconsistency immediately refutes the flow hypothesis.

The actual cause of the unevenness is manufacturing. Medieval flat glass was produced using the crown glass process, in which a blown glass bubble was spun into a flat disc. The resulting disc had a natural thickness gradient — thinner at the edges, thicker near the central "bull's-eye" where the pontil rod was attached. When glaziers cut panes from these discs, the pieces inherited uneven thickness. When installing them, glaziers would typically place the thicker, heavier edge at the bottom for structural stability — a sensible installation practice that coincidentally creates the appearance of downward flow (Rasmussen, S.C., 2012, *How Glass Changed the World*, Springer).

### Plausibility Mapping

Cognitive science offers insight into why the myth is so tenacious. The claim succeeds because it maps a familiar observation (uneven windows) onto a plausible-sounding mechanism (slow liquid flow) via a genuine scientific nuance (the ambiguous status of glass). This three-layer structure — observation, mechanism, and supporting technical context — is characteristic of what Mercier, H. & Sperber, D. (2017, *The Enigma of Reason*, Harvard University Press) describe as argumentatively coherent beliefs: beliefs that persist not because of direct evidence but because they can be defended with a plausible chain of reasoning.

The myth also benefits from what Rozenblit, L. & Keil, F. (2002, "The Misunderstood Limits of Folk Science," *Cognitive Science*, 26, 521–562) call the "illusion of explanatory depth" — the widespread tendency to overestimate one's understanding of complex physical processes. People who cannot articulate the glass transition temperature or define viscosity in formal terms nevertheless feel confident that they understand why glass flows. The myth provides just enough technical scaffolding to sustain this feeling.

## §4. Verdict

**Propositional status:** The claim that glass is a slow-moving liquid whose gravitational flow explains the thickness of old windows is **false**.

**Confidence:** Very high. The scientific evidence is unambiguous and has been stable for more than two decades. There is no credible dissent in the materials-science literature on this point.

**Epistemic basis:** The verdict rests on two independent lines of evidence: (1) empirical measurements showing that the viscosity of common soda-lime-silica glass at room temperature is approximately 10^20 Pa·s, which would require timescales exceeding the age of the universe for any perceptible flow (Zanotto, E.D. & Gupta, P.K., 1999, "Do Cathedral Glasses Flow? — Additional Remarks," *American Journal of Physics*, 67, 260–262); and (2) the historical-technological explanation of medieval glassmaking, which fully accounts for the observed thickness variations without invoking flow.

**Phenomenological status:** The observation that motivates the myth — uneven medieval windows — is real. The phenomenon exists. What is false is the causal attribution.

**Mechanistic status:** The proposed mechanism (gravitational flow of a supercooled liquid) is physically incoherent at the relevant temperatures and timescales. The viscosity of window glass at room temperature is roughly 10^17 to 10^21 Pa·s depending on composition, orders of magnitude above any threshold at which gravitational flow could produce measurable displacement in historical time (Doremus, R.H., 1994, *Glass Science*, 2nd ed., Wiley).

**Folk-wisdom status:** The claim functions as a prestige factoid — something people share to signal scientific literacy. Its persistence reflects genuine gaps in public understanding of the glass transition rather than willful ignorance. It is a teaching opportunity, not merely an error to correct.

## §5. The Wider Picture

### Historical Glassmaking: From Crown to Float

The story of window glass is, in large part, the story of the struggle to make flat things out of a material that wants to be round.

The earliest flat glass was made by the **crown glass** process, practiced in Europe from at least the 14th century. In this method, a glassblower gathered a ball of molten glass, blew it into a hollow sphere, then transferred it to a pontil rod and spun it rapidly in a reheating furnace. Centrifugal force flattened the sphere into a disc, typically 1.0 to 1.5 meters in diameter. The resulting disc — called a "crown" or "table" — was thicker at the center (near the pontil mark) and thinner at the edges. Rectangular panes were cut from this disc, each inheriting a slight thickness gradient. The central bullseye, being the thickest and most distorted portion, was the cheapest piece and was often used in less prestigious locations or houses of lower social standing (Macfarlane, A. & Martin, G., 2002, *Glass: A World History*, University of Chicago Press).

A later improvement was the **broad sheet** or **cylinder glass** process, in which a glassblower created an elongated cylinder, cut off the ends, slit the cylinder lengthwise, and reheated it until it unrolled into a flat(ish) sheet. This produced somewhat more uniform glass, but the panes were still far from perfectly flat. Both methods were labor-intensive and inherently produced glass with thickness variations.

The modern era of flat glass begins with Sir Alastair Pilkington's invention of the **float glass process** at the Pilkington Brothers factory in St Helens, Lancashire, in 1952 (with the first commercially viable production line operating by 1959). In this process, molten glass is poured continuously onto a bath of molten tin. Because the glass is less dense than tin, it floats on the surface and spreads into a perfectly flat, uniform sheet, its thickness controlled by the speed of the conveyor that draws it off the tin bath. The float process produces glass of extraordinary flatness and optical quality — surfaces parallel to within a fraction of a wavelength of visible light. It is the process used for virtually all flat glass manufactured today (Pilkington, L.A.B., 1969, "Review Lecture: The Float Glass Process," *Proceedings of the Royal Society of London A*, 314, 1–25).

The transition from crown glass to float glass is directly relevant to the myth. Anyone comparing a medieval crown-glass window to a modern float-glass window will immediately notice the difference in uniformity. The old glass is wavy, bubbly, and uneven. The modern glass is flawless. It is natural to attribute this difference to the passage of time — to assume that the old glass has *become* distorted rather than that it was *made* distorted. The myth, in this sense, is a failure to consider the null hypothesis: that the starting conditions, not subsequent processes, explain the observations.

### Zanotto's Viscosity Calculations

The definitive quantitative refutation of the glass-flow myth was provided by Edgar Dutra Zanotto in a pair of papers in the *American Journal of Physics*. In the first paper (Zanotto, E.D., 1998, "Do Cathedral Glasses Flow?" *American Journal of Physics*, 66, 392–395), Zanotto estimated the relaxation time of medieval soda-lime-silica glass at ambient temperature. The relaxation time is a measure of how long it takes a material to undergo significant structural reorganization — essentially, how long you would have to wait to see flow.

Zanotto's estimate placed the relaxation time of window glass at room temperature on the order of 10^32 years — far exceeding the age of the universe (approximately 1.4 × 10^10 years) by a factor of more than 10^21. The conclusion was stark: at ambient temperature, cathedral glass simply cannot have flowed by any measurable amount since the windows were installed, whether those windows are 200 years old or 2,000.

In the follow-up paper (Zanotto, E.D. & Gupta, P.K., 1999, "Do Cathedral Glasses Flow? — Additional Remarks," *American Journal of Physics*, 67, 260–262), Zanotto and Gupta refined the analysis and addressed several objections. They considered the effects of compositional variation, temperature fluctuations, and stress-enhanced flow. In every case, the conclusion held: the timescales required for observable flow were many orders of magnitude beyond anything relevant to human history.

These papers are not controversial. They represent the settled consensus of the materials-science community on the question of whether old glass flows. The myth, in the face of this evidence, is not merely unlikely — it is physically absurd.

### Doremus and Glass Science

Robert H. Doremus's authoritative textbook *Glass Science* (1994, 2nd ed., Wiley) provides the broader context for understanding glass viscosity. Doremus describes how viscosity in glass-forming liquids increases by roughly 15 orders of magnitude between the melting temperature and the glass transition temperature. Below T_g, the viscosity continues to increase, though the material is no longer in internal equilibrium. At room temperature, the viscosity of soda-lime glass reaches approximately 10^20 Pa·s — comparable to the viscosity of the Earth's mantle.

For comparison, water has a viscosity of approximately 10^-3 Pa·s at room temperature. Honey is roughly 10 Pa·s. Pitch — the famously slow-flowing material used in the University of Queensland's pitch drop experiment (Edgeworth, R., Dalton, B.J. & Parnell, T., 1984, "The Pitch Drop Experiment," *European Journal of Physics*, 5, 198–200) — has a viscosity of about 10^8 Pa·s. Window glass is therefore approximately 10^12 times more viscous than pitch, a material that produces one drop every eight to thirteen years in the Queensland experiment. By straightforward scaling, even if glass could be treated as a Newtonian fluid at room temperature, one would need to wait trillions of years to observe a comparably tiny displacement.

### The Myth as a Teaching Case

The glass-flow myth has become, somewhat ironically, more valuable as a pedagogical tool in its debunked form than it ever was as a supposed fact. It illustrates several important principles simultaneously.

First, it demonstrates the danger of *post hoc ergo propter hoc* reasoning: old windows are uneven *and* old, but the unevenness is not *because* they are old. Second, it illustrates the importance of considering alternative explanations before attributing a phenomenon to a favored mechanism — in this case, the manufacturing explanation is simpler, better supported, and consistent with all the evidence, while the flow explanation is not.

Third, and most importantly for condensed matter physics education, it provides an accessible entry point into the glass transition problem — one of the genuinely deep open questions in physics. The myth fails, but the question it raises (what is glass, exactly?) is excellent. Instructors who begin with the myth and work backward through the debunking arrive naturally at the glass transition, at the distinction between thermodynamic and kinetic definitions of phase, and at the fascinating borderlands between order and disorder in condensed matter (Angell, C.A., 1995, "Formation of Glasses from Liquids and Biopolymers," *Science*, 267, 1924–1935).

## §6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes approaches the glass-as-liquid claim with its characteristic format: state the claim, rate it, provide a concise explanation. The site correctly identifies the key facts — glass does not flow at room temperature on any relevant timescale, and the thickness of old windows is explained by manufacturing techniques. The verdict of **False** is delivered cleanly and unambiguously.

What Snopes does well is accessibility. The explanation is written for a general audience, avoids unnecessary jargon, and provides enough context for a reader to understand *why* the claim is false without requiring a background in materials science. This is the fundamental gift of the fact-checking genre: it provides clear, actionable answers to specific questions.

What the format inherently constrains is the *depth of engagement* with the underlying science. Snopes does not, and cannot reasonably be expected to, explain why the myth is plausible — why the categorical status of glass is genuinely murky, why "supercooled liquid" is a real term with a real referent, or why the glass transition represents one of the deepest unsolved problems in condensed matter physics. The fact-checking format prioritizes verdict over nuance, and for good reason: most readers want to know if the claim is true, not why the question is harder than it looks.

But this compression has consequences. A reader who encounters only the Snopes debunking may come away thinking that the claim is simply stupid — an obvious error that no scientifically literate person would believe. This is misleading. The claim is wrong, but it is *sophisticated* wrong. It fails for subtle reasons that touch on real physics, and a significant fraction of its believers are people with genuine scientific education who were taught the incorrect claim by well-meaning instructors who themselves did not fully understand the glass transition.

The deeper fact-checking challenge — one that Snopes cannot easily address within its operational constraints — is distinguishing between claims that are false because they contradict straightforward evidence and claims that are false but *grounded in genuine observational and theoretical complexity*. The glass-flow myth belongs firmly in the second category. It is false, but it is not trivially false. The observation is real, the underlying physics is complicated, and the incorrect conclusion follows from a plausible (if ultimately wrong) chain of reasoning.
