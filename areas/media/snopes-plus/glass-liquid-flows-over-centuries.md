# Glass is a liquid that flows over centuries, making old windows thicker at the bottom

## 1. The Claim

The claim is a pleasing piece of popular science: glass, it is said, is not truly a solid but a supercooled liquid with extraordinarily high viscosity. Because it is still technically flowing, just at a rate imperceptible to human observation, old glass panes show direct physical evidence of this movement — they are noticeably thicker at the bottom than at the top, because over centuries, the material has crept downward under gravity. Medieval cathedral windows are frequently cited as the prime exhibit. Look closely at the panes, the story goes, and you will see that the glass is not uniform: the lower portions are measurably thicker. This is the glass, still moving, after seven or eight hundred years.

The claim circulates widely and does so in contexts that give it apparent authority. It appears in museum docent talks, in science communication columns, in middle-school science curricula, in pub trivia, and in popular books about physics. It has the quality of a good science story: it upends intuition (glass seems solid), invokes a long time scale (centuries), and connects an everyday observation (thick-bottomed old windows) to a surprising mechanism (ongoing molecular flow). It treats counterintuitive scientific knowledge as more real than naïve appearances. These qualities make it memorable and socially transferable in a way that more qualified or complex truths are not.

The claim comes in varying degrees of strength. The strong version says glass is a liquid. The moderate version says glass is technically a supercooled liquid or that it occupies an ambiguous category between solid and liquid. The weakest version says only that old glass is thicker at the bottom and that this might have something to do with flow. Each version contains different mixtures of error and distortion of genuine scientific complexity.

## 2. What's Actually True

**Glass is an amorphous solid, not a liquid.**

The classification of glass as a "supercooled liquid" is an outdated and contested description that has been largely abandoned in modern materials science. The contemporary understanding is that glass is an amorphous solid — a material in which the atomic or molecular structure is disordered and lacks the long-range periodicity of a crystal, but which nonetheless behaves mechanically as a solid. It has a definite shape, it supports shear stress without flowing, and it fractures rather than deforms plastically under applied stress. These are solid behaviors.

The confusion arises from a historical use of the term "supercooled liquid" that was common in materials science before the thermodynamic and kinetic understanding of the glass transition was fully developed. When a molten material is cooled, it can either crystallize — atoms settling into a regular lattice structure — or, if cooled rapidly enough to bypass crystallization, enter a progressively more viscous disordered state that eventually arrests into a glass. Because the glass transition does not involve a sharp thermodynamic phase transition equivalent to the solid-liquid boundary in crystalline materials, early theorists sometimes described glass as a liquid that had been cooled below its crystallization point while remaining disordered. The "supercooled liquid" label attached to this interpretation.

Modern condensed matter physics views the glass transition differently. The transition from a liquid-like to a solid-like amorphous material is now understood as primarily a kinetic phenomenon — the mobility of atoms or molecules falls so dramatically with decreasing temperature that the material's structural rearrangements cannot keep pace with the cooling rate, and the disordered structure is effectively frozen in. The resulting material is not a slow-moving liquid; it is a mechanically solid material whose microstructure happens to be amorphous rather than crystalline. Whether glass is strictly a solid or occupies a distinct category is still discussed in specialized thermodynamics literature, but the practical and mechanical answer is clear: glass at room temperature is a solid for all physical purposes.

**The viscosity numbers are definitive.**

The viscosity of common soda-lime glass at room temperature is approximately 10²⁰ to 10²⁴ pascal-seconds, depending on composition and precise temperature. For comparison, water has a viscosity of about 10⁻³ pascal-seconds and honey about 10 pascal-seconds. Pitch — famously demonstrated to flow in the University of Queensland pitch drop experiment, which has documented approximately nine drops over more than ninety years — has a viscosity of roughly 10⁸ pascal-seconds. Glass at room temperature is approximately ten trillion to ten quadrillion times more viscous than pitch.

Edgar D. Zanotto, a materials scientist at the Federal University of São Carlos in Brazil, published a calculation in the American Journal of Physics in 1998 that has become a standard reference on this question. Zanotto calculated the time scale over which medieval glass could be expected to show visible flow under gravity. His result: approximately 10²³ to 10²⁵ years. The current age of the universe is approximately 1.4 × 10¹⁰ years — roughly 14 billion years. Zanotto's figure exceeds the age of the universe by about twelve to fifteen orders of magnitude. Even accounting for variations in glass composition and temperature, the fundamental conclusion is inescapable: room-temperature glass does not flow on any timescale relevant to human artifacts, human history, or even the history of the Earth.

**The old windows are thicker at the bottom for a manufacturing reason.**

The most commonly cited evidence for glass flow — medieval cathedral windows, thicker at the bottom — has a straightforward mechanical explanation rooted in pre-industrial glassmaking.

Before the development of drawn glass production in the twentieth century, flat window glass was made by one of two primary methods: crown glass and cylinder glass (also called broad glass).

In the crown glass process, a glassblower gathered molten glass on a blowpipe, blew it into a bulb, cut the bulb open, and then reheated the piece and spun it rapidly. Centrifugal force spread the glass into a roughly circular flat disk. The disk was thickest near the center, where the pontil was attached, and thinnest at the outer edges. When the disk was cut into panes for use in windows, the glazier was left with pieces of varying thickness. The irregularity was intrinsic to the manufacturing process.

In the cylinder glass process, a blower made a large elongated bubble of glass, cut off the ends to create a cylinder, then cut the cylinder along its length and allowed it to flatten into a sheet in a furnace. Cylinder-blown sheets were more uniform than crown glass but still varied in thickness across the piece due to the mechanics of the blowing and flattening process. Thicker regions were not distributed in a predictable direction.

When glaziers installed these uneven panes, they routinely placed the thicker edge downward. There are practical reasons for this: a pane with more mass at the bottom is more stable in the frame, less likely to rock or rattle in the wind, and less likely to slide downward under its own weight during installation or over time in a loosely fitted frame. Glaziers chose this orientation deliberately, not because the glass had moved.

Studies of medieval stained glass windows — including surveys conducted for the purpose of testing the flow hypothesis — have found that thick-bottomed panes are more common than would be expected if orientation were random, consistent with deliberate placement. This is precisely what the manufacturing hypothesis predicts: glaziers put the heavy end down. There is no evidence of glass flow in these windows.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**The story has the structure of a satisfying scientific revelation.**

The glass-is-a-liquid claim follows a narrative template that is maximally persuasive in science communication: it takes something familiar and reveals that our intuitive category for it is wrong. Glass seems solid, therefore telling someone it is actually a liquid feels like genuine enlightenment. The story pairs this with an observable fact — the thickness variation in old windows — that appears to provide direct physical evidence. This is exactly the kind of claim that gets enthusiastically shared because it makes the sharer seem knowledgeable and the listener feel they have learned something profound.

Misinformation that takes the form of "the real truth is counterintuitive" is particularly durable because it comes pre-equipped with a rhetorical defense: anyone who disputes it seems to be defending naive common sense against sophisticated science.

**It contains a genuine kernel of scientific complexity.**

Glass does lack crystalline structure. It does not have a sharp melting point in the way that ice does; it softens gradually over a range of temperatures. The glass transition is real, scientifically interesting, and genuinely not fully understood at a fundamental level. Physicists continue to publish on the thermodynamics and kinetics of amorphous solids and the glass transition. The claim is therefore not pure invention — it is a simplification of real scientific complexity that distorts the conclusion while retaining just enough genuine content to resist easy dismissal.

This is what places it in the distorted-but-grounded category. The ground truth involves real materials science. The distortion is severe: "glass is technically unusual and the glass transition is still theoretically interesting" becomes "glass is a liquid that flows."

**Historical science communication made the claim credible.**

The "supercooled liquid" terminology was used by physicists and materials scientists for decades. Popular science books, museum exhibits, and educational materials repeated it. Once embedded in science education materials, claims have long half-lives: a textbook from the 1960s that describes glass as a supercooled liquid shapes the knowledge of multiple generations of students, who then communicate it onward. The error propagated through legitimate educational channels.

**The old windows observation seems like direct evidence.**

The thickness variation in medieval windows is real and visually verifiable. Visitors to medieval churches can see it. Tour guides and docents encounter it regularly and, without a manufacturing history background, reach for the culturally available explanation — glass flow — which they have heard from other guides, read in books, or encountered in museum materials. The claim has propagated partly because it provides a seemingly satisfying explanation for an actually observable phenomenon.

The manufacturing explanation — crown glass and cylinder glass production, glaziers installing panes thick-side-down — is not intuitively accessible. It requires historical knowledge about pre-industrial glassmaking that most people do not have. The flow explanation is cognitively cheaper. In the absence of the manufacturing history, the flow story wins by default.

**Authority laundering.**

The claim has been repeated by physicists in public communication contexts, sometimes carelessly. When scientists use "supercooled liquid" as a colloquial shorthand for "amorphous solid with interesting thermodynamic properties," non-specialist audiences hear it as confirmation that glass is a liquid. The authority of the speaker transfers to the oversimplification. This is a general failure mode in science communication: technical vocabulary used loosely in public contexts carries technical authority without technical precision.

## 4. Verdict

**FALSE on the core mechanism; DISTORTED on the underlying science.**

The specific claim — that glass flows over centuries and that old windows are thicker at the bottom because of this flow — is false. Both components fail:

1. **Glass does not flow at room temperature on any observable timescale.** Zanotto's calculation establishes that visible flow in medieval glass would require 10²³ to 10²⁵ years. This is not a matter of scientific controversy; it follows directly from measured viscosity values.

2. **Old windows are thicker at the bottom because of manufacturing methods, not flow.** Crown glass and cylinder glass production created uneven sheets, and glaziers deliberately installed panes thick-side-down for stability. Archaeological and historical surveys confirm this pattern.

The underlying science — that glass is an amorphous solid, that it lacks crystalline structure, that the glass transition is thermodynamically interesting, that the classification of glass has historically been contested in materials science — is real and worth communicating accurately. The accurate version is genuinely interesting. The error lies in the leap from "glass is amorphous and unusual" to "glass is a liquid that flows."

The EARC classification is E (Exaggerated) because the claim does not invent a false phenomenon from nothing — it takes a genuine scientific complexity (the unusual phase classification of amorphous solids) and inflates it into a mechanistic claim that the data do not support.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**Amorphous solids and the glass transition are genuinely fascinating.**

What glass actually is — at a deep level — remains an active area of research. The glass transition, the process by which a viscous liquid slows and arrests into a glassy solid as temperature decreases, is sometimes described as one of the deepest unsolved problems in condensed matter physics. The transition does not correspond to a sharp thermodynamic singularity (like the liquid-to-crystal transition), yet it produces a qualitative change in material behavior. Understanding why the relaxation time of a material diverges so dramatically as it approaches the glass transition temperature, and whether there is an underlying thermodynamic phase transition hidden beneath the kinetic phenomenon, is the subject of a substantial research literature.

This context matters for evaluating the popular claim. Physicists who describe glass as "technically not a solid" or as "occupying a boundary state" are not necessarily wrong in a deep thermodynamic sense. The honest statement is that glass is mechanically and practically solid — it does not flow, it maintains shape, it fractures rather than deforms — while its microstructure and thermodynamic classification are genuinely complex. The popular version collapses all of this nuance into "glass is a liquid," which takes the thermodynamic complexity and discards the mechanically important facts.

**The manufacturing history of glass is rich and underappreciated.**

The transition from hand-blown crown glass to drawn flat glass — the process now used to make virtually all flat glass globally — was a significant technological development. The Fourcault process (1905), the Colburn/Libbey-Owens process (1917), and the float glass process developed by Alastair Pilkington at Pilkington Brothers in 1959 each represented step-changes in the uniformity, scale, and cost of glass production. Float glass, in which molten glass is floated on a bath of molten tin and solidifies into a perfectly flat sheet of uniform thickness, made the uneven panes of earlier centuries an artifact of history. Understanding why medieval windows were uneven requires knowing what manufacturing methods preceded float glass.

The fact that pre-industrial glass was made by labor-intensive hand processes that inherently produced variable thickness is not widely known among non-specialists. This knowledge gap leaves the field open for the flow hypothesis, which provides an explanation that does not require historical manufacturing knowledge.

**What the Zanotto paper established and how it changed the conversation.**

Before Zanotto's 1998 paper, the glass-is-a-liquid claim had been circulating for decades without a precise quantitative refutation widely accessible to non-specialists. The paper provided exactly that: a clear calculation, using measured viscosity data for medieval glass compositions, of the time scale for observable flow. The result — a figure exceeding the age of the universe by many orders of magnitude — was dramatic enough to function as a definitive rebuttal even in popular communication contexts. The paper was published in the American Journal of Physics, which is specifically aimed at physics educators, a choice that accelerated its uptake in teaching contexts.

Zanotto subsequently published additional work on this topic and has been active in public communication about glass science, including direct engagement with the popular claim. His consistent message is that glass is best described as an amorphous solid and that the "supercooled liquid" terminology, while historically understandable, is misleading and should be retired from popular science communication.

**The pitch drop experiment as a useful comparison.**

The University of Queensland pitch drop experiment, begun in 1927, offers a valuable calibration point. Pitch — a coal tar derivative — is genuinely a liquid with measurably high viscosity. The experiment has documented approximately nine drops of pitch falling from a funnel over nearly a century, with each drop taking approximately eight to thirteen years to form and fall. Pitch is visibly flowing on a decadal timescale.

Glass at room temperature is, as noted, approximately ten to fifteen orders of magnitude more viscous than pitch. If pitch takes a decade per drop, glass at room temperature would require something on the order of 10¹² to 10¹⁵ times longer — figures that dwarf the age of the universe by comfortable margins. The comparison usefully illustrates why "viscous materials flow slowly" cannot be linearly extrapolated from the pitch analogy to glass: the viscosity differences are not merely quantitative but cross regimes of physical behavior entirely.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim offers a useful case study in the challenges of correcting distorted-but-grounded misinformation, because both a simple "false" verdict and an uncritical acceptance of the "technically true" defense miss the important content.

**The simple debunk approach** — "glass doesn't flow, the windows are thicker at the bottom because of manufacturing" — is factually correct and useful. It gives readers the bottom line. Its limitation is that it leaves untouched the genuine scientific complexity that gave the claim its original credibility. Readers who have heard that glass is "technically a liquid" from a physicist may not find the debunk satisfying without an explanation of what the physicists actually meant and why the popular version distorts it.

**The "kernel of truth" approach** — acknowledging that glass is amorphous, that the glass transition is interesting, that the classification question is genuinely not simple — is important context but requires care. Fact-checkers who over-emphasize the kernel risk validating the distorted version. The kernel does not rescue the claim; it explains why the claim exists.

**Effective fact-checking of this claim requires three steps:**
1. Directly address the observational claim (old windows thicker at bottom): manufacturing history, not flow.
2. Directly address the mechanistic claim (glass flows over centuries): Zanotto viscosity calculation, age-of-universe comparison.
3. Explain what is actually true about glass's unusual nature — amorphous structure, the glass transition, the historical "supercooled liquid" terminology — in a way that satisfies the reader's legitimate curiosity without validating the false conclusion.

**The EARC (E) classification** indicates that the evaluation framework should look for the genuine scientific content that has been exaggerated and communicate it accurately alongside the debunk. The goal is not merely to stop the false claim but to replace it with something true and interesting — which, in this case, is available: the real story of glass, amorphous solids, and pre-industrial manufacturing is genuinely worth knowing.

**Snopes and similar organizations** have addressed this claim, generally finding it false. The most thorough treatments note the manufacturing history and cite viscosity data. Fewer engage substantively with the materials science context that gave the claim its credibility. For a general audience debunk, the short form (manufacturing + viscosity) is sufficient. For an educational context — museum exhibits, classroom use, science journalism — the fuller treatment is warranted.

The claim that glass flows over centuries, producing the observed thickness variation in old windows, is false. The more interesting truth — what glass actually is, how the confusion arose, and how pre-industrial craftspeople made flat glass without modern continuous processes — is richer than the myth it replaces.
