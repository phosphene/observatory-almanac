## The Claim

Walk through any cathedral in Europe — Chartres, Canterbury, Cologne — and someone in your group may point to the thick, rippled glass in the medieval windows and offer an explanation: glass is actually a very slow-moving liquid, and over centuries, it has flowed downward under the influence of gravity, leaving the panes thicker at the bottom and thinner at the top. The claim often comes with a note of wonder: the very glass you are looking at is in the act of flowing, invisibly, imperceptibly, over geological timescales.

The idea is seductive. It offers a sense of hidden dynamism beneath the apparent solidity of everyday objects — a reminder that what appears still is, on a deep level, in motion. It connects the human timescale with something larger. It also has the particular appeal of being exactly the kind of counterintuitive fact that sounds too strange to be made up. People do not expect to be told that their windows are moving. The surprise of the claim is part of its credibility.

The claim is false. Medieval window glass is thicker at the bottom because of how it was manufactured, not because it has flowed. At room temperature, glass does not flow on any timescale relevant to human history or even to the age of the Earth. The flow timescale for glass at room temperature, calculated from first principles using measured viscosity data, is longer than the current age of the universe. Glass is, for all meaningful purposes, a solid — and understanding why this myth persists requires understanding a genuine and interesting ambiguity in materials science that the myth exploits without ever resolving correctly.

## What's Actually True

Glass occupies an unusual position in the taxonomy of materials, and the confusion surrounding its behavior begins with legitimate scientific complexity rather than pure fabrication.

Most solids are crystalline: their constituent atoms or molecules are arranged in a regular, repeating three-dimensional lattice. This ordered structure is a defining characteristic of the solid state for most familiar materials — metals, ice, salt, quartz. The regular lattice produces characteristic properties including sharp melting points, where the material transitions abruptly from solid to liquid as the lattice structure breaks down.

Glass is different. Glass is an amorphous solid — its atoms are arranged in a disordered, irregular network rather than a crystalline lattice. The structure of glass at the atomic level more closely resembles the arrangement of atoms in a liquid than in a crystalline solid: there is short-range order (each silicon atom in common soda-lime glass is bonded to four oxygen atoms in a tetrahedral arrangement) but no long-range periodic order extending across the material. When glass cools from a molten state, it does not crystallize. Instead, the disordered liquid structure is retained as the material increases in viscosity and stiffens into what we call glass.

This structural fact gave rise to the description of glass as a "supercooled liquid" — a liquid that has been cooled below its nominal melting point without crystallizing. The term is used in materials science to describe the thermodynamic state of glass, and it is technically defensible in that context. However, the term is frequently misunderstood as implying that glass behaves like a liquid at room temperature: that it flows, deforms under stress, or exhibits the kind of viscous dynamics we associate with fluids. It does not. The term "supercooled liquid" describes glass's atomic structure and its thermodynamic history, not its mechanical behavior at ambient conditions.

The relevant material property for understanding whether glass flows is viscosity. Viscosity is a measure of a material's resistance to flow under stress — honey has high viscosity, water has low viscosity, and crystalline solids have effectively infinite viscosity (they do not flow at all under normal conditions). Glass at its working temperature — around 1000 to 1300 degrees Celsius for common soda-lime glass — has a viscosity low enough to be shaped by blowing, casting, or drawing. As glass cools, its viscosity increases dramatically. By the time glass reaches room temperature, its viscosity is astronomically high.

In a landmark paper published in 1999 in the American Journal of Physics, Edgar Zanotto and Prabhat Gupta calculated the timescale over which observable flow deformation could be expected in glass at room temperature. Using measured viscosity data for various glass compositions and applying the standard equations relating viscosity to deformation rate, they found that common glass compositions at 25 degrees Celsius would require timescales on the order of 10^23 years or more to undergo any measurable change in shape due to viscous flow. The current age of the universe is approximately 1.4 × 10^10 years. Glass would need to exist for trillions of times longer than the universe has existed before flow would become detectable.

This calculation effectively ends the empirical question. Whatever variation exists in the thickness of medieval window glass, it was not produced by the glass flowing over centuries. The timescales are simply not compatible.

The actual explanation for the thickness variation observed in many medieval windows is well-documented by historians of glassmaking. In the medieval and early modern periods, window glass was produced by two primary techniques: the crown glass method and the cylinder glass method.

Crown glass was made by gathering a ball of molten glass on a blowpipe, blowing it into a bubble, then spinning the blowpipe rapidly so that centrifugal force spread the bubble outward into a flat disk. The spinning process created a disk that was thinner toward the edges and thicker toward the center — where the glassblower's rod had been attached, leaving a circular thickening known as a "bullseye." This disk was then cut into panes. Because crown glass disks were not perfectly uniform, and because panes of uneven thickness were the normal product of the technique, installers and glaziers regularly had to work with glass of varying thickness. When installing panes in windows, it was common practice — whether for stability, aesthetics, or to accommodate the irregular leading — to orient thicker portions of panes downward. This means the orientation of thick-bottom panes in medieval windows was a deliberate installation choice, not the result of subsequent deformation.

The cylinder glass method involved blowing a long cylinder of glass, slitting it, and unrolling it flat. Cylinders were rarely perfectly uniform along their length, and again, the resulting flat sheets had thickness variations built in from the manufacturing process.

Compounding these natural manufacturing variations, surviving medieval windows represent a highly non-random sample of the glass that was originally produced. Glass is fragile; over centuries, thinner, more delicate panes were more likely to break and be replaced. The glass that survives in cathedral windows today may be systematically biased toward examples with greater average thickness, simply because thicker glass was more likely to survive intact.

## Why People Believe This

The glass-flows myth owes its persistence to a combination of genuine scientific complexity, visual evidence that seems to support it, and the particular cognitive appeal of claims about hidden dynamics beneath familiar surfaces.

The visual evidence — thick-bottomed medieval windows — is real and observable. When people visit cathedrals and actually look at the old glass, they see variation that the myth explains. The explanation is wrong, but it accounts for the observation in a way that feels plausible. Alternative explanations (medieval manufacturing limitations, glaziers' installation practices, survivorship bias) require knowledge of historical glassmaking that most people do not have and may find less compelling even when provided.

The scientific terminology provides false support. "Supercooled liquid" is a real term from materials science, and anyone who has encountered it — in a popular science article, in a chemistry class, in a documentary — retains the impression that glass is, on some level, a liquid. The logical leap from "glass is technically a supercooled liquid" to "glass flows, just very slowly" seems small. What this misses is the vast difference between structure and behavior: the atomic structure of glass resembles a liquid's, but the mechanical behavior at room temperature is emphatically that of a solid.

The claim also benefits from an interesting property of timescale arguments. When someone says "glass flows so slowly you can't see it in a human lifetime," the statement is technically structured in a way that seems falsifiable but practically isn't — no one can watch a window for a thousand years. The myth exploits the phenomenological inaccessibility of very long timescales. People cannot personally verify that glass has not moved in the time since a window was installed. The calculation that demolishes the claim (flow would require longer than the age of the universe) is not intuitively accessible and requires engagement with materials science quantification.

There is also the appeal of the poetic idea itself. The image of glass as an imperceptibly slow river, of solid objects secretly flowing across human timescales, connects to a broader cultural fascination with hidden processes beneath the surface of the visible world. It suggests that physics operates differently from our intuitions at the extremes of time. This is genuinely true for some phenomena — but glass at room temperature is not one of them.

## Verdict

The claim that glass is a liquid that flows very slowly over time is false. Glass does not flow at room temperature on any human-relevant timescale. Rigorous physical calculations establish that the flow timescale for glass at ambient conditions exceeds the age of the universe by many orders of magnitude. The observed thickness variation in medieval window glass results from pre-industrial manufacturing processes and installation practices, not from any post-installation deformation.

The kernel of truth in the myth is that glass has an amorphous atomic structure, sometimes described using the term "supercooled liquid," that distinguishes it from crystalline solids. This is a real and interesting fact about glass's microstructure. But it does not mean glass behaves as a liquid at room temperature or that it flows under gravity on any accessible timescale.

**Rating: False** — with the acknowledged nuance that the myth draws on a real materials science concept (the amorphous/supercooled-liquid character of glass) but misapplies it to mechanical behavior in a way that is quantitatively refuted by direct calculation.

## The Wider Picture

Glass is one of the oldest and most versatile materials in human technological history, and the ambiguity surrounding its physical classification has generated genuine discussion in materials science as well as popular mythology.

The question of how glass should be classified — solid, liquid, supercooled liquid, amorphous solid — is not merely semantic. It touches on fundamental questions in condensed matter physics about what the solid state is, how the glass transition differs from crystallization, and whether there is a sharp thermodynamic distinction between glass and liquid or merely a practical one. Researchers have spilled considerable ink on the precise definitions involved. The glass transition temperature — the temperature below which a glass-forming material becomes mechanically solid — is well-defined experimentally but has proven difficult to characterize from first principles. Understanding the microscopic dynamics of the glass transition remains an active area of research.

At the same time, these nuances do not rescue the popular myth. Whatever the precise ontological status of glass as a material, the claim that flows at room temperature on human timescales is simply false — and it is false by quantitative calculation, not merely by theoretical argument. The Zanotto-Gupta paper is notable precisely because it takes the question seriously enough to calculate an actual answer rather than dismissing it on the basis of intuition alone.

The glass myth is part of a broader family of physics myths that exploit the inaccessibility of extreme conditions or timescales. Similar myths attach to other familiar materials: some popular accounts have claimed that iron "creeps" visibly over ordinary timescales, or that concrete flows. In all these cases, real phenomena (creep is a real phenomenon in metals under sustained stress at elevated temperatures; concrete does exhibit some long-term deformation under load) are stripped of their conditions and timescales and presented as if they apply to everyday objects under everyday conditions.

The myth also illustrates a specific failure mode in popular science communication: the import of technical terminology into lay contexts without adequate explanation of scope and conditions. "Supercooled liquid" is a technical term with a specific meaning in the context of glass physics. When it travels out of that context and into general circulation, its meaning is interpreted through the frame of everyday liquid behavior, producing exactly the misunderstanding the myth embodies. Technical vocabulary is precise within its domain; it becomes misleading when extracted from the domain without its conditions.

There are also historical and archaeological dimensions to the correct understanding of medieval glass thickness. The development of flat glass production techniques — from the crown and cylinder methods of the medieval and early modern periods through the development of plate glass in the seventeenth century and eventually float glass in the twentieth century — is a significant chapter in the history of technology. Float glass, invented by Alastair Pilkington at Pilkington Brothers in 1959, produces perfectly flat glass by floating molten glass on a bath of molten tin, allowing it to achieve an even thickness without mechanical contact. Modern window glass is so uniform that the variation observable in medieval cathedral glass strikes contemporary eyes as irregular and exotic — which feeds the perception that something has happened to it over time.

Understanding the real explanation for medieval glass thickness — manufacturing technique, not temporal flow — connects visitors to a richer and more accurate history of craft and technology. The glaziers who produced and installed that glass were working within the capabilities and constraints of pre-industrial production, and the windows they created were triumphs of skill within those limits. The glass has not secretly moved since they installed it. It stands as they left it.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The glass-flows myth is one of the more thoroughly addressed science myths in popular fact-checking and science communication. It has been discussed in science journalism, skeptical publications, and academic outreach venues for several decades, and the debunking is consistent and well-sourced.

The Zanotto-Gupta 1999 paper in the American Journal of Physics is the key citation in most careful treatments. The paper was written explicitly as a response to the popular myth — the authors noted that the claim was circulating in popular science contexts and set out to calculate whether it was physically plausible. Their finding, that glass would require timescales exceeding the age of the universe to flow observably at room temperature, has become the standard quantitative rebuttal. The American Journal of Physics, which focuses on pedagogically valuable physics rather than frontier research, was a fitting venue for this kind of corrective calculation.

Popular science outlets including Scientific American, the Guardian science section, and various physics communication platforms have addressed the myth, consistently providing the manufacturing-history explanation for medieval window thickness alongside the viscosity calculation. The Richard C. Plumb 1989 article in the Journal of Chemical Education, which predated Zanotto-Gupta and examined medieval glass thickness directly, was an early careful treatment of the manufacturing explanation.

The myth has not been formally addressed by Snopes, which focuses primarily on viral claims circulating in social media and political contexts. But it appears regularly in science education debunking resources and "top science myths" roundups, where it is consistently and correctly identified as false.

For educators, the glass myth presents an opportunity to teach two distinct lessons simultaneously. The first is the straightforward factual correction: glass does not flow at room temperature, and medieval window thickness is a manufacturing artifact. The second is a lesson in how technical language travels: the legitimate materials science concept of the supercooled liquid, stripped of its conditions and quantitative context, becomes the foundation of a persistent and plausible-sounding myth. Teaching students to ask "under what conditions does this apply?" and "at what scale is this relevant?" is as important as teaching them the correct factual content.

The glass myth is ultimately a case where the truth is not less interesting than the myth — it is differently interesting. The real history of medieval glassmaking, the genuine complexity of the glass transition in materials science, and the quantitative reasoning required to calculate flow timescales all offer genuine intellectual content. The myth offers a false sense of wonder; the corrected account offers wonder grounded in what the universe actually does.
