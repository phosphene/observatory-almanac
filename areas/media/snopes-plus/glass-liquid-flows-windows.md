# Glass is a slow-moving liquid that flows over centuries — why old windows are thicker at the bottom


## 1. The Claim

The claim has achieved a special status in popular science communication as a "surprising fact" that gets passed from teacher to student, from science enthusiast to newspaper, across decades: that glass is technically not a solid but a "supercooled liquid" or "very viscous liquid," and that this supposedly explains why the panes in old European cathedral windows and historic buildings are visibly thicker at the bottom than the top—the glass has been slowly flowing downward under gravity over centuries, gradually accumulating at the base like very, very slow honey.

The claim is often delivered with a tone of pleased counterintuitiveness, as though it reveals a hidden physical reality beneath the apparent solidity of familiar objects. It has appeared in science museum exhibits, in popularizing books, in introductory chemistry and physics courses, and in general-audience science journalism. In some versions, it is extended further: not only windows, but ancient glass vessels, Roman glassware, and Egyptian artifacts are supposedly deforming imperceptibly on geological timescales.

This claim is false on two separable grounds. First, the physical premise—that glass is a liquid flowing at room temperature on any perceptible human or geological timescale—is not supported by viscosity measurements of historical glass compositions. Second, the proximate explanation of why old window panes are thicker at the bottom—that gravity has pulled flowing glass downward over centuries—is incorrect even by reference to manufacturing history, because pre-industrial glassmaking techniques produced inherently non-uniform panes that were installed with deliberate orientation.

Both halves of the claim must be addressed, because the cultural stickiness of the myth derives from the apparent explanatory connection between them: the physical premise seems to elegantly explain the observed asymmetry. Showing that the physical premise is false does not by itself explain the asymmetry; showing that the manufacturing history correctly explains the asymmetry does not by itself settle the physical question. Full correction requires both.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Physical State of Glass at Room Temperature**

Glass is an amorphous solid. This classifcation warrants careful unpacking because the rhetorical premise of the myth—that glass is "really" a liquid—depends on a partial truth about glass structure that is then extended beyond its valid implications.

Glass lacks the long-range crystalline order characteristic of crystalline solids like metals, ionic crystals, or ice. In a crystalline solid, atoms are arranged in a regular repeating lattice extending over macroscopic distances. In glass, the atomic arrangement—typically consisting of silicon-oxygen tetrahedra bridged by oxygen atoms, with various modifier ions depending on composition—exhibits short-range order (the immediate bonding environment of each silicon is well-defined) but no long-range periodicity. This is also true of liquids: molecular liquids have local short-range order but no long-range periodicity. On the basis of this structural similarity, glass is sometimes called an "amorphous solid" or, historically, described as having liquid-like structure.

This structural similarity does not imply dynamical similarity. The defining characteristic of a liquid is flow in response to shear stress—not its molecular arrangement, but its mechanical response to applied force. Liquids flow because molecules can diffuse past one another on experimentally accessible timescales, overcoming the potential energy barriers between local structural minima. At room temperature, the mobility of atoms in silicate glass is effectively zero on any human or geological timescale. The glass transition temperature—the temperature below which a glass-forming material transitions from a relatively mobile supercooled liquid state to a kinetically arrested amorphous solid—for common soda-lime window glass is approximately 530°C–570°C. Below this temperature, atomic mobility is so suppressed that diffusive rearrangement is negligible. At room temperature, roughly 25°C, the system is approximately 500°C below its glass transition, which in Arrhenius terms corresponds to an astronomical suppression of molecular mobility.

The quantitative case was made definitively by Edgar Dutra Zanotto in a 1998 article titled "Do cathedral glasses flow?" published in the *American Journal of Physics* (Zanotto, E.D., 1998, *American Journal of Physics*, 66, 392–395). Zanotto applied viscosity data and glass transition theory to calculate the flow rate of medieval-composition glass at room temperature. His central result: room-temperature glass would flow approximately 1 nanometer over a time period many times greater than the current age of the universe (~13.8 billion years). This is not a small effect; it is an effect so far below any measurement threshold—and so far below geological time—that it can be described as physically zero for any practical purpose.

Subsequent work by John Mauro and colleagues, published in the *Journal of the American Ceramic Society* (Gulbiten, O., Mauro, J.C., Guo, X., & Boratav, O.N., 2018, *Journal of the American Ceramic Society*, 101, 5573–5584), characterized the viscosity of medieval glass compositions more precisely, finding that medieval window glass—enriched in potassium oxide and magnesium oxide relative to modern soda-lime compositions—has a somewhat lower viscosity at room temperature than modern glass. Even accounting for this difference, the calculated flow rate remains approximately 1 nanometer per billion years. The 2018 paper was significant precisely because critics of Zanotto's original calculation had sometimes argued it used modern glass viscosity, and Mauro's work addressed the most favorable possible historical glass compositions. The conclusion was unchanged.

If other ancient glass artifacts provide a test, they confirm these calculations: Egyptian New Kingdom glass vessels over 3,000 years old show no detectable sagging or deformation. Roman blown-glass pieces from the first century CE are geometrically stable. Obsidian—a natural volcanic glass—has been in geological circulation for millions of years without measurable flow deformation. All of this is consistent with the calculated negligible flow rate and inconsistent with any observable glass flow on human timescales.

**Why Old Windows Are Actually Thicker at the Bottom**

The historical record of glassmaking techniques directly explains the thickness asymmetry that the myth attributes to flow.

Before the industrial era, two glassmaking techniques dominated European flat glass production: crown glass and cylinder glass. In the crown glass process, a glassblower gathered a ball of molten glass on a blowpipe, blew it into a hollow globe, and then, by reheating and rapidly spinning the pipe while the glass was still plastic, caused the globe to open out into a flat disc by centrifugal force. This disc—called a "table" or crown—had a characteristic structure: the center bore the mark of the blowpipe (the "bullseye" or "pontil mark," prized in decorative glazing), while the glass tapered in thickness from the center outward, with the outer rim slightly thicker than the middle but the overall distribution nonuniform. Individual panes cut from different radial positions of the crown had different thickness profiles depending on where they were cut.

In the cylinder glass process, a glassblower gathered glass and blew a long cylindrical bubble, cut the ends off the cylinder, split it lengthwise, and flattened it in an oven to make a rectangular pane. This process produced glass that was consistently slightly thicker at the ends of the original cylinder—corresponding to the top and bottom of the blown shape—than in the middle, and also introduced variation from the heterogeneous gathering and blowing process.

Both methods produced panes that varied in thickness across their surface, often by a factor of two or more. The medieval glass industry had neither the technology nor, apparently, the consistent commercial incentive to sort panes by uniform thickness. Glaziers installing these panes had to make choices about orientation. Contemporary glazing manuals and the historical record of surviving windows document that the standard practice was to orient panes with the heavier (typically thicker) edge downward, both for mechanical stability—a heavier bottom lowers the center of gravity and makes the pane less likely to slip or crack under its own weight in the lead came—and for drainage, since the thicker bottom edge seated more firmly in the lower lead channel and prevented water pooling.

This installation practice is the direct and complete explanation of why old windows are thicker at the bottom. The glass was already non-uniform when installed; the installer placed the thicker edge down deliberately. It has been in that position ever since, not moving.

Further evidence against the flow hypothesis comes from windows where this conventional installation was not followed: historical surveys of medieval window glass have found panes installed with the thicker edge at the top, at the side, and at various angles, in what appear to be cases of glazier carelessness or reversed installation. If the thickened-bottom observation were caused by glass flow, all windows—regardless of installation orientation—should show accumulated material at the bottom after sufficient centuries. They do not. Panes installed with the thicker edge at the top retain the original thickness distribution: no material has migrated downward.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The glass-is-a-slow-liquid myth is an unusually well-engineered piece of false science communication. It successfully combines several properties that maximize belief persistence.

**Elegant False Explanation**: The myth has the structure of a good scientific explanation: it invokes a plausible mechanism (viscous flow), applies it to a real observation (thickness asymmetry in old glass), and provides a quantitative prediction (bottom-heaviness proportional to age). This explanatory structure creates what psychologists call an "aha" experience—the sense of understanding an previously puzzling observation—which is cognitively rewarding in a way that makes the explanation resistant to relinquishment. Once someone has experienced the pleasure of the explanation fitting the observation, rejecting the explanation requires abandoning that sense of resolution without an equally satisfying replacement.

**Exploitation of Structural Ambiguity**: The claim's premise—that glass is not really a solid—contains a genuine ambiguity in materials science that creates entry points for the myth. There is a legitimate ongoing discussion in the condensed matter and materials science communities about how to classify amorphous materials, what the glass transition represents theoretically, and whether the category "solid" should be defined by crystalline order or by mechanical response (shear modulus). In this context, a statement like "glass is not a typical crystalline solid" is accurate. Simplified to "glass is a liquid," it becomes a category error that confuses structural with mechanical properties. The myth exploits this ambiguity by presenting a simplification as the corrected-for-accuracy version: "it seems solid, but technically it's a liquid."

**Epistemic Novelty—The Missing Alternative Explanation**: Standard corrections of glass-is-a-liquid typically establish the viscosity calculation (glass cannot flow on human timescales) but do not always provide, with equal prominence, the correct explanation for the asymmetry (non-uniform crown/cylinder glass, installed thick-side-down). This creates an explanatory gap: the listener's anomaly (why is old glass thicker at the bottom?) has been deprived of its previously accepted explanation without receiving a satisfying replacement. In the absence of an alternative explanation, even a convincingly refuted explanation tends to rebound and be retained as the least-bad available narrative. The full correction of this myth must therefore supply the manufacture-and-installation explanation with enough detail that it fills the explanatory role previously occupied by the false flow claim. This is an instance of the general principle from Lewandowsky and colleagues that effective debunking requires not just refutation but narrative replacement—providing an alternative causal account that satisfies the cognitive demand for explanation (Lewandowsky, S., Ecker, U.K.H., Seifert, C.M., Schwarz, N., & Cook, J., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 13, 106–131).

**The Teacher Pipeline Effect**: This myth has achieved unusual penetration in science education, where it is sometimes presented as a corrective to naive notions of matter states—"glass seems solid, but it's technically a liquid." Teachers drawn to counterintuitive science examples have propagated it across generations. This gives the myth a specific characteristic: audiences who believe it often received it from an authority figure in an educational context, which creates a strong anchoring effect and makes it semantically encoded not as "a thing I heard" but as "a thing I was taught." Correcting information encoded as learned educational content is substantially harder than correcting casual conversational beliefs.


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: FALSE**

Glass does not flow at room temperature on any human timescale. The viscosity of soda-lime silicate glass at room temperature is extremely high—on the order of 10²⁰–10²⁵ Pa·s for different compositions—and the corresponding flow rate is negligible by many orders of magnitude: approximately 1 nanometer per billion years, well below any possible measurement threshold and far shorter than the universe's age. Ancient glass artifacts of all types—Egyptian, Roman, medieval—confirm this by showing no detectable flow deformation.

Old window panes are thicker at the bottom because pre-industrial crown and cylinder glass production created panes of non-uniform thickness, and glaziers conventionally installed them with the thicker, heavier edge downward for stability. The classification of glass as an amorphous material is accurate; collapsing this classification into "glass is a liquid" conflates structural characterization with mechanical behavior in a way that is specifically and quantifiably wrong for room-temperature glass flow.

**Gap Category: Materials Science Misattribution**

A real manufacturing artifact is attributed to a physical process that cannot have produced it. The observation is genuine; the causal claim is false; the correct causal explanation is available but culturally invisible.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The glass-is-a-liquid myth is a specimen of a broader class of materials science misconceptions in which the "weird" structural properties of materials at one scale are conflated with anomalous macroscopic behavior. Analogous misconceptions arise about quasicrystals (which violate crystalline symmetry rules but are perfectly stable solids), certain polymer glasses (whose mechanical properties vary oddly with temperature without "flowing"), and aerogels (which are solids but have densities approaching air). In each case, the genuinely interesting structural properties are real, but the inference to exotic macroscopic behavior under everyday conditions is not warranted.

The viscosity of glass across its temperature range is genuinely interesting and technically important. At elevated temperatures near and above the glass transition, glass viscosity drops dramatically and is critical for shaping processes in industrial glassmaking. The Vogel-Fulcher-Tammann (VFT) relation describes glass viscosity as a function of temperature: η = η₀ · exp[B / (T − T₀)], where η₀, B, and T₀ are material-specific fitting parameters. This non-Arrhenius temperature dependence—the viscosity decreases more steeply with temperature than simple activation energy models predict—reflects the cooperative nature of structural rearrangement in glass-forming liquids as they approach the glass transition. Near the working temperature (roughly 1000°C for soda-lime glass), viscosity is around 10³ Pa·s—similar to thick honey—allowing skilled glassblowers to shape and work the material. At room temperature, the same relation extrapolates to extraordinarily high values with enormous uncertainty, but both independent viscosity measurements and the flow-rate calculations confirm that the room-temperature value, however precisely defined, corresponds to zero observable flow.

The crown glass technique that produced the uneven medieval window panes survived as a specialty glassblowing art form even after cylindrical sheet glass and modern flat glass production made it commercially obsolete. The "bullseye" panes with their concentric rings and central pontil mark are still produced by hand and are architecturally valued for their optical character—the natural imperfections and thickness variations scatter and refract light in ways that have aesthetic presence in historic restoration work. The uneven thickness that misleads people into the flow myth is also, in this sense, a valued aesthetic property. Modern float glass—developed by Pilkington in the late 1950s, in which molten glass is floated on a bath of molten tin to produce perfectly flat panes of uniform thickness—has no such character, and its perfection is part of why old glass looks distinctive and alive.

The glass transition itself remains an active research area in condensed matter physics. There is no complete microscopic theory of why glass-forming liquids undergo the glass transition at specific temperatures, what determines whether a material is a "good" or "bad" glass former, or what precisely happens to structural dynamics at the transition. This genuine intellectual richness—the glass transition is on the shortlist of important unsolved problems in condensed matter physics—is entirely separate from, and should not be confused with, any claim about observable flow of window glass at room temperature.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has addressed the glass-is-a-liquid/flowing-windows claim under a False rating, citing both the viscosity calculations (referencing Zanotto's work) and the manufacturing history explanation for why old glass is thicker at the bottom. The treatment appropriately presents both halves of the correction—the physical impossibility and the correct historical explanation—which is essential for effective debunking as argued above.

The challenge this case presents for science-focused fact-checkers is the need to explain materials science quantitatively to a general audience without either oversimplifying (losing the precision that distinguishes the amorphous structure claim from the flow claim) or overcomplicating (losing the reader). The Zanotto 1998 paper provides an exemplary public-facing treatment: written for the *American Journal of Physics*, which targets physics educators, it explains the calculation clearly while deferring technical viscosity modeling details to appendices. The 2018 Mauro et al. paper addresses the potential objection that Zanotto used modern glass compositions rather than medieval compositions—the most technically sophisticated version of pushback the myth receives—and confirms the conclusion is robust to this correction.

One underappreciated dimension of this myth's cultural life is its geographic concentration. The claim is more commonly transmitted in northern Europe and North America, where medieval stained glass and early modern window glass are culturally proximate objects—seen in churches, historic buildings, and museums. In regions where pre-industrial window glass is not a familiar visual reference, the myth has less traction. This geographic distribution suggests that the myth specifically exploits pre-existing perceptual familiarity with old glass, using the actually observed thickness asymmetry as confirming evidence for a false causal story. Effective correction in culturally proximate contexts should always include time at the observable site—the asymmetric pane in the church window—because correction linked to the specific object that triggered the false explanation is substantially more memorable than correction given in the abstract.
