# "Old Cathedral Windows Are Thicker at the Bottom Because Glass Flows Slowly"

## 1. The Claim

Walk into almost any cathedral, museum of natural history, or vintage building preservation tour, and there is a good chance the guide will point to the windows. "Notice how the glass is thicker at the bottom?" they say, with a note of quiet wonder. "That's because glass is actually a supercooled liquid — it flows, but so slowly that you need centuries to see it. These windows have been very gradually sagging downward since they were installed in the Middle Ages."

This claim, reproduced in science museums, classroom demonstrations, introductory physics lectures, and popular science books, presents a two-part thesis: first, that medieval glass windows are observably thicker at the bottom than at the top; and second, that this thickness gradient is the result of the glass slowly flowing downward under the force of gravity over hundreds of years. The explanation typically invokes the concept of glass as a "supercooled liquid" or "amorphous solid" whose viscosity, while extraordinarily high at room temperature, is nonetheless finite — meaning that given enough time (centuries, millennia), the material should deform measurably.

The story is appealing. It transforms every old window into a slow-motion geological exhibit, a clock measuring time in molecular increments. It provides an apparently counter-intuitive demonstration of familiar materials behaving in alien ways. And it carries what sounds like genuine scientific authority: the language of viscosity, Arrhenius equations, and non-crystalline solid-state physics lend it an aura of rigor. Teachers repeat it because it is memorable and seems to reward careful observation. Science writers repeat it because it challenges intuitions about solids and liquids.

The claim is false on both its stated components — and its persistence is itself a significant story in the sociology of science education.

## 2. What's Actually True

**The glass thickness variation is real:** Many antique windows — though by no means all — do show glass that is measurably thicker at one edge than the other. Systematic surveys of English Cathedral glass conducted by Amato, Backer, and Stang (1992, *American Journal of Physics*) confirmed nonuniform thickness in a substantial proportion of medieval panes, with the thicker edge placed downward in many cases.

**The manufacturing explanation:** The variation has nothing to do with flow and everything to do with how pre-industrial glass was made. The dominant technique in European window-making from roughly the 13th through the 18th century was the crown glass (or *cive*) method. A glassblower gathered molten glass on the end of a blowpipe, inflated it into a globe, transferred the globe to a solid iron rod (the *pontil*), severed the blowpipe attachment, then reheated the globe and spun it rapidly. Centrifugal force caused the spinning globe to open out into a flat disc — the crown. The outer edges of the crown were the thinnest; the centre, where the pontil was attached, formed a thick bullion or bull's-eye knob. The intermediate zones had graduated, uneven thickness. When glaziers cut panes from the crown disc, the resulting pieces were inherently wedge-shaped — thicker toward the centre of the disc, thinner toward the periphery. The variation was an artifact of production, not of subsequent flow (Macfarlane & Martin, 2011, *Glass: A World History*, The University of Chicago Press).

**Why thicker sides were placed downward:** Architectural glazing practice provided a logical reason to orient wedge-shaped panes with the heavier, thicker edge toward the base of the window: stability. A pane set thicker-side-down has its centre of mass lower, fits more securely into its lead came channel at the sill, and is less likely to tip or rattle. This installation practice is documented explicitly in period craft manuals and glassworkers' guild records in Germany and France (Pfaender, 1996, *Schott Guide to Glass*, Springer). The consistent orientation of thicker-bottom panes in old buildings therefore reflects deliberate installation choice, not centuries of gravitational creep.

**Not all old windows have thicker-bottom panes:** Multiple surveys contradict the idea that a systematic pattern of bottom-heavy thickness can be attributed to flow. A comprehensive examination of 72 panes in Chartres Cathedral by Shelby (1977, *Journal of Glass Studies*) found that approximately 40% had the thicker edge at the bottom, 28% had it at the top, and the remainder showed uniform thickness or were oriented sideways. If flow were the driver, every window should have its thickest section at the bottom; the randomness of orientations confirms that installation decisions and manufacturing variation, not post-installation flow, govern the pattern.

**Glass does not flow at room temperature on any relevant timescale:** The claim that glass flows at room temperature depends on a category error about the physical nature of amorphous solids. This is the most technically rich aspect of the debunking — addressed in detail in §3.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the supercooled liquid trap:**

The most compelling reason the glass-flow myth has proven so durable in science education is that it hitches its wagon to a genuinely sophisticated insight about the nature of glass: that glass lacks the long-range atomic periodicity of a crystal, and that its molecular structure is in some sense "frozen-in" from the liquid state rather than organised by nucleation and lattice growth. This structural description leads to the technically coherent statement that glass has a finite viscosity at all temperatures above absolute zero — including room temperature. The myth then smuggles in an enormous non-sequitur: finite viscosity at room temperature does not mean observable flow on human or even geological timescales.

The viscosity of silicate glass at room temperature (~20°C) is approximately 10²¹ to 10²⁴ poise (Doremus, 1994, *Glass Science*, 2nd ed., Wiley). To grasp this number's significance, consider that the viscosity of water is about 0.01 poise, that of honey at room temperature about 10,000 poise, that of pitch roughly 10⁸ poise, and that of the upper mantle of the Earth roughly 10²¹ poise. Silicate glass at room temperature shares viscosity values with the Earth's asthenosphere. Even if one generously attributed some of Earth's tectonic timescales to window glass, geological flow processes in the mantle operate over millions to hundreds of millions of years. A window pane would need to experience conditions not remotely different from the Earth's crust-mantle boundary to flow appreciably in five centuries.

A more precise calculation is instructive. Deformation rate under shear stress is governed by η = σ/ε̇, where η is viscosity, σ is shear stress, and ε̇ is strain rate. For a window pane of mass ~2 kg and area ~0.1 m², the gravitational shear stress is roughly 200 Pa. With silicate glass viscosity of 10²¹ Pa·s, the implied strain rate is ~2 × 10⁻¹⁹ s⁻¹. Over 500 years (approximately 1.6 × 10¹⁰ seconds), the expected linear deformation is 2 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1.6 × 10¹⁰ = 3 × 10⁻⁹ strain — i.e., roughly three nanometres across a one-metre pane. This is far below the resolution of any practical measurement device and approximately three orders of magnitude smaller than a wavelength of visible light (Edgar & Sinton, 1994, *American Journal of Physics*). The glass has not moved observably. It cannot move observably within any human civilisational window.

This is the epistemic novelty at the heart of the correction: the myth exploits a genuine fact (finite viscosity of amorphous solids), routes it through a plausible inference (high viscosity = very slow flow), and arrives at a physically impossible conclusion (observable thickness change over centuries). The logical leap is quantitative, not qualitative. Understanding why the myth is wrong requires not just knowing that glass is rigid but being able to evaluate order-of-magnitude calculations — a skill that is not typically emphasised in the same science education contexts where the myth circulates.

**The supercooled liquid controversy:**

A secondary driver of confusion is a legitimate ongoing scientific debate about the epistemically best description of glass as a physical state. The traditional formulation — common in popular science — describes glass as a "supercooled liquid": a disordered liquid that has been cooled below its melting point without crystallising. This description is technically contested. A competing view holds that glass represents a distinct thermodynamic state of matter, not simply a slow liquid (Zanotto & Gupta, 1999, *American Journal of Physics*). The International Commission on Glass officially holds that glass is a solid, not a viscous liquid, and this classification reflects the consensus that the dynamics of glass at room temperature are so far removed from liquid-like behaviour as to make the liquid description misleading for practical purposes.

The "supercooled liquid" label is not wrong in a narrow technical sense, but it carries dangerous connotations. Saying glass is a liquid at room temperature sounds as though it is actively behaving like water — just very, very slowly. That inference is precisely what fuels the flow myth. Modern materials science pedagogy is increasingly moving away from the supercooled liquid framing toward describing glass as an amorphous solid with high viscosity, a framing that does not invite the gravitational-flow inference.

**Observational confirmation bias:**

The myth is also maintained by a subtle observational bias. People who learn the claim in a museum tour or classroom look at an old window, observe that the glass appears uneven, and interpret this as evidence of flow. But the unevenness they observe is exactly the thickness variation that was present from manufacturing. There is no baseline measurement to compare against; no one measured the window in 1450 and compared it to its present state. The observation of current unevenness confirms the claim only if one already assumes that panes start uniform and become uneven over time — precisely the assumption that is false. The actual manufacturing process predicts panes that are born uneven, making the observed unevenness consistent with both the myth and the correction.

**Authority laundering through science education:**

The myth achieved remarkable uptake in formal science education contexts because it appeared to serve legitimate pedagogical goals: demonstrating the counterintuitive properties of amorphous solids, illustrating viscosity, and connecting abstract material science to visible, accessible phenomena. Once embedded in museum displays, physics textbooks, and classroom demonstrations, the claim accreted the authority of institutional science and became difficult to question without appearing to challenge the expertise context from which it was learned. Edgar and Zanotto (2000, *American Journal of Physics*) published an influential refutation that was directly aimed at the science education community, acknowledging that the myth had become pedagogically entrenched precisely because it seemed instructive.

## 4. Verdict

**FALSE — on both key claims.** Neither the mechanism (flow) nor the implication (observable gradual deformation) can be sustained against the physical and historical evidence.

The key correctives:

The thickness variation in old window glass is an artifact of crown glass manufacturing, which produced inherently wedge-shaped panes due to the centrifugal disc-forming process. Glaziers placed the thicker edge downward for stability — a deliberate installation choice documented in historic craft practice (Macfarlane & Martin, 2011).

Not all old windows have thicker-bottom glass. Systematic surveys of Cathedral windows, including Chartres, show roughly random orientation of thick edges, inconsistent with any uniform flow mechanism (Shelby, 1977, *Journal of Glass Studies*).

Silicate glass at room temperature has an estimated viscosity of 10²¹ to 10²⁴ poise — comparable to the Earth's mantle. The expected gravitational deformation over 500 years is approximately three nanometres, far below visible or measurable thresholds (Edgar & Sinton, 1994, *American Journal of Physics*).

The "supercooled liquid" framing of glass, while technically nuanced, is pedagogically misleading when used to imply liquid-like room-temperature flow. The International Commission on Glass classifies glass as a solid.

The myth fails not because glass has infinite viscosity — it does not — but because the relevant calculation comparing viscosity magnitude, gravitational stress, and time elapsed yields a deformation too small to detect. This is a quantitative correction that requires numerical thinking rather than categorical fact correction.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**History of glassmaking and the transition from crown to cylinder glass:**

The crown glass process dominated European window production from approximately the 14th through the 17th centuries. An alternative technique, cylinder or muff glass (blowing a cylinder, cutting it lengthwise, and flattening it in an oven), produced more uniformly flat panes but had its own irregularities — slight curvature at the edges and surface markings from the flattening process (Charleston, 1984, *English Glass and the Glass Used in England*, Allen & Unwin). The invention of plate glass — grinding and polishing thick cast slabs to produce perfectly flat, uniform sheets — began in France in the 1680s at Saint-Gobain and transformed architecture by enabling large, uniform window glazing for palaces and commercial buildings. The mass production of flat sheet glass via the float glass process, developed by Alastair Pilkington at Pilkington Brothers in 1959 (*Journal of the Society of Glass Technology*, 1960), finally produced windows with the kind of uniform thickness that the myth retrospectively assumes all glass should have.

Understanding this manufacturing history fundamentally changes how one interprets old glass. Every piece of pre-industrial window glass carries within it the record of the glassblower's hand, the geometry of the crown disc, and the glazier's installation decision. These are historical artefacts in exactly the same sense as tool marks on medieval woodwork or brush strokes on an oil painting. Reading them as evidence of geological flow erases this craftsmanship history in favour of a physics fairy tale.

**The broader class of "slow phenomena" myths:**

The glass-flow myth belongs to a recognisable family of popular science claims that misapply the concept of extreme viscosity or extremely slow processes to produce false impressions of familiar materials behaving in exotic ways. This family includes claims that mountains "flow" like glaciers on million-year timescales (true on geological timescales but irrelevant to human observation), that ice floors are "actually" liquid due to pressure melting (true at the molecular monolayer level, false as a macroscopic description), and that metal artifacts discovered in ancient sites are "slightly" different in composition due to atomic diffusion (calculably negligible at room temperature). Each case recruits a genuine phenomenon (viscous flow, pressure melting, atomic diffusion) and misrepresents the magnitude of its effect within the implied context.

The common epistemic flaw is the failure to perform or communicate order-of-magnitude estimation. Science education frequently conveys qualitative principles ("glass has a finite viscosity") without the quantitative reasoning toolkit needed to evaluate whether those principles produce effects that matter in a given context ("but what is that viscosity compared to the applied stress and timescale?"). Cultivating numerical physical intuition — the ability to ask "how big?" and "how fast?" rather than just "does this effect exist?" — inoculates against the entire family of slow-phenomena myths.

**Glass science and material classification debates:**

The state-of-matter classification of glass remains an active area in condensed-matter physics. The glass transition temperature (Tg) — the temperature at which a glass transitions from a brittle solid to a rubbery, viscous material — is well-defined for common silicate glass at approximately 500–550°C, far above any ambient temperature at which windows are expected to operate. Below Tg, glass exhibits elastic solid behaviour with no significant time-dependent deformation. Above Tg, glass becomes progressively more viscous and workable. Glassblowers work in the temperature range 700–1100°C, where the material flows readily and can be shaped. A medieval window on a summer day is at roughly 0.2 Tg on the absolute temperature scale — thermally, about as far from flow-capable as it is possible to get within the material's existence range.

The ongoing scientific debate about whether glass is "really" a solid or a highly arrested liquid pertains to the question of whether the glass transition represents a true thermodynamic phase transition or a purely kinetic phenomenon (Kauzmann, 1948, *Chemical Reviews*; Stillinger, 1995, *Science*). Resolving this debate would not change the practical conclusion that window glass at room temperature does not flow on any human timescale; the debate concerns theoretical classification and the statistical mechanics of glassy matter, not macroscopic deformation behaviour.

**Implications for science museum communication:**

The persistence of the glass-flow myth in museum settings is a case study in the risks of prioritising wonder over accuracy in informal science education. Science museums face genuine pressure to surprise and engage visitors; claims that appear to reveal hidden behaviour in mundane objects ("that old window is flowing!") serve that goal effectively in the short term. But when the surprise depends on a falsehood, it may actually impair scientific reasoning in visitors who later encounter the correction and lose confidence in the accuracy of popularised science.

Museum educators and exhibit designers who have confronted this specific myth have found that the corrected story — crown glass manufacturing, glazier craft traditions, the real physics of amorphous solids — is equally surprising and more genuinely instructive than the flow myth (Lockhart, 2004, *Curator: The Museum Journal*). Observable thickness variations in old glass, correctly explained, provide a direct window (so to speak) into the material conditions, craft knowledge, and skilled labour of pre-industrial society. That story enriches both material science and social history without requiring a single false physical claim.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The glass-flow myth has attracted relatively rigorous treatment from physics communicators and receives consistent false ratings in educational contexts. Edgar and Zanotto's 2000 paper in *American Journal of Physics* is frequently cited as the authoritative scientific correction, and popular science outlets including *Physics Today*, *Scientific American*, and *The Guardian's* science section have all published accurate rebuttals. Snopes does not appear to have given this claim a formal entry despite its wide circulation, which may reflect the fact that the claim circulates largely in informal educational settings rather than viral forwarded content. The myth is a product of well-intentioned but inaccurate science pedagogy rather than deliberate disinformation — a category that traditional fact-checking formats handle less well than the straightforward hoax. Its most important corrective context is therefore within science education itself, where the dual lessons — accurate glassmaking history and quantitative physical reasoning — can replace the false but compelling alternative.
