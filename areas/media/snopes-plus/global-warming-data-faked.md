## 1. The Claim

Since at least 2014, a persistent piece of climate misinformation has circulated across social media, alternative news websites, and conspiratorial blogs. The claim holds that NASA and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) were "caught red-handed" fabricating global temperature data to support a false narrative of anthropogenic global warming. The most widely circulated version of this claim originated from the website Natural News, which published an article in June 2014 with the headline "Global Warming Data Faked by Government to Fit Climate Change Fictions." That article asserted that NASA and NOAA had "deliberately altered historical temperature data" in a "politically-motivated attempt to rewrite history" and that these alterations constituted "the largest scientific fraud ever uncovered."

The specific evidence cited by Natural News and earlier blog posts centered on the Goddard Institute for Space Studies Surface Temperature Analysis, known as GISTEMP. This is the flagship global temperature record produced by NASA, a foundational dataset used by climate scientists worldwide to track changes in average global surface temperature since the late nineteenth century. The claim focused on a comparison between an early version of the GISTEMP record for the United States, published in 1999, and a later version published in 2012. The two versions showed different temperature trends for the United States, with the later version exhibiting a slightly steeper warming trajectory. This discrepancy, the claim argued, proved that scientists had manipulated the numbers to manufacture a warming trend where none existed.

The claim spread widely. It was shared on Facebook hundreds of thousands of times. It was cited in congressional testimony, repeated by conservative commentators, and used as a talking point by politicians who opposed climate regulation. For millions of people who encountered it, the claim served as definitive proof that climate science was irredeemably corrupted. If the temperature data itself had been faked, then every conclusion built on that data—every climate model, every policy recommendation, every international agreement—rested on a foundation of lies.

At its core, the claim traded on a deeply intuitive epistemological appeal. The argument was simple: the original 1999 data set showed less warming; the revised 2012 data set showed more warming; therefore, someone revised the data upward with the intent to deceive. This narrative satisfied a preexisting suspicion many people held about institutional science—that it was capturable by political interests—while also offering a clear villain (government scientists) and a clear remedy (dismiss the entire body of climate research as fraudulent).

## 2. What's Actually True

The truth about GISTEMP and climate data adjustments is both more mundane and more scientifically interesting than the conspiracy narrative suggests. Adjustments to the GISTEMP record are not only routine but are the hallmark of responsible scientific practice. Temperature data, like all observational data collected over long timescales, is raw material that requires careful processing before it can yield reliable scientific conclusions. The raw readings from individual weather stations are subject to a variety of "non-climatic biases" that must be identified and corrected before the data can be used to detect real climate signals.

These biases are numerous and well-documented. Weather stations move. Over the past century, many stations in the Cooperative Observer Network in the United States have relocated at least once, and some have moved multiple times. A station that moves from a rooftop to ground level, or from an urban center to an airport field, will produce a different temperature baseline after the move even if the underlying climate has not changed. Observation times shift. The National Weather Service changed standard observation times from afternoon to morning at many stations starting in the 1960s, which altered the recorded temperatures because morning readings tend to be cooler than afternoon ones. Instruments are upgraded. The transition from liquid-in-glass thermometers to the Maximum-Minimum Temperature System in the 1980s introduced a systematic bias that needed to be corrected. Urban heat island effects mean that stations in growing cities will record warmer temperatures over time simply because asphalt and buildings retain heat better than soil and vegetation, not because the global climate has changed.

Every one of these biases was addressed in the post-1999 versions of GISTEMP. NASA published multiple peer-reviewed papers—in 1999, 2001, and subsequent years—that described exactly what adjustments were being made and why. The papers are publicly available on the NASA website. The methodological changes were also summarized in accessible language for non-specialist audiences. The most significant adjustment involved correcting for time-of-observation biases, which alone added approximately 0.15 degrees Celsius of warming to the U.S. record over a century. This is not a large number, but in climate science, where global average temperature changes are measured in tenths of a degree, it matters. The total net effect of all adjustments applied to the U.S. record was about 0.3 degrees Celsius of additional warming over 100 years.

It is crucial to understand that "unadjusted" data does not exist in any meaningful sense for a global temperature product. As climate scientist Zeke Hausfather has explained, the very concept of a pure, unadulterated temperature record is a myth. Even the most basic process of calculating a global average temperature from thousands of individual station readings requires decisions about spatial weighting, infilling of missing data, and quality control. The question is never whether to adjust but how to adjust, and whether those adjustments are made transparently and with scientific justification. In the case of GISTEMP, they were.

Far from being faked, the GISTEMP record has been independently validated by multiple research groups using different methodologies. Berkeley Earth, a nonprofit organization initially funded in part by climate skeptics, produced its own independent temperature record that closely matched the NASA and NOAA results. The adjustments made by government agencies were neither secret nor controversial within the scientific community. They were the routine work of improving a dataset by removing known sources of error.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes published its fact-check of this claim in October 2019, written by science journalist Alex Kasprak. The article gave the claim a rating of "False," and the substantive analysis was sound. Snopes correctly identified the origin of the claim in the Natural News article and traced it back to an even earlier blog post. The article explained the concept of data adjustment in clear terms, describing the specific biases that the post-1999 GISTEMP version addressed: time of observation, station location history, changes in thermometer type, and urban heat effects. Snopes linked to NASA's documentation of the changes and to the relevant peer-reviewed papers. The article also noted that by the time of the fact-check in 2019, the GISTEMP v3 dataset at issue had already been succeeded by GISTEMP v4, which incorporated even more data and further methodological improvements.

Where Snopes fell short was in not fully addressing the deeper epistemic dimensions of the controversy. The article treated the claim largely as a straightforward factual error—a misunderstanding of standard data practices by a dishonest author. This is accurate as far as it goes, but it fails to account for why the claim has been so durable and persuasive to so many people. The appeal of the "faked data" narrative is not simply that people are ignorant of how climate science works. It is that the narrative maps onto a genuine tension in the relationship between science and public trust.

The Snopes article did not adequately address the role of motivated reasoning in how people interpret data adjustments. When someone already believes that climate scientists are politically biased, the discovery that temperature data has been "changed" looks like confirmation of that bias. The Snopes article assumed that providing the correct technical explanation—yes, data are adjusted, but here is why that is legitimate and transparent—would be sufficient to neutralize the claim. In practice, the technical explanation often reinforces the suspicion it seeks to allay. The very fact that adjustments are necessary can feel like an admission of wrongdoing to someone primed to distrust the institution.

Furthermore, the Snopes article may have overstated the clarity of NASA's public communications about these adjustments. While the papers and documentation do exist, they are not always easy for a layperson to find or interpret. The NASA GISTEMP history page provides a summary of changes, but navigating it requires some understanding of climate science terminology. A genuinely curious skeptic who tries to verify the Snopes account by visiting the NASA website may encounter jargon and data visualizations that are hard to parse. This creates a vulnerability: if the official source feels inaccessible, the simpler, more dramatic narrative of fraud becomes more cognitively attractive.

The Snopes article also implicitly endorsed a model of scientific authority that many audiences reject. The argument that adjustments are valid because they were published in peer-reviewed journals and posted on a government website only works if the audience trusts those institutions. For the audience most likely to encounter and believe the "faked data" claim—people who are already skeptical of government and academic institutions—that assumption is precisely the problem. Snopes was fact-checking for a rational audience that respects scientific institutions, but the misinformation it was addressing was designed for a different audience entirely.

## 4. Why This Matters

The "faked climate data" claim matters not because it is sophisticated—it is not—but because it is a perfect case study in how epistemic warfare is waged. The claim weaponizes the normal, transparent practices of science against science itself. It takes a routine methodological improvement—the correction of known biases in a long-term observational dataset—and re-frames it as a concealment. It exploits the gap between how science actually works and how non-scientists imagine it should work.

This dynamic has consequences. In the United States, climate change denial has been a major obstacle to the adoption of carbon pricing, emissions regulations, and international climate agreements. The claim that the data itself is fraudulent provides a convenient justification for inaction. If the entire premise of global warming is a hoax, then there is no need to restructure the economy, invest in renewable energy, or cooperate with international partners on emissions reductions. The claim functioned as a rhetorical firewall, protecting its believers from the uncomfortable implications of accepting climate science.

At a broader level, the claim illustrates a fundamental challenge for fact-checking in an age of institutional distrust. The Snopes article was technically correct but strategically incomplete. It debunked the claim without debunking the worldview that made the claim attractive. For the audience that needed to hear it most, the fact-check likely changed no minds. It was preaching to a choir that was already disposed to trust NASA and NOAA, while failing to reach the skeptics who doubted those institutions in the first place.

The problem is not one Snopes can solve alone. It is a structural feature of the modern information environment. When large segments of the population have lost trust in the institutions that produce and validate knowledge, no amount of fact-checking will close the gap. The adjustment is not the problem; the distrust is. Addressing the "faked data" claim effectively would require not just explaining the technical details of GISTEMP adjustments but also rebuilding the epistemic relationship between science and the public. That is a project that requires more than fact-checking—it requires cultural change, educational reform, and a reckoning with the political economy of misinformation.

## 5. Research Notes

The GISTEMP record and its evolution from version 3 to version 4 are well-documented in the primary scientific literature. The key papers include Hansen et al. (1999), "GISS Analysis of Surface Temperature Change," published in the Journal of Geophysical Research, and Hansen et al. (2001), "A Closer Look at United States and Global Surface Temperature Change," in the same journal. These papers describe the specific methodological changes that became the target of the Natural News article and subsequent misinformation. The NASA GISTEMP website hosts a "History of GISTEMP" page that provides a chronological overview of the changes, though the page assumes some technical familiarity with climate data processing.

Zeke Hausfather's 2015 article on Skeptical Science, "Understanding Adjustments to Temperature Data," remains one of the best accessible explanations of why climate data requires adjustment and what the specific adjustments to the U.S. temperature record actually involved. Hausfather, who was at the time a research scientist at Berkeley Earth, brought both technical expertise and an awareness of the public communication challenge to his analysis. Berkeley Earth's independent temperature record provides a useful validation of the adjusted GISTEMP results; their analysis shows warming trends that are very similar to NASA's, despite using different methodologies and data sources.

The role of Natural News in amplifying climate misinformation has been documented by multiple researchers. BuzzFeed News published an investigation in 2017, "How Anti-Science Forces Thrive On Facebook," which traced how Natural News content achieved viral reach through Facebook's algorithmic amplification. The original blog post that the Natural News article copied was published on a WordPress site run by a prominent climate skeptic. The recycling of the same core claim across multiple platforms is a pattern typical of climate misinformation campaigns.

One notable limitation of the existing Snopes article is its omission of any discussion about the funding and political context of the sources spreading the claim. The fact-check treats the Natural News article as an isolated error rather than as part of a coordinated misinformation ecosystem funded in part by fossil fuel interests. Including this context would have strengthened the article's credibility with audiences who might otherwise assume the claim was produced by independent investigators rather than motivated actors.

The persistence of this claim online, more than a decade after it was first debunked, underscores a key insight from misinformation research: factual corrections rarely reach the same audience as the original false claim, and even when they do, they may not change beliefs because the false claim serves an identity-protective function. This is known as the "continued influence effect" in the cognitive science literature. The claim persists not despite being debunked but because being debunked has become part of the narrative of persecuted truth-tellers.

## 6. Verdict

The claim that NASA and NOAA faked global warming data to "fit climate change fictions" is false. The changes to the GISTEMP temperature record between 1999 and 2012 were not acts of fraud but standard, transparent, and scientifically justified adjustments to account for known biases in the raw observational data. These adjustments were documented in peer-reviewed publications, summarized on NASA's public website, and independently validated by third-party researchers. The Snopes fact-check correctly rated the claim as false.

However, the Snopes analysis, while technically sound, did not go far enough in addressing why the claim persists. The fact-check assumed that providing the correct technical explanation would suffice, underestimating the degree to which the claim's appeal is rooted in distrust of institutions rather than ignorance of data practices. The adjustment controversy is a microcosm of a larger problem: climate misinformation is not primarily a failure of factual accuracy but a failure of epistemic trust. Until that deeper problem is addressed, fact-checks like this one will continue to be correct but ineffective.

The Truth Vault rating reflects the Snopes verdict of False while recognizing the need for a more comprehensive approach to debunking that addresses the underlying drivers of belief in such claims.
