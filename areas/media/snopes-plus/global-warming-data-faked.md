# Scientists Did Not Fake Global Warming Data — Climategate Emails Showed No Fraud

## 1. The Claim

On 17 November 2009, thousands of emails and documents were stolen from servers at the Climatic Research Unit (CRU) at the University of East Anglia (UEA) and released publicly on Russian and North American servers. The timing was deliberate: the United Nations Climate Change Conference (COP15) in Copenhagen was less than three weeks away, at a moment when momentum for an ambitious international climate agreement appeared unusually strong.

Within hours of the leak, selected quotations from the emails — stripped of context and circulated through climate-sceptic blogs before mainstream media had time to read the full archive — had generated a set of powerful claims that crystallised into what commentators quickly labelled "Climategate":

**Claim 1 — The "hide the decline" fraud:** Phil Jones, director of the CRU, wrote in a 1999 email about using "Mike's Nature trick" to "hide the decline." This, according to the narrative, was scientists openly admitting they had manipulated temperature graphs to conceal inconvenient data showing that temperatures had been declining.

**Claim 2 — Suppression of dissent:** Other emails showed CRU scientists discussing how to keep sceptical papers out of peer-reviewed journals, pressure editors, and exclude dissenters from IPCC processes. This showed the scientific consensus was manufactured through gatekeeping rather than earned through evidence.

**Claim 3 — Data destruction:** Emails revealed discussions of deleting data subject to freedom of information requests, showing scientists actively obstructing scrutiny of their work.

**Claim 4 — The models don't work:** Internal emails expressed private doubts about the robustness of climate models and temperature reconstructions, showing that scientists privately held views far more cautious than their public pronouncements.

Together these claims were assembled into a narrative of systematic, deliberate scientific fraud: climate scientists had fabricated or manipulated data, suppressed opposition, and deceived the public and policymakers about the reality of global warming. The narrative was widely repeated in conservative media and political commentary around the world, was used in arguments against the Copenhagen agreement, and remains in active circulation.

## 2. What's Actually True

The emails are real, the CRU servers were genuinely hacked, and many of the quotations circulated in the Climategate narrative appear verbatim in the archive. The scientific conclusions drawn from them do not.

**On "hide the decline":** This phrase — from an email by Phil Jones, referencing a technique used by Keith Briffa and Michael Mann — referred to a specific, well-documented issue in palaeoclimatology known as the **divergence problem**. Certain tree ring records from high-latitude Northern Hemisphere sites show anomalous growth suppression after approximately 1960, diverging from instrumental temperature records. This divergence was known, published, and discussed openly in the scientific literature before the email was written; Briffa et al. published papers discussing it in 1998 (Briffa, K.R. et al., 1998, "Reduced sensitivity of recent tree-growth to temperature at high northern latitudes," *Nature*, 391, 678–682) and again in 2001. The "trick" referenced was a graphical technique — described and justified in published literature — of showing instrumental temperature records alongside tree ring proxies in the recent period rather than the diverging later tree ring data, which was not being used as a proxy for temperatures it no longer reliably tracked. "Hide the decline" referred to declining **tree ring measurements as temperature proxies**, not to declining **actual temperatures**. The phrase, extracted from its context, implies the opposite of its actual meaning.

The IPCC Third Assessment Report (2001) included a figure showing this approach, with a caption explicitly describing it. The divergence problem is not a secret; it is an ongoing area of research in dendroclimatology with a substantial published literature.

**On suppression of dissent:** The emails show researchers discussing, sometimes in intemperate language, the quality of certain sceptic papers being submitted to journals. Several emails expressed frustration with the peer review process and with editors who had published what the CRU scientists regarded as methodologically deficient papers. An independent review commissioned by the journal *Climate Research* — one that had published a controversial paper by Soon & Baliunas (2003) that several editors resigned over — found that the paper's peer review had been inadequate. The CRU scientists' frustration was not without professional basis. However, the emails also showed discussions that went beyond frustration: one exchange involved discussion of whether an upcoming assessment report should include specific papers, in ways that the House of Commons Select Committee noted could undermine confidence in the review process.

What the emails did not show was systematic exclusion of legitimate scientific challenges from the literature. The papers most prominently associated with "sceptic suppression" — by John Christy, Roy Spencer, and Richard Lindzen, among others — continued to be published throughout the period covered by the emails. The IPCC itself includes minority and dissenting views within its Working Group reports.

**On data destruction:** An email from Jones discussed deleting emails in response to anticipated FOI requests. This was investigated by the Information Commissioner's Office, which found that the deletion, if it occurred, may have constituted a breach of FOI law — but that the university's statutory timelines for prosecution had already passed. No finding of actual deletion of primary data was made. The CRU's temperature dataset, CRUTEM (now HadCRUT), is based on records held by national meteorological services worldwide, not on CRU-held unique data that could be meaningfully "destroyed."

**On private doubts:** Scientists expressing uncertainty and disagreement in private correspondence is not evidence of fraud; it is evidence of normal scientific practice. The emails show a community working through genuine methodological debates — about the robustness of proxy reconstructions, the interpretation of statistical methods, the limitations of model ensembles. These debates are the mechanism of science, not its subversion.

## 3. Why People Believe This

Climategate is the most studied and documented case of what researchers in science communication call a **manufactured controversy** — a situation in which genuine ambiguities in a scientific record are selectively amplified to create an impression of crisis in a field where no such crisis of fundamental findings exists. Understanding its persistence requires analyzing the specific epistemic mechanisms through which selective quotation from authentic documents can generate false beliefs in careful readers.

**The authenticity heuristic.** The Climategate emails were real. They had not been fabricated — they were genuine CRU communications. This authenticity provided a strong prior in favour of treating the interpretation circulated with them as trustworthy. Most forms of misinformation involve fabricated evidence; the fact-checking reflex is accordingly calibrated to ask "is this real?" When the answer is yes, many readers proceed to accept the interpretation alongside the fact of authenticity. The Climategate operation exploited this by attaching false interpretations to genuine documents. Readers who had no reason to know what the "divergence problem" was, who had not read Briffa et al. (1998), and who encountered "hide the decline" as a two-second clip had no cognitive tools to separate authentic quotation from false contextualisation. Authentication and interpretation were bundled together and delivered simultaneously.

**Out-of-context quotation as epistemic weapon.** The phrase "Mike's Nature trick" to "hide the decline" is extraordinarily well designed as a piece of misleading communication. It contains the word "trick," which in contemporary usage connotes deception rather than its legitimate scientific usage meaning "device" or "technique." It contains "hide," which is unambiguously deceptive in common usage. And it contains "decline," which reads as a reference to declining temperatures. Every word does opposite meaning-work in scientific versus colloquial reading, and the Climategate campaign deployed it in contexts where only the colloquial reading would be available to the audience. This is the epistemic novelty of the Climategate operation: it was not primarily a disinformation campaign built on fabrications but one built on **systematically exploiting semantic ambiguity across expert and lay registers of the English language.** Phil Jones was using "trick" in the sense any mathematician or statistician would — a standard technique or device — and "hide the decline" to refer to a methodological decision to use instrumental rather than proxy data in the recent period, where proxy data had known limitations. Both usages appear in published literature. Translated into lay vernacular through selective quotation, the sentence sounds like an admission of deliberate deception. The gap between these readings is so large, and so dependent on domain knowledge to bridge, that correction requires more explanation than the original claim requires assertion.

**Motivated scepticism and institutional distrust.** The Climategate narrative arrived at a moment when trust in expert institutions was declining in the Western democracies most important to climate negotiations. In the United States and United Kingdom, the financial crisis of 2008 had generated widespread public anger at expert failure; in both countries, the run-up to the Iraq War a decade earlier had demonstrated that expert consensus (on weapons of mass destruction) could be manufactured for political purposes. Audiences primed by these experiences to distrust institutional expert consensus were receptive to narratives confirming that climate science was another case of manufactured consensus. Psychologists identify this as **confirmation of an established prior**: Climategate was persuasive not because it provided strong evidence of fraud but because it confirmed a distrust that was already structurally stable for unrelated reasons.

**The speed asymmetry.** The Climategate narrative was constructed and globally distributed within 48 hours of the email release. The independent inquiries that cleared the CRU scientists of fraud took five months (the House of Commons Science and Technology Committee report) and eight months (the independent Scientific Assessment Panel chaired by Lord Oxburgh) to complete and publish. By the time the official findings were released, the narrative had been circulating for nearly a year, had influenced the Copenhagen negotiations, and had been incorporated into the belief structures of millions of readers who had no reason to encounter or attend to subsequent corrections.

## 4. Verdict

**False.** No scientific fraud was found. Eight independent investigations were conducted:

1. **House of Commons Science and Technology Committee** (UK Parliament, March 2010): Found no evidence of fraud or scientific malpractice; noted some lack of openness by CRU but found the scientific reputation of the researchers intact.

2. **Independent Climate Change Email Review** (Muir Russell Review, July 2010): Found that "the honesty and rigour of CRU as scientists are not in doubt" and that "we did not find any evidence of behaviour that might undermine the conclusions of the IPCC assessments." It noted some problematic attitudes to information requests.

3. **International Science Assessment Panel** (Oxburgh Review, April 2010): Examined CRU's research publications and found no evidence of manipulation of data for inappropriate reasons.

4. **Environmental Protection Agency Independent Review** (US EPA, February 2010): Found the petitions for reconsideration of the EPA's greenhouse gas endangerment finding based on Climategate unsupported.

5. **National Science Foundation's Office of Inspector General** (2011): Investigated Michael Mann (a peripheral figure in the emails) and found no evidence of scientific fraud.

6. **Penn State University Internal Review** (2010): Cleared Michael Mann; found the allegations not supported by evidence.

7. **NOAA Inspector General** (2011): Reviewed NOAA scientists' conduct and found no evidence of fraud.

8. **UK Information Commissioner's Office**: Found possible FOI violation but noted prosecution was time-barred and no finding of data destruction was made.

The "hide the decline" phrase has been publicly contextualised by multiple scientific bodies, the divergence problem is discussed in the IPCC reports, and the temperature record on which the fraud allegation focused — the late-20th-century warming trend — is supported by multiple independent datasets maintained by institutions with no connection to the CRU: the NASA GISS Surface Temperature Record, NOAA's Global Surface Temperature dataset, the Berkeley Earth surface temperature reconstruction, satellite-based lower tropospheric temperature measurements from RSS (Remote Sensing Systems) and UAH, and ocean heat content measurements from the Argo float network. A conspiracy sufficient to fabricate the global warming record would require coordinated fraud across these independent institutions across multiple governments and academic systems.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Climategate did not arise in a vacuum. The theft and release of the emails was a targeted operation, timed to the Copenhagen COP15 conference, executed with knowledge of how climate-sceptic media networks would distribute the selected quotations. The identity of the hacker or hackers has never been publicly established. UK police investigated and found sufficient evidence to seek a named suspect, but the statutory investigation period expired without prosecution (Norfolk Constabulary formally closed the investigation in 2012 without identifying the perpetrator publicly).

The operation's success — in generating major news coverage, influencing the atmosphere at Copenhagen, and seeding years of "data fraud" claims — demonstrated several things about the information environment of the late analogue/early digital transition period: that email archives contain material easily stripped of context; that scientific communication norms (technical language, private debate, informal register in email) create abundant ammunition for misinterpretation; and that the speed of blog-to-mainstream-media transmission had outpaced the pace of institutional scientific response.

Phil Jones temporarily withdrew from his directorship of the CRU while investigations were conducted, citing the personal impact of harassment. He was subsequently reinstated and the Scientific Assessment Panel's review restored his scientific standing. Michael Mann, the Penn State climate scientist whose "hockey stick" temperature reconstruction was a particular target of Climategate-adjacent attacks, has been the subject of ongoing legal proceedings relating to publications by climate contrarians; a 2024 trial in DC found in his favour (Mann v. National Review / Steyn, DC Superior Court, February 2024), awarding substantial damages for defamation.

The scientific community's response to Climategate also produced important changes in data sharing practice and in transparency norms for climate research. The Berkeley Earth project, founded in 2010 by physicist Richard Muller partly in response to concerns about CRU methodology, conducted a fully open, independently funded reanalysis of global surface temperature records — and confirmed the warming trend (Rohde, R. et al., 2013, "A New Estimate of the Average Earth Surface Land Temperature Spanning 1753 to 2011," *Geoinformatics & Geostatistics: An Overview*, 1(1)). Muller, who had been sympathetic to some Climategate-era concerns, publicly stated that the reanalysis confirmed the consensus. The episode illustrated both the healthy function of independent scientific verification and the asymmetry in media attention: the original Climategate allegations received vastly more coverage than the subsequent clearances and confirmatory analyses.

The claim that scientists faked global warming data also continues to circulate independently of Climategate, attached to newer controversies: adjustments to historical temperature records (normal practice in any long-running dataset to account for station moves, instrumentation changes, and time-of-observation bias), the "Karl et al. pause-buster" paper (Karl, T.R. et al., 2015, "Possible artifacts of data biases in the recent global surface warming hiatus," *Science*, 348(6242), 1469–1472), and various NASA and NOAA temperature dataset updates. Each revision triggers fresh allegations of fraud; each revision is explicable by documented scientific methodology available on agency websites.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Climategate is one of the most-checked single events in the history of climate misinformation. Snopes, PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, FullFact (UK), Climate Feedback, and RealClimate (a blog run by working climate scientists) all addressed it extensively. The challenge for general-purpose fact-checkers was significant: the claims required not only verification of what the emails said (easily done — the archive is still publicly available) but contextualisation requiring knowledge of palaeoclimatology methods that most fact-checkers did not have in-house.

RealClimate's response, published within 24 hours of the release, remains the most technically thorough early treatment (Schmidt, G. & Mann, M., 2009, "The CRU hack," *RealClimate*, November 20, 2009). It was written by climate scientists who understood the divergence problem, the meaning of "trick" in statistical contexts, and the published history of the IPCC temperature reconstructions. Its limitation was audience: it was hosted on a blog written for audiences already familiar with climate science, and its technical depth made it inaccessible to readers encountering the claims in tabloid or political media.

The UK parliamentary inquiry offered the highest-authority institutional clearance and the most readable prose, but it was published in March 2010 — three months after Copenhagen — when its exculpatory findings attracted far less media attention than the original story. This temporal dynamic, in which allegations and clearances operate on asymmetric media timelines, has been a persistent challenge for fact-checking climate (and other) scientific controversies. The speed of modern misinformation circulation, combined with the pace of institutional investigation, means that allegations routinely have months to consolidate in audiences' memory structures before official findings provide correction.

The most important gap in standard Climategate coverage — and the one most relevant to long-term epistemic impact — is the failure to address the multiple independent confirmation argument at equal prominence to the CRU allegations. The CRU's temperature record is one of at least five independent global temperature datasets (GISS, NOAA, Berkeley Earth, RSS, and UAH satellite records). A conspiracy theory broad enough to include all five, plus the Argo float ocean heat content record and the independently measured sea level rise from satellite altimetry, would require coordinated multi-institutional, multi-national fraud of a scale and coherence that no historical precedent supports. Presenting this "consilience of evidence" case — the fact that the warming signal appears identically in datasets with entirely different methods, instruments, and institutional homes — is arguably more effective at establishing the scientific record than technical contextualisation of individual email phrases, because it addresses the structural argument for conspiracy rather than defending a particular institution's conduct.
