## The Claim

Throughout 2024, and with renewed intensity in the final months of the year, social media users on Facebook, Instagram, LinkedIn, Pinterest, and Threads shared images described as showing a "solar-powered, glow-in-the-dark bicycle path in Poland that charges during the day and glows at night." Representative posts included one from September 24, 2024, which accumulated more than 118,000 reactions on Facebook, and another that described the path as located in the town of Pruszków. Text accompanying some shared versions read: "In Poland, bicycle paths glow at the night which are charged by the sun."

The images being shared fell into two distinct categories. One — the more visually spectacular and more widely shared — showed a brilliantly glowing blue trail at night, featuring what appeared to be stylised constellations and starburst patterns lit from within the path surface. The second — shared less dramatically but more accurately — showed a more modest night-time photograph of a softly luminescent blue stripe on a path situated in a natural rural setting. The two images were not identified as different in most sharing contexts; they were treated as equivalent depictions of the same installation.

Public responses illustrated the state of information about the path. Polish Facebook commenters stated they had never heard of such an installation and suspected fabrication. Some described the image as AI-generated from the outset. A Reddit post in the Damnthatsinteresting subreddit, viewed widely, shared the more accurate image and described one path near Lidzbark Warmiński. The claim arrived at Snopes as a complex bundle of potentially true and potentially false elements requiring disentanglement.

## What's Actually True

The situation involves multiple distinct claims, each with a different truth value, producing a compound accuracy assessment rather than a simple true-or-false verdict.

**The real path exists.** A phosphorescent bicycle path does exist in Poland. It was constructed in September 2016 near the town of Lidzbark Warmiński in the Mazury (Masurian) region of northeastern Poland. The path was first reported by local media including Gazeta Olsztyńska and Wyborcza Olsztyn and by nationwide Polish outlets including Polsat News and Wirtualna Polska. It is 100 metres long.

The path was designed and built by TPA Sp. z o.o., a Polish construction and materials technology company. TPA is based in Pruszków — a city in the Warsaw metropolitan area — which explains the incorrect location attribution that appeared in many social media posts. TPA designed the path; the path is not in TPA's home city.

Dr. Igor Ruttmar, CEO of TPA, explained the mechanism in an interview with Gazeta Olsztyńska: the path uses a luminescent aggregate — a mineral or synthetic phosphorescent material incorporated into the path surface — that absorbs solar energy during daylight hours and emits visible blue light after dark. Dr. Ruttmar stated that the material is "capable of emitting light for over 10 hours," and that the choice of blue-glowing phosphors was made "to maintain consistency with the Masurian landscape." TPA press officer Dominika Górska described the installation as a "100-metre test section" and indicated that the company planned to monitor how the surface performs in varying weather conditions and temperatures.

Importantly, the mechanism is phosphorescence, not solar power in the conventional sense. The path does not contain solar panels or any active electrical generation system. It stores optical energy as excited electron states in phosphorescent materials and releases that energy as visible light — a passive, materials-based luminescence process rather than an active energy conversion system. The "solar-powered" description in social media posts is broadly accurate in the sense that solar exposure drives the process, but it implies active electrical storage that the path does not have.

The path underwent renovation in 2022. TPA confirmed in January 2025 that the renovation was necessitated by faulty asphalt layers installed by prior contractors, through which vegetation had begun to grow. As of early 2025, TPA stated the path is operational.

**The widely-circulated image is almost certainly fake.** The spectacular image sharing the constellation-patterned glowing blue trail that appeared in most of the higher-engagement social media posts does not depict the actual TPA path near Lidzbark Warmiński. Analysis by Snopes journalist Aleksandra Wrona identified several characteristics inconsistent with genuine night photography of a real luminescent surface. The stars and constellations depicted on the path look "highly stylised and artificial" — meaning they correspond to a graphic design aesthetic rather than the natural, diffuse glow of a phosphorescent mineral aggregate. The blue glow is "overly vibrant and evenly distributed," which is not typical of real-life glowing materials, which show intensity gradients, surface texture variation, and edge effects. The image has characteristics consistent with AI-generated or digitally manipulated content.

**The accurate image is real.** The second image — the one shared less widely, showing a more modest and naturalistically lit blue path — is a genuine photograph. It was shared by a Reddit user with attribution to the actual Lidzbark Warmiński installation. This image is consistent with what phosphorescent aggregate surfaces actually look like: a relatively soft, diffuse glow with visible surface texture and intensity variation.

**The location claims are mixed.** Correctly: Mazury/Lidzbark Warmiński. Incorrectly: Pruszków (which is TPA's address, not the path's location).

## Why People Believe This

The viral spread of this claim — and particularly the prioritisation of the spectacular fake image over the less-striking genuine photograph — illustrates several interlocking mechanisms of misleading content propagation.

**The genuine innovation provides epistemic cover.** The actual TPA path represents a real technological and infrastructural achievement. The concept of a bicycle path surface that glows at night using absorbed sunlight is inherently interesting, surprising-but-plausible, and environmentally appealing. It does not violate known physics; photoluminescence is a well-understood phenomenon. Unlike a claim about, say, a bicycle path that violates thermodynamics, this claim falls within the boundaries of what seems achievable and what would be desirable to achieve. The genuine existence of the real path — confirmable by anyone who searches Polish-language media archives — means the claim passes the first filter of plausibility assessment. "Is there a glow-in-the-dark bike path in Poland?" has the answer "yes." This accurate core insulates the surrounding misinformation from dismissal.

**Epistemic novelty: The compound truth problem.** The fundamental epistemically novel feature of this case is what might be called the compound truth structure: a claim in which the interesting and shareable elements are true (the path exists, it glows, it uses solar energy) while the specific image being shared as documentation is false. This structure is more resistant to debunking than a simple false claim because each debunking attempt faces a complication: "the path is real," which is true, validates the sharer's underlying belief, even when "this specific image is fake" is also asserted. Receivers processing debunking information undergo a partial adjustment — they accept that the specific image might not be authentic — while retaining belief in the core claim, which is accurate. This is entirely rational. The pathology is the missing step: awareness that the most-shared visual documentation is fabricated means the documentation cannot be used as evidence even for a true underlying claim.

**The AI-generated image's aesthetic advantage.** The fake image showing constellation-patterned blue glow is, by any conventional aesthetic measure, more visually spectacular than the real photograph. It is saturated, crisp, dramatically lit, and aesthetically composed. The real path is luminescent but modest: a 100-metre test section in rural Mazury, not a grand urban cycling infrastructure. The AI-generated image presents the platonic ideal of what a glow-in-the-dark path should look like — what would make you stop scrolling, what would win an Instagram engagement optimisation tournament. There is a systematic selection pressure in social media content toward the more aesthetically impressive version of true stories, irrespective of accuracy. When a fake image is more compelling than the real photograph, the fake image propagates more successfully — not because viewers don't care about truth but because they don't know the authentic photograph also exists and is less compelling.

**Geographic unfamiliarity.** Most of the international audience sharing this content had limited direct knowledge of Poland's cycling infrastructure, regional geography, or the media coverage landscape in which the actual path was documented in 2016. For this audience, there was no ready reference point against which to check: "Does Pruszków have a famous bike path?" or "Has this area of Mazury been in the news for cycling?" The unfamiliarity of the geographic context reduced the probability that errors in location claims would be caught. This is a general pattern: false location attributions in viral content succeed more frequently when the named location is unfamiliar to the majority of the audience.

## Verdict

**Compound.** The claim that a solar-powered, glow-in-the-dark bicycle path exists in Poland is **substantially true**: such a path was built in 2016 near Lidzbark Warmiński by the TPA company, using phosphorescent luminescent aggregate that charges via sunlight and emits light for up to 10 hours. It was a 100-metre test section. The path underwent renovation in 2022 and is reported operational as of 2025.

The widely-shared image used to illustrate this true story is **almost certainly false**: it appears to be an AI-generated or digitally manipulated image that does not depict the actual path, characterised by stylised aesthetic qualities inconsistent with real phosphorescent surface photography.

The location claim that the path is in Pruszków is **false**: Pruszków is where TPA (the designer) is based, not where the path is located.

Snopes elected not to issue a binary verdict, describing the situation as involving "a mixture of true and false information presented in various forms." This is an accurate characterisation. The case illustrates the limitations of binary verdict formats when applied to compound-accuracy claims.

## The Wider Picture

The Polish glow-in-the-dark bike path story opens into several substantive contexts that extend well beyond the narrow question of whether a specific image is authentic.

**Photoluminescence materials science.** The technology underlying the TPA path is photoluminescence — a process in which a material absorbs photons (in this case, from solar radiation) and re-emits them at lower energy (visible wavelengths) through a delayed fluorescence mechanism. Phosphorescent materials that function this way have been known since the 17th century (the discovery of "Bologna stone," barium sulfide, documented by Vincenzo Casciarolo circa 1603) and are now produced with high efficiency using rare-earth-doped strontium aluminate compounds, where strontium aluminate (SrAl₂O₄) doped with europium provides the bright blue-green luminescence characteristic of high-performance glow materials. Research by Matsuzawa et al. (1996, *Journal of the Electrochemical Society*) established the exceptional performance of europium-doped strontium aluminate, which achieves luminescence after-glow durations orders of magnitude longer than earlier zinc sulfide-based materials. The TPA path's claim of 10-hour afterglow duration is consistent with the properties of europium-doped strontium aluminate.

**Cycling infrastructure innovation.** The TPA path sits within a broader wave of innovation in cycling infrastructure design, driven by urbanisation, sustainability goals, and the design ambitions of a generation of urban planners who have championed cycling as both functional mobility and public space. The Dutch cycling infrastructure system, arguably the world's most sophisticated, has long treated cycling paths as design objects rather than mere functional surfaces. Phosphorescent and solar-integrated path surfaces represent an extension of this design philosophy: infrastructure that is functional by day and experiential by night. Research on cycling infrastructure design has documented relationships between path quality characteristics and cycling uptake (Pucher et al., 2010, *Preventive Medicine*), suggesting that aesthetically appealing path infrastructure contributes positively to mode-shift toward cycling in urban and peri-urban areas.

**The AI-generated image ecology.** The appearance of an AI-generated image as the primary viral documentation for a real event represents one of the more challenging dynamics in contemporary misinformation management. Earlier misinformation models often involved entirely fabricated events (fake claims with fake documentation) or entirely real events with misrepresented significance. The compound truth category — real event, fake documentation — creates symmetrical vulnerabilities: fact-checkers asserting "the image is fake" can be countered with "but the path is real," while fact-checkers asserting "the path is real" implicitly legitimate the broader post even when the image is fabricated. As image generation AI improves in visual quality, this compound structure will likely become more prevalent — especially for claims about real but obscure phenomena, where the authentic photographic record is limited and the generated image can compete aesthetically without facing close comparison to a well-known reference photograph.

**Poland's sustainable infrastructure reputation.** Poland has been an active participant in European cycling infrastructure development and in broader sustainable urban design. The TPA path was not an isolated innovation but was part of a pattern of Polish construction and materials companies applying advanced materials science to infrastructure problems. Poland's infrastructure investment trajectory has accelerated since EU accession, with significant EU structural fund investment in transport and cycling infrastructure. The path's location in the Mazury region — a major tourism destination known for its lake district and outdoor recreation culture — gave it natural visibility for a cycling innovation: it was clearly positioned as a pilot project with potential tourist as well as functional appeal.

**Verification challenges in 2016 versus 2024.** The TPA path was built and initially documented in 2016, in Polish-language media primarily. The viral spread occurred in 2024, eight years later. By this time, the original 2016 source documentation was archival — accessible but requiring deliberate search in Polish-language archives. The gap between original documentation and viral spread creates a fact-checking challenge: the claim is old enough to have generated thick contextual documentation in a primary language, but new enough to the viral audience that this documentation is unknown. Reconstructing accurate claims about an eight-year-old infrastructure project requires different search strategies than fact-checking a current news event, and the distance in time also means the path's status (renovation, current operational condition) requires fresh verification rather than reliance on 2016 reporting.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**The "no verdict" decision.** Snopes journalist Aleksandra Wrona's decision not to assign a binary verdict to this claim is itself informative. The Snopes rating system is designed for claims that resolve to a clear true-false axis; it does not have a built-in rating for compound-accuracy claims. The decision to note that "we have elected not to provide a rating in this article" reflects editorial honesty about the limitations of the rating format rather than any ambiguity about the facts. Wrona identified the facts clearly: the path exists, this image is fake, the location claim is wrong. What lacks is a single-dimension verdict that captures all of this.

**The importance of distinguishing image from claim.** The analytical structure of the Snopes piece is exemplary in one key way: it treats "is the image authentic" and "does the underlying thing exist" as separate questions rather than collapsing them. Many fact-checks of viral content treat these as equivalent — if the image is fake, the claim is false. Here, they are genuinely separate. The resolution of the image question (probably fake) does not resolve the underlying claim question (is there a glow-in-the-dark path in Poland — yes there is). This distinction, maintained cleanly in the reporting, prevents both overclaiming ("the whole story is fake") and underclaiming ("the story checks out because the path is real").

**The TPA direct source.** Wrona contacted TPA directly and obtained a 2025 response about the path's current status and the 2022 renovation. This direct-source engagement is valuable: it updates an eight-year-old archival record with current operational information, confirming the path remains functional rather than abandoned. It also provides institutional confirmation of the design rationale (luminescent aggregate, not active solar power) from the company with authoritative knowledge of the installation.

**The compound accuracy model as a pattern.** This case study is an example of a broader class of viral content worth flagging as a distinct category in fact-checking practice: real events or installations that gain renewed (and distorted) viral circulation years after their initial documentation, typically paired with AI-generated or heavily manipulated imagery that is more visually compelling than the original photographs. The fact-checking challenge here is not about whether a current claim is true but about accurately reconstructing what a real past event actually was, while simultaneously evaluating new visual content claiming to document it. This temporal complexity — old fact, new fabrication, compound accuracy — will grow more common as AI image generation makes it cheaper to produce spectacular documentation for any real event, regardless of authenticity.
