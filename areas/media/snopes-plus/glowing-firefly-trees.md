# Are Scientists 'Literally Creating Glow-in-the-Dark Trees' With Firefly DNA?

The image is irresistible: city streets lined with softly glowing trees, their leaves and bark suffused with a cool bioluminescent light, firefly DNA rewritten and woven into bark and cambium, nature and engineering merged into something simultaneously ancient and futuristic. No power lines, no utility poles, no carbon footprint. Just light emerging from living wood.

It is a genuinely beautiful idea. It has been the subject of art, speculative design, conceptual architecture, and real scientific research. And thanks to a 15-second TikTok video posted in January 2021, it became the subject of one of the most comprehensively wrong viral science claims of the year — a claim that packed an impressive density of errors into an extraordinarily small amount of time.

This entry examines what the video claimed, what is actually true about the science of bioluminescent plants, how the errors in the video arose, and what the case reveals about the mechanics of science misinformation in the social media era.

---

## 1. The Claim

On January 30, 2021, a TikTok user posted a short video making the following claim:

> "Dutch scientists have located and isolated the gene that makes fireflies able to glow, recombined the DNA, and put that DNA in plants, literally creating glow-in-the-dark trees. Eco-friendly outdoor nightlights!"

The video was brief — approximately 15 seconds of narration over imagery of strikingly bright, luminescent trees glowing in the dark. It accumulated a substantial viewership and propagated through the social media ecosystem, inspiring admiration, wonder, and considerable credulity. The video invited viewers to "follow for more" and presented the development as a current, achieved scientific breakthrough.

The specific claims embedded in this short statement are: (1) that Dutch scientists are responsible for this development; (2) that they located and isolated the gene that makes fireflies glow; (3) that they inserted this gene into plants; (4) that these plants are now "literally" glowing trees; and (5) that these trees are suitable as "eco-friendly outdoor nightlights."

Every one of these claims is false or substantially misleading. Some of them are wrong in ways that involve a single factual error. Others are wrong in ways that require unpacking layers of confused attribution, overhyped reporting, and the significant gap between scientific research and commercial or practical deployment. The imagery used in the video makes things worse: the trees shown glowing brilliantly in the video were not products of genetic modification at all — they were part of an art installation that achieved its glow by literally shining lights onto trees in the dark.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

The Snopes fact-check of this viral claim, published February 4, 2021, methodically dismantles each assertion in the video. What emerges is a picture not of one simple lie but of a cascade of confusions — some attributable to the TikTok creator, and some traceable back to sloppy journalism from years earlier.

**The Dutch connection: an artist, not a scientist.** The "Dutch scientists" referred to in the video appear to be a reference to Daan Roosegaarde, a Dutch artist and designer whose studio, Studio Roosegaarde, is known for creating technology-inspired art installations. In 2014, Roosegaarde received significant U.S. media coverage while promoting his work at the SXSW festival in Austin, Texas. During an interview there, he discussed using biologically inspired technology to address environmental problems — and he held up an early product from a company called Bioglow as an example of the kind of biomimicry that inspired his thinking.

What happened next is a case study in how journalism can corrupt information. Several reporters who covered Roosegaarde at SXSW apparently concluded — inaccurately — that he had personally developed the glowing plants he was discussing. Outlets including The Huffington Post and DogoNews published pieces attributing the development to Roosegaarde. These pieces were incorrect. Roosegaarde was an artist who had referenced a scientist's work as an inspiration for his own conceptual thinking. He had not located or isolated any genes, had not created any genetically modified plants, and had not built any glowing trees. Some reporting, including a more careful piece in The Verge, did note that there were theoretical discussions about Roosegaarde and the scientist potentially collaborating — but no such collaboration was ever documented as having produced results.

The imagery in the TikTok video makes the confusion worse. The trees shown glowing in the video were from Roosegaarde's art installation "Glowing Nature" — which achieved its luminescent effect by shining lights onto trees, not through any form of genetic modification. These were not products of bioengineering; they were lit trees, presented in the video as evidence of a genetic breakthrough.

**The gene attribution: luciferase, not firefly DNA.** The claim that scientists "isolated the gene that makes fireflies glow" and inserted it into plants is also misleading, though in a more nuanced way. The chemistry of bioluminescence — the biological production of light — does involve an enzyme called luciferase, which catalyses a reaction with chemicals called luciferins to produce light. Fireflies use a form of this reaction, as do many other bioluminescent organisms including deep-sea fish, glowing jellyfish, and certain species of fungus.

What is misleading is the implication that "the gene that makes fireflies glow" is a single, discrete, neatly isolatable gene that can simply be transplanted into a tree to produce an equivalent glow. The biology is more complex. Multiple research groups working on glow-in-the-dark plants have used different systems and different genetic materials. Crucially, many of the most prominent efforts have not used firefly genes at all. The luciferase enzyme and the luciferins it interacts with can both be produced or applied from non-firefly sources. Bioglow, the company whose plants Roosegaarde referenced, developed plants through a different mechanism entirely. MIT researchers who published notable work on nanobionic glowing plants in 2017 used luciferase sourced from fireflies but delivered it through nanoparticles embedded in leaves — not through heritable genetic transformation.

The claim about "firefly DNA" specifically is the most popular and most evocative version of these developments, but it is a simplification that papers over significant variation in technique, approach, and biological mechanism across different research efforts.

**The brightness problem: "some people over 40 may not be able to see the glow."** Perhaps the most striking gap between the viral claim and reality is on the question of how brightly these plants actually glow. The TikTok video, with its dramatic imagery of brilliantly luminescent trees, implies a level of light output that would indeed make them viable as outdoor nightlights. The reality is dramatically different.

Bioglow's actual plants — the ones that existed and could be purchased — were described in the company's own promotional materials as glowing so faintly that "some people over forty may not be able to see the glow." A Bioglow promotional video from 2015 shows plants in a completely darkened room emitting a barely perceptible faint luminescence. These were not outdoor lighting solutions. They were scientific curiosities requiring complete darkness to see at all.

The gap between "plant produces faint, barely visible bioluminescence under ideal laboratory conditions" and "eco-friendly outdoor nightlight" is not a matter of incremental improvement. It is an engineering, energy, and biological challenge of enormous proportions. Living cells have metabolic constraints. The amount of energy available for bioluminescence is limited by the organism's biology. Scaling bioluminescence to the brightness needed for streetlighting would require fundamental advances not just in genetic engineering but in plant metabolism that remain entirely theoretical.

**Are glowing trees real yet?** As of the time of the Snopes fact-check (February 2021), glowing trees — as in full-sized trees producing practically useful levels of light through biological mechanisms — did not exist and remained theoretical. The research landscape at that time included: (1) Bioglow's faintly glowing houseplants, which the company appeared to have dissolved by 2021; (2) MIT's nanoparticle-based approach, which was not heritable genetic modification but rather a treatment applied to existing plants; (3) various academic research groups working on bioluminescent plant systems; and (4) an ongoing research interest in using the "fungal" bioluminescence pathway — distinct from firefly bioluminescence — which had shown more promise in plants. None of these constituted glowing trees suitable for outdoor lighting.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The viral spread of this TikTok claim followed a pattern that is well understood in science communication research. Several distinct factors made this particular claim especially prone to uncritical acceptance.

**The idea is genuinely beautiful.** This is not a trivial factor. Science communication research consistently shows that claims about science are more likely to be believed and shared when they are aesthetically appealing — when they describe a world that people wish existed. Bioluminescent trees as street lighting hit every note: they are ecologically appealing (no electricity, no carbon), visually striking, suggestive of a harmonious relationship between human engineering and natural systems, and fundamentally hopeful in their vision of technological possibility. The desire to believe this is real is entirely understandable, and the desire to share it with others so that they can experience the same wonder is equally understandable.

**The underlying science is real and genuinely impressive.** The reason the TikTok claim gained traction is not that the idea emerged from nowhere. Bioluminescent plant research is a real field. MIT really did publish widely covered work on nanobionic glowing plants. Bioglow really did create plants that glowed. Real scientists are genuinely interested in the possibility of bioluminescent trees as lighting sources. The existence of real science in the same general space as the claim makes it much easier for a credulous viewer to accept the specific claims in the video — particularly when those specific claims are presented confidently, without qualifications.

**Confudsed journalism from 2014 did the groundwork.** The errors in the TikTok video did not originate with the TikTok creator, at least not entirely. The false attribution of bioluminescent plant research to Daan Roosegaarde was established in media coverage from 2014 that incorrectly reported Roosegaarde as having developed the plants rather than simply referencing another scientist's work. By the time the TikTok was made in 2021, these incorrect reports had circulated for six years and had been reproduced, shared, and treated as established background knowledge by various online sources. The TikTok creator appears to have drawn on this established — but incorrect — framing without knowing it was wrong. This is a common mechanism of science misinformation: error enters the record, is not corrected or is under-corrected, and is then propagated by subsequent reportage that treats the previous error as a reliable source.

**Visual evidence is presented that seems to confirm the claim.** The TikTok video used imagery from Roosegaarde's "Glowing Nature" art installation — trees glowing brilliantly in the dark. For a viewer watching a claim about glowing trees, the presence of vivid images of glowing trees functions as powerful confirmation of the claim. There is no obvious visual signal that these trees were glowing because of external lighting rather than internal biology. The aesthetic gap between "tree lit by a light" and "tree lit from within by engineered bioluminescence" is, at night in a photograph, essentially invisible. The visual evidence is persuasive precisely because it is real — these trees do look exactly like what the claim describes — while being entirely causally disconnected from the claimed mechanism.

**TikTok's format rewards confident brevity.** The 15-second format rewards confident, vivid claims over careful qualification. A more accurate version of the same video — "researchers have created plants that glow very faintly through a mechanism that uses some of the same biochemistry as firefly bioluminescence, but these are not trees, they are not bright enough to use as lighting, and the glowing trees shown in this video are actually an art installation" — does not perform well on TikTok. The platform structurally selects for the kind of claim that was made.

## 4. Verdict

Snopes rated the claim "False." That rating is entirely appropriate given the density of errors in the original claim.

The specific assertions in the TikTok video are false. The Dutch scientist who is apparently the source of the story was actually an artist who neither created nor developed any glowing plants or trees. The visual evidence presented was from an art installation, not genetic engineering. The plants that have actually been created through bioluminescent research glow so faintly they are barely visible to the naked eye in complete darkness and are in no sense viable as outdoor lighting. Glowing trees, as the claim describes them, do not exist as of the fact-check date.

At the same time, this is not a case where the underlying idea has no relationship to reality. Bioluminescent plant research is real, it is interesting, it is ongoing, and some researchers do envision, in the long term, practical applications that could include low-level lighting. The claim is "distorted-but-grounded" — a viral oversimplification and misattribution layered on top of real, if more modest and more complicated, scientific work.

For readers encountering similar viral science claims, this case illustrates a useful heuristic: the more spectacular the implied achievement, the more carefully the specific mechanism and evidence should be examined. "Scientists have made progress toward" and "scientists have literally created" are very different claims, and the latter is almost always wrong when attached to cutting-edge research.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The glowing trees story is, on one level, about a TikTok video that was wrong. On another level, it is about a set of structural problems in how scientific research travels from laboratories to public understanding.

**Science communication and the hype cycle.** The errors in the 2021 TikTok video can be traced all the way back to 2014 media coverage that overhyped Roosegaarde's work and misidentified him as a scientist rather than an artist. This is not unusual: science journalism operates under significant pressure to produce engaging, shareable stories, and research near the speculative or early-stage frontier is routinely covered in ways that overstate how close developments are to practical application. The overhyped coverage of 2014 was not corrected efficiently, was reproduced in various forms over the following years, and ultimately resurfaced in a version stripped of even the caveats that the 2014 articles may have included.

This is one of the most common mechanisms of science misinformation: not deliberate fabrication, but repeated compression and distortion as a piece of reporting passes through successive retellings, each of which strips away a little more context, a few more caveats, another degree of uncertainty. By the time it reaches TikTok in 2021, a speculative discussion of a Dutch artist's conceptual vision for bioluminescent streetlights has become "Dutch scientists literally created glow-in-the-dark trees."

**The genuine science deserves attention.** One cost of misinformation corrections is that careful debunking of spectacular false claims can inadvertently make the underlying real science seem less interesting than it is. The development of bioluminescent plants, even at the modest scale achieved by Bioglow and MIT, is genuinely remarkable. Growing plants that produce any measurable biological light involves overcoming significant challenges in genetic engineering and cellular metabolism. The research is ongoing and has potential applications not just in decorative or lighting contexts but in biosensing — plants that glow in the presence of specific pollutants or pathogens, for instance.

The luciferase-luciferin bioluminescence system, broadly shared across many organisms, is one of the most elegant and well-studied biochemical systems known. The fact that researchers have been able to engineer versions of this system into plant cells at all represents a genuine achievement that popular audiences might find as interesting as the fictional glowing trees, if it were communicated with appropriate context.

**On the relationship between art, science, and public communication.** The Roosegaarde case raises an interesting and under-discussed problem: what happens when artists who work at the intersection of art and technology are mistakenly reported as scientists? Roosegaarde explicitly described his work as inspired by science and as exploring the aesthetic and social dimensions of technological possibility. He did not claim to have created bioluminescent trees. The misidentification was created by sloppy journalism. But the broader pattern — where artists working in science-adjacent spaces are covered in ways that blur the distinction between artistic vision and scientific achievement — is worth noting. Art and science are both valuable, but they operate by different standards of evidence and have different relationships to factual claims about the world. Conflating them, even inadvertently, generates exactly the kind of confusion that allows viral misinformation to take root.

**On the ethics of science social media.** The TikTok video in question was one of millions of pieces of science content on the platform. The platform's format, recommendation algorithm, and engagement metrics all reward content that generates strong positive reactions — wonder, excitement, pride in human ingenuity. These are not bad reactions; they are valuable parts of a healthy relationship to science. But they can be exploited, inadvertently or otherwise, by content that delivers the emotional satisfaction of genuine scientific wonder without the factual accuracy. Building media literacy around science content means helping audiences ask not just "is this amazing?" but "is this true, and how would I check?"

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check of this TikTok claim, published February 4, 2021, is an example of thorough, well-structured science debunking that goes beyond a simple true/false verdict to unpack the full architecture of the claim's errors.

The piece identifies the claim's asserted elements individually — "Dutch scientists," "the gene that makes fireflies able to glow," the existence of actual glowing trees, the suitability as nightlights — and evaluates each one separately. This structural approach prevents the kind of partial endorsement that can result from a more holistic verdict: a response that says "well, there is something true here" without specifying which parts are true and which are not tends to legitimate more of the false claim than it should.

The piece does excellent work tracing the 2014 journalism that created the false Roosegaarde attribution, which is important both for readers who want to understand the full chain of error and for the broader fact-checking ecosystem, which benefits from having sloppy originating journalism identified clearly. This kind of upstream error attribution helps future journalists and fact-checkers by flagging unreliable sources.

The Snopes verdict is "False," which is appropriate given that the specific claims in the TikTok video are false. A "Misleading" or "Mostly False" rating might have been defensible as a way of acknowledging that the underlying science is real — but given the density and severity of the errors, "False" is not unreasonable.

What the fact-check handles more briefly — though this is a perennial challenge given space constraints — is the positive explanation of what the real science involves. The piece mentions Bioglow and the MIT nanoparticle research and notes that these produce faint light, but does not go into sufficient technical depth for readers to come away with a clear, accurate picture of where bioluminescent plant research actually stands. This is a common limitation of science fact-checking: debunking a specific false claim is easier than constructing, in the same space, a positive and accurate account of the underlying science. More ambitious science communication would do both.

For readers encountering similar viral science claims, the glowing trees case offers a useful mental checklist: Is the claim specific about what has been achieved versus what is theoretically possible? Does the visual evidence actually show the thing being claimed, or something that looks like it? Can the named individuals or institutions be verified, and do they claim credit for what the video attributes to them? How old is the underlying science, and has it advanced beyond what is being claimed? Applying these questions to the TikTok video would have identified its errors within minutes.
