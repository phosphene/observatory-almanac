## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In May 2013, an image showing the ingredients label on a package of Kraft Macaroni & Cheese reportedly purchased at a Tesco supermarket in Ponders End, England, circulated widely on social media. The label bore two unusual features: a warning that the product "may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children" and a "GMO Declaration" stating the product was "Made from genetically modified wheat (may contain GMO)." These two statements were interpreted by many online commenters as confirming that Kraft used illegal GMO wheat and that the European Union government had classified GMO foods as hazardous to children via the same warning label used for the controversial dyes.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2013) assigned this claim the verdict **False**. The verdict rests on three independently sufficient grounds: (1) no commercially authorized GMO wheat exists anywhere in the world, making it logistically and legally impossible for Kraft to have used it; (2) Kraft Foods confirmed to Snopes that the GMO label was not placed by Kraft and that the company importing the product to the UK was not an authorized Kraft distributor; and (3) even if the label were genuine, the "adverse effect on activity and attention" warning is mandated by EU regulations governing artificial food colorings — not GMO ingredients — making the implied causal chain between the two statements factually incorrect.

The claim thus involves two distinct errors layered on top of an authentic underlying fact: Kraft Macaroni & Cheese did, at the time, contain synthetic dyes (Yellow No. 5 / E102 and Yellow No. 6 / E110) that EU regulations required to carry a behavioral warning. The misattribution of that warning to GMO content rather than to synthetic dyes, and the fabrication of a GMO declaration for a legally nonexistent substance, compounded minor regulatory literacy gaps into a viral false claim with significant resonance in anti-GMO advocacy communities.

## §2 The Anatomy of a Label Forgery: Documentary Analysis

The physical label shown in the circulating image originated with a photo posted to Facebook by Flo Wrightson Cross, who purchased the Kraft Mac & Cheese package at a Tesco store in Ponders End, England. Wrightson Cross shared the image without commentary on the unusual label components; subsequent reposting by others added interpretive text claiming the product was admitting to GMO wheat content. This two-step spread pattern — authentic photograph, secondary misinterpretation — is characteristic of a subset of food-labeling misinformation cases where the evidentiary material is real but its content is either misread or supplemented with fabricated context.

The "GMO Declaration" section of the label is the locus of the falsification. Kraft Foods confirmed to Mikkelson (2013) in direct correspondence that: (a) they do not export Macaroni & Cheese to the United Kingdom and have no authorized UK distributor; (b) the company applying the sticker to the package was not a Kraft customer and was obtaining the product through unauthorized secondary channels; (c) Kraft does not use GMO wheat in any product, and no such product is commercially available anywhere. This statement establishes that the label as shown was applied by an unauthorized reseller, making the traceability of the label's origin to an actual labeling authority (Kraft or a government body) effectively impossible.

The implausibility of the claim is underscored by the regulatory context. As of 2013 — and as of 2026 — no country in the world has authorized commercial planting, harvest, or sale of genetically modified wheat. Numerous experimental transgenic wheat strains have been developed by researchers; Monsanto (since acquired by Bayer) developed a Roundup-Ready (glyphosate-tolerant) wheat variety and conducted field trials in the early 2000s but voluntarily withdrew it from regulatory review in 2004 after agricultural market concerns about export contamination (USDA APHIS, 2004). Subsequent regulatory frameworks have remained closed to GMO wheat commercial approval in both the United States and the EU. The practical consequence is that the supply chain through which wheat enters a processed food product like Kraft Macaroni & Cheese would have no GMO wheat available to purchase at commercial scale from any legitimate supplier anywhere in the world.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The EU Dye Warning and its Misreading — A Case Study in Regulatory Literacy Gaps

The epistemic novelty of this case sits at the intersection of two distinct regulatory domains that the circulating image and its commentators conflated: EU regulations governing food coloring and EU regulations governing GMO ingredients. That conflation produced a claim that is false but that contains a true embedded kernel — the "adverse effect on activity and attention in children" warning is genuinely an EU regulatory requirement — misleadingly applied to a false content claim (GMO wheat). Understanding why this confusion occurred requires examining the architecture of both regulatory systems and the lay knowledge gaps that make such convergence plausible to uninformed readers.

The behavioral warning on UK food labels derives from EU Regulation No. 1333/2008 on food additives and its implementing measure in 2010, which mandated that any food containing one of six specific azo dyes display the warning "may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children." The six dyes — Sunset Yellow (E110), Quinoline Yellow (E104), Carmoisine (E122), Allura Red (E129), Tartrazine (E102), and Ponceau 4R (E124) — were implicated by the "Southampton Study": a randomized controlled trial published by McCann et al. (2007, *The Lancet*, Vol. 370, No. 9598) in which children given mixtures containing these dyes showed significantly increased hyperactivity ratings compared to a control group receiving a placebo mixture. The study's findings were influential despite methodological debates; the UK Food Standards Agency subsequently recommended voluntary removal, and the EU mandated the warning label as a precautionary measure.

Kraft Macaroni & Cheese products sold in the US at the time contained Yellow No. 5 (tartrazine / E102) and Yellow No. 6 (sunset yellow / E110), both of which are among the Southampton Six. Products sold or imported to EU-jurisdiction markets therefore required the warning, which explains its genuine presence on the label's "Allergen Information" section. This portion of the label was real and was placed by whoever applied the UK-market labeling.

The GMO declaration was not real in the same sense. However, the lay reading that conflates these two warning systems rests on a plausible but wrong assumption: that warning labels co-appear on a food package because they refer to the same ingredient or concern. EU GMO labeling requirements (Regulation 1829/2003 and Regulation 1830/2003) are entirely separate from the dye-warning regime and require labels stating "genetically modified" or "produced from genetically modified [ingredient]" whenever a food contains more than 0.9% GMO-derived content. If both a synthetic dye warning and a GMO declaration appeared on the same label, the correct inference would be that the product contains both qualifying dyes and qualifying GMO ingredients — but each warning statement relates exclusively to its own trigger, not to the other.

This multi-layered regulatory literacy gap — unfamiliarity with which specific dyes trigger the behavioral warning, unfamiliarity with the legal nonexistence of GMO wheat, and unfamiliarity with the independent triggering structures of different EU warning label regimes — is at the **R (regulatory)** EARC level. The gap is not primarily about scientific evidence but about institutional knowledge of food safety governance frameworks that neither consumer audiences nor general-interest media typically possess.

## §4 The Anti-GMO Misinformation Ecosystem and How This Claim Circulated

The 2013 Kraft GMO claim did not emerge in a vacuum. It circulated during a period of heightened public attention to GMO food labeling, particularly in the United States, where ballot initiatives in California (2012) and Washington State (2013) had recently contested mandatory GMO labeling requirements. The claim gained traction specifically through Facebook pages and websites associated with anti-GMO advocacy, where it served as an apparent piece of evidence that European regulators had officially declared GMO foods harmful to children — a narrative consistent with the advocacy position that US food regulators were concealing similar risks.

The scientific consensus on GMO food safety at the time — and since — does not support the narrative that GMO foods pose categorical health risks. The National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2016, *Genetically Engineered Crops: Experiences and Prospects*) conducted a comprehensive review of hundreds of studies and concluded there is no substantiated evidence of a difference in risks to human health from currently commercialized GE foods compared to conventionally bred crop varieties. This consensus does not settle policy debates about labeling, environmental impacts, or corporate control of agriculture, but it does bear on the specific factual claim — embedded in the viral image — that GMO content causes behavioral harm to children.

Broadly, the structural feature exploited by the claim is the **legitimacy borrowing** that occurs when regulatory documents are repurposed by advocacy narratives. EU food safety institutions carry high perceived legitimacy in U.S. discourse, particularly in contexts where individuals believe U.S. regulatory agencies are more captured by industry interests. Claims formatted as "the EU requires a warning label because they know [X is harmful]" carry disproportionate persuasive weight with audiences who defer to European regulatory authority. The fact that the UK-market Kraft label genuinely had a warning (for dyes) made the falsified GMO addition appear plausible by proximity.

## §5 Kraft's Subsequent Reformulation and Dye Policy

A significant postscript to this case is that Kraft Foods partially responded to public pressure around the dye controversy by undertaking reformulations beginning in 2013 and accelerating through 2016. In October 2013 — four months after the Snopes fact-check was published — Kraft announced it would remove artificial dyes from three of its shaped macaroni and cheese varieties (SpongeBob Squarepants, Halloween, and winter shapes), replacing them with natural color sources while also reducing sodium and saturated fat. The reformulation did not initially extend to the product's core "Original Flavor" elbow-shaped variety.

By 2016, Kraft Foods Group (then merged with Heinz as Kraft Heinz) completed a broader reformulation of its Original Flavor Macaroni & Cheese, replacing Yellow No. 5 and Yellow No. 6 with colorants derived from paprika, annatto, and turmeric. The company launched this reformulated product without announcing the change publicly, reportedly selling approximately 50 million boxes under the new formula before publicly revealing the reformulation (Kraft Heinz, press communications, 2016). This unusual stealth-reformulation strategy was partly a response to having observed that consumers who knew in advance that the color had been changed reported dissatisfaction with the product despite being unable to detect the change in blind-taste tests.

This behavioral finding — that product acceptance is influenced by top-down beliefs about ingredient changes rather than solely by sensory detection — is consistent with the broader literature on food neophobia and ingredient-belief effects documented by Rozin et al. (2004, *Appetite*, Vol. 43, No. 2), who found that consumers' acceptance of reformulated products is strongly influenced by how the reformulation is framed and whether notions of "naturalness" are activated. The stealth approach, while commercially effective, raised separate transparency concerns about consumer notification of formulation changes.

## §6 Verdict Stability and Ongoing Relevance

The False verdict assigned by Mikkelson (2013) remains fully stable. No GMO wheat has been approved for commercial production in any jurisdiction between 2013 and 2026. No subsequent evidence has emerged suggesting that the GMO label shown in the viral image originated with Kraft or any authorized distributor. The evidentiary basis for the claim is a label applied by an unauthorized reseller that contained a fabricated declaration for a legally nonexistent ingredient category.

Several dimensions of this case retain current relevance. First, the underlying behavioral warning mechanism — the EU dye-warning regime triggered by the Southampton Six — continues to operate and remains often misunderstood outside specialist food safety and regulatory communities. Products containing the relevant dyes and sold in EU markets still carry the warning as of regulatory compliance records through 2024. Second, the claim's life cycle illustrates the durable challenge facing fact-checkers addressing viral food labeling claims: the combination of an authentic visual artifact (the real label), a genuine true element (the dye warning was real), and a fabricated addition (the GMO declaration) creates a mixed-truth structure that is more resistant to simple debunking than wholly fabricated content.

Third, the case illustrates the jurisdictional complexity of food safety misinformation in a globally connected supply chain: an unauthorized UK reseller of a US product applied a label that generated international viral attention claiming regulatory determinations that no entity in either jurisdiction had made. The lack of authorized distribution made the full genealogy of the label impossible to trace within the timeframe of media attention — a pattern that has repeated in numerous subsequent food-labeling viral claims.

The **R (regulatory)** EARC gap identified here — insufficient public understanding of how different EU food warning label regimes interact and what each specifically signals — could be partially addressed through consumer education initiatives accompanying mandatory label changes. Broader interventions targeting GMO-literacy gaps in general public knowledge remain a priority for science communication research and practice. As of 2026, the basic structure of the confusion documented here — belief that European food safety warnings about one issue (artificial dyes) represent a general endorsement of concern about GMO safety — remains plausible and capable of generating similar future viral claims.
