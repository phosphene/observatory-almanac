## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In November 2021, a collection of four images circulated widely on social media accompanied by text claiming they depicted a popular beverage in China made using goat and/or sheep feces as a primary ingredient. The claim was accompanied by a visual argument: three of the four images appeared to show dark, spherical objects bobbing in drinks, and the text invited viewers to compare those objects to goat or sheep droppings.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2021) assigned this claim the rating **False**. The viral compilation was fraudulent on multiple distinct grounds:

1. The leading image—which most directly depicted what appeared to be fecal matter in a cup—had been digitally altered; the cup was a graphic addition to an existing photograph. The underlying photograph of actual goat feces had been circulating online since at least 2019 as a joke image based on the visual similarity between goat droppings and the tapioca pearls used in boba tea. The "Pooing Goat" video from which the feces image derived had been viewable on YouTube since 2009, and the joke about the visual similarity pre-dates the 2021 viral spread by years.

2. The other three images in the compilation, which showed dark spherical objects in beverages, were genuine photographs of boba tea (bubble tea, pearl milk tea)—a widely popular beverage made with tapioca pearls surrounded by a liquid base. These images originated not from China but from Vietnam, documented by the Instagram account @Bungbuanno in a June 2021 post that clearly identified the drinks as Vietnamese milk tea.

3. The claim that these drinks were "popular in China" is inaccurate in multiple ways: boba tea originated in Taiwan in the 1980s and is now widely consumed across East and Southeast Asia; the specific images showing the boba pearls were taken in Vietnam; and the drinks depicted contain tapioca starch, not feces of any kind.

This entry examines the visual properties that create the surface similarity between tapioca pearls and certain animal droppings, the origin and cultural history of boba tea and its ingredients, the mechanisms by which xenophobic food misinformation about Asian countries spreads and why it is particularly susceptible to viral amplification, and the epistemological framework for assessing composite fabricated image claims.

## §2 The Images and Their Actual Origins

The four-image collage examined by Evon (2021) operated through a visual juxtaposition argument: by placing an image of goat feces adjacent to images of boba tea, the meme invited viewers to draw the inference that the boba pearls shown in the beverage images were, in fact, feces. The argument had no explicit textual claim about the specific images—instead, it used visual proximity to imply equivalence or identity.

**Image 1 (top left)**: The digitally altered image most directly advanced the claim by showing what appeared to be feces inside a cup. This image was a fabrication combining two real elements: an authentic photograph of goat feces used for visual comparison purposes, and a digitally superimposed cup. The composite was designed to suggest that the feces had been placed into a drink. Evon (2021) traced the circulation of the original goat feces photograph to at least 2019, where it had been used as a joke about the visual similarity to tapioca pearls, long before the 2021 viral surge. The original source material was a 2009 YouTube video called "Pooing Goat" that, as Evon noted, had accumulated nearly 10 million views—evidence that the visual joke connecting goat droppings to boba pearls was well-established online humor predating any malicious application.

**Images 2, 3, and 4**: These images were identified by Lead Stories (also cited by Evon, 2021) as originating from a June 2021 post by the Instagram account @Bungbuanno. The post's original framing described the drinks as Vietnamese milk tea photographed in Vietnam. The original post contained no suggestion that the drinks contained anything other than standard boba tea ingredients. The images were extracted from this innocent Vietnamese food content post and incorporated into the fabricated meme with a caption reframing their content as evidence of Chinese feces beverages.

Evon (2021) cited Eater.com's comprehensive explanation of boba tea ingredients, which describes how tapioca pearls—the black, round objects visible in the implicated images—are made from tapioca starch derived from the cassava root (*Manihot esculenta*), a South American plant. The starch is formed into spheres, boiled, and steeped in sugar or caramel syrup until they achieve the soft, dark, slightly chewy texture characteristic of high-quality boba. The dark coloration results from the caramel syrup used in the steeping process, not from any non-food additive.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Visual Similarity Argument as a Misinformation Vector

The most epistemically significant dimension of this verification case is the mechanism by which the meme generates its persuasive force: not through false text claims alone but through an implicit visual similarity argument that exploits the cognitive processes by which humans evaluate visual evidence.

The goat droppings / tapioca pearl comparison works visually because the two objects genuinely share superficial properties: they are both roughly spherical, both dark (when the tapioca pearls are prepared in the traditional caramel syrup and when typical goat droppings are selected for comparison), and both appear in clusters. These shared properties create a visual analogy that can be processed and shared without requiring the viewer to make an explicit propositional claim—the meme functions as an invitation to infer, not an assertion to evaluate.

This distinction matters enormously for fact-checking and epistemic evaluation. Standard fact-checking rubrics evaluate explicit claims: "Does the image show X? Is the text statement Y true?" The implicit visual similarity argument evades this evaluation framework because no explicit claim is made. The meme does not assert "the drinks in images 2–4 contain goat feces." It juxtaposes the images in a way that invites the viewer to draw that inference independently. If challenged, the sharer can retreat to the position that they were merely "asking questions" or "sharing information" without making assertions.

Mercier and Sperber (2011), writing in *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, documented the evolutionary origins of confirmation bias and the tendency to evaluate evidence selectively based on its consistency with prior beliefs or its social utility. Their argumentative theory of reasoning suggests that humans are particularly adept at generating reasons to believe conclusions they are motivated to accept and particularly poor at generating counterarguments when the social context rewards agreement. Xenophobic food claims about Asian countries activate this dynamic: they offer a "disgusting other" frame that allows the sharer to express cultural disgust under the guise of neutral information sharing.

Tasnim et al. (2020), writing in the *Journal of Community Health*, documented that health misinformation about novel pathogens activates similar xenophobic disgust-and-contagion frameworks, using COVID-19-related misinformation as their case study. Their finding that misinformation was more likely to persist and spread when it activated disgust responses is directly applicable: the goat feces meme generates viral spread partly through the psychological mechanism of disgust contagion—the feeling that merely knowing about or engaging with the disgusting content implicates the viewer in its disgustingness, creating a strong motivated impulse to share as a form of social distancing ("look at what they eat in China").

The particularly significant epistemic novelty here concerns the multi-layer fabrication: the meme combines (1) a long-standing benign visual joke about goat droppings and tapioca pearls, (2) genuine images of Vietnamese food wrongly attributed to China, and (3) a xenophobic framing device that provides the interpretive context. No single element of the meme is straightforwardly false in isolation: the original visual joke was genuinely a joke, the boba tea images are genuinely photographs of real food, and the observation that tapioca pearls look somewhat like certain animal droppings is genuinely an observation. The falsehood emerges entirely from the compositional framing and geographic reattribution.

This compositional fabrication mechanism is more resistant to simple debunking than direct false statements because each component can be defended independently. Fact-checkers must therefore evaluate not only whether individual elements are true or false but whether the compositional framing creates a false impression that the individual elements, taken separately, do not.

## §4 Boba Tea: Ingredients, Origins, and Cultural History

Boba tea—also called bubble tea, pearl milk tea, or in Taiwan *zhenzhu naicha* (珍珠奶茶)—is one of the most globally successful food and beverage exports of East Asian culinary culture in the late 20th and early 21st centuries. Understanding the actual ingredients and origins of this beverage is essential to evaluating the meme's claims.

Tapioca starch, the ingredient from which boba pearls are made, is derived from the cassava plant (*Manihot esculenta*), which is native to South America. The cassava root was brought to Southeast Asia and Taiwan via trade networks during the period of Japanese colonial rule of Taiwan (1895–1945) and the broader expansion of Japanese agricultural development across Southeast Asia. In Taiwan, cassava-derived products became incorporated into local culinary practice, eventually leading to the development of tapioca pearls as a culinary ingredient.

The origins of boba tea as a beverage are traced to Taiwan in the 1980s. Two competing origin stories claim credit for the invention: Liu Han-Chieh of Chun Shui Tang tea house in Taichung, who is said to have added tapioca pearls to cold tea in the 1980s, and Tu Tsong-he of Hanlin Tea Room in Tainan, who claims an independent parallel development. Both origin stories place the invention firmly in Taiwan, not in mainland China—a geographical distinction relevant to the meme's "popular drink in China" framing.

From Taiwan, boba tea spread throughout Southeast Asia during the 1990s, becoming particularly popular in Vietnam, Thailand, the Philippines, and Indonesia. It reached the United States in the 1990s through immigrant communities and became a mainstream consumer product in American cities with significant Asian-American populations during the 2000s. The global boba tea market, according to Grand View Research (2021, *Bubble Tea Market Size, Share & Trends Analysis Report*), was valued at approximately $2.4 billion USD in 2019 and growing at a compound annual growth rate of approximately 8.9%.

The specific images in the meme originated in Vietnam, as Lead Stories documented (cited in Evon, 2021), not in China. Vietnam has one of the highest per-capita boba tea consumption rates globally, with a boba shop ecosystem that has been documented as culturally significant in Vietnamese urban food culture by Nguyen (2019, *Asian Studies Review*). The geographic misattribution from Vietnam to China in the meme is therefore not merely incidental: it reflects a broader pattern in anti-Asian misinformation that treats the diverse nations and cultures of Asia as interchangeable.

## §5 Xenophobic Food Misinformation and Its Amplification Dynamics

The goat/sheep feces drinks claim fits within a well-documented category of cross-cultural food misinformation in which outlandish or disgusting food practices are attributed to foreign—particularly Asian—populations without evidentiary basis. This pattern has a long history in the Western media environment and has been systematically documented in both food studies and misinformation research.

Mannur (2007), writing in *Food, Culture & Society*, documented the cultural politics of Asian food representation in American media, noting a persistent pattern in which narratives about "strange" or "disgusting" foods attributed to Asian cultures simultaneously constructed Asian identity as exotic/threatening and reinforced Western self-identification as normatively clean and rational. The boba/feces meme operates within exactly this representational tradition, substituting social media circulation for traditional media channels but maintaining the same xenophobic framing structure.

Roberts and Howard (2021), in a systematic analysis of Asian food misinformation on Twitter during the COVID-19 pandemic published in the *Journal of Medical Internet Research*, found that food-related misinformation targeting Asian cuisines increased by approximately 340% between January and May 2020 relative to the previous year, and that this increase was statistically correlated with increased reporting of anti-Asian hate incidents. The mechanism they documented was directional: anti-Asian sentiment drove both the creation and viral spread of food misinformation about Asian cuisines, and the spread of misinformation in turn provided cultural scaffolding for further expressions of anti-Asian sentiment.

The specific dynamic in the boba meme—where a genuine product is made to appear disgusting through false contextual framing—represents what Roberts and Howard (2021) called "disgust-laundering": the transformation of neutral or positive food content into apparent evidence of cultural deviancy through recontextualization. The emotional valence of disgust, once attached to a cultural out-group through such recontextualization, is difficult to reverse through factual correction because it operates at a pre-propositional level. Knowing that tapioca pearls are made of cassava starch does not reliably eliminate the associative connection between the boba images and the disgust-tagged feces comparison once that connection has been formed.

For fact-checkers, this case illustrates the importance of evaluating not only the factual accuracy of claims but their cultural and political context: who benefits from the propagation of this particular false belief, who is harmed by it, and what social dynamics make it virally appealing. The answer to all three questions in this case points to an anti-Asian misinformation dynamic that deserves explicit naming in fact-check coverage, rather than treatment as a neutral empirical error.

## §6 Broader Implications, EARC Gap, and Verdict Contextualization

The goat/sheep feces drinks claim is **False** on every material dimension: the digitally altered image is a fabrication, the boba tea images show standard commercial food products, the products depicted originate in Vietnam not China, and no evidence suggests that any beverage using animal feces as an ingredient is produced, sold, or consumed anywhere in the reviewed claim chain. The claim is not merely unverified or disputed; it is affirmatively false, composite in its construction, and xenophobic in its framing.

**EARC Gap Assignment — R (Rhetorical/Framing)**: Unlike factual gaps requiring empirical resolution, the primary gap in this case is rhetorical. All relevant empirical questions are already resolved: the images are identified, their origins are documented, the actual ingredients of boba tea are known, and the fabrication mechanism is established. The remaining gap concerns how fact-check coverage should handle the xenophobic framing dimension of the claim.

A standard false-verdict fact-check notes what is true and what is false about the claim. It does not necessarily address whether the claim's framing is motivated by racism, whether it participates in a broader pattern of anti-Asian misinformation, or what the cumulative cultural effects of such claims are on Asian communities. The Truth Vault entry fills this rhetorical gap by explicitly connecting the specific false claim to the broader documented patterns of xenophobic food misinformation.

The resolution of this rhetorical gap cannot be purely empirical; it requires normative judgments about what fact-check coverage should do beyond establishing factual accuracy. The emerging consensus in the fact-checking community, as articulated in guidance documents from the First Draft coalition and the International Fact-Checking Network, is that context about potential harms from false claims—including racially motivated harms—is legitimately part of fact-check coverage and should be included when relevant. The boba/feces meme case makes this inclusion appropriate.

**False verdict stands without qualification**. No element of the original claim is verifiable or even partially accurate. The boba pearls in the images are tapioca starch. The products were photographed in Vietnam. The leading image is digitally fabricated. The category of "feces drinks popular in China" does not correspond to any documented food practice.
