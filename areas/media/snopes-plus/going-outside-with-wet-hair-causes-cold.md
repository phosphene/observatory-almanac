# Going Outside With Wet Hair Gives You a Cold

## 1. The Claim

If you go outside with wet hair in cold weather, you will catch a cold. The mechanism is intuitive: your head is cold and wet, your body temperature drops, your immune system is weakened, viruses take advantage. The rule is transmitted primarily by mothers and grandmothers, applied most urgently to children leaving the house on winter mornings, and treated as so self-evident that questioning it seems like arguing with gravity.

The claim combines two layers of apparent evidence: the subjective experience of feeling cold and miserable when your wet hair is exposed to wind, and the statistical fact that colds cluster in winter. Both things are real. Neither supports the claim.

Rhinoviruses cause colds. Cold temperatures and wet hair do not. The association between winter and cold viruses is genuine but causally mediated through mechanisms — indoor crowding, dry air, changes in mucosal immunity — that have nothing to do with the temperature of your scalp.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

### Viruses Cause Colds, Not Cold Temperatures

The common cold is caused by a family of viruses, predominantly rhinoviruses (accounting for 30–50% of cases), but also coronaviruses (not the COVID-19 type), adenoviruses, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), and others. You cannot develop a cold without exposure to one of these viruses. The virus must enter your respiratory tract, attach to appropriate cell receptors, begin replicating, and trigger the inflammatory immune response you experience as cold symptoms.

This causal mechanism does not include wet hair, cold air on the head, or body temperature as an initiating factor. There is no biological pathway from "hair is cold" to "viral infection." Viruses are not spontaneously generated by chilling; they must be transmitted from another infected person or contaminated surface.

### The Douglas et al. 1968 Study

The most direct experimental test of the "cold causes colds" hypothesis was conducted by R.G. Douglas and colleagues at the University of Virginia and published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* in 1968. The study divided volunteers into two groups: one group was inoculated with rhinovirus and kept warm; the second was inoculated with rhinovirus and exposed to chilling (cold air, wet feet, cold-water immersion). A control group received saline rather than virus.

The results: there was no significant difference in cold rates between the warm and chilled virus-exposed groups. Being cold did not increase susceptibility to rhinovirus infection among people who had been exposed to the virus. Critically, the chilled group that had not been exposed to virus did not develop colds at all — demonstrating that cold temperature alone cannot produce illness.

This was a well-controlled study that directly tested the folk belief and found it unsupported. Its conclusions have been replicated and are consistent with our understanding of viral transmission mechanisms.

### Why Colds Are More Common in Winter

If cold temperatures don't cause colds, why do colds cluster so heavily in winter? The seasonality is real and requires explanation. The explanation is multivariate and does not involve wet hair or direct chilling, but it is more interesting.

**Indoor Crowding**

Winter drives people indoors and keeps them there. In schools, offices, homes, and public transport, close-proximity contact between people increases substantially in cold months. Rhinoviruses spread primarily through respiratory droplets and direct contact — touching a contaminated surface and then touching your nose or eyes. Dense indoor populations with sustained proximity create ideal transmission conditions. The increase in colds in September and October in temperate climates correlates with school resumption, not with the first cold air of autumn.

**Low Humidity and Mucosal Drying**

Cold outdoor air carries less moisture than warm air. Indoor heating further reduces relative humidity. Dry air dehydrates the mucosal layer lining the nasal passages — the mucous layer is a physical barrier that traps and expels viruses before they can attach to epithelial cells. When this layer is compromised by dryness, viral particles that reach the nasal passage have a better chance of reaching cell surfaces.

A 2009 study by Mäkinen et al. found that both cold temperature and low humidity independently associated with increased respiratory tract infection rates — but the mechanism for humidity effects runs through mucosal barrier function, not immune suppression.

**Rhinovirus Replication Temperature**

A more recent layer of explanation came from a 2015 study by Foxman et al. in *PNAS*, which found that rhinovirus replicates more efficiently at temperatures slightly below core body temperature (around 33°C, the typical temperature of upper airway tissues, rather than 37°C). At the higher temperatures maintained in the lower respiratory tract, innate immune responses against rhinovirus are more robust.

This finding provides a biological rationale for why rhinovirus infections establish themselves preferentially in the upper airway — and why cold, dry air that further reduces nasal passage temperatures might marginally increase viral replication efficiency. This is a subtle and indirect mechanism that is entirely different from "getting cold makes you sick." The temperature effect is on viral replication efficiency within already-infected tissue, not on the probability of infection from exposure.

**Vitamin D and Immune Seasonality**

Vitamin D, synthesized by skin exposure to UV-B radiation, plays roles in immune function. Winter reduces sun exposure, and vitamin D insufficiency is more common in winter months at temperate latitudes. Some research has linked lower vitamin D levels to increased susceptibility to respiratory infections, though the effect size and causal directionality remain subjects of ongoing study.

This is another indirect mechanism: reduced winter sun exposure → lower vitamin D → marginally reduced immune function → slightly increased susceptibility to infection. Again, no wet hair in the causal chain.

### What About Stress and Immune Function?

A 1991 study by Sheldon Cohen and colleagues — a landmark in psychoneuroimmunology — demonstrated that psychological stress does increase susceptibility to the common cold. Volunteers completed questionnaires about life stress, were exposed to rhinovirus via nasal drops, and then quarantined for observation. Participants with higher stress scores were significantly more likely to develop cold symptoms.

The mechanism involves stress hormones (particularly cortisol) affecting immune cell activity, reducing the efficacy of specific immune responses. This is a real immune modulation effect — but it operates over the timescale of chronic stress, not the timescale of a cold morning walk to school.

Getting caught without an umbrella in a rainstorm might generate transient stress, but it does not create the chronic psychological stress load that Cohen's study measured. The "stress weakens immunity" finding does not rehabilitate the "cold weather weakens immunity" claim.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### The Phenomenological Reality of Feeling Cold

The rule is phenomenologically real in a specific sense: the experience of going outside with wet hair in winter is genuinely unpleasant. The evaporative cooling effect of water on hair in cold air produces a distinctive, uncomfortable sensation of chill. The head has relatively poor insulation compared to the torso, and wet hair dramatically reduces what little insulation dry hair provides.

This subjective experience — a real, physical sensation of coldness — is vividly associated with the conditions the folk rule specifies. The sensation feels like something is happening to your body, and the body interpreting that sensation as a health event is a short inferential step.

### Temporal Correlation Creates Causal Inference

If a child goes outside with wet hair on a cold morning and develops a cold three days later, the causal attribution to the wet hair is psychologically natural. The wet hair is a salient, rule-violating event. The cold is a salient, unpleasant outcome. The interval between them is consistent with a plausible causal story and also consistent with rhinovirus incubation (1–4 days).

The problem is that the child's cold was caused by a virus they inhaled or touched before developing symptoms — possibly at school the day before, possibly from a sibling, possibly from a shared cup. The wet hair was a coincidence in the causal chain, salient enough to be remembered and attributed while the actual transmission event went unnoticed.

### The Association Between Winter and Colds Is Real

The population-level correlation between cold weather and cold viruses is genuine, and it provides a substrate that makes the folk rule feel empirically grounded. If colds are more common in winter and winter involves wet hair and cold temperatures, it is reasonable — if not quite valid — to infer that wet hair and cold temperatures cause colds.

The association is real; the causal direction is wrong. The population-level correlation is mediated by indoor crowding and mucosal humidity, not by direct immune suppression from chilling. But the correlation is close enough to the folk rule's prediction to make the rule feel true.

### Parental Authority and Transmission

The wet-hair rule is typically transmitted from parent to child as protective instruction, not as an empirical claim open to evaluation. Children who question it are told they will understand when they get sick, or that their parent knows better, or simply that this is what people do. The rule carries the authority of care rather than the authority of evidence.

This transmission mode makes the belief unusually durable. It is not held as a hypothesis about viral biology — it is held as a piece of received wisdom from a trusted authority in a context of vulnerability (childhood illness). Revising it requires not just encountering counter-evidence but also reclassifying the belief from "knowledge transmitted by parent" to "hypothesis about the world that can be wrong."

## 4. Verdict

**Eliminated:** The claim is false. Rhinoviruses cause colds; cold temperatures and wet hair do not. Controlled experimental studies, including the definitive 1968 Douglas et al. study, found no increase in cold rates among subjects exposed to chilling. The association between winter and cold viruses is real but causally mediated through indoor crowding, low humidity affecting mucosal barriers, and possibly vitamin D reduction — not through direct immune suppression from wet hair or cold air.

**EARC Classification: E — Eliminated**
Unlike Refined claims where a core truth survives correction, the wet-hair-causes-cold claim has no valid causal mechanism. There is no pathway from "hair is cold and wet" to "viral infection." The claim is an association misread as causation, transmitted as received wisdom, and durable because it feels right and because the real causal structure is less visible and less memorable.

The phenomenological experience is real — going outside with wet hair in winter is unpleasant. The seasonal association is real — colds are more common in winter. But "unpleasant" and "associated" do not produce "causes colds." The causal claim is eliminated.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### The Classic Correlation-Causation Error in Medicine

The wet-hair belief is a textbook example of confounded causal inference. Cold weather causes both wet hair (people shower more in winter? children are bathed in the morning before school?) and increased cold transmission (indoor crowding, dry air). The correlation between wet-hair events and cold events is driven by a common cause — cold season — not by a direct causal link.

This type of confounding is among the most common sources of false causal beliefs in medicine and epidemiology. Identifying it requires either experimental control (deliberately holding other variables constant while varying wet-hair status, as in the Douglas study) or statistical adjustment for potential confounders (as in epidemiological studies that control for season, humidity, and crowding). The folk rule never had access to either method and so could not self-correct.

### Historical Context: Miasma, Chilling, and Pre-Germ Theory Medicine

Before germ theory was established in the late nineteenth century, Western medicine attributed many illnesses to environmental factors including cold air, dampness, foul smells, and "miasmas." The idea that cold and wet conditions cause illness was not merely a folk belief — it was the operating medical model. Hippocrates wrote extensively on the influence of climate and season on disease; Galenic medicine built weather and temperature into its humoral framework for illness causation.

The germ theory revolution (Pasteur, Koch, 1860s–1880s) demonstrated that specific diseases are caused by specific microorganisms, not by environmental conditions per se. The discovery of viruses came later (end of the nineteenth century, early twentieth), and rhinovirus was not identified until 1956.

The wet-hair belief survived this scientific revolution not because the evidence supported it but because the belief was transmitted culturally, primarily within families, outside channels where the new medical understanding circulated. Generations of parents instructed children to cover their heads before germ theory's implications had fully diffused into household practice.

### The Eccles Hypothesis: Chilling and Viral Reactivation

Some researchers, including Ronald Eccles at Cardiff University, have proposed a modified mechanism: while chilling does not cause new viral infections, it may reactivate latent viral infections or reduce the effectiveness of the nasal mucosal barrier sufficiently to allow pre-existing subclinical infections to become symptomatic.

Eccles (2002) proposed that cooling of the nasal lining could trigger vasoconstriction, reducing mucosal blood flow, impairing the mucociliary transport that clears viral particles, and potentially allowing viruses already present in the nasal passage to establish productive infection.

This hypothesis is more sophisticated than the simple "cold = sick" model, and it is not without supporting plausibility. But it has not been experimentally confirmed in the way the chilling-does-not-cause-colds finding has been confirmed. It remains a minority position in the research literature. If it were true, the relevant mechanism would be cooling of the nasal passage interior, not the temperature of the scalp — wet hair affecting nasal passage temperature is indirect at best.

### What Actually Reduces Cold Risk

For anyone interested in what the evidence does support for cold prevention:

- **Hand hygiene:** Rhinoviruses are transmitted by contact. Frequent handwashing and avoiding touching the face reduce transmission risk substantially.
- **Avoiding close contact with infected people:** The most direct intervention in the transmission chain.
- **Indoor air humidity:** Maintaining indoor humidity above 40–50% may reduce both virus viability in the air and mucosal drying.
- **Adequate sleep:** Sleep deprivation impairs immune function; Cohen et al.'s later work showed that people who sleep less than seven hours per night are substantially more susceptible to rhinovirus infection.
- **Not smoking:** Tobacco smoke damages the mucociliary apparatus, the primary mechanical defense of the respiratory tract.

None of these interventions involves hair-drying rituals.

### The Emotional Function of Protective Rules

The wet-hair rule functions partly as an expression of parental care. Instructing a child to dry their hair before going outside is a low-cost, visible action that signals attention to the child's wellbeing. The rule's survival may have less to do with its validity than with its role in the performance of parental protection.

This is not cynical — the impulse to protect children from illness is genuine and important. But it means that debunking the rule may feel to some parents like an attack on their protective instincts rather than a correction of a factual error. Effective communication about the evidence might need to acknowledge the protective motivation while redirecting it toward interventions that actually work.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

### Near-Universal Consensus

The wet-hair-causes-cold claim is one of the most thoroughly debunked health myths in popular science. Snopes, the Mayo Clinic, WebMD, and numerous medical journalism outlets have all addressed it. The Douglas et al. 1968 study is frequently cited. The consensus among medical fact-checkers is strongly against the claim.

This is a case where the debunking infrastructure has worked well: the myth is widely known to be false among medically informed audiences. The challenge is the last-mile problem — the debunking has not penetrated household transmission of the belief.

### The Complexity of the Seasonality Question

Fact-checkers handling this claim occasionally oversimplify in the other direction: "colds are caused by viruses, weather has nothing to do with it." This is not quite right. Weather has indirect effects on cold transmission through the mechanisms described above — humidity, indoor crowding, possibly vitamin D. A rigorous fact-check should:

1. Clearly eliminate the direct causal claim (wet hair/cold temperatures do not directly cause colds).
2. Acknowledge that winter conditions are associated with more colds — and explain why.
3. Note the indirect mechanisms without allowing them to restore credibility to the direct-cause claim.

The distinction between "weather has nothing to do with colds" and "weather affects colds indirectly through mechanisms unrelated to wet hair" is important for accuracy.

### The Foxman 2015 PNAS Finding

The 2015 Foxman study on rhinovirus replication temperature attracted some popular coverage with headlines suggesting that cold air does cause colds. This required careful fact-checker response. The finding — that rhinovirus replicates more efficiently at cooler temperatures within airway tissue — is interesting but does not support the folk rule.

The distinction: the folk rule claims that getting cold suppresses your immune system and makes you susceptible to viral infection. The Foxman finding concerns replication efficiency within already-infected tissue at different temperatures. These are different stages of the infection process. The Foxman result does not mean cold air on your head initiates viral infection; it means that if rhinovirus is already replicating in your upper airway, it may do so somewhat more efficiently at the cooler temperatures that prevail there compared to lower airway temperatures. This is a subtle intra-host mechanism, not a population-level transmission factor, and it is entirely consistent with the Douglas finding that chilling does not increase cold rates in virus-exposed individuals.

### Recommended Framing

For a general-audience correction:

- Lead with the virus point: colds require viruses; cold temperatures don't produce viruses or make you vulnerable to them in any direct sense.
- Acknowledge that winter colds are genuinely more common, and explain why (indoor crowding, humidity, not wet hair).
- Note the Douglas 1968 study as the direct experimental test.
- Be honest about the indirect-effect literature (humidity, Foxman) without allowing it to rehabilitate the wet-hair claim.
- Offer the "what actually works" summary: hand hygiene, sleep, avoiding sick contacts.

The tone matters: this myth is transmitted as parental care. A dismissive debunking may harden resistance. A tone that respects the protective motivation while redirecting it toward evidence-based interventions is more likely to change behavior.
