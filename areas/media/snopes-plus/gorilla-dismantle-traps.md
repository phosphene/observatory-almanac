## The Claim

In late June 2021, a photograph on Reddit's r/NatureIsFuckingLit subreddit depicting what appeared to be young gorillas actively dismantling a poacher's snare trap received enormous attention, generating tens of thousands of upvotes and sparking widespread discussion about the intelligence, agency, and awareness of mountain gorillas. The post's caption described gorillas engaged in deliberate trap-destruction, and the image showed what appeared to be juvenile gorillas manipulating the components of a traditional hunter's snare — the bent-branch mechanism, the looped noose wire, the anchor stakes.

The immediate question, repeated in comments and subsequent social media shares, was whether this was staged, manipulated, or misdescribed. Could these animals really have done what the image suggested? Did the event actually take place? Snopes journalist Bethania Palma, writing on June 25, 2021, traced the photograph's origins and confirmed it was genuine, authenticated by the Dian Fossey Gorilla Fund and originally published in 2012 by National Geographic. The rating is unambiguously "True."

## What's Actually True

The photograph is real and was not staged for a dramatic effect. It was taken at the Karisoke Research Center in the Volcanoes National Park of Rwanda, one of the primary field sites for mountain gorilla (*Gorilla beringei beringei*) research and conservation, founded by primatologist Dian Fossey in 1967. The image was credited to the Dian Fossey Gorilla Fund and published by National Geographic in July 2012 following the incident.

The specific event, as documented by Karisoke Research Center program coordinator Veronica Vecellio and reported in National Geographic (2012), unfolded as follows: tracker John Ndayambaje spotted a snare close to the Kuryama gorilla clan. As he moved to deactivate it, a silverback named Vubu grunted a caution — instructing the human tracker to stay back. At that point, two juvenile gorillas, a male named Rwema and a female named Dukore, both approximately four years old, ran toward the trap. Rwema jumped on the bent tree branch used as a spring-pole and broke it, while Dukore freed the noose. The pair then identified a second snare nearby — one the tracker had not yet seen — and moved to destroy it as well, this time joined by a third juvenile, a teenager named Tetero.

This sequence of events is remarkable for several reasons that the fact-checking verdict correctly identifies as true but does not fully explore. The gorillas acted without human prompting. They identified the trap components, understood their functional relationship, and applied the correct physical intervention — not random manipulation but targeted breakage of the trigger mechanism and removal of the capture loop. They then *proactively sought out* additional traps beyond the one that had initially caught their attention.

The incident occurred one week after an infant gorilla had been caught in a similar snare and died from its injuries before conservationists could intervene — a loss that would have been visible to members of the Kuryama group, though the extent to which this specific context motivated the juvenile gorillas' behaviour cannot be established from the available evidence.

## Why People Believe This

The viral success of this photograph — first as a 2012 news story, then recycled in 2021 — taps into a cluster of beliefs and desires that sit at the intersection of conservation sentiment, animal cognition research, and popular animism.

The first and most direct is the desire for evidence of animal moral agency. Mountain gorillas are enormously charismatic animals, located near the apex of popular cultural representations of non-human intelligence. Footage and photographs of gorillas behaving in ways that appear to involve planning, emotion, and social coordination have a consistent track record of viral success, going back to early internet-era footage of gorilla play and maternal behaviour. A photograph of gorillas *fighting back against a human threat* — actively dismantling the tools of their own persecution — satisfies a narrative of resistance and self-determination that humans readily map onto charismatic animals.

The second is the reinforcement loop between conservation culture and compelling evidence. People who care about mountain gorilla conservation, and there are millions who do, find this photograph validating in an emotionally immediate way. It demonstrates that the gorillas are not passive victims of poaching — they are active agents with the cognitive sophistication to recognise, classify, and neutralise threats. This framing supports conservation fundraising narratives and advocacy positions, and it has circulated accordingly within conservation-adjacent communities.

The photograph was shared in June 2021 partly because Reddit's r/NatureIsFuckingLit community provides an amplification platform for exactly this kind of emotionally satisfying true-but-extraordinary nature content. The community's premise — that nature does things that produce a specific flavour of astonished approval — generates consistent circulation of genuine biological wonders and spectacular behaviour documentation.

## The Epistemic Gap

Snopes's verdict is accurate, but the entry's reach is limited by its journalistic scope. The confirmation that the photograph is real and the description accurate is appropriate and important. The epistemic gap lies in what the incident *means* for ongoing debates in primate cognition research — a layer of interpretation the fact-check format necessarily excludes.

The gorillas' behaviour in the 2012 Karisoke incident sits within a broader scientific discussion about the cognitive prerequisites for tool-related problem-solving in great apes. To dismantle a snare you must, at minimum: identify the object as a trap rather than an inert structure; understand its causal mechanism sufficiently to target the critical components; and apply the correct intervention rather than random manipulation. These requirements map onto the cognitive architecture of causal reasoning, which has been a major focus of comparative cognition research in the past two decades.

Visalberghi and Tomasello (1998, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*) established an early framework for distinguishing causal reasoning from associative learning in tool use contexts; their concern was that animals could learn effective interventions through trial-and-error without understanding the causal structure of the problem. The gorilla snare-dismantling incident was interpreted by Vecellio and colleagues (via National Geographic, 2012) as evidence of genuine causal understanding — an interpretation supported by the proactive search for additional snares, which suggests the gorillas had formed a generalised category ("snare") and were actively scanning the environment for additional instances of that category rather than simply responding to the immediate stimulus.

Byrne and Whiten (1988, *Machiavellian Intelligence*, Clarendon Press) proposed the influential hypothesis that primate intelligence evolved primarily in the context of complex social environments — that the cognitive demands of managing social relationships, tracking alliances, and deceiving competitors drove the expansion of great ape cognitive capacity. The snare-dismantling incident offers data that cuts slightly orthogonally to this framework: here is a case where gorilla intelligence appears to be deployed against an environmental threat rather than a social one. Whether this represents flexible generalisation of socially-evolved cognition or whether it taps into independent environmental problem-solving modules remains an open question.

The gap_category is "animal-cognition-depth" because the Snopes verification establishes truth but leaves entirely untouched the question of what this behaviour tells us about gorilla cognition — which is the intellectually generative question the incident actually raises.

## Broader Scientific Context

Mountain gorillas (*Gorilla beringei beringei*) occupy one of the most precarious conservation positions of any great ape subspecies. Confined to a few hundred square kilometres across the Virunga Massif (spanning Rwanda, Uganda, and the Democratic Republic of Congo) and Bwindi Impenetrable Forest in Uganda, the total population is estimated at approximately 1,063 individuals as of the most recent census (Hickey et al., 2019, *PLOS ONE*). This makes mountain gorillas the only great ape subspecies whose wild population has increased in recent decades — a conservation success story attributable in large part to intensive monitoring, anti-poaching efforts, and community-based conservation initiatives in the Virunga range.

Poaching snares represent one of the primary threats to mountain gorillas, particularly to juveniles who lack the strength to free themselves. The snares are typically not set specifically for gorillas — they target small-to-medium mammals for bushmeat — but gorillas encounter them incidentally, and the consequences for entrapped individuals are often fatal. The Karisoke Research Center's anti-snare work, conducted in partnership with the Rwanda Development Board and local community monitors, has destroyed hundreds of snares per year across the Virunga Massif. The gorillas' spontaneous contribution to this effort in 2012 was a single documented incident, but it became one of the most widely circulated pieces of evidence for great ape cognitive sophistication in conservation communication.

The Dian Fossey Gorilla Fund, which operates the Karisoke Research Center, has continued long-term research on mountain gorilla behaviour, social structure, and cognition since Fossey's founding of the centre in 1967 and her death in 1985. The centre's documentation of the snare-dismantling incident follows in a tradition of observational research that prioritises longitudinal, individual-level knowledge of specific animals — a methodological approach that produces exactly the kind of named-individual, behavioural-sequence documentation that makes the 2012 account so verifiable and narrative-rich.

## Verdict and Reflection

The Snopes verdict of "True" is correct and fully warranted. The photograph is authentic, the description of the behaviour is accurate, and the publication in National Geographic in 2012 with full attribution establishes a clear provenance chain that eliminates any doubt about whether the image was staged or misrepresented.

The broader significance of the incident — the cognition questions it raises, the conservation narrative it feeds, the methodological tradition of long-form primate observation that makes such documentation possible — is necessarily beyond Snopes's scope. But for Truth Vault purposes, these contextual threads are precisely where the most durable intellectual value of the story lies.

What the image shows is not just a cute or inspirational moment. It is evidence — carefully documented, corroborated by multiple witnesses, and subsequently verified through institutional channels — of great ape behaviour that requires at minimum some form of causal understanding of human-engineered trap mechanisms. That evidence is arresting in 2012 and remains arresting on its 2021 revival. The virality is not a mystery. The story earns its repeated circulation.

## Conservation Significance and Long-Term Primate Research

The Karisoke Research Center's documentation of the snare-dismantling incident is not isolated from a broader tradition of long-form, intensive field observation. Dian Fossey's foundational work on mountain gorilla social behaviour, published cumulatively across the 1970s and 1980s, established the methodological precedent: individual gorillas should be identified by name, tracked across their life histories, and understood as individuals with personal histories rather than as interchangeable specimens. This methodology — sometimes called "individual-focussed longitudinal observational research" in the primatology literature — produces datasets and accounts of a qualitatively different kind from either laboratory cognition experiments or short-term cross-sectional field surveys.

Fossey's work, and that of her successors at Karisoke, established that the social dynamics of mountain gorilla groups are complex, durable, and individually specific. The silverback Vubu's caution grunt to tracker Ndayambaje — telling the human to stay back — is interpretable in this context not as a random vocalisation but as a socially situated communication: Vubu knew Ndayambaje, had interacted with trackers from the research centre many times, and applied a specific caution signal to prevent interference. Whether this represents a generalised inhibitory signal or a specifically calibrated communication to a known individual is ambiguous from the available account, but in either case it is behaviour that makes sense only against the background of long-term human-gorilla familiarity established by decades of habituation work.

The habituation of mountain gorillas to human presence — a prerequisite both for research and for the gorilla tourism that provides significant revenue for Rwanda's conservation programme — is itself a complex process with ethical dimensions. Groups of mountain gorillas are gradually acclimatised to the presence of specific human trackers and researchers over months or years, until they allow close approach and observation without fleeing or displaying aggressively. The research benefit of this habituation is enormous: it is what makes the detailed observational record that validates the 2012 snare-dismantling account possible. The conservation revenue benefit is similarly substantial. The ethical cost — habituating wild animals to human presence in ways that may reduce their wariness of humans generally — is a standing concern in conservation management.

The three named juvenile gorillas — Rwema, Dukore, and Tetero — were members of the Kuryama group at the time of the 2012 incident. Their individual identities, ages, and social positions were known to the Karisoke trackers because of this long-term monitoring programme. The specificity of that knowledge — these particular individuals, of these particular ages, in this particular social group — is what allows the published account to be so precise and verifiable, and it is a direct product of the methodological tradition that Fossey established.
