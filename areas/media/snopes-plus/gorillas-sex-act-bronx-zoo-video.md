# Were Gorillas at Bronx Zoo Filmed Engaging in a Sex Act?


## 1. The Claim

In September 2021, visitors to the Bronx Zoo's Congo Gorilla Forest exhibit filmed video footage showing two western lowland gorillas (*Gorilla gorilla gorilla*) in what appeared to be a sexual interaction. The footage was first reported and amplified by the celebrity tabloid TMZ, which characterized it as an "oral sex peep show," and the story spread rapidly through viral media channels. Social media responses ranged from shocked to amused to philosophically curious, with significant public commentary about whether this constituted genuine sexual behavior, whether it was appropriate to film and share, and what it meant about primate sexuality.

The claim circulated in several versions. The most basic version — that a video at the Bronx Zoo showed gorillas seeming to engage in sexual behavior — was factually verifiable and became the subject of the Snopes fact-check. A secondary layer of claims involved the characterization of the behavior: was it truly sexual? Was it for pleasure? Was it for dominance? Were both participants willing participants? A third layer involved the implied human framing: did the footage suggest that gorillas have a sex life similar to humans, or more complex or simpler?

Snopes fact-checked the claim in September 2021 (Dapcevich, 2021) and assigned a verdict of True, supported by an email response from Vikki McCloskey, a curator and biologist at the California Academy of Sciences, who explained the behavior as consistent with documented submissive signaling in primate social hierarchies. The article contextualizes the behavior within the ethological framework of dominance hierarchy maintenance in gorilla troops and notes the broader phenomenon of non-reproductive sexual behavior across animal species.

The epistemological interest of this case lies well beyond the simple factual verification. The social scandal that the video created — the "shock" of the zoo visitors, the TMZ framing as voyeuristic spectacle, the widespread online amusement — reflects a profound and specific gap between scientific knowledge of primate sexual behavior and public understanding of it. This gap is itself scientifically and socially significant.


## 2. What's Actually True

The factual claims in the Snopes fact-check are well-supported. The Bronx Zoo's Congo Gorilla Forest exhibit is documented as housing a troop of western lowland gorillas; in 2021 the troop comprised 20 individuals (Bronx Zoo, 2021, "Congo Gorilla Forest," bronxzoo.com). Western lowland gorillas (*Gorilla gorilla gorilla*) are the smallest of the four recognized gorilla subspecies; they are the most common gorilla subspecies in captivity and the most studied of the four (Groves, 2001, *Primate Taxonomy*, Smithsonian Institution Press, Washington, DC).

The social structure of gorilla troops is characterized by male dominance hierarchies in which a single adult silverback male holds primary social authority. "Silverback" refers to the distinctive silver-gray saddle of hair that develops across the back of adult males beginning at about 12 years of age and that is a visual signal of maturity and dominance rank (Watts, 1994, "Social Relationships of Immigrant and Resident Female Mountain Gorillas," *Animal Behaviour*, 48(4), 935–946). The silverback's social role includes protection of the troop, conflict resolution, access to mating opportunities, and maintenance of group cohesion through a combination of display behaviors, physical dominance, and social bonding.

Vikki McCloskey's expert characterization in the Snopes email — that "sometimes in primate groups (and other groups!) the beta males will perform a submissive behavior to the alpha (in this case the silverback) to keep the group dynamics in sync" — is consistent with the ethological literature on gorilla dominance interactions. The specific behavior described and visible in the video (a subordinate male performing oro-genital contact on the dominant silverback) is a documented form of submissive behavior in primates that functions to signal social subordination and reduce conflict potential (Vasey, 1995, "Homosexual Behaviour in Primates: A Review of Evidence and Theory," *International Journal of Primatology*, 16(2), 173–204).

The Snopes article also notes the broader context of non-reproductive sexual behavior in animal species. The article's characterization is accurate: non-reproductive sexual behavior — sexual acts engaged in outside of fertile periods or between partners who cannot conceive — is documented across numerous animal species and is most extensively studied in primates. The article specifically mentions bonobos and dolphins; both references are legitimate. Bonobos (*Pan paniscus*) are famous in primatology for using sexual contact as a social bonding and conflict resolution mechanism across sex and age categories (de Waal, 1987, "Tension Regulation and Nonreproductive Functions of Sex in Captive Bonobos (*Pan paniscus*)," *National Geographic Research*, 3(3), 318–335). Dolphin sexual behavior including hetero- and homosexual interactions outside of reproductive contexts is extensively documented (Wells, 2003, "Dolphin Social Complexity: Lessons from Long-Term Study and Life History," in *Animal Social Complexity*, Harvard University Press, pp. 32–56).

The specific claim about the structure of the video — that the silverback has his back turned and the beta male is the one initiating the submissive sexual behavior — is presented in the Snopes article as McCloskey's interpretation of the footage, and it is consistent with the social dynamics of gorilla dominance interactions as described in the ethological literature. Whether the animal identified as the silverback is specifically the dominant male in the Congo Gorilla Forest troop is not independently verified; the Snopes fact-check notes that it contacted the Bronx Zoo for identification of the specific animals but did not receive a response in time for publication. An earlier version of the article incorrectly identified the silverback as "Ernie," a troop member who had died in 2019, which was corrected in the September 27, 2021 update.

The Snopes verdict of True is well-calibrated. The footage exists, the behavior depicted is real, and expert characterization of the behavior as sexual in nature and consistent with documented primate dominance signaling is provided by a credentialed biologist.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes article provides accurate basic context but stops well short of engaging with the research landscape on primate non-reproductive sexual behavior, which is extensive and reveals something considerably more complex than the dominance-signaling framing alone suggests. Three areas deserve attention.

**The spectrum of primate non-reproductive sexual behavior**: The Snopes article frames the gorilla behavior primarily as dominance signaling — a "submissive behavior to keep the group dynamics in sync." This is accurate as far as it goes, but the literature on primate non-reproductive sexual behavior identifies at least four distinct functional categories that can overlap in any given instance: dominance signaling, social bonding, conflict resolution, and pleasure-seeking. Attributing the behavior solely to dominance maintenance implies a more purely strategic, less motivationally complex account than the evidence supports.

Vasey (1995, cited above) provides the foundational systematic review of same-sex sexual behavior in primates and argues that the evidence does not support a purely functional-dominance interpretation for all instances. Many documented primate same-sex sexual interactions occur between individuals of similar rank rather than across a dominance differential, in contexts with no apparent conflict resolution function. The motivational and hedonic dimensions of these behaviors — whether they are experienced as pleasurable or aversive by the participants — are methodologically difficult to study but are not simply absent from the question.

Wallen and Lloyd (2011, "Female Sexual Arousal: Genital Anatomy and Orgasm in Intercourse," *Hormones and Behavior*, 59(5), 780–792) argue for taking hedonic states seriously in the study of non-reproductive primate sexual behavior, noting that the presumption that non-human animals engage in sexual behavior purely for functional reasons rather than for pleasure represents a cognitive bias rather than a scientifically supported position. The discovery of the anatomical basis of clitoral innervation in dolphins and subsequent confirmation of structurally homologous female arousal anatomy across mammals (Orbach et al., 2021, "Functional Morphology of the Dolphin Clitoris," *Current Biology*, 32(1), 126–133.e3) strengthens the case for taking hedonic motivation seriously as a factor in non-reproductive sexual behavior across mammalian species including primates.

For gorillas specifically, the literature on captive versus wild social behavior is important context. Captive groups, confined in defined enclosures with no opportunity for dispersal and with structured social hierarchies that cannot be renegotiated through emigration, show higher rates of tension-reducing behaviors including sexual ones than wild groups. The Congo Gorilla Forest group's behavior cannot be simply generalized to wild gorilla populations without adjustment for the captive context (Stoinski et al., 2009, "Factors Affecting Group Cohesion in Zoo-Housed Western Lowland Gorillas," *American Journal of Primatology*, 71(4), 316–326).

**The public ignorance gap and its consequences**: The viral spread and "scandal" framing of the video reflects a specific and documentable gap between scientific knowledge and public understanding that has real consequences for primate conservation and welfare. The shock response from zoo visitors and the TMZ framing as voyeuristic titillation both depend on the implicit assumption that sexual behavior of the type filmed is anomalous, inappropriate, or human-specific behavior that "shouldn't" occur in gorillas. Neither of these assumptions is supported by the ethological literature, but they are extremely prevalent in public discourse.

This ignorance gap matters for conservation because public attitudes toward great apes significantly affect conservation funding, zoo accreditation support, and anti-poaching investment. Framing gorilla sexual behavior as scandalous or shocking may inadvertently deepen the psychological distance between human audiences and great apes rather than fostering the identification and kinship that is most effective at generating conservation support. Research on moral circle expansion and species empathy (Crimston et al., 2016, "Moral Expansiveness: Examining Variability in the Extension of the Moral World," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 111(4), 636–653) suggests that accurate understanding of primate social complexity, including sexual behavior, is likely more conducive to conservation attitude formation than framing that alternates between anthropomorphizing and sensationalizing.

The specific public communication challenge here is compounded by the social taboo structure around sexual content, which makes it difficult for science communicators to accurately present primate sexual behavior in educational contexts. Zoo educators, wildlife documentarians, and science journalists routinely omit or sanitize primate sexual behavior even when it is directly relevant to understanding social dynamics. This omission creates the expectation vacuum that makes videos like the Bronx Zoo footage seem scandalous rather than scientifically unremarkable.

**Western lowland gorilla conservation status and captive population management**: The Snopes article correctly notes that western lowland gorillas are critically endangered. The IUCN Red List classification of *Gorilla gorilla gorilla* as Critically Endangered reflects population declines of more than 60% over three generations, driven by hunting, habitat loss, and (in the wild) disease outbreaks from the Ebola virus (Maisels et al., 2016, "Gorilla gorilla ssp. gorilla (Western Lowland Gorilla)," *IUCN Red List of Threatened Species*, e.T9406A17963949). Wild population estimates are uncertain but current estimates range from approximately 100,000 to 200,000 individuals across their range in central and west Africa.

The captive population managed in accredited zoos plays an important role in conservation genetics through coordinated breeding programs (Species Survival Plans in North America; European Endangered Species Programme in Europe). The Congo Gorilla Forest at the Bronx Zoo, housing 20 individuals in a 6.5-acre naturalistic exhibit, represents one of the largest captive groups in North America. The social dynamics of captive groups — including the dominance behaviors filmed in the viral video — are directly relevant to breeding management, because the social structure of the group affects mate access, stress levels, and ultimately reproductive success of managed populations (Stoinski et al., 2009, cited above).

This conservation context gives the "scandal" video a dimension that the public framing entirely misses: the behavior filmed is not merely an amusing spectacle but a window into the social mechanics of a critically endangered species, and understanding it accurately is relevant to the conservation management of the captive population that constitutes an insurance policy for the wild population.


## 4. Verdict and Calibration

The Snopes verdict of True is correct and appropriate. The video is genuine, the behavior depicted is real, and the expert characterization as consistent with documented submissive primate behavior is accurate. The verdict is not in question.

The more important calibration concerns the completeness of the contextual framing. The Snopes article correctly corrects the "scandal" framing by situating the behavior within primate ethology, but it arguably over-simplifies the behavior by framing it primarily as dominance signaling. The full ethological picture is more complex: non-reproductive sexual behavior in gorillas, as in other primates, reflects a multidimensional motivational landscape that includes dominance, social bonding, conflict resolution, and potentially hedonic motivation, and the relative weight of these factors in any given interaction cannot be confidently determined from video observation alone.

Calibrated confidence on the factual sub-claims:

- A video filmed at the Bronx Zoo showed two gorillas in sexual behavior: **very high confidence** (video is documented and sourced)
- The behavior is consistent with documented submissive signaling in gorilla social hierarchies: **high confidence** (supported by expert commentary and ethological literature)
- Non-reproductive sexual behavior is widely documented across primate species: **very high confidence** (extensive literature, decades of research)
- Bonobos engage in sexual acts for social bonding: **very high confidence** (foundational de Waal research, extensively replicated)
- Western lowland gorillas are critically endangered: **very high confidence** (IUCN Red List classification)
- The specific animals were correctly identified in the original article: **low confidence** (original identification as "Ernie" was incorrect and corrected; final identification not definitively confirmed)


## 5. Research Gaps and Open Questions

**Individual animal identification**: The Snopes article notes that the Bronx Zoo did not respond to requests for identification of the specific animals involved. Proper identification would enable the behavior to be situated within the specific social context of the Congo Gorilla Forest troop — whether the sex, age, rank, and social history of the specific individuals is consistent with the submissive-signaling interpretation or whether other dynamics might be more relevant.

**Captive versus wild behavior rates**: The rate of non-reproductive sexual behavior in captive gorilla populations compared to wild populations is not systematically studied across comparable methodology. Available data suggest captive rates are higher, consistent with the hypothesis that captive social structure produces elevated tension-reducing behavior, but controlled comparative data are limited.

**Audience psychology at great ape exhibits**: The response of zoo visitors to sexual behavior in great ape exhibits has not been studied systematically. Some zoo design research addresses how visitors respond to naturalistic ape exhibits generally, but the specific question of how witnessed sexual behavior affects visitor attitudes toward apes and toward conservation is not resolved.

**Science communication ethics around animal sexual behavior**: There is no established framework for how science communicators, zoo educators, and documentary producers should handle footage of animal sexual behavior in the context of conservation education. The choice to include such footage, exclude it, or contextualize it shapes public understanding of animal social complexity in ways that have conservation implications.
