## §1 Claim Overview

The claim under review holds that the United States federal government—specifically, the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA)—was in 2021 paying American farmers to destroy their already-harvested or growing crops, with the stated or implied intent of artificially creating a domestic food shortage. This claim was disseminated primarily through TikTok videos in the summer of 2021, with multiple creators presenting themselves as farmers or agricultural workers who had received government communications instructing them to destroy crops, abandon harvests, or otherwise reduce food production in ways that would damage the national food supply.

Snopes fact-checker Dan Evon assessed this claim in August 2021 and rated it **False**. Evon's investigation found that the videos driving the claim were either jokes that their creators later acknowledged as such, based on documents that predated the claim period by several years, or based on a fundamental mischaracterization of existing but legitimate agricultural programs whose goals are unrelated to creating food shortages.

This is a particularly significant claim because it belongs to a well-established genre of food-supply conspiracy theories—claims that government or corporate actors are deliberately destroying food to engineer scarcity, drive up prices, or reduce population—that have historically appeared in times of genuine economic disruption and public anxiety about supply chains. The COVID-19 pandemic period created exactly the conditions most fertile for such claims, as real disruptions to agricultural supply chains occurred throughout 2020 and into 2021.

The claim requires careful handling because it has a genuine factual kernel—real crop destruction did occur in 2020—but converts that real event into a false causal story that assigns malicious government intent to what were economically driven market failures during an unprecedented public health emergency.

## §2 Evidence Review

**USDA spokesperson denial.** The most direct evidence against the claim is a formal statement from a USDA spokesperson, obtained by Snopes, stating explicitly that "these rumors are not true." This represents primary official denial from the federal agency named in the claim. While government agency denials are not infallible—agencies do sometimes deny true information—the categorical character of the denial, combined with the absence of documentary evidence supporting the claim, carries significant evidentiary weight.

**Analysis of the TikTok videos.** Evon examined the specific TikTok videos circulating the claim and found material problems with each:

- One video, presented as a farmer receiving a government letter instructing crop destruction, showed a document dated 2018—three years before the claimed events—and related to land management training rather than crop destruction orders. The creator subsequently clarified in comments that the video was a joke, not documentation of genuine government directives.
- Videos claiming that the government instructed the destruction of cooking oil to manipulate gas prices—presented as parallel evidence of government food supply interference—were similarly identified as jokes. The creator, Ryan Peter (@bushelsandbarrels), posted follow-up videos explicitly stating that his oil-dumping video was satirical, not documentary.

These findings establish that the viral evidence chain for the claim consists primarily of satirical or misrepresented content that was stripped of its satirical framing as it spread across platforms—a well-documented mechanism in viral misinformation propagation (Pennycook & Rand, 2019, *Psychological Science*).

**The real kernel: COVID agricultural disruption.** While the claim of government-directed crop destruction is false, the underlying anxiety is rooted in a real phenomenon. The *New York Times* reported in April 2020 that farm operators across the United States were destroying significant volumes of produce, milk, and other perishables due to the sudden collapse of institutional food markets at the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic. In Wisconsin and Ohio, farmers dumped thousands of gallons of milk; in Idaho, a farmer buried one million pounds of onions; in South Florida, tractors plowed perfectly ripe vegetables back into the soil. These events were economically rational responses to a catastrophic market disruption—restaurants, schools, and hotels represented roughly half of all food service demand, and their sudden closure left many growers with no buyers for inventory that had been planted weeks or months before pandemic conditions emerged.

The critical distinction is between farmers destroying food because they had no buyers under pandemic market conditions (true, documented, and economically explicable) and the government paying farmers to destroy crops to create a food shortage (false, undocumented, and without a coherent causal mechanism).

**The Conservation Reserve Program.** The Biden administration's promotion of expanded enrollment in the Conservation Reserve Program (CRP) appears to have contributed additional material for conspiracy theorization. The CRP, signed into law by President Ronald Reagan in 1985 and administered by the USDA Farm Service Agency, pays farmers to voluntarily remove environmentally sensitive land from agricultural production for a period of years, in exchange for annual rental payments. The program's goals are stated and documented: restore land cover, improve water quality, prevent soil erosion, and reduce wildlife habitat loss.

Paying farmers not to farm on marginal land is philosophically distinct from paying farmers to destroy standing crops—the former is a conservation program enrolling land that may not have been productively farmed, while the latter would represent active destruction of food that would otherwise enter the food supply. The CRP's existence does not support the crop-destruction conspiracy claim; it supports only the far weaker claim that government programs sometimes pay farmers to take certain actions with their land, which is uncontroversial and has been publicly known since 1985.

**USDA supply chain statement.** Responding to the broader wave of food shortage conspiracy theories circulating in the summer of 2021, the USDA issued a public statement that "food production and manufacturing are widely dispersed throughout the U.S., and there are currently no wide-spread disruptions reported in the supply chain." This statement directly addresses the food shortage narrative that the crop-destruction conspiracy supported.

## §3 Epistemic Analysis

**Epistemic novelty.** This claim provides a textbook example of what misinformation scholars call the "kernel of truth" exploitation pattern: a real, documented event (COVID agricultural disruption in 2020) is selectively extracted from its causal context (market failure due to pandemic) and recontextualized within a false causal framework (government malice aimed at food shortage creation). The transformation does not fabricate the underlying event; it reassigns its cause.

This pattern is epistemically dangerous precisely because it is partially accurate. A person who knows that food was genuinely destroyed in 2020 and encounters the government-malice narrative faces a lower-than-normal cognitive resistance to the false claim, because the confirmatory kernel ("yes, food was destroyed") partially overrides the need to critically evaluate the causal mechanism ("but why?"). Research on motivated reasoning in political and conspiratorial cognition has found that partial factual confirmation significantly increases acceptance of false embedded claims, even when the causal mechanism is separately implausible (Ecker et al., 2014, *Cognition*).

**Platform dynamics and satirical stripping.** The specific spreading mechanism here—satirical TikTok videos repurposed as documentary evidence—illustrates a well-documented degradation process in viral information propagation. Short-form video platforms create information units that are highly portable but contextually fragile: a video that is interpretable as satire within its original account's context may be stripped of satirical signals when re-shared as an isolated clip. This "satirical stripping" phenomenon has been identified as a distinctive hazard of TikTok's sequential feed structure, which provides viewers no context from an account's profile or comment section unless they actively seek it. The crop-destruction claim benefited specifically from this mechanism, with farmers' satirical self-presentations being repurposed as documentary proof by conspiratorial recontextualists (Ling et al., 2021, *Misinformation Review*).

**The structural vulnerability of food-supply claims.** Conspiracy theories about deliberate food supply manipulation occupy a psychologically potent territory because food security is both fundamental to survival and opaque to most urban and suburban consumers, who have no direct visibility into agricultural markets, supply chain mechanics, or commodity price formation. This combination—high stakes and low legibility—creates fertile conditions for conspiratorial theorization. When people cannot easily verify or falsify claims about a complex system that they depend on but do not understand, their epistemic vulnerability increases. Agricultural supply chain conspiracy theories have accordingly appeared wherever food anxiety and information opacity coincide, from historical European famine narratives attributing grain shortages to Jewish merchants to contemporary claims about deliberate food supply destruction (Fine & Turner, 2001, *Whispers on the Color Line*, University of California Press).

**The gap: what remains unaddressed.** The Snopes fact-check definitively establishes that the specific claim (government directing farmers to destroy crops to create food shortage) is false. What it does not address—and what represents a genuine epistemic gap in the public discussion—is the adequacy and fairness of the USDA's emergency response to the 2020 agricultural disruptions. Farmers who destroyed food because there were no buyers were not adequately compensated through existing agricultural safety net mechanisms; the COVID disruption exposed structural weaknesses in the intersection of the agricultural commodity insurance system and the food service demand collapse. This policy failure, while distinct from the conspiracy claim, represents a real grievance that the conspiracy narrative may be parasitically exploiting. Addressing the legitimate underlying grievance would likely be more effective than debunking alone in reducing susceptibility to the false version of the claim (Amazeen & Bucy, 2019, *Journalism Practice*).

## §4 Contextual Background

**Agricultural supply chain fragility and the pandemic.** The COVID-19 pandemic's impact on the American agricultural system in 2020 was one of the most significant acute disruptions to domestic food supply dynamics in the modern era. The speed of the disruption—within weeks, institutional food service demand collapsed from roughly 50% of all food consumed in America to near-zero in many categories—caught supply chains designed for stable demand patterns completely unprepared. Long-lead production cycles in agriculture (crops planted months before harvest; dairy herds that produce continuously regardless of market demand) meant that supply could not be quickly reduced to match the collapsed demand, resulting in the food waste events the *New York Times* documented.

This context is critical for understanding why the government-crop-destruction conspiracy theory found a receptive audience: the visible destruction of food was real, was widespread, and was genuinely distressing. The conspiracy theory answered the emotional need for a responsible party—for agency and malice where the actual explanation involved impersonal market mechanics and public health emergency conditions (van Prooijen & Van Lange, 2014, *Psychological Review*).

**Conservation Reserve Program history and politics.** The CRP, created under the 1985 Farm Bill, represents one of the federal government's largest land conservation programs. Enrollment varies by year but has historically covered tens of millions of acres. The program is explicitly voluntary—farmers choose whether to enroll eligible land—and the annual rental payments are calculated based on local agricultural land values. The program's conservation goals are its primary rationale, but critics from both right and left have periodically questioned whether paying farmers not to farm represents a subsidy to agricultural landowners rather than a genuine conservation intervention.

The Biden administration's emphasis on CRP expansion in 2021 was part of a broader climate and conservation agenda that included increased environmental set-asides as climate mitigation strategies. This political context—an administration expressing interest in reducing agricultural land use—created a vulnerability to misrepresentation by people already primed to view government environmental programs as pretexts for anti-farmer agendas. The sequence from (real) CRP enrollment promotion to (false) government-ordering-crop-destruction is not logically valid, but it is psychologically plausible for audiences predisposed to view government action with suspicion (Sunstein & Vermeule, 2009, *Journal of Political Philosophy*).

**The satire-to-misinformation pipeline on TikTok.** The specific mechanism identified in this fact-check—satirical farm content re-shared as documentary proof—reflects TikTok's structural features as an information platform. Unlike text-based platforms where original context often accompanies shared content, TikTok's "duet" and "stitch" features allow users to embed another user's video while adding new framing, but simple re-posts strip the original context entirely. A satirical video about receiving government letters that functions clearly as obvious parody within an account known for agricultural humor becomes potentially ambiguous when algorithmically recommended to users with no knowledge of the creator's established comedic identity. This contextual fragility is a structural feature of the platform, not a failure of any specific user's judgment (Zannettou et al., 2018, *IMC Proceedings*).

**Broader food conspiracy landscape.** The crop-destruction conspiracy participated in a broader wave of food-related conspiratorial content that circulated throughout the pandemic period, including claims about deliberate contamination of food supplies, false reports of factory farm fires set by government agents, and allegations of Bill Gates purchasing farmland to eliminate family farming. Understanding the crop-destruction claim in isolation understates the scope of the information environment: it was one element of a large and mutually reinforcing conspiratorial ecosystem that portrayed the American food supply as under active attack from powerful actors. Evaluating any single claim requires understanding the ecosystem in which it circulates (Aupers, 2012, *European Journal of Communication*).

## §5 Verdict Assessment

The Snopes verdict of **False** is fully supported by the evidentiary record. The US government was not paying farmers to destroy crops in order to create a food shortage. The claim lacks documentary support, is directly contradicted by a USDA spokesperson statement, and is rooted in satirical or anachronistic TikTok videos whose creators subsequently clarified their satirical intent.

This entry is assigned **EARC: R** (Refuted) on the basis of: (1) direct official denial by the referenced agency, (2) documented satirical origin of key viral evidence, (3) the absence of any corroborating documentary source, and (4) the incoherence of the proposed mechanism (the government paying farmers to create a food shortage simultaneously with operating food assistance programs whose goal is food security).

The claim does contain a kernel of genuine historical accuracy—food was destroyed in 2020 during COVID agricultural disruptions—but this kernel was produced by market failure rather than government direction, and its recontextualization within a malice narrative represents a materially false causal claim.

The policy-level question this claim obscures is real and worth examining independently: whether emergency support mechanisms for farmers and food workers during the 2020 supply chain disruptions were adequate. That is a legitimate policy debate. It is not evidence for the conspiracy claim.
