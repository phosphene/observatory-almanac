# A Government Shutdown Stops All Healthcare Services Including Medicare and VA


## 1. The Claim

When the United States federal government faces a shutdown—the suspension of funding resulting from Congress's failure to pass appropriations legislation or a continuing resolution before the expiration of existing budget authority—a widely shared claim holds that the shutdown will stop or severely curtail all healthcare services: Medicare stops processing claims, VA hospitals close, Medicaid payments halt, and Americans dependent on federal healthcare programs will find themselves suddenly without coverage or services.

This claim surfaces reliably with each budget deadline, debt-ceiling confrontation, or continuing-resolution crisis. It appears in social media posts, opinion journalism, advocacy fundraising appeals, and political speeches designed to communicate the urgency of avoiding a shutdown. Its emotional resonance is high: the specter of veterans turned away from VA hospitals, dialysis patients denied Medicare-funded treatment, or seniors unable to fill prescriptions is a powerful political argument for passing a budget, and the claim functions as a persuasive device even when its literal accuracy is contested.

Versions of the claim range from broad ("healthcare stops") to specific ("Medicare payments will stop during a shutdown," "VA hospitals will close," "the ACA insurance marketplaces will go dark"). Some framings are more careful—"services will be disrupted" rather than "services will stop"—but the most viral formulations tend toward absolute language that implies total cessation. In politically charged moments where a shutdown was either imminent or ongoing, this claim has been shared millions of times on social media platforms and has appeared in major media coverage without consistent correction.

The claim also interacts with a legitimate policy concern: even if the specific services named do not stop, shutdowns do cause real healthcare disruptions, and the fact that some services are protected does not mean healthcare is fully insulated from shutdown effects. Understanding the claim requires distinguishing which services are genuinely protected, which are genuinely affected, and where the concern is directionally correct even if the literal statement is inaccurate.

The stakes are significant because this claim, if accepted uncritically, shapes public understanding of federal health programs, the distinction between mandatory and discretionary spending, and the practical meaning of a federal shutdown. Getting this right matters for policy literacy as well as for accurate threat assessment of shutdown risks to health.


## 2. What's Actually True

The broad claim—a shutdown stops all healthcare services including Medicare and VA—is false. However, the partial concerns embedded in various versions of the claim have real-world grounding that requires careful unpacking.

**Medicare: Claims continue to be paid.** Medicare is a mandatory spending program. Its legal authority to pay claims is not derived from annual discretionary appropriations; it flows from permanent authorizing legislation that obligates the Treasury to pay for covered services as claims are submitted. During a government shutdown—including the longest shutdown in U.S. history, 35 days from December 2018 to January 2019—Medicare claims processing continued without interruption. The Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (CMS) maintains a continuity-of-operations plan that designates Medicare claims processing as an excepted activity that continues regardless of funding lapse. Beneficiaries continued to receive covered services, providers continued to be reimbursed, and no Medicare patient was denied care due to shutdown-related funding disruption during any shutdown in the program's history.

**Medicaid: State-administered programs continue.** Medicaid is a jointly funded federal-state program in which federal matching payments are made to states. Like Medicare, federal Medicaid matching funds are provided through mandatory spending authority and do not require annual discretionary appropriations. States administer the program independently and continue doing so during shutdowns. No state has had to suspend Medicaid services because of a federal shutdown, and state managed care contracts and fee-for-service systems have continued to function normally during past shutdowns.

**VA healthcare: Hospitals remain open.** The Department of Veterans Affairs provides healthcare to approximately 9 million enrolled veterans through a system of hospitals, clinics, and community-based outpatient centers. VA healthcare funding is subject to annual discretionary appropriations, which technically makes it vulnerable to a funding lapse. However, VA hospitals are designated as essential services under the Anti-Deficiency Act's exception for activities involving the protection of life and property, meaning they operate with available carryover funding and emergency authority during shutdowns. Throughout past shutdowns, VA hospitals have remained open and continued providing care to enrolled veterans. VA benefits payments—disability compensation, pension, and education benefits—have historically flowed continuously during short shutdowns because the VA operates with significant advance appropriations for these purposes.

**ACA insurance marketplaces: Continue operating.** The Affordable Care Act's insurance marketplaces, through which millions of Americans purchase subsidized coverage, are largely funded through premium tax credits (mandatory spending) and insurer fees rather than annual discretionary appropriations. During past shutdowns, HealthCare.gov and state-based exchanges continued operating, including during open enrollment periods that fell within shutdown windows. The federal Premium Tax Credit subsidies, which make ACA plans affordable for most marketplace enrollees, would continue to flow under mandatory spending authority.

**What actually stops or is disrupted:**

The disruption is real but targeted at specific areas of federal health capacity rather than the broad service delivery infrastructure. Key affected functions include:

**NIH clinical trials:** The National Institutes of Health operates research hospitals and clinical research programs that pause enrollment during shutdowns. The NIH has reported that patients cannot be accepted into clinical trials during funding lapses, which is particularly consequential for trials involving experimental treatments for cancer, rare diseases, and other conditions without established therapies. During the 2013 shutdown (16 days), NIH reported turning away approximately 200 patients per week from its clinical research center, including some with active cancer diagnoses seeking trials for experimental treatments.

**FDA inspections:** The Food and Drug Administration pauses most domestic and foreign facility inspections during shutdowns, except for those involving imminent public health threats. Routine food safety inspections, pharmaceutical manufacturing site reviews, and medical device facility assessments are suspended. This creates accumulating inspection backlogs and—in prolonged shutdowns—potentially delays detection of safety problems at facilities that would otherwise have been inspected.

**CDC and public health operations:** Centers for Disease Control and Prevention staff are furloughed in significant numbers during shutdowns, affecting disease surveillance, outbreak response capacity, and public health program management. The CDC's ability to respond to emerging infectious disease events is demonstrably reduced during shutdowns. During the 2019 shutdown, initial concerns were raised about CDC's capacity to respond to a potential influenza outbreak during peak flu season.

**Indian Health Service:** The IHS, which provides healthcare to approximately 2.6 million American Indian and Alaska Native people, operates on annual discretionary appropriations without the same mandatory-spending backstop as Medicare or Medicaid. During prolonged shutdowns, IHS facilities have faced significant operational constraints, including deferred maintenance, stretched staffing, and delayed procurement for medical supplies and pharmaceuticals. The IHS serves communities that already have the worst health outcomes of any population group in the United States, and service disruptions have disproportionate public health consequences.

**CHIP (Children's Health Insurance Program):** CHIP is a jointly funded program with both mandatory and discretionary funding components. Its treatment during shutdowns depends on the status of its reauthorization, and during the late 2017 CHIP funding lapse (a funding gap distinct from a full government shutdown), states faced imminent exhaustion of CHIP funding that would have forced coverage lapses for approximately 9 million children. While this resolved before children lost coverage, it illustrates the vulnerability that exists at the intersection of CHIP and annual appropriations cycles.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of the "shutdown stops healthcare" claim through multiple shutdown cycles—despite it being provably incorrect with each shutdown's closure—reflects structural features of both the information environment and the political incentives at play.

**The complexity of the mandatory/discretionary distinction is genuinely difficult.** Federal spending is divided into mandatory spending (outlays governed by permanent authorizing legislation: Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, interest on the debt) and discretionary spending (subject to annual appropriations: defense, education, most agency operations). A government shutdown involves the lapse of discretionary appropriations, meaning only the discretionary component is affected. But this distinction is not intuitive, is not widely taught, and is not well-represented in everyday political vocabulary. Most people encounter federal spending as a unified phenomenon: the government either has money or it doesn't. The nuanced reality—some programs continue automatically, some require annual action, and they coexist within the same federal apparatus—requires institutional knowledge that most voters do not have occasion to acquire.

**Political incentives run entirely in one direction.** For politicians and advocates who oppose a shutdown—which is most politicians and most advocates in most moments—overstating the healthcare consequences serves their argument. The political logic of "a shutdown will cut off Medicare to seniors" is considerably more powerful than the accurate "a shutdown will disrupt NIH clinical trials and slow FDA inspections." The simpler, scarier version travels further and generates more political pressure. There is no significant political constituency with incentives to publicly correct the overclaim, because doing so amounts to reducing public urgency about shutdowns, which few people in political life want to do during a funding crisis.

**Past shutdowns have genuinely created some healthcare disruptions.** Because there are real healthcare effects—the NIH trial disruptions, the FDA inspection pauses, the IHS operational constraints—the general category of "shutdowns affect healthcare" is not wrong. The information environment tends to generalize from these real effects to the broader claim that all healthcare is affected. The availability of vivid anecdotes (cancer patients turned away from NIH trials, for instance) makes the broader claim feel validated even when it overstates the scope.

**Media coverage conflates partial disruption with total stoppage.** During shutdown standoffs, news coverage is episodic and urgency-driven, treating each day's federal agencies-going-dark updates as a cumulative story of governmental collapse. The language of "services shutting down" is accurate for some discretionary programs and inapplicable to Medicare and VA hospitals, but the same rhetorical frame of "shutdown = services stop" applies to all coverage. Readers absorbing this coverage without careful attention to program-by-program distinctions understandably conclude that all services, including healthcare, have stopped.

**"Medicare is funded by the government and the government shut down" has surface plausibility.** The logical chain—Medicare is a federal program, the federal government is shut down, therefore Medicare should also be shut down—is facially reasonable and incorrect. Understanding why it is incorrect requires knowing that Medicare operates on mandatory spending authority, which is a more specialized piece of fiscal knowledge than most political discussions demand. In the absence of that knowledge, the intuition that "Medicare is part of the federal government and the government closed" feels like a sound inference.


## 4. Verdict

**False as broadly stated; directionally important concerns are real.** The claim that a government shutdown stops Medicare, VA healthcare, Medicaid, and ACA marketplace insurance is false. These programs continue operating during shutdowns because of the mandatory spending framework, prior-year advance appropriations, essential-services exceptions, and state-level administration of joint federal-state programs. Throughout the history of federal government shutdowns—including the longest and most disruptive ones—no Medicare beneficiary has been denied coverage due to a shutdown, no VA hospital has closed, and no Medicaid program has been suspended.

The claim is not entirely without merit as a category: shutdowns do affect federal healthcare infrastructure in ways that generate genuine public health risk, particularly through NIH clinical trial pauses, FDA inspection backlogs, CDC operational constraints, and IHS service disruptions. These effects deserve serious public attention and would be better served by accurate, specific description than by the overstated "all healthcare stops" formulation that crowds them out.

The gap between the false broad claim and the true specific concerns represents a missed opportunity for public health communication: if audiences understood that the actual shutdown healthcare risk involves NIH trial patients and FDA inspection gaps rather than Medicare stoppage, pressure on policymakers to resolve shutdowns quickly might be better targeted and more durable.

Viral claim: **False.** Concern underlying the claim: **Partially well-founded for specific programs.**


## 5. The Wider Picture

The mandatory/discretionary spending distinction at the heart of this claim has become a fault line in American fiscal governance with implications far beyond government shutdowns. Understanding why certain healthcare programs are protected from shutdowns while others are not illuminates the deeper architecture of how the federal government commits money to health.

When Medicare was created in 1965 as part of Lyndon Johnson's Great Society legislation, the political design deliberately placed it in the mandatory spending category—often called "entitlement spending"—so that its benefits could not be terminated through the annual appropriations process. This design choice was a political achievement: it insulated the program from year-to-year congressional fluctuation and gave beneficiaries predictable access. The same logic applied to Medicaid and later to the Medicaid expansion under the ACA. The political parties have fought extensively over whether to "convert" these programs to discretionary block grants (the Republican approach in multiple budget proposals) or to maintain and expand mandatory spending status (the Democratic approach). A shutdown is, in miniature, a demonstration of why this distinction matters: programs built on mandatory authority continue; programs built on annual appropriations are exposed.

Veterans' healthcare presents an instructive contrast case. VA healthcare funding is largely discretionary—it requires annual appropriations—which technically makes it more vulnerable to shutdown disruption than Medicare. In practice, it has been protected through a combination of essential-services doctrine, public and political sensitivity to veteran disruption, and advance appropriations vehicles specifically designed to prevent VA healthcare gaps. The VA Mission Act of 2018 included advance appropriations provisions extending funding for VA healthcare on a two-year forward cycle, specifically to insulate veterans' care from shutdown risk. This is an example of the political system recognizing a shutdown vulnerability and engineering a solution—though one that required legislative action that took decades to materialize after the first veteran-facing shutdown concerns arose.

The international comparison is limited but instructive. Parliamentary systems that require a vote of confidence to govern typically handle appropriations differently than the U.S. bicameral-presidential system, which creates the specific constellation of conditions that produces shutdowns: a separately elected president, two chambers that must agree, and a hard deadline for funding lapse if they cannot. Most parliamentary democracies operate on continuing resolutions by convention or on automatic budget rollover provisions that prevent the funding-lapse trigger entirely. The U.S. shutdown mechanism is genuinely unusual in comparative democratic context, and the healthcare vulnerability questions it raises are correspondingly unusual.

From a public health preparedness standpoint, the history of shutdown effects on CDC and FDA operations has led to growing academic and policy interest in how pandemic preparedness infrastructure would function during a prolonged funding lapse. Tabletop exercises conducted by health security researchers have explored scenarios in which a shutdown coincides with an emerging infectious disease event, finding that the combination of CDC staff furloughs, state health department communication gaps, and FDA operational pauses would materially degrade outbreak response capacity. These findings have informed arguments for advance-funding public health preparedness infrastructure on the same mandatory basis as Medicare. As of this writing, those arguments have not yet translated into legislation.

The IHS disruption dimension carries particular moral weight because it involves a treaty obligation, not merely an administrative convenience. The federal government's responsibility to provide healthcare services to federally recognized tribes flows from treaties negotiated over more than a century and from the federal trust responsibility doctrine. When a shutdown degrades IHS capacity, it is not merely an administrative inconvenience; it is a federal failure to honor legally binding commitments to sovereign nations. This framing—shutdown as treaty violation—rarely appears in mainstream shutdown coverage despite its constitutional salience.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The "shutdown stops healthcare" claim has received more formal fact-check attention than most consumer health misinformation entries in this series, partly because each shutdown cycle resurfaces it with a population of new political commentators and social media users encountering shutdown mechanics for the first time.

**PolitiFact** has rated various forms of the claim across multiple shutdown cycles. In 2013, during the 16-day shutdown, PolitiFact rated the claim that "Medicare will stop paying bills during the shutdown" as "Mostly False," noting that Medicare claims processing continued and that the mandatory spending framework protected benefit payments while acknowledging that some HHS administrative functions were affected. In subsequent shutdown coverage, PolitiFact has consistently flagged overstated healthcare-stoppage claims while confirming the specific NIH and FDA disruptions.

**FactCheck.org** addressed Medicare specifically during the 2013 shutdown, producing a detailed piece explaining the mandatory/discretionary distinction and confirming that benefit payments continued. Its coverage of the VA during the 2019 shutdown similarly confirmed that hospitals remained open while noting the advance appropriations mechanism Congress had by then enacted to protect VA healthcare specifically.

**Washington Post Fact Checker** has engaged with "will seniors lose Medicare during a shutdown?" questions across multiple cycles, generally landing on ratings of two to three Pinocchios for claims that Medicare payments would stop, while awarding the specific-disruption claims (NIH, FDA) considerably more charitable assessment.

A consistent limitation in fact-checker coverage is the episodic nature of shutdown coverage: each shutdown generates a fresh round of fact-checks addressing the same basic questions, but fact-checkers rarely produce a durable reference document that persists across news cycles and is discoverable by audiences as the next shutdown approaches. The result is that each shutdown produces a new cohort of fact-checks that reaches audiences already in the middle of a political emergency, at a point when persuasion is difficult and partisanship is running high. A format designed for discovery between shutdowns—when audiences are less defensive and more open to foundational policy literacy—might provide better public health value than episodic fact-check articles published during political crisis.

**Social media platform labels** have shown inconsistent application, frequently not catching the healthcare-stoppage claim because it is phrased in terms of policy prediction rather than factual assertion, which presents classification challenges for automated content review systems calibrated to identify clear factual falsehoods.

The broader lesson for information consumers: the appropriate question during any shutdown is not "will Medicare stop?" (it will not) but rather "which specific federal health functions are disrupted, by how much, and what patient populations are affected?" That question leads to the NIH trial patients, FDA inspection gaps, CDC capacity degradation, and IHS operational constraints that represent the genuine healthcare stakes of prolonged funding lapses—and it leads away from the falsely reassuring conclusion that, since Medicare is fine, healthcare is fine.
