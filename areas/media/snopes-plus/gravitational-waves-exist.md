# Gravitational waves were finally detected, confirming Einstein's 100-year-old prediction


## 1. The Claim

A claim that has circulated extensively in science journalism, popular media, and online discussion holds that gravitational waves — distortions in the fabric of spacetime predicted by Albert Einstein in 1915 as a consequence of his general theory of relativity — were finally detected in 2015 or 2016 after a century of searching, confirming one of physics' longest-standing theoretical predictions. The claim was widely reported following the announcement on 11 February 2016 by the Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory (LIGO) collaboration, which stated that the first direct detection of gravitational waves had been made on 14 September 2015.

In most popular framings, this story is told as the culmination of a hundred-year quest: Einstein publishes his general theory of relativity in November 1915 and predicts that accelerating massive objects will produce ripples in spacetime that propagate outward at the speed of light; for the next century, physicists and experimentalists work toward instruments sensitive enough to detect these vanishingly small distortions; and in 2015, the upgraded Advanced LIGO detectors achieved the necessary sensitivity and detected a signal produced by the merger of two black holes approximately 1.3 billion light-years away. Nobel Prizes are awarded. The prediction is confirmed.

This claim is not a rumour, a misrepresentation, or an oversimplified folk rendering of contested evidence. It is a description of one of the most significant and most thoroughly documented experimental achievements in twentieth and twenty-first century physics. The purpose of this entry is not to debunk the claim but to trace the full evidence base behind it, examine the nature of what was actually confirmed, address some of the common misconceptions that attach to the popular telling, and explain why the discovery is as robust as the scientific community and major fact-checkers treat it.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Einstein and the theoretical background.** Albert Einstein published his general theory of relativity in a series of papers culminating in November 1915. The theory reframes gravity not as a force in the Newtonian sense but as a curvature of four-dimensional spacetime caused by the presence of mass and energy. The central field equations of the theory relate the geometry of spacetime to the distribution of mass-energy within it. In 1916, Einstein showed that these equations implied the existence of gravitational waves: oscillating perturbations of spacetime curvature that would be produced when massive objects accelerate and that would propagate outward at the speed of light.

Einstein himself was ambivalent about whether gravitational waves were physically real or merely mathematical artefacts of the theory. He published a paper in 1936 — co-authored with Nathan Rosen — that appeared to argue gravitational waves did not exist, but this work contained an error that was identified during peer review, and the published version was significantly altered. The question of whether gravitational waves were physical predictions of the theory or coordinate artefacts was not fully resolved until the 1950s and 1960s, when work by Richard Feynman, Hermann Bondi, and others established rigorously that gravitational waves carry real energy and should in principle be detectable.

**Indirect evidence before LIGO.** The first indirect evidence for gravitational waves came not from their detection but from observations of their effects on a binary pulsar system. In 1974, Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor discovered a binary pulsar — a system in which two neutron stars orbit each other, one of them a pulsar emitting detectable radio pulses at precise intervals. By timing these pulses over years, they were able to measure the orbital decay of the system with great precision. General relativity predicted that a binary system radiating gravitational waves would lose energy and thus gradually spiral inward — its orbital period shortening over time at a predictable rate. The measured orbital decay of the Hulse-Taylor binary matched general relativity's gravitational-wave emission predictions to within fractions of a percent. Hulse and Taylor received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1993 for this discovery, which provided compelling indirect evidence that gravitational waves were real features of the universe.

**The LIGO project.** The Laser Interferometer Gravitational-Wave Observatory was conceptualised in the 1980s by Kip Thorne, Rainer Weiss, and Ronald Drever. The basic principle of LIGO is interferometry: laser light is split and sent down two perpendicular arms several kilometres long, reflected back, and recombined. When a gravitational wave passes through, it compresses spacetime in one direction and stretches it in the perpendicular direction, causing a tiny difference in the lengths of the two arms that shows up as a change in the interference pattern of the recombined laser beams. LIGO's two detectors — one in Hanford, Washington, and one in Livingston, Louisiana — were built specifically to be geographically separate, so that seismic noise affecting one site would not simultaneously affect the other, allowing the two instruments to cross-check signals.

The initial LIGO detectors operated from 2002 to 2010 without detecting gravitational waves, which was consistent with theoretical predictions about the sensitivity needed to reach astrophysically interesting sources. A major upgrade programme produced the Advanced LIGO detectors, which began operation in September 2015 with approximately a factor of ten improvement in sensitivity over the initial instruments.

**The detection: GW150914.** On 14 September 2015, at 09:50:45 UTC, both LIGO detectors recorded a signal that lasted approximately 0.2 seconds and swept upward in frequency from roughly 35 Hz to 150 Hz — the characteristic "chirp" of a compact binary merger. The signal arrived at Livingston approximately 7 milliseconds before Hanford, consistent with a source direction in the southern sky. The amplitude of the displacement was extraordinary in its smallness: at most about 10^-18 metres, roughly a thousandth the diameter of a proton, over the four-kilometre arm length of each detector.

The LIGO scientific collaboration, comprising approximately 1,000 scientists from institutions in approximately 15 countries, spent five months performing detailed analysis of the signal before announcing it. They compared the observed signal against a library of computational models (template waveforms) derived from numerical relativity simulations of black hole mergers, and found an extraordinary match with the theoretical waveform for the merger of two black holes with masses of approximately 36 and 29 solar masses, at a distance of approximately 1.3 billion light-years (410 megaparsecs), producing a 62 solar mass final black hole and radiating approximately 3 solar masses worth of energy as gravitational waves in a fraction of a second.

The statistical significance was assessed at greater than 5 sigma — the conventional threshold for a discovery-level claim in experimental physics — meaning the probability of the signal being random noise was less than one in three million. The identical signal appeared in both detectors within the light-travel-time window between the two sites, ruling out local instrumental artefacts as the explanation.

**Subsequent detections and independent verification.** GW150914 was not an isolated event. By the time of this writing, over 90 confirmed gravitational wave events have been detected by the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA network (the Virgo detector near Pisa, Italy, and the KAGRA detector in Japan having joined the network). These events include black hole-black hole mergers, neutron star-neutron star mergers, and probable neutron star-black hole mergers. The neutron star merger GW170817, detected on 17 August 2017, was also observed optically across the electromagnetic spectrum by approximately 70 observatories worldwide, providing the landmark confirmation of multi-messenger astronomy — the direct association of a gravitational wave source with an independently observable astrophysical counterpart.

The Nobel Prize in Physics 2017 was awarded jointly to Rainer Weiss, Barry Barish, and Kip Thorne "for decisive contributions to the LIGO detector and the observation of gravitational waves."

**What was actually confirmed.** The popular framing — "Einstein's prediction confirmed" — is accurate but requires slight nuance. What LIGO confirmed is not a single prediction but a package of predictions that flow from general relativity: (1) that gravitational waves exist as physical phenomena; (2) that they propagate at the speed of light; (3) that their waveform structure matches numerical relativity calculations based on the theory; (4) that black holes exist and merge in the manner GRT predicts; and (5) that the energy radiated in gravitational waves corresponds to the mass and orbital parameters inferred from the signal. All of these are confirmed by GW150914 and subsequent events. The confirmation is not of a single qualitative prediction but of an extraordinarily detailed quantitative framework.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Because it is true and exceptionally well-documented.** The reason this claim circulates is straightforwardly because a major scientific discovery was made, verified, and communicated to the public. This is not a case where belief outruns evidence; it is a case where the evidence is overwhelming and the scientific communications infrastructure — press releases, peer-reviewed papers, Nobel Prize committees, subsequent replications — has functioned as intended.

**The narrative structure aids communication.** The "100-year-old prediction finally confirmed" framing is emotionally resonant and narratively complete. It has a protagonist (Einstein), a long quest structure, a dramatic moment of discovery, and an authoritative institutional confirmation (the Nobel Prize). This narrative is essentially accurate, which is relatively rare for science stories that achieve wide popular circulation. The accuracy of the narrative likely contributes to its persistence and to its reliability as an example of successful science communication.

**Some misconceptions attach to the popular version.** Despite the basic accuracy of the claim, the popular telling sometimes carries misconceptions worth noting. One is that Einstein was fully confident about gravitational waves; as noted above, he was not, and he briefly believed he had shown they did not exist. Another is that LIGO detected gravitational waves directly in the sense of seeing the waves themselves; what LIGO measured was the effect of passing gravitational waves on the relative positions of the detector mirrors. The waves themselves — distortions in spacetime — cannot be "captured" in the way a radio signal can; the detection is of their imprint on a physical system sensitive enough to measure it. A third misconception is that the confirmation of gravitational waves closes general relativity as an open question; in fact, each new detection provides new precision tests of the theory's predictions, and ongoing work seeks to identify deviations that might point toward quantum gravity or other physics beyond general relativity.

**Public scepticism exists at the fringes.** A small number of researchers and commentators have raised questions about whether the LIGO detection represents a genuine signal or is an artefact of systematic correlations between the two widely separated detectors. The most prominent version of this critique appeared in a 2017 paper by a group including Andrew Jackson that raised questions about correlated noise between the LIGO sites. The LIGO collaboration published detailed responses addressing the technical claims in that paper, and the critique has not been adopted by the broader experimental physics community. The subsequent detection of GW170817 with electromagnetic counterpart confirmation, and the pattern of detections across multiple instrument configurations and multiple sites including Virgo, makes instrumental artefact explanations increasingly difficult to sustain. The scientific consensus supporting the detections is robust and informed; this is not a case where consensus reflects political or institutional pressure rather than evidence.


## 4. Verdict

**Well-evidenced: the claim accurately describes a major confirmed scientific discovery.** The detection of gravitational waves by LIGO in September 2015, publicly announced in February 2016, is a real and thoroughly verified experimental result. The claim that this confirms Einstein's theoretical prediction is accurate: gravitational waves were predicted as a consequence of general relativity, their existence was contested for decades within physics before theoretical consensus was established, and the LIGO observation matched the theoretical predictions with extraordinary precision using an instrument specifically designed and built to perform the test.

The EARC mode for this entry is E: the claim is evidence-based, empirically supported, and consistent with the extended track record of confirmed subsequent detections, independent multi-instrument verification, and Nobel Prize recognition. There is no substantial basis for the claim to be classified as distorted, contested in good-faith scientific terms, or phenomenologically real but theoretically uncertain. The popular telling is accurate in its essentials and the underlying science is among the most secure empirical results in contemporary physics.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The LIGO detection sits at the intersection of several of the most important developments in late twentieth and early twenty-first century physics and carries implications that extend well beyond the confirmation of a single theoretical prediction.

**Multi-messenger astronomy.** The detection of GW170817, the neutron star merger, inaugurated multi-messenger astronomy as an operational discipline. The same event was observed in gravitational waves and in gamma rays, optical light, infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays, and radio waves by instruments around the world, produced the first direct constraints on the expansion rate of the universe (the Hubble constant) from gravitational wave sources, confirmed that short gamma-ray bursts are produced by neutron star mergers, and identified a kilonova — the explosive synthesis of heavy elements — in the merger's optical counterpart. The event demonstrated that gravitational-wave sources are not exotic theoretical objects but are the same astrophysical events that drive known electromagnetic phenomena, and it produced a new independent method for measuring fundamental cosmological parameters.

**Black hole demography.** Each gravitational wave detection adds to a growing catalogue of compact object mergers, revealing the mass distribution of black holes and neutron stars in a way that electromagnetic observations cannot easily access (since black holes do not emit light). Some of the mergers detected by LIGO-Virgo have involved objects in mass ranges — the "lower mass gap" between heavy neutron stars and light black holes, and the "upper mass gap" where pair-instability supernovae should prohibit black hole formation — that complicate existing models of stellar evolution and compact object formation. Gravitational-wave astronomy has become a precision tool for probing the demography of the compact object population in the Universe.

**Tests of general relativity.** Each detected event provides new precision tests of GRT predictions: the speed of gravitational waves (constrained by GW170817 to be equal to the speed of light to one part in 10^15), the polarisation of gravitational waves, the inspiral phase dynamics, the ringdown of the final merged object. These tests have all been consistent with GRT, providing increasingly stringent evidence against proposed modifications to the theory, and have placed the strongest constraints yet obtained on alternative theories of gravity.

**The detection technology itself.** The engineering achievement represented by Advanced LIGO — measuring displacements of 10^-18 metres over four-kilometre baselines, isolating a mirror from terrestrial vibration to a degree that allows quantum fluctuations in the laser to be the dominant noise source — represents a frontier of precision measurement with applications beyond gravitational wave astronomy. techniques developed for LIGO are feeding into proposals for even more sensitive detectors, including the Einstein Telescope (European) and Cosmic Explorer (American) proposals that would extend the detectable universe for gravitational wave sources to near-cosmological distances, and the space-based LISA mission that would detect gravitational waves at much lower frequencies, including from supermassive black hole mergers.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Major fact-checking organisations have not specifically addressed the claim that gravitational waves were detected, because it is not the kind of misinformation that requires correction — it is an accurate report of a confirmed scientific discovery. The absence of a Snopes verdict on this topic reflects that it falls outside the typical fact-checking use case, which is oriented toward identifying false or misleading claims rather than authenticating true scientific reports.

Where science fact-checking does engage with this territory, the relevant questions are typically about specific scientific details within the story: whether LIGO detected the waves or merely inferred them (the latter is more precisely accurate), whether Einstein predicted gravitational waves with confidence (he was ambivalent), whether the discovery proves the existence of black holes in the classical sense (it provides overwhelming indirect evidence), and whether the detection is subject to any credible scientific challenge (the fringe critiques noted above are not accepted by the relevant expert community).

The LIGO result illustrates what a claim that should be trusted looks like from a media literacy perspective: the discovery was published in a peer-reviewed journal (*Physical Review Letters*) after extensive review by a collaboration of approximately 1,000 scientists; it was independently confirmed by a second instrument at a separate geographic location within a time window consistent with the signal's propagation speed; it was subsequently replicated through a pattern of additional detections across multiple upgraded instruments in multiple countries; and it received the highest recognition of the international scientific community in the form of the Nobel Prize. This convergence of institutional validation mechanisms is precisely what mature scientific claims look like when they are genuinely supported by evidence. Users evaluating scientific news claims can use the LIGO story as a reference case for what robust confirmation looks like, contrasting it with premature or contested claims that circulate before multi-instrument replication and broad expert acceptance have been achieved.

For science communicators and educators, the LIGO detection offers a particularly useful teaching case because the chain of evidence — from theoretical prediction through instrument design through experimental result through subsequent replication — is publicly documented in detail and accessible to anyone with physics background sufficient to engage with the primary literature. The story is also instructive about the timescales of major physics discoveries: the conceptual work on gravitational wave detection spanned six decades, the instrument construction and initial operations took approximately fifteen years, and the confirmation involved a collaboration of a thousand scientists working across national lines. This production scale for a major experimental physics discovery is difficult to convey in science journalism formats that emphasise individual protagonists and single dramatic moments of discovery.
