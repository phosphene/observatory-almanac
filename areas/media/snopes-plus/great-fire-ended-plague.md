# "The Great Fire of London Ended the Plague"

**Claim Statement**: The Great Fire of London in September 1666 ended the Great Plague that had devastated the city since 1665.

**EARC Assessment**: **Contrast** — This claim requires systematic correction against historical evidence, demographic data, and epidemiological understanding of plague dynamics in 17th-century Europe.


## 1. The Claim

The assertion that London's Great Fire of September 1666 ended the Great Plague represents one of history's most persistent post hoc ergo propter hoc fallacies. This claim suggests a causal relationship between the fire's destruction of plague-infected areas and the subsequent absence of major plague outbreaks in London. The narrative has become so embedded in popular understanding that it appears in educational materials, historical documentaries, and casual explanations of both events.

The claim typically presents as: "The Great Fire burned down the rat-infested, plague-ridden medieval city, destroying the conditions that allowed plague to flourish, and London never again experienced a major plague outbreak." This framing implies both immediate causation (the fire stopped the current outbreak) and long-term prevention (the fire prevented future outbreaks).


## 2. What's Actually True

### Timeline Discrepancies

The most fundamental problem with the fire-ended-plague claim lies in basic chronology. London's Bills of Mortality—weekly death records maintained by parish clerks—provide precise documentation of plague mortality patterns that directly contradict the causal narrative.

**Peak plague mortality**: August-September 1665, with weekly deaths reaching approximately 7,000 at the height of the outbreak.

**Pre-fire plague decline**: By September 1666 (when the fire occurred), weekly plague deaths had already fallen to roughly 1,000—a decline of approximately 85% from peak mortality.

**Seasonal patterns**: The 1665-66 plague followed typical seasonal mortality patterns for bubonic plague, peaking in late summer and declining through winter, independent of any fire-related intervention.

### Geographic Misalignment

The fire's destruction pattern and the plague's demographic impact show crucial misalignment that undermines causal claims:

**Fire damage concentration**: The Great Fire primarily destroyed the wealthy commercial City of London within the medieval walls, consuming approximately 436 acres including 13,200 houses, 87 churches, and most major civic buildings.

**Plague impact distribution**: Historical evidence indicates the plague hit hardest in the poorest areas, particularly the overcrowded parishes outside the city walls and across the Thames in Southwark—areas largely untouched by the fire.

**Survival bias in records**: Wealthy areas within the walls, which the fire did destroy, maintained better records but actually showed lower plague mortality rates, partly due to the ability of wealthy residents to flee to country estates during the outbreak.

### Rat Population Dynamics

Modern understanding of plague transmission reveals additional problems with the fire-as-solution narrative:

**Rattus rattus habitat**: The black rats that served as primary plague vectors in medieval Europe preferred grain stores, ship holds, and the upper floors of buildings—not just the ground-level areas where fire damage would be most effective at destroying harborage.

**Post-fire rat populations**: Archaeological evidence suggests rat populations recovered quickly after urban fires, as new construction provided fresh harborage opportunities, often with improved food access during rebuilding periods.

**Regional plague decline**: The decline of plague in London coincided with similar declines across Western Europe, including cities that experienced no major fires, undermining the fire-specific causation model.


### The Broader European Context

Understanding why plague declined requires examining patterns across multiple cities and regions:

**Rattus species replacement**: Some historians propose that the replacement of Rattus rattus (black rat) by Rattus norvegicus (brown rat) altered plague transmission dynamics, as brown rats are less susceptible to plague and live in different ecological niches.

**Quarantine improvements**: Maritime quarantine practices improved significantly in the mid-17th century, reducing the introduction of plague from endemic areas in the Eastern Mediterranean and Central Asia.

**Urban transformation**: Many European cities underwent significant reconstruction and sanitation improvements in the late 17th century, changes that may have reduced overall disease transmission without requiring dramatic events like the Great Fire.

**Climate factors**: The Little Ice Age cooling period may have affected flea reproduction cycles and rat population dynamics in ways that reduced plague transmission efficiency.

### Methodological Implications

The persistence of the fire-ended-plague claim illustrates broader challenges in historical epidemiology:

**Causal attribution complexity**: Distinguishing between correlation and causation in historical disease patterns requires sophisticated analysis of demographic, ecological, and social factors that may not be well-documented in historical sources.

**Narrative versus analytical history**: Compelling historical narratives often survive longer than complex analytical explanations, even when evidence supports the more complex account.

**Interdisciplinary requirements**: Understanding historical disease patterns requires integration of historical method, epidemiological knowledge, and ecological analysis—a combination that exceeds the typical scope of popular historical accounts.


## 3. Why People Believe This

### Historical Method Problems

The fire-ended-plague claim exemplifies several methodological issues that compromise historical understanding:

**Narrative compression**: The tendency to link dramatic events occurring in temporal proximity creates compelling but inaccurate causal stories. The fire (September 2-6, 1666) and plague decline (ongoing through 1666-67) occurred close enough in time to suggest connection without requiring evidence of causation.

**Hindsight coherence**: Post hoc narratives gain strength when they provide satisfying explanations for complex phenomena. The idea that London's renewal through fire-driven reconstruction ended plague provides a more emotionally satisfying narrative than gradual demographic and ecological changes.

**Source limitations**: Contemporary accounts of both fire and plague come primarily from educated, wealthy observers who experienced both events from positions of relative safety and whose understanding of disease causation reflected pre-microbial theories.

### Quantitative Evidence Standards

Modern historical epidemiology requires attention to demographic data that was unavailable or misunderstood in the 17th century:

**Bills of Mortality analysis**: Weekly mortality data shows plague deaths declining months before the fire, from approximately 7,000 weekly deaths in late August 1665 to fewer than 500 by July 1666.

**Seasonal mortality patterns**: Bubonic plague characteristically peaks in late summer (when flea populations are highest) and declines through winter, a pattern that matches London's 1665-66 experience independent of fire timing.

**Comparative urban analysis**: Cities across Europe experienced similar plague declines in the late 17th century without major fires, suggesting broader ecological or demographic factors rather than London-specific interventions.


## 4. Verdict

**CONTRAST NEEDED**: The claim that the Great Fire of London ended the plague contradicts available historical evidence on multiple dimensions and requires systematic correction rather than simple confirmation or denial.

**Primary contrast points**:
- **Temporal sequence**: Plague mortality had declined by 85% before the fire occurred
- **Geographic distribution**: Fire damage and plague impact occurred in different areas of London 
- **Comparative context**: Plague declined across Europe without similar fires
- **Biological mechanism**: No plausible mechanism connects fire damage to plague transmission reduction

**Accurate alternative**: The Great Plague of London declined following typical seasonal patterns for bubonic plague and broader European epidemiological trends. The Great Fire was a separate disaster that occurred during the final phase of plague decline but did not cause that decline.

**Educational value**: This case demonstrates the importance of examining temporal sequences, demographic data, and comparative evidence when evaluating historical causal claims. It also illustrates how compelling narratives can persist despite contradictory evidence when they provide satisfying explanations for complex historical phenomena.

The persistence of this claim in educational materials suggests a need for historical literacy that emphasizes evidence-based analysis over narrative coherence, particularly for claims that link dramatic historical events in causal relationships. Educational approaches should emphasize the complexity of historical causation and the importance of distinguishing between correlation and causation in interpreting past events.

**Methodological lessons**: This case demonstrates the value of quantitative historical analysis, demographic data interpretation, and comparative case studies in evaluating causal claims about historical events. Students should learn to examine temporal sequences, geographic distributions, and alternative explanations before accepting intuitive causal narratives.

**Public health implications**: Understanding historical disease patterns provides insights into contemporary epidemic management. The London plague's decline through natural demographic and ecological processes, rather than dramatic interventions, parallels modern understanding of epidemic dynamics and the importance of sustained public health measures over dramatic single interventions.

**Narrative construction in history**: The fire-ended-plague claim illustrates how compelling historical narratives can emerge from temporal coincidence and emotional appeal rather than evidence-based analysis. Teaching critical evaluation of historical claims serves broader educational goals of developing analytical thinking and evidence-based reasoning skills.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### Plague Ecology and Urban Environments

Modern understanding of plague as a zoonotic disease reveals the inadequacy of simple environmental explanations:

**Yersinia pestis transmission**: Plague bacteria require specific ecological relationships between fleas, rodent hosts, and environmental conditions. Urban fires might temporarily disrupt these relationships but are unlikely to permanently alter them without broader ecological changes. The bacterium survives in soil for extended periods and can reestablish transmission cycles when suitable hosts and vectors return.

**Flea lifecycle dependencies**: Plague transmission depends on flea populations that complete their lifecycles in rodent nests and burrows. While fire might destroy some harborage, it cannot eliminate the broader urban ecosystem that supports rodent-flea-bacteria relationships. Fleas can survive in protected microenvironments—cellars, wall cavities, stored grain—that escape fire damage entirely.

**Reintroduction pathways**: Even if fire had temporarily eliminated plague from London, the city's position as a major port would have ensured reintroduction unless broader regional or global factors were reducing plague prevalence. Ships arriving from plague-endemic regions carried infected rats and fleas regardless of London's local conditions.

**Ecological succession patterns**: Post-fire environments often create improved conditions for rodent populations during reconstruction phases. Abundant food waste, disturbed soil suitable for burrowing, and temporary structures provide enhanced harborage opportunities that could actually increase plague transmission potential.

### Comparative Urban Fire History

Other major urban fires provide comparative cases that test the fire-ends-plague hypothesis:

**Chicago Fire (1871)**: Destroyed much of central Chicago but had no notable impact on infectious disease patterns, as the city's disease burden was primarily from cholera, typhoid, and other water- and food-borne pathogens rather than vector-borne diseases like plague. The fire's aftermath saw increased disease transmission due to overcrowding in temporary shelters and disrupted sanitation systems.

**San Francisco earthquake and fire (1906)**: Occurred during a documented bubonic plague outbreak in the city's Chinatown. The fire and subsequent rebuilding did not end the outbreak, which continued until 1908 and was controlled through rat control and sanitation measures, not fire damage. This case provides the strongest counter-example to the fire-ends-plague hypothesis.

**Lisbon earthquake and fire (1755)**: Destroyed much of Lisbon but had no documented impact on disease patterns, as plague had already ceased to be a major threat in Western European ports by the mid-18th century. However, the disaster disrupted public health infrastructure and created conditions conducive to other epidemic diseases.

**Hamburg fire (1842)**: Destroyed approximately one-third of the city but occurred during a period when plague was not endemic in Northern Europe. The reconstruction period saw cholera outbreaks due to disrupted water and sanitation systems, demonstrating that urban fires typically worsen rather than improve disease transmission conditions.

### The Demographic Context of Plague Decline

Understanding the 1665-66 plague's decline requires examining broader demographic and social changes in 17th-century London:

**Population mobility patterns**: Wealthy residents' ability to flee to country estates during the plague outbreak created complex demographic dynamics. The fire primarily affected areas where these residents lived, while plague mortality concentrated in areas where the poor remained during the outbreak.

**Urban density and housing quality**: London's rapid population growth in the early 17th century created overcrowded conditions that favored disease transmission. However, the areas most affected by overcrowding were not the same areas most damaged by the fire.

**Trade and commerce disruption**: The plague outbreak significantly disrupted London's commercial activity, reducing ship traffic and trade relationships that could reintroduce plague from external sources. This commercial disruption preceded the fire and may have contributed to plague decline independently of any environmental changes.

**Seasonal mortality patterns**: The 1665-66 plague followed typical seasonal patterns for bubonic plague in temperate climates, with peak transmission in late summer when flea populations are highest and declining transmission through winter months. This natural seasonal decline would have occurred regardless of fire-related interventions.

### Historical Epidemiology Standards

The fire-ended-plague claim fails to meet modern standards for historical epidemiological analysis:

**Temporal specificity**: Credible causal claims require precise temporal relationships between proposed causes and effects. The plague's decline preceded the fire by months, not the reverse. Weekly mortality data shows the steepest decline occurring in autumn and winter of 1665, with only residual transmission remaining by September 1666.

**Mechanism identification**: Causal claims require plausible biological or social mechanisms. No mechanism explains how fire damage to wealthy commercial districts would affect plague transmission in poor residential areas where the disease was most prevalent. The social geography of fire damage and plague mortality show minimal overlap.

**Comparative evidence**: Strong causal claims require evidence that similar causes produce similar effects in comparable cases. No evidence suggests that urban fires consistently reduce plague transmission in other historical contexts. Available comparative cases either contradict the hypothesis or occur in periods when plague was not endemic.

**Dose-response relationships**: Epidemiological analysis typically requires demonstrating that greater exposure to the proposed cause produces greater effects. In this case, areas with more extensive fire damage should show greater plague decline, but available evidence suggests the opposite pattern.

### Economic and Social Reconstruction

The Great Fire's aftermath created economic and social conditions that complicate simple causal narratives:

**Rebuilding boom**: The fire triggered massive reconstruction projects that attracted laborers, merchants, and craftsmen from across England and Europe. This population influx could have reintroduced plague if the disease remained active in other regions.

**Building regulations**: Post-fire reconstruction followed new building codes requiring wider streets, brick construction, and improved sanitation facilities. While these changes may have improved long-term public health, they occurred too late to affect the 1665-66 plague outbreak.

**Economic disruption**: The fire's economic impact extended beyond physical destruction to include disrupted trade relationships, insurance losses, and reduced commercial activity. These economic effects created social conditions that typically worsen rather than improve disease transmission.

**Social displacement**: The fire displaced approximately 100,000 residents, creating temporary camps and overcrowded housing conditions in undamaged areas. Such displacement typically increases disease transmission risk rather than reducing it.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed this specific claim in a standalone fact-check, though elements of the fire-plague relationship appear in broader historical content. This represents a significant gap in popular fact-checking coverage, given the claim's prevalence in educational contexts and its intersection with both historical accuracy and public understanding of epidemic disease patterns.

The absence of dedicated fact-checking attention may stem from the claim's position at the intersection of historical narrative and scientific causation—a space where traditional fact-checking often defers to historical scholarship rather than rendering definitive verdicts.
