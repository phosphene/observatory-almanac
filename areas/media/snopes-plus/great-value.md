# WalMart Ice Cream Sandwiches Don't Melt?


## 1. The Claim

In July 2014, Christie Watson, a Cincinnati resident, noticed that an ice cream sandwich her son had left outside on a warm summer morning — an 80-degree day — had not melted into the puddle she expected after more than twelve hours. She reported this observation to Cincinnati television station WCPO, which conducted its own melting comparison test and aired a segment on 15 July 2014. The WCPO segment compared a cup of Haagen Dazs ice cream, a Klondike Bar ice cream sandwich, and a Walmart Great Value ice cream sandwich, and found that the Great Value sandwich did indeed melt substantially more slowly than its competitors under identical conditions.

This segment circulated across social media and spawned numerous YouTube recreations over the following months, culminating in a second wave of viral attention in early 2015. The claim as commonly stated was striking in its simplicity: Walmart's Great Value ice cream sandwiches "don't melt." The implication drawn by many who shared the story was alarming: the product must contain artificial, non-food substances — "plastic" was a word used in some versions — or otherwise be so far removed from genuine ice cream that even heat refuses to affect it. One widely shared video clip appended the observation "and we wonder why cancer is on the rise" to the footage of an unmelted sandwich.

The claim simultaneously touched on several powerful anxieties: corporate food production, the opacity of industrial food manufacturing processes, the presumed dangers of processed foods, and the suggestion that large retailers knowingly sell products that fail some intuitive test of "realness." The ice cream sandwich that refused to melt seemed like tangible, reproducible proof of something wrong with modern food. It was the kind of empirical observation anyone could repeat at home — buy the sandwich, leave it outside, observe — which gave it a particularly democratic and compelling evidential quality compared to claims that depend on laboratory testing.

Walmart and consumer food scientists responded to the viral attention by explaining the actual mechanism behind the slow melt, but their explanations failed to quell the story's circulation. The claim was rated "Partly True" by Snopes founder David Mikkelson in a piece published 17 February 2015, and last updated 21 June 2015.


## 2. What's Actually True

The core observation is genuine: Great Value ice cream sandwiches do melt more slowly under some conditions than competing products. The WCPO experiment — while not a controlled scientific study — represented a reasonable real-world test that produced a result consistent with what food science would predict given the product's ingredient list and formulation.

Walmart's own response was instructive. When asked about the slow-melting phenomenon, a Walmart spokesperson stated: "Ice cream melts based on the ingredients including cream. Ice cream with more cream will generally melt at a slower rate, which is the case with our Great Value ice cream sandwiches." This explanation invokes a genuine food science principle. The fat content of ice cream significantly affects its rheological properties when heated. Higher fat content slows the melting process because fat provides structural stability and because the emulsified fat network inhibits water mobility within the product.

Consumer Reports, which examined the phenomenon, identified additional contributing factors: the presence of food stabilisers including calcium sulfate and guar gum. These ingredients are hydrocolloids — polysaccharide polymers that bind water and create viscous networks within the ice cream matrix. Their function in ice cream formulation is explicitly to control the melting rate and prevent the ice crystals and liquid phase from separating rapidly upon warming. Guar gum, derived from the guar bean (*Cyamopsis tetragonoloba*), is one of the most widely used food thickeners and stabilisers in the food industry, appearing in hundreds of products from yogurt to cream cheese to ice cream (Mudgil et al., 2011, *Food Research International*). Locust bean gum, carrageenan, and cellulose gum serve similar functions in competing products.

Virginia Tech food scientist Sean O'Keefe confirmed the mechanism to the Pittsburgh Post-Gazette: fat content is the primary driver, with higher fat yielding slower melting. This is consistent with established ice cream science literature. Sofjan & Hartel (2004, *International Dairy Journal*) demonstrated experimentally that increasing fat content and stabiliser concentration in ice cream formulations significantly extends melt-down time and reduces serum loss during melting. This is neither a secret nor a proprietary process — it is standard ice cream manufacturing practice.

All ingredients in the Great Value ice cream sandwich have been approved by the FDA as safe food additives. Calcium sulfate is approved under 21 CFR § 184.1230; guar gum under 21 CFR § 184.1339. Both are classified as generally recognized as safe (GRAS) under their intended conditions of use. The FDA's extensive review process for food additives requires demonstration of both safety and technological functionality before approval.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty

The ice cream sandwich melting story is a particularly revealing case study in what food communication researchers have called *naturalness heuristics* — the cognitive tendency to evaluate food safety and quality based on whether the product behaves in ways that match intuitive expectations derived from experience with "natural" unprocessed foods (Rozin, 2006, *Appetite*). When an ice cream sandwich fails to melt in the expected way, the violation of this expectation triggers an intuitive safety alarm: if it doesn't behave like "real" ice cream, it must not be "real" food.

This heuristic, while sometimes useful as a rough guide to food quality, fails systematically in the domain of processed foods, where technological modifications to natural properties are the explicit goal of food manufacturing. The entire function of stabilisers in ice cream is to produce characteristics that diverge in consumer-desired ways from the behaviour of raw cream, eggs, and sugar in their unprocessed state. An ice cream that maintains structural coherence on a warm day is not a chemical trick — it is an engineered improvement that serves consumers' practical interests.

The "cancer" appended to the circulating video represents a distinct epistemic move: the translation from "unusual behaviour" to "health danger" without any intermediate claim about mechanism. The implication that slow-melting ice cream causes or contributes to cancer has no scientific basis. None of the stabiliser ingredients in the Great Value sandwich have been assessed as carcinogens in doses relevant to food use. The International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) has not classified guar gum, calcium sulfate, locust bean gum, or any standard ice cream stabiliser as a human carcinogen. The cancer claim appears to have been appended as an affective amplifier — exploiting existing cultural anxieties about processed food and cancer risk without any factual grounding.

A genuinely novel implication of the melting test story is that it reveals how *differential behaviour* between competing products functions as an implicit accusation. The fact that Haagen Dazs melted faster and the Great Value product melted slower was construed not as evidence that Haagen Dazs has a simpler, less stabilised formulation (which it does — Haagen Dazs famously uses a short ingredient list as a marketing point) but as evidence that Great Value contains something abnormal or dangerous. The comparison implicitly constructed Haagen Dazs behaviour as the "natural" baseline against which Great Value was judged deviant. In fact, both products represent choices within a spectrum of legitimate ice cream formulation approaches, each with different cost, texture, stability, and nutritional trade-offs.

The episode also illustrates a communication failure on the part of food manufacturers. Walmart's response, while scientifically accurate, was corporate-toned and insufficiently explanatory — it mentioned cream content but did not clearly explain what stabilisers are, why they are used, or why they are safe. An unusually communicative response would have included the kind of explanation food scientists could readily provide and would have treated consumers as capable of understanding basic food chemistry. Instead, the defensively vague corporate language fed rather than resolved the suspicion.


## 4. The Scientific and Regulatory Record

Ice cream melting behaviour is a subject of substantial academic research with implications for product quality, consumer satisfaction, and cold chain management. The principal physical mechanisms governing ice cream melting have been extensively characterised.

Muse & Hartel (2004, *Journal of Dairy Science*) conducted a comprehensive study of ice cream melting properties, establishing that melt rate is primarily determined by: (1) the volume fraction of fat in the emulsion; (2) the degree of fat agglomeration and partial coalescence during freezing; (3) the concentration and type of stabilising hydrocolloids; and (4) the size distribution of ice crystals. Products with higher fat content, higher stabiliser concentrations, and smaller, more uniform ice crystals consistently showed slower melting rates. These findings precisely predict the behaviour observed in the WCPO experiment.

Guar gum's functionalty in ice cream is well established. As a galactomannan polysaccharide, guar gum forms highly viscous solutions at low concentrations by binding water through hydrogen bonding, effectively reducing the mobility of the water phase in the ice cream matrix (Mudgil et al., 2011, *Food Research International*). Its addition to ice cream slows the propagation of the melting front and reduces serum drainage — the liquid runoff that makes melted ice cream particularly photogenic and alarming in time-lapse videos. A sandwich stabilised with guar gum will soften and lose its frozen texture if eaten, but the structural matrix of bound water and fat will resist visual "puddle formation" for longer than an unstabilised product.

Calcium sulfate in food applications serves both as a firming agent and as a calcium fortification source. Its role in ice cream products is primarily as a firming agent that contributes to maintaining structural integrity. The safety profile of calcium sulfate as a food additive is well established; it is naturally present in many mineral waters and is used in the preparation of tofu as a coagulant (a use long antedating any concerns about food additives).

The FDA's GRAS designation for these ingredients reflects a substantial body of safety review. Under the Delaney Clause and broader food safety authorities in the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, food additives must be demonstrated safe under their intended conditions of use. The scientific literature supporting the GRAS status of guar gum and calcium sulfate includes multiple chronic dietary exposure studies in animal models showing no adverse effects at doses substantially exceeding any plausible human dietary exposure from ice cream consumption.


## 5. Verdict Analysis

Snopes' "Partly True" verdict is accurate and appropriately calibrated. The core behavioural observation — that the Great Value product melts substantially more slowly than some competing products — is genuinely true and reproducibly demonstrated. The mechanism is well understood and scientifically uncontroversial. The claim that this slow melting is evidence of dangerous, plastic-like, or cancer-causing ingredients is false and unsupported by any scientific evidence.

The "Partly True" categorisation navigates the important difference between the empirical claim (slow melting, which is true) and the evaluative claim (the slow melting is dangerous or suspicious, which is false). Treating the entire claim as false would misrepresent the genuine physical observation. Treating it as true would validate the distorted health-risk framing that accompanied the viral spread.

The case is a clean example of what philosophers of science might call a *theory-laden observation* — the same empirical fact (slow melting) can be interpreted as either evidence of superior stabiliser engineering or evidence of dangerous adulteration, depending on the background theory of food safety the observer brings to the observation. The WCPO investigation, to its credit, attempted to provide the scientific context needed to interpret the observation correctly, but that context was routinely stripped away as the clips circulated.


## 6. Implications

The Great Value ice cream story carries several broader lessons for food communication and consumer literacy. Most immediately, it illustrates the inadequacy of intuitive "naturalness" tests for food safety assessment. No food product should be evaluated as safe or dangerous based on whether it behaves as a pre-modern unprocessed version of itself would behave. Processed foods are designed to behave differently from their raw ingredients — that is, in large part, the technological point of food processing.

More broadly, the story highlights the challenge of communicating food additive safety to a population that has developed deep suspicions of corporate food production and "chemical ingredients." As Scrinis (2013, *Nutritionism: The Science and Politics of Dietary Advice*) has argued, modern food culture is characterised by "nutritionism" — an ideology that evaluates foods by their nutrient and ingredient composition rather than their actual health effects — and this framework makes ingredient lists a site of consumer anxiety in ways that are not well correlated with actual health risk.

The episode also raises questions about the media ecosystem that amplifies food fear claims. The WCPO piece was responsibly done — it consulted food scientists and included their explanations. But the clips stripped of that context circulated far more widely than the full segment. This asymmetry between contextualised reporting and decontextualised viral content is a structural feature of digital media that systematically amplifies alarming food claims and disadvantages nuanced correction.

For consumers, the practical implications are reassuring: the Great Value ice cream sandwich is a legitimate food product. If the stabiliser complement or caloric profile is not to a given consumer's preference, other formulations are available. But there is no scientific basis for treating slow-melting behaviour as a safety indicator, and the specific health fears generated by the viral story — adulteration with plastic, cancer risk — are unfounded.
