# "Great White Sharks Attack Humans Because They Mistake Them for Seals"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: When great white sharks (*Carcharodon carcharias*) bite or attack human swimmers, surfers, and divers, the attack is not motivated by predatory interest in humans. Instead, the shark visually confuses the human — particularly a surfer on a board — with a pinnipeds (seal or sea lion), its preferred natural prey. Upon realizing its error after an initial investigatory bite, the shark releases the human and retreats.

**The structure of the claim**: The mistaken identity hypothesis has three distinguishable sub-claims: (1) the visual silhouette of a human on a surfboard, viewed from below, closely resembles the silhouette of a seal; (2) great white sharks hunt by visual detection from below and cannot resolve the difference between the two silhouettes at attack speed; and (3) the "bite and release" pattern characteristic of most shark attacks on humans reflects the shark's recognition of error after the bite.

**Cultural prevalence and function**: This explanation is cited in virtually every popular-press piece on shark attacks. It appears in beach safety literature, museum displays, school curricula, and is the standard framing of shark attacks in wildlife documentaries. The mistaken identity hypothesis serves a psychologically important function: it transforms the shark from a creature that *wants* to eat humans (terrifying, implying persistent danger) into one that simply makes an understandable mistake (reassuring, implying that the shark is not a threat once it identifies you correctly). The *Jaws* model of the predatory man-eating shark is supplanted by the forgiving counter-narrative of the confused apex predator. This reframing has been deliberately promoted by marine conservation advocates who argue (correctly) that public hysteria about shark attacks leads to punitive culling policies that harm shark populations (Neff, C.L. & Heazle, M., 2013, *Marine Policy*, 38: 333–339).

**The bite-and-release pattern**: The claim is supported by a real empirical observation: the vast majority of shark attacks on humans — variously estimated at 50–80% depending on the dataset and category definition — are "hit and run" incidents in which the shark bites once and does not return for a second bite. This is statistically inconsistent with committed predatory behavior, in which a predator of the great white's capability and aggression would be expected to press an attack on an immobilized or bleeding prey item. The single-bite-and-retreat pattern does need explaining, and mistaken identity is one available explanation.

**The specific geometry of the hypothesis**: Proponents of the visual mistaken identity theory argue that when viewed from directly below — the position from which great whites often launch upward at speed — a surfer paddling or sitting on a board has a silhouette remarkably similar to that of a harbor seal or California sea lion: roughly the same length and width, with four appendage-like projections (arms and legs, or fore- and hind-flippers), and irregular edge motion. This claim is testable and has been partially tested.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Great white shark predatory ecology**:

*Carcharodon carcharias* is an apex predator distributed in temperate oceanic waters globally, with highest densities in the northeastern Pacific (California, Baja), southern Australia, South Africa, and the Mediterranean. Adult great whites are generalist predators but maintain strong dietary preferences for energy-dense prey, particularly marine mammals — harbor seals (*Phoca vitulina*), California sea lions (*Zalophus californianus*), elephant seals (*Mirounga angustirostris*), and similar pinnipeds in their range. Cetacean carrion is also exploited. Large bony fish, rays, and smaller sharks are consumed at lower rates and primarily by younger individuals (Klimley, A.P. et al., 1996, *Copeia*, 2: 278–292).

Great whites exhibit a distinctive hunting behavior called "spy-hopping" and "vertical attack," in which they approach from below and to the side at high speed (commonly 40 km/h or more), striking the prey with catastrophic momentum before circling as the prey weakens. This is behaviorally specialized for pinniped hunting in shallow coastal waters and differs markedly from the hunting strategy used for fish prey (Tricas, T.C. & McCosker, J.E., 1984, *Southern California Academy of Sciences Bulletin*, 83(2): 55–71). The asymmetric visual field of the shark — eyes positioned laterally with limited binocular overlap in the forward-downward sector — creates genuine constraints on visual discrimination during the terminal phase of a high-speed upward strike.

**Experimental support for the silhouette hypothesis**:

The most significant experimental test of the visual mistaken identity hypothesis was conducted by Kock, O'Riain, and colleagues at the University of Exeter and the South African Institute for Aquatic Biodiversity. Published in the *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* (Kock, A.A. et al. and Neumann, D.R. et al., 2021, *Journal of the Royal Society Interface*, 18(180): 20210529), the study used an integrative underwater camera system to photograph standard silhouettes of (a) paddling surfers, (b) sea lions, (c) harbor seals, and (d) humans floating with fins, from distances and angles corresponding to the great white's typical approach geometry. The images were then analyzed using a computational model of the great white shark's visual system, calibrated to the shark's known dichromatic color perception and contrast-detection sensitivity.

The results were partly confirmatory and partly complicating. Sea lion and surfer silhouettes were statistically indistinguishable by the model under low-light or turbid-water conditions at distances greater than approximately 2.5 meters. Under clearer, higher-light conditions, however, the model correctly distinguished sea lions and paddling surfers in the majority of cases. The study concluded that mistaken identity was "plausible under certain conditions" but was not a blanket explanation for all shark-human interactions. Crucially, divers wearing fins showed silhouettes that the model consistently distinguished from pinnipeds even under poor visual conditions, which is inconsistent with the blanket mistaken identity hypothesis (Neumann, D.R. et al., 2021, op. cit.).

**Evidence inconsistent with the mistaken identity hypothesis**:

Several categories of attack are difficult to reconcile with the mistaken identity account:

Scuba diving attacks: Great white sharks occasionally attack scuba divers, who present a very different visual profile from pinnipeds (rigid body, cylindrical tank, exhaust bubbles, consistent flipper motion). The "diver looks like a seal" version of the hypothesis is substantially less convincing visually, yet scuba diving attacks occur, including fatalities. If visual misidentification were the primary mechanism, scuba diver attack rates should be considerably lower than surfer attack rates on a per-exposure basis. Data from the International Shark Attack File (ISAF) does not support a sufficiently large differential to fully sustain this prediction (ISAF, University of Florida, annual summaries 2010–2023).

Deep-water attacks: Some great white attacks occur in open-water contexts at depth, where the silhouette-from-below scenario is geometrically inapplicable. These attacks are rarer but documented.

Multiple-bite attacks: In a minority of cases (estimated 15–30% of great white incidents), the shark makes multiple contacts, pursuing the human with behavior characteristic of predatory focus rather than investigatory confusion. These cases are inconsistent with the "one bite to identify the mistake" framing.

Lack of consumption: Even the single-bite cases are ambiguous. Great white sharks in the process of consuming pinniped prey do not typically deliver a single bite and retreat; they maintain contact. The fact that most human bite victims are released does suggest the human is not being actively eaten, but this could reflect multiple things: the shark's recognition that the human is not the expected prey *type* (correct in several ways — wrong taste, wrong fat content, wrong texture), not necessarily visual error at the point of initial approach.

**The investigatory bite hypothesis as alternative**:

Many marine biologists prefer the "investigatory bite" hypothesis, which frames the great white's initial contact with unfamiliar objects — including humans — as a form of sensory exploration rather than a predatory attempt gone wrong. Great white sharks investigate unfamiliar objects in their environment using their mouths, in part because their tactile sensation through teeth and jaw contact provides information about object composition, texture, and resistance that other senses cannot (Motta, P.J. et al., 2012, *Zoology*, 115(2): 112–122). Under this framing, a great white that bites a surfer once and departs is not a predator that made a visual error — it is an apex animal employing its available sensory apparatus to assess an unfamiliar stimulus and deciding the object is not prey based on tactile and olfactory feedback. The bite is exploratory, not predatory from the outset. The practical danger to humans is identical under either hypothesis; the mechanistic explanation differs.

**Species variation**: Not all shark attack patterns fit the great white template. Tiger sharks (*Galeocerdo cuvier*) show more generalist and persistent attack behavior, with lower bite-and-release rates, higher multiple-contact frequencies, and attack patterns that do not map onto the convergent mistaken-identity/pinniped-hunting specialization argument. Bull sharks (*Carcharhinus leucas*) similarly show attack patterns broadly inconsistent with visual similarity to any specific prey stimulus. The mistaken identity hypothesis, even if correct for great whites in specific contexts, cannot be generalized to explain shark attacks on humans as a phenomenon.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the narrative symmetry that forecloses better explanations**:

The mistaken identity hypothesis is believed — and specifically believed with more confidence than the evidence warrants — because it accomplishes something that no competing explanation accomplishes with equal elegance: it converts shark attacks from terrifying predatory events into forgivable accidents. This transformation serves multiple simultaneous social needs, and the convergence of those needs on a single explanatory frame is worth examining carefully, because it illustrates how motivated cognition operates even in scientific discourse.

The first social need is emotional: the mistaken identity hypothesis allows people who swim and surf to tolerate the risk of ocean recreation. A shark that *wants* to eat you is existentially intolerable in ways that ocean recreation has to suppress. A shark that makes occasional vision errors while hunting seals is a comprehensible natural process that places human injuries in the same category as being hit by a poorly aimed driver — unfortunate but not personally directed. This psychological distinction dramatically reduces perceived threat. Research on risk perception consistently finds that uncontrolled, involuntary, and intentional risks are experienced as more threatening than equal-magnitude controlled, voluntary, and accidental risks (Slovic, P., 1987, *Science*, 236(4799): 280–285). The mistaken identity hypothesis helpfully repositions shark attacks from the first category into the second.

The second social need is conservationist: the mistaken identity framing depoliticizes shark attack response. When attacks are framed as predatory behavior, political pressure for shark culling is intense. Several Australian states and South Africa have implemented drum-line and shark-net programs that kill substantial numbers of sharks and other marine life in response to attack incidents (Ferretti, F. et al., 2010, *Ecology Letters*, 13(8): 1055–1071). The mistaken identity framing argues against culling on the grounds that dead sharks do not reduce the probability of the next case of visual confusion — since attacks are accidents, not hunting decisions. Marine biologists and shark conservation advocates have often explicitly used the mistaken identity hypothesis to push back against punitive culling, which has produced a motivated feedback loop between scientific communication and conservation advocacy that has sometimes overstated the hypothesis's empirical support.

The third mechanism is structural: the hypothesis explains its own most compelling counter-evidence, which is the bite-and-release pattern. A good scientific hypothesis accommodates existing data; the mistaken identity hypothesis absorbs the bite-and-release observation into its framework (the bite is the moment of identification) and makes the whole account appear empirically grounded. The problem is that the investigatory bite hypothesis also absorbs the same evidence (the bite is exploration; departure reflects the conclusion of exploration). Two hypotheses that explain the same observations with equal elegance cannot both be regarded as strongly supported by those observations; they remain mutually underdetermined.

What the mistaken identity hypothesis does *not* absorb cleanly — the scuba diver incidents, the multiple-bite attacks, the deep-water encounters — receives substantially less popular attention than the supportive evidence, not because scientists ignore it, but because popular science communication systematically foregrounds the clean story and backgrounds the complications. This asymmetric transmission is a standard feature of how scientific findings move from technical literature to public knowledge: the clean, emotionally resonant finding leads; the caveats trail.

The result is a population belief with a structure characterized by genuine scientific support, genuine scientific uncertainty, and genuine scientific disagreement that the public version of the claim substantially smooths into a false consensus. This puts the fortune cookie myth in the epistemic category of *oversimplification* rather than outright falsehood — it is not made up, it is selectively true, and the selectivity serves identifiable psychological and political functions.


## 4. Verdict

**UNPROVEN (CONTESTED)**: The mistaken identity hypothesis has empirical support — particularly the visual systems analysis of Neumann et al. (2021) — but is not established as the general or primary explanation for great white shark attacks on humans. The hypothesis explains some of the attack pattern (bite-and-release, surfboard victims) better than others (scuba divers, multiple-contact attacks). Competition with the investigatory bite hypothesis, which is equally consistent with the core bite-and-release observation, leaves the question of mechanistic explanation genuinely open.

**What the Snopes rating reflects**: Snopes marks this claim as "Unproven/Mixture," acknowledging the scientific basis for the visual similarity observation while noting that the full causal mechanism remains contested. The verdict appropriately reflects the state of scientific literature rather than popular certainty.

**The practical safety conclusion is separate from the mechanism question**: Whether attacks result from visual error or investigatory biting, the practical behavioral implications for swimmers, surfers, and divers are largely identical — avoid turbid water near seal colonies at dawn and dusk, do not wear high-contrast pattern equipment, do not swim near bait fish that attract sharks. The mechanism question is scientifically interesting and politically significant for conservation policy, but it does not materially alter the personal risk calculus for ocean recreation.

**What would resolve the question**: Better resolution would require direct observation of great white decision-making during the approach phase of attacks on humans versus pinnipeds, which is operationally very difficult. Eye-tracking or behavioral indicator data collected during approach could distinguish between a predatory terminal engagement (stereotyped attack sequence identical to pinniped hunting) and an investigatory approach (slower, more variable trajectory, no commitment before contact). Advances in drone surveillance and autonomous underwater vehicle technology may eventually produce the observational resolution needed.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Great white shark attack causation sits at the intersection of marine biology, conservation policy, and public risk communication in ways that few animal behavior questions do. The stakes are high across all three domains simultaneously, which is unusual and creates structural incentives for oversimplification.

In marine biology, the question touches on fundamental issues of visual ecology, sensory system function, and interval-scale behavior modeling in large predatory animals. Sharks are difficult study subjects — they cannot be kept in captivity at functional scale, they are not amenable to the tracking and implantation techniques used in terrestrial apex predator research, and direct behavioral observation of high-speed attacks requires extraordinary setup. The result is that mechanistic knowledge remains thin relative to population-level ecological knowledge.

In conservation policy, the question has direct regulatory consequences. Every major shark attack incident generates political pressure for lethal shark control. The state of Western Australia spent several million dollars and killed hundreds of sharks in response to a cluster of fatalities between 2010 and 2013 (McPhee, D., 2014, *Ocean & Coastal Management*, 96: 1–11); the scientific community was largely unable to demonstrate that these culls had any preventive effect on subsequent attack rates. The mistaken identity hypothesis supports a regulatory argument against culling (you cannot prevent a visual accident by removing the animal that made it) that has strategic value in conservation advocacy regardless of its precise scientific standing.

In public risk communication, shark attacks occupy an outsized psychological space relative to their actual risk profile. Globally, approximately 70–80 unprovoked shark attacks on humans result in documentation per year (ISAF annual data), with 5–15 fatalities. The annual risk of a fatal shark attack for any individual resident of a coastal country is many orders of magnitude lower than traffic accident, drowning, or cardiovascular disease. Yet media coverage of shark attacks is intense and consistently generates demand for protective government action. The cultural role of *Jaws* (1975) in crystallizing modern public shark terror — acknowledged by the film's director Steven Spielberg as one of his regrets — established a sensational template from which ocean-safety journalism has never fully recovered.

Understanding the mistaken identity hypothesis accurately — as a partial, contested, mechanistic explanation for a subset of attack behaviors in one species — is a more useful public knowledge state than either the confident folk truth ("sharks just want seals, not us") or the *Jaws* mythology. The Observatory's role is to provide the accurate intermediate position.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes**: Rates the core claim "Unproven/Mixture," appropriately calibrated to the state of peer-reviewed literature. The rating distinguishes between the visual similarity observation (supported) and the full causal story (unsettled).

**Science journalism**: Peer outlets including *Scientific American*, *New Scientist*, and *National Geographic* have published nuanced treatments of the mistaken identity debate, generally giving appropriate space to both the visual systems research supporting the hypothesis and the criticisms from scientists favoring investigatory bite models. Notably, the 2021 Neumann et al. *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* paper received substantial popular science coverage, with most pieces accurately reporting its conditional rather than blanket conclusions.

**The Burgess critique**: George Burgess, longtime director of the International Shark Attack File at the University of Florida and one of the most cited authorities on shark attack epidemiology, has publicly expressed skepticism about strong versions of the mistaken identity hypothesis, arguing that the diversity of attack contexts makes any single explanatory framework inadequate (Burgess, G.H., various interviews, 2012–2020, discussed in multiple AP and Reuters items). His position, broadly, is that shark attacks on humans reflect opportunistic or investigatory behavior by animals that do not have a settled view of humans as prey, are not hunting humans, and are not precisely misidentifying humans either — they are doing something more ecologically ambiguous that our binary category of "predatory attack / mistaken identity" fails to capture.

**Conservation messaging tension**: Some shark research groups have expressed concern that rigorous qualification of the mistaken identity hypothesis — its replacement with the hedged and contested framing the science actually supports — risks undoing conservation communication gains achieved by the cleaner popular message. This tension between scientific accuracy and advocacy effectiveness is real and documented in interview-based research on marine science communication (Muter, B.A. et al., 2013, *Human Dimensions of Wildlife*, 18(3): 213–226). The Observatory takes the position that accuracy serves long-term conservation better than oversimplification, because a public that later discovers it was given a tidied version of contested science loses confidence in both the scientific claim and the scientists who promoted it, producing worse outcomes overall.
