# Are Birds Eye and Green Giant Vegetables 'All from China'?


## The Claim

In late May 2020, a meme began circulating on Facebook claiming that Birds Eye and Green Giant brand vegetables were "all from China." The image-macro format—bold text over branded packaging—urged consumers to switch to PictSweet, described as "a smaller company" based in Tennessee, because its products were supposedly grown domestically. The meme carried an implicit warning: popular frozen vegetable brands had outsourced their entire supply chains to China, and conscientious Americans should boycott them.

The timing was not accidental. The meme appeared roughly five months after the COVID-19 pandemic was first identified in Wuhan, China, in the winter of 2019, and as the United States was experiencing an acute escalation in anti-Asian racism. By May 2020, Stop AAPI Hate had already begun cataloguing thousands of incidents of verbal harassment, shunning, and physical assault directed at Asian Americans, many of which explicitly invoked China as a source of contamination, danger, or blame. The Washington Post documented widespread discrimination directed at people of Asian heritage in the United States during this period, covering incidents ranging from workplace hostility to assaults on public transit.

Within this environment, the "all from China" vegetable meme functioned as a form of soft xenophobia—one that avoided overtly racist language while channeling the same anxieties. It repackaged anti-China sentiment in the culturally acceptable vocabulary of consumer advocacy and food safety. By framing the issue as a question of where one's broccoli was grown rather than who was to blame for a pandemic, the meme could circulate among audiences who might have rejected an explicitly bigoted message but were receptive to supply-chain nationalism dressed up as purchasing advice.

This pattern—xenophobic sentiment laundered through superficially neutral consumer claims—is not unique to the COVID-19 era. Similar memes have targeted everything from pet food to pharmaceuticals, typically surging during periods of geopolitical tension or health crises involving China. What distinguishes the May 2020 vegetable meme is the specificity of its false claims: it named two identifiable brands, made a factually testable assertion ("all from China"), and offered a concrete alternative (PictSweet). This combination of emotional resonance and apparent specificity is precisely what makes such memes effective. They feel like insider knowledge rather than propaganda, which is why they spread so efficiently through social sharing.

The meme was flagged to Snopes by multiple readers who encountered it on Facebook feeds and wanted to know whether the sourcing claims were accurate. Bethania Palma investigated for the fact-checking organization and published her findings on May 27, 2020, with an update the following day.


## What's Actually True

Snopes reached out directly to both Birds Eye and Green Giant for comment and received a definitive response from one of the two companies. Dan Hare, a spokesman for Birds Eye (a brand owned by Conagra Brands), told Snopes that the vast majority of the company's vegetables are sourced from farms in the United States. His statement was unambiguous: "Only approximately 5% of the brand's vegetables come from China, and those are primarily edamame." This single data point demolishes the meme's central claim. If 95 percent of Birds Eye vegetables are grown outside China, characterizing the brand's products as "all from China" is not merely an exaggeration—it is a near-total inversion of reality.

The edamame sourcing detail is significant because it reflects the realities of global agricultural specialization. Edamame—immature soybeans harvested in the pod—has deep roots in East Asian cuisine and agriculture. While domestic U.S. edamame production has expanded (notably in Arkansas, where the crop has been commercially grown since the 2000s), China remains a major global producer of soybeans generally. For a niche product like frozen edamame, sourcing a portion from Chinese growers represents standard supply-chain economics rather than any kind of wholesale outsourcing. Birds Eye's transparency on this point, offered without prompting beyond a fact-checker's inquiry, suggests a company confident in its sourcing rather than one trying to conceal it.

Green Giant did not respond to Snopes' inquiry by the time of publication. However, Palma investigated the company's own publicly available sourcing information. The Green Giant website enumerated specific locations for its packaging and growing operations: frozen vegetables packaged in Illinois, California, Minnesota, Idaho, Washington, and Mexico; canned vegetables processed in Minnesota, Idaho, Wisconsin, New York, the Netherlands, and Peru. China was notably absent from every location listed. While the absence of a mention does not constitute definitive proof that no Green Giant products are ever sourced from China—supply chains are complex and can change—it strongly contradicts the meme's claim that the brand's vegetables are "all from China."

The corporate history of Green Giant further contextualizes its supply chain. General Mills sold the North American Green Giant business to B&G Foods in 2015. Under B&G Foods' ownership, the brand emphasized sourcing from domestic agricultural regions, particularly Minnesota and the Pacific Northwest for sweet corn. B&G Foods subsequently sold the U.S. Green Giant frozen business to Seneca Foods in 2026, but throughout these ownership transitions, the brand's publicly stated sourcing has consistently centered on North American production facilities.

Snopes also contacted PictSweet, the Tennessee-based company held up by the meme as the virtuous domestic alternative. Jo Winters, PictSweet's Consumer Affairs Specialist, confirmed that "most of our vegetables are grown in the USA. However, some are grown south of the border." Winters noted that the country of origin is listed on every package under the best-by date and lot code, and that all PictSweet processing facilities are located in the United States. This response is illuminating for two reasons. First, it confirms that even PictSweet—the meme's preferred brand—sources some vegetables internationally, undermining the dichotomy the meme establishes between foreign and domestic producers. Second, it highlights an existing labeling practice: PictSweet already discloses country of origin on its packaging, using the framework mandated by federal law for fresh and frozen produce.

Snopes rated the claim "Mostly False." The "mostly" qualifier acknowledges that a small percentage of Birds Eye vegetables (approximately 5 percent, primarily edamame) do come from China, and that Green Giant's sourcing could not be fully confirmed due to their non-response. The ruling also left one element as "undetermined"—whether any Green Giant vegetables are sourced from China—an appropriate concession to the limits of the available evidence.


## The Epistemic Gap

Snopes' fact-check is accurate within its scope. The rating is defensible, the evidence is clearly presented, and the direct quotes from company representatives are appropriately contextualized. But the article, by design, answers only the narrow question posed by the meme: are these vegetables "all from China"? The deeper questions—why the meme spread, what it reveals about gaps in America's food-labeling regime, and how pandemic xenophobia exploits those gaps—go entirely unexamined. This is the epistemic gap.

### Pandemic Xenophobia and Food Nationalism

The "all from China" vegetable meme did not emerge from a genuine consumer investigation into agricultural supply chains. It emerged from the same cultural moment that produced the "China virus" rhetoric, the surge in anti-Asian hate incidents documented by Stop AAPI Hate (which recorded over 10,900 incidents between March 2020 and December 2021), and a broader pattern of pandemic-era scapegoating. The meme exploits a psychological mechanism well-documented in social science: when people feel threatened by an invisible hazard (like a novel virus), they seek visible, tangible targets for their anxiety. Food—something we literally put inside our bodies—is an especially potent vessel for contamination fears.

This is not to say that every person who shared the meme harbored conscious anti-Asian prejudice. The psychology is subtler than that. Research on stigma during the COVID-19 pandemic, including studies published in Stigma and Health, has shown that disease-related anxiety readily attaches to national and ethnic categories, often without the individual being fully aware of the association. A person might share a "buy American produce" meme while genuinely believing they are motivated by food safety or economic patriotism rather than xenophobia. But the meme's viral success during a period of acute anti-China sentiment—and its functional falsehood—suggests that the emotional charge driving its spread was not primarily about agricultural trade policy.

Shannon Harper's research on anti-Asian hate crime during the COVID-19 pandemic, published in the American Journal of Criminal Justice, establishes a framework for understanding how health crises have historically been linked to the stigmatization and "othering" of people of Asian descent. The Green Giant meme fits squarely within this pattern: it uses the language of consumer protection to reinforce a narrative of Chinese contamination without ever having to say so explicitly.

### The Country of Origin Labeling Regime

A fact-check of a viral meme is not the place for a deep dive into agricultural trade policy, and Snopes cannot reasonably be faulted for omitting this context. But the omission leaves readers without the tools to evaluate similar claims independently. The United States has a specific legal framework for country-of-origin labeling (COOL) that is relevant here—and it is considerably more fragmented than most consumers realize.

COOL requirements were established under the Farm Security and Rural Investment Act of 2002 and expanded under the 2008 Farm Bill. They mandated country-of-origin labels for a range of commodities including fresh and frozen fruits and vegetables, certain nuts, fish and shellfish, and muscle cuts of meat. However, in December 2015, Congress repealed the COOL requirements for beef and pork as part of a federal spending bill, after the World Trade Organization ruled that U.S. labeling rules for those products discriminated against imports from Canada and Mexico. The WTO authorized retaliatory tariffs potentially exceeding one billion dollars, and Congress opted to repeal rather than face those penalties.

The critical detail that most consumers do not know: COOL requirements remain in effect for fresh and frozen fruits and vegetables, peanuts, pecans, macadamia nuts, ginseng, fish, shellfish, chicken, lamb, and goat. This means that the information the meme purports to reveal—where your frozen vegetables come from—is already legally required to appear on the packaging. Every bag of Birds Eye frozen vegetables sold in the United States must disclose its country of origin. The meme's implicit premise, that companies are hiding Chinese sourcing from consumers, is undermined by the very labeling regime that covers these products.

Academic research on COOL has examined both the economic effects of these requirements and consumer attitudes toward origin labeling. Lusk and Briggeman, in their 2009 study in the American Journal of Agricultural Economics, found that while consumers express support for country-of-origin information, origin actually ranked among the least important food values compared to safety, nutrition, taste, and price. Lusk and colleagues later examined COOL specifically in Applied Economic Perspectives and Policy, finding that the policy's measurable economic benefits—in terms of shifting consumer purchasing patterns—were modest. Consumers say they care about where their food comes from, but price and convenience dominate actual purchasing behavior. This gap between stated preferences and revealed preferences is itself an important finding: it suggests that "buy American" memes tap into identity and anxiety rather than the considered trade-offs consumers actually make at the grocery store.

### What the Import Data Actually Shows

The USDA Economic Research Service maintains detailed data on U.S. vegetable imports by commodity, country, and category. These data tell a story considerably more nuanced than "all from China." For fresh vegetables (excluding potatoes), the import landscape is dominated overwhelmingly by North American trade partners: Mexico supplies approximately 77 percent of U.S. fresh vegetable imports, and Canada accounts for roughly 13 percent. China's share of fresh vegetable imports to the United States is minimal.

China's role is more significant in specific processed vegetable categories. In 2008, the value of food imports from China reached $5.2 billion, with juices and processed vegetables (pickled, dried, and canned) accounting for 25 percent of that value. For certain individual commodities, China's market share has been substantial: as of 2007, imports from China constituted approximately 60 percent of the U.S. apple juice supply and over 50 percent of the garlic supply. These figures are noteworthy, but they describe specific commodity markets rather than the frozen vegetable category as a whole.

The aggregate picture contradicts the "all from China" narrative: less than one percent of the overall U.S. food supply originates from China. The frozen vegetable market in particular is served overwhelmingly by domestic production and North American partners. When China does figure in the supply chain—as with Birds Eye's edamame—it is for specific products where Chinese producers have genuine agricultural advantages, not because American brands have secretly relocated their entire sourcing operations overseas.

### The "All from China" Pattern

The Green Giant meme is not an isolated case. Virtually identical claims—"Product X is all from China, buy Product Y instead"—have circulated on social media about pet food, vitamins, pharmaceuticals, garlic, honey, and dozens of other consumer products. Some of these claims contain kernels of truth (Chinese honey has been subject to documented anti-dumping cases; garlic imports from China are genuinely significant). Others are flatly false. What unites them is a rhetorical structure: the vague, totalizing claim ("all from China"), the implication of concealment (companies are hiding this from you), and the offer of a patriotic alternative. This structure functions as a template that can be applied to any product category, and it tends to surge during periods when China features prominently in American anxieties—whether due to trade wars, food safety scandals, or pandemics.

The pattern's durability suggests that debunking individual instances, while valuable, does not address the underlying mechanism. Each time a specific "all from China" meme is fact-checked, the template survives to be applied to the next product. A more effective intervention would equip consumers to evaluate these claims independently—by understanding COOL requirements, knowing how to read origin labels on packaging, and recognizing the emotional heuristics that make these memes persuasive.

## What This Means

The "all from China" vegetable meme sits at the intersection of three larger problems, each of which matters more than the specific factual claim Snopes debunked.

**First, food-labeling transparency remains incomplete despite existing law.** COOL requirements do cover frozen vegetables, meaning consumers who examine packaging can determine where their produce was grown. But the existence of a legal requirement does not guarantee effective communication. COOL disclosures are typically printed in small text near lot codes and expiration dates—not in the prominent, front-of-package format that would make origin information immediately visible to shoppers. Academic research consistently shows that consumers support origin labeling in surveys but do not actively seek out this information at the point of purchase. This gap between the availability of information and its practical accessibility creates space for viral memes to fill with misinformation. When consumers do not know that origin labeling already exists, they are more susceptible to claims that companies are concealing sourcing information.

**Second, pandemic-era xenophobia found sophisticated channels for diffusion.** The meme did not include slurs, did not reference the virus directly, and did not target Asian individuals. It presented itself as consumer advice. This made it shareable in social contexts where overtly racist content would be rejected. The result is a form of "laundered xenophobia"—prejudice expressed through the idiom of consumer sovereignty rather than explicit bigotry. This style of messaging is particularly difficult for platform-level content moderation to address because each individual element (a purchasing recommendation, a claim about sourcing, a preference for domestic production) is innocuous in isolation. Only in context—the timing, the pattern, the factual inaccuracy—does the xenophobic function become apparent. Understanding this mechanism matters beyond the specific meme in question; it describes a template that recurs across consumer categories and geopolitical moments.

**Third, the structure of supply-chain misinformation resists fact-checking at scale.** Snopes debunked this particular meme effectively. But the underlying template—"Brand X is all from Country Y, buy Brand Z instead"—requires no special knowledge to produce and can be applied to any product. It exploits the opacity of global supply chains, the emotional salience of food safety, and consumers' general unfamiliarity with trade policy. Each fact-check addresses one instance while leaving the generative mechanism intact. A more durable response would involve improving food-labeling visibility, teaching media literacy around supply-chain claims, and building public understanding of how COOL actually works.

The frozen vegetable market itself continues to grow, projected to expand significantly through the 2030s. As supply chains diversify globally and trade tensions with China persist, memes targeting the origin of consumer products will continue to appear. The question is whether consumers will have the tools to evaluate them—or whether viral misinformation will continue to exploit the gap between what labeling law requires and what people actually know.


## Verdict and Classification

**Rating: Mostly False.** The claim that Birds Eye and Green Giant vegetables are "all from China" is contradicted by direct statements from company representatives, publicly available sourcing disclosures, and the structure of U.S. vegetable import markets. Only approximately 5 percent of Birds Eye vegetables come from China, and those are primarily edamame. Green Giant's own website lists packaging and processing locations in multiple U.S. states, Mexico, the Netherlands, and Peru—with no mention of China. Even PictSweet, the meme's preferred alternative, sources some vegetables internationally.

**EARC Mode: E (Expand).** Snopes' verdict is correct, and the evidence supporting it is solid. The epistemic gap is not in the ruling itself but in what falls outside its scope. The fact-check answers "is this true?" without addressing "why is this circulating?" or "what should consumers actually know about food origin labeling?" A fuller account would contextualize the meme within the documented surge of pandemic-era anti-Asian xenophobia, explain the COOL regulatory framework that already mandates origin labeling for frozen vegetables, present USDA data on the actual composition of U.S. vegetable imports, and identify the "all from China" claim as a recurring template rather than an isolated falsehood.

**Gap Category: xenophobia-amplified-claim.** The factual core of this claim is demonstrably false. Its viral spread was driven not by genuine consumer concern about agricultural sourcing but by pandemic-era anxieties about China that found expression through consumer-protection rhetoric. The meme leveraged real consumer ignorance about existing labeling law and real complexity in global supply chains to construct a narrative of concealment and foreign contamination. Recognizing the xenophobic amplification mechanism is essential for understanding both why the meme spread and why similar claims will continue to appear in different forms.

A complete analysis of this claim requires three layers that standard fact-checking, by its operational mandate, does not typically provide: the xenophobic context that gave the meme its emotional charge, the regulatory framework that already addresses the meme's underlying concern, and the trade data that show how far the meme's claims deviate from the actual composition of U.S. food imports. Each layer adds necessary depth to a verdict that is, in isolation, entirely correct.
