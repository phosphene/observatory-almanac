# "Green M&Ms Are Aphrodisiacs"


## 1. The Claim

The claim under examination asserts that green M&M candies possess aphrodisiac properties—that consuming them enhances sexual desire or performance. This belief has persisted in American popular culture for over five decades, spawning countless references in media, comedy, and casual conversation. The claim typically presents in two forms: the strong version suggesting green M&Ms contain actual aphrodisiac compounds, and the weaker version treating them as a cultural symbol or placebo for romantic enhancement.

The myth has proven remarkably durable, surviving transitions from penny candy to premium brand, from local rumor to internet meme, and from genuine belief to knowing wink. Its persistence across generations suggests something deeper than simple misinformation—it represents a confluence of color psychology, marketing savvy, and the human desire to find magic in the mundane.


## 2. What's Actually True

Examining the claim's internal logic reveals several critical gaps. First, the proposed mechanism is absent—no advocate of the green M&M theory has ever articulated how the green coloring would produce aphrodisiac effects. The claim lacks even pseudoscientific explanation, existing purely as assertion.

Second, the claim fails basic chemistry. Food colorings used in confectionery are inert dyes chosen specifically for their stability and non-reactivity. Blue No. 1 (brilliant blue) and Yellow No. 5 (tartrazine), which combine to create the green coloring, are among the most thoroughly tested food additives in the FDA arsenal. Neither has any documented psychoactive properties.

Third, the claim's historical trajectory undermines its credibility. Unlike many food folklore beliefs that emerge from traditional medicine or cultural practices, the green M&M myth appears to be modern, coinciding with mass production and marketing of colored candies. It has no roots in herbalism, traditional aphrodisiac beliefs, or cultural food practices.

The claim also suffers from what might be called "arbitrary specificity"—why green M&Ms specifically, rather than green candy generally? If green coloring possessed aphrodisiac properties, we would expect to see similar claims about green Skittles, green gumdrops, or green-frosted desserts. The specificity to M&Ms suggests brand-specific mythology rather than ingredient-based belief.


Snopes' "False" verdict operates within a narrow evidential framework focused on chemical composition and physiological mechanism. This approach is methodologically sound but potentially incomplete when addressing phenomena that exist primarily as social constructions rather than material realities.

The verdict's strength lies in its empirical grounding. There is simply no chemical pathway by which green M&M consumption could produce aphrodisiac effects. The standardized manufacturing process ensures compositional consistency across color variants, and the food-grade dyes used have extensive safety profiles with no documented psychoactive properties.

However, the binary "False" classification may obscure important nuances about how beliefs function in social reality. While the green M&M claim is chemically false, it has achieved a form of cultural truth through widespread acceptance and corporate endorsement. The belief has real consequences—influencing purchasing decisions, inspiring marketing campaigns, and shaping romantic rituals.

The epistemological limitation here is the fact-check format itself, which privileges propositional truth over social meaning. A more complete analysis might recognize the claim as "chemically false but culturally functional"—acknowledging both its scientific invalidity and its social reality.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemological framework underlying this claim reveals fascinating patterns in how modern folklore emerges and spreads. Unlike traditional myths rooted in religious or cultural narratives, the green M&M belief represents "commercial mythology"—folklore that emerges around branded products in consumer society.

The claim operates through what anthropologist Sarah Banet-Weiser calls "commodity activism"—the attribution of special properties to mass-produced goods as a form of consumer agency. By believing green M&Ms have special powers, consumers transform a standardized industrial product into something personalized and meaningful. This represents a form of resistance to mass production's homogenization, allowing individuals to carve out spaces of personal significance within corporate-controlled consumption landscapes.

The psychological dynamics underlying this belief system reveal sophisticated patterns of meaning-making in modern consumer culture. Cognitive anthropologist Scott Atran's work on "intuitive biology" suggests that humans possess innate tendencies to categorize natural substances by color and attribute properties accordingly. Green's association with plant life activates these deep cognitive patterns, making green-colored foods seem inherently more "natural" and potentially bioactive.

The myth also demonstrates "chromatic essentialism"—the belief that colors inherently possess psychological or physiological properties beyond their visual impact. Green's cultural associations with nature, fertility, and growth provide a plausible foundation for aphrodisiac beliefs, even without mechanistic support. Cross-cultural analysis reveals that green holds similar symbolic weight across diverse societies: Celtic folklore associates green with fertility deities, Chinese traditional medicine links green foods to liver health and vitality, and Islamic traditions connect green to paradise and divine blessing.

Color psychology research by Eva Heller and others has documented measurable psychological effects of color exposure, lending partial credibility to chromatic thinking. While these effects operate through visual and emotional pathways rather than biochemical ones, they create a scientific-sounding foundation for folk beliefs about colored foods. The green M&M myth exploits this partial truth, extrapolating from documented visual effects to imagined physiological ones.

Perhaps most importantly, the claim illustrates how corporate marketing can amplify and legitimize folk beliefs. Mars Incorporated's embrace of the myth through their Green M&M character creates a feedback loop: the company profits from the belief while simultaneously validating it through official representation. This strategy, known as "folklore marketing," has become increasingly common as brands seek authentic cultural connections in saturated markets.

The Green M&M character's design evolution reveals careful corporate cultivation of the aphrodisiac association. Introduced in 1995, the character initially appeared in relatively modest advertising contexts. However, as market research confirmed the myth's cultural resonance, Mars progressively emphasized the character's sultry attributes: the signature white go-go boots became higher heels, the voice became more seductive, and advertising scenarios became increasingly suggestive. By 2012, the character was appearing in Super Bowl commercials that directly referenced her "reputation."

The epistemology here is essentially sympathetic magic—the belief that symbolic similarity creates causal connection. Green equals nature equals fertility equals aphrodisiac power. This reasoning pattern, identified by anthropologist James Frazer as fundamental to magical thinking, proves remarkably persistent even in scientifically literate societies. Modern cognitive science has confirmed Frazer's insights: the human brain's pattern-recognition systems often prioritize symbolic connections over causal analysis, particularly in domains involving health, sexuality, and enhancement.

Neuropsychologist V.S. Ramachandran's research on synesthesia and cross-modal perception provides additional insight into why color-based food beliefs feel intuitively plausible. The brain regions processing color, taste, and emotional significance show extensive interconnection, creating natural pathways for cross-sensory associations. When these normal perceptual processes encounter cultural suggestions about green foods, the result can feel like genuine insight rather than learned belief.


## 4. Verdict

**Classification: Manufactured Mythology**

The green M&M aphrodisiac claim represents a sophisticated case of manufactured mythology—folklore that emerges from commercial rather than cultural origins. While Snopes' "False" verdict accurately captures the claim's chemical invalidity, it understates the phenomenon's social complexity and cultural significance.

The belief persists not because of evidence, but because it serves multiple cultural functions: providing consumer agency in standardized markets, creating shared cultural references, and satisfying desires for magical enhancement in mundane products. Mars Incorporated's commercial exploitation of the myth demonstrates how corporate interests can amplify and legitimize folk beliefs for profit.

The case reveals important patterns in modern folklore formation, particularly how branded products can become focal points for cultural meaning-making in consumer societies. It also illustrates the limitations of binary fact-checking when addressing socially embedded beliefs that operate primarily through symbolic rather than literal truth.

From an educational perspective, the green M&M myth serves as an ideal case study for understanding the intersection of commerce, culture, and epistemology in contemporary society. It demonstrates how scientific literacy must be combined with media literacy and cultural analysis to fully comprehend modern misinformation phenomena.

The Observatory's assessment recognizes both the claim's factual falsity and its cultural authenticity as a piece of American commercial folklore. Understanding why such beliefs emerge and persist is often more valuable than simply dismissing them as false. The green M&M case offers insights into consumer psychology, marketing ethics, and the social construction of meaning that extend far beyond candy coloring.

The phenomenon's longevity suggests deep compatibility with fundamental human cognitive patterns. Anthropologist Claude Lévi-Strauss's concept of "bricolage"—the human tendency to create meaning by recombining existing cultural elements—helps explain how a simple colored candy becomes a vehicle for complex beliefs about enhancement, nature, and personal agency. The green M&M myth represents successful bricolage: it combines accessible materials (candy), universal desires (enhancement), and cultural symbols (green as nature) into a compelling narrative package.

Future research directions might examine how similar myths emerge around new products and technologies. The rapid development of functional foods, nutraceuticals, and personalized nutrition creates rich opportunities for new folklore formation. Understanding the green M&M phenomenon provides a baseline for recognizing and analyzing emerging commercial mythologies before they become culturally entrenched.

The case also raises important questions about corporate responsibility in an era of declining institutional trust. As traditional authorities lose credibility, commercial brands increasingly fill explanatory roles once occupied by religious or educational institutions. Mars Incorporated's cultivation of the green M&M myth represents a relatively benign example of this trend, but it illustrates dynamics that could prove more problematic in other contexts.

Climate change and environmental concerns add another dimension to the analysis. The green M&M myth's success partly derives from green's association with nature and environmental health. As consumers increasingly seek "natural" products and experiences, color-based food folklore may become more rather than less influential. The challenge lies in distinguishing legitimate environmental concerns from marketing-driven mythology.

**Recommended Response:** Neither uncritical acceptance nor dismissive ridicule, but rather informed engagement with both the claim's scientific invalidity and its cultural significance. The phenomenon deserves serious analysis as a window into how modern societies create meaning around consumption, even when that meaning lacks empirical foundation. Educational approaches should acknowledge the myth's cultural reality while maintaining scientific accuracy, using it as a starting point for broader discussions about evidence, marketing, and meaning-making in consumer society.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The green M&M phenomenon exists within a broader landscape of food-based sexual folklore that reveals important patterns in how modern societies construct meaning around consumption. Similar claims exist for oysters, chocolate, ginseng, and numerous other foods, suggesting a persistent human desire to find enhancement through ingestion.

Food anthropologist Sidney Mintz has documented how industrialized societies develop "pseudo-traditional" beliefs around processed foods, creating modern folklore to replace disrupted cultural practices. The green M&M myth represents this process in miniature—a manufactured tradition that fills the void left by disconnection from traditional aphrodisiac knowledge.

The phenomenon also intersects with what sociologist Pierre Bourdieu called "cultural capital"—knowledge that signals social sophistication. Knowing about the green M&M myth (whether believing it or dismissing it) becomes a marker of cultural literacy in American popular culture. This dynamic helps explain the myth's persistence even among skeptical populations.

From a gender studies perspective, the Green M&M character raises important questions about how food marketing sexualizes consumption. The anthropomorphized candy serves as a vehicle for adult themes in ostensibly family-friendly advertising, normalizing the association between female sexuality and consumable products.

The international dimension is equally revealing. While M&Ms are sold globally, the green aphrodisiac myth remains primarily American, suggesting culture-specific factors in its development and persistence. European markets show no equivalent folklore around colored confectionery, highlighting the role of particular cultural conditions in myth formation.

The digital age has transformed but not eliminated such beliefs. Internet communities have created elaborate taxonomies of food-based enhancement claims, with green M&Ms occupying a canonical position in online folklore collections. Social media platforms have become vectors for both perpetuating and debunking such myths, creating parallel information ecosystems where belief and skepticism coexist in separate filter bubbles.

Wikipedia's treatment of the phenomenon illustrates this digital complexity. The platform's article on "List of aphrodisiac foods" includes green M&Ms alongside historically recognized substances like ginseng and oysters, creating false equivalence through encyclopedic formatting. This demonstrates how digital reference formats can inadvertently legitimize folklore by presenting it within scholarly organizational structures.

Reddit communities dedicated to "life hacks" and "weird facts" regularly resurface the green M&M claim, often accompanied by elaborate personal testimonials that read like parody but maintain ambiguous sincerity. These platforms create what danah boyd calls "context collapse"—the flattening of social contexts that makes it difficult to distinguish between genuine belief, ironic performance, and deliberate misinformation.

TikTok has spawned numerous videos testing the green M&M hypothesis, typically featuring young people consuming large quantities of green M&Ms and reporting (usually humorous) results. These videos blur entertainment and empirical testing, creating a pseudo-scientific veneer for what amounts to performance art. The platform's algorithm amplifies engaging content regardless of accuracy, allowing folklore to spread through entertainment value rather than truth value.

The phenomenon also intersects with contemporary wellness culture and its complex relationship with both traditional knowledge and scientific authority. Instagram influencers promoting "natural enhancement" often include green M&Ms in broader discussions of color therapy, chakra alignment, and food energetics. While these contexts are explicitly non-scientific, they provide alternative epistemological frameworks where the green M&M claim can seem plausible.

The commercialization of folklore represents a broader trend in consumer capitalism. Companies increasingly mine folk beliefs for marketing opportunities, creating feedback loops where corporate messaging reinforces cultural myths. Mars Incorporated's strategy with the Green M&M character exemplifies this approach—profiting from folklore while simultaneously legitimizing it.

The persistence of the green M&M belief also reflects broader patterns in risk and enhancement thinking. In societies where medical enhancement is common and expected, food-based improvement claims become more plausible. The myth piggybacks on legitimate discussions about nutrition, supplementation, and dietary influence on health and performance.

Educational implications are significant as well. The green M&M case study offers valuable lessons about critical thinking, scientific literacy, and media manipulation. It demonstrates how seemingly harmless beliefs can reveal important patterns in reasoning, evidence evaluation, and cultural influence.

Psychology professor Hal Herzog has used the green M&M myth in undergraduate courses to illustrate confirmation bias and the placebo effect. Students who believe in the myth often report feeling different after consuming green M&Ms, demonstrating how expectation can create subjective experiences that feel like objective evidence. This provides a safe, relatable context for discussing more serious forms of medical misinformation.

The myth also serves as an ideal case study for media literacy education. Students can trace the claim's evolution across different media platforms, analyzing how the same basic assertion adapts to different communicative contexts: playground rumors, comedy routines, advertising campaigns, and social media memes. This multi-platform analysis reveals how modern folklore spreads through diverse channels rather than single transmission pathways.

Science educators have found the green M&M case particularly useful for teaching the concept of burden of proof. The claim's persistent popularity despite complete absence of supporting evidence illustrates how beliefs can survive and thrive without meeting basic evidentiary standards. This provides concrete grounding for more abstract discussions about scientific methodology and skeptical thinking.

Business schools have begun incorporating the Mars marketing strategy into case studies about brand management and consumer psychology. The company's decision to embrace rather than dispel the folklore offers lessons about turning cultural phenomena into competitive advantages. Students analyze the ethical implications of profiting from scientifically false beliefs while exploring the business dynamics of modern mythology.

The legal and regulatory context adds another layer of complexity. The FDA's oversight of food additives provides strong consumer protection against actual harm from food colorings, but offers no protection against false beliefs about food properties. This regulatory gap allows folklore to flourish in spaces where science has already established safety.

The Federal Trade Commission's guidelines on advertising substantiation create interesting boundaries around the green M&M phenomenon. While Mars cannot legally make explicit aphrodisiac claims about their products, they can create suggestive marketing that allows consumers to draw their own conclusions. This legal distinction between direct claims and implied suggestions enables the corporate cultivation of folklore while maintaining regulatory compliance.

International regulatory differences have shaped the myth's global reception. European Union regulations on food marketing are generally more restrictive than U.S. standards, leading to different advertising strategies for the Green M&M character in different markets. European campaigns typically emphasize the character's confidence and independence rather than her sexuality, reflecting both cultural differences and regulatory constraints.

The intersection with dietary supplement regulation is particularly revealing. The FDA's relatively permissive approach to supplement marketing has created a landscape where far more dubious enhancement claims receive official tolerance. Against this background, the green M&M myth appears almost quaint in its modesty—a folk belief that at least involves a real, regulated food product rather than unproven compounds with grandiose health claims.

Product liability law creates additional complexity. If someone were hypothetically harmed by consuming excessive quantities of green M&Ms in pursuit of aphrodisiac effects, the legal framework for assigning responsibility remains unclear. The combination of corporate suggestion, folk belief, and individual choice creates a distributed accountability structure that challenges traditional liability models.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has maintained a consistent "False" rating for this claim since addressing it in their archives. Their assessment focuses on the factual core: there is no chemical difference between M&M candies of different colors that would confer aphrodisiac properties. The food coloring used to create the distinctive green shell is identical in chemical structure to other M&M dyes—it simply absorbs and reflects different wavelengths of light.

Snopes traces the rumor's documented history to at least the 1970s, well before the internet could amplify such claims. They note that Mars Incorporated, rather than dispelling the myth, has actively embraced it in their marketing campaigns, particularly through the anthropomorphized "Green M&M" character introduced in the 1990s. This character was deliberately designed with sultry characteristics—high heels, long eyelashes, and a flirtatious demeanor—essentially monetizing the folk belief.

The fact-checking site emphasizes the absence of any peer-reviewed research supporting aphrodisiac claims for chocolate generally, let alone for specific food colorings. They note that while chocolate contains phenylethylamine and other compounds that can affect mood, these are present in all M&M varieties regardless of shell color.
