## 1. The Claim

The internet-circulated warning in question asserts that the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA) issued an official advisory connecting raw and lightly cooked green onions — also known as scallions — to outbreaks of Hepatitis A. The claim, which spread through email chains and early social media forwarding throughout the 2000s, holds that consumers should avoid or thoroughly cook green onions due to documented contamination events. The warning specifically references genuine outbreaks that occurred in 2003, including a large event in Pennsylvania that resulted in hundreds of illnesses and several deaths.

The forwarded message typically took one of two forms. In the first, the email reproduced the FDA advisory text verbatim, sometimes with minor formatting changes or a forwarded-chain preamble ("Pass this on to everyone you know!"). In the second, the email summarized the advisory in less formal language, urging recipients to stop eating green onions at restaurants and to cook them at home. Both versions referenced real events — the November 2003 FDA advisory, the Pennsylvania restaurant outbreak, and earlier outbreaks in Tennessee, North Carolina, and Georgia — but the email-chain format, combined with the alarmist tone of the informal variants, led many recipients to suspect a hoax. The claim, in its accurate form, is straightforward: the FDA did issue a warning about green onions and Hepatitis A, the outbreaks were real, and the deaths were real. What makes this entry notable is not that the warning was fabricated but that a genuine public health advisory, when filtered through the email-forwarding ecosystem of the early 2000s, took on the stylistic markers of a hoax — and was consequently disbelieved by a significant share of the people who received it.

## 2. What's Actually True

On November 15, 2003, the FDA's Center for Food Safety and Applied Nutrition (CFSAN) issued an advisory to consumers, food retailers, and food service operators warning that raw or lightly cooked green onions (scallions) had been associated with multiple Hepatitis A outbreaks (FDA, 2003, "FDA Advises Consumers About Green Onions," official advisory, U.S. Food and Drug Administration). The advisory was issued in response to a cluster of Hepatitis A outbreaks that had begun the previous September.

In September 2003, outbreaks of Hepatitis A were identified among patrons of restaurants in Tennessee, North Carolina, and Georgia. These outbreaks were epidemiologically linked to the consumption of raw or lightly cooked green onions (CDC, 2003, Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report, vol. 52, no. 47). Subsequent traceback investigations identified green onions sourced from specific farms in Mexico as the likely origin of contamination in at least the Tennessee outbreak, where molecular epidemiology and supply-chain analysis converged on a common supplier (Amon et al., 2005, Clinical Infectious Diseases, vol. 41, no. 6, pp. 811–818).

The most significant outbreak, however, occurred in late October and early November 2003 in western Pennsylvania. At least 520 people developed Hepatitis A after eating at a single restaurant — subsequently identified as a Chi-Chi's restaurant in the Beaver Valley Mall, near Monaca, Pennsylvania (Mandak, Joe, Associated Press, November 19, 2003). Three people died as a direct result of the outbreak, and approximately 9,000 individuals who had eaten at the restaurant during the exposure window sought prophylactic immune globulin injections in a mass clinic operation (CDC, 2003, MMWR, vol. 52, no. 47; Wheeler et al., 2005, Journal of Infectious Diseases, vol. 191, no. 7, pp. 1094–1101). The Pennsylvania outbreak was, at the time, the largest single-restaurant foodborne Hepatitis A outbreak in recorded U.S. history.

The investigation that followed implicated green onions as the most likely vehicle, though the precise mechanism of contamination was never definitively established. The green onions served at the restaurant were traced back through the supply chain to distributors and, ultimately, to farms in Mexico. However, unlike the Tennessee investigation, which produced a stronger molecular and epidemiological linkage to specific Mexican-origin green onions, the Pennsylvania investigation could not conclusively identify the exact farm or irrigation source responsible. Contamination may have occurred at the farm level through contaminated irrigation water, or further along the supply chain — during handling, transport, or in-restaurant food preparation practices (Wheeler et al., 2005, Journal of Infectious Diseases, vol. 191, no. 7, pp. 1094–1101).

The FDA advisory recommended that consumers cook green onions thoroughly, that food service operators verify the source of their green onions, and that suppliers implement enhanced traceability measures. The advisory was further amplified by press coverage, including extensive Associated Press reporting that confirmed the basic facts: the warning was genuine, the outbreaks were real, and the deaths had occurred (Mandak, Joe, Associated Press, November 19, 2003).

## 3. Why People Believe This Is a Hoax When It's True

The green onion warning occupies a counterintuitive position in the information ecosystem. It is a genuine public health advisory that was widely suspected of being a hoax — not because the facts were wrong, but because the mode of transmission (email forwarding) and the tone of the informal variants resembled the structure of scare-chain emails that were rampant in the early 2000s. This is an instance of what we might call the inverse problem in health misinformation detection.

**EPISTEMIC NOVELTY:** People trained to disbelieve internet health warnings sometimes dismiss real alerts. The internet-forwarding culture of the 2000s created a population conditioned to treat any alarming health email as a probable hoax. When the email-forwarding format is applied to a genuine advisory, the format itself becomes a signal of unreliability — even when the underlying content is factually accurate. This is the inverse of the usual misinformation problem: rather than false information being believed, true information is disbelieved. The "cry wolf" effect, documented in public health communication research, describes a phenomenon in which repeated exposure to false alarms reduces subsequent compliance with genuine warnings (Lichtenstein, Slovic, Fischhoff, Layman, & Combs, 1978, "Judged Frequency of Lethal Events," Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Learning and Memory, vol. 4, no. 6, pp. 551–578). Lichtenstein et al. found that people systematically overestimate the frequency of dramatic, media-salient causes of death and underestimate common ones — and that repeated exposure to alarmist framing, whether accurate or not, distorts risk perception over time. In the green onion case, the accumulated weight of years of food-scare chain emails had trained a generation of recipients to file FDA advisories in the same cognitive category as microwaving-water-releases-toxins and aspartame-causes-cancer warnings.

The credibility halo of Snopes itself plays a complex role. Snopes.com, for over two decades the leading fact-checking resource for internet-spread claims, occupies a unique epistemic position: many internet users specifically visit Snopes when they receive a forwarded health warning, expecting to find it debunked. The expectation structure itself is revealing. When a user receives a health warning email, their default action is often to "check Snopes" — and the act of checking is motivated, at least in part, by the assumption that the warning is probably false. If Snopes confirms the warning as true, the user is forced into a brief cognitive recalibration: the heuristic "forwarded health emails are probably hoaxes" has been violated. The irony is that Snopes, the very tool designed to combat misinformation, can function as a confirmation device for skepticism about genuine advisories — users visit expecting a "False" rating and are mildly surprised to see "True." This "checking to debunk" behavior pattern was observed in early web-forum and USENET discussions of the green onion warning, where users posted responses along the lines of "I thought this was a hoax, but Snopes says it's real."

The result is a peculiar epistemic failure mode: the signal of alarm (email forwarding) has been so thoroughly associated with noise (fabricated scares) that even genuine signals are filtered out. Public health authorities, aware of this problem, have increasingly shifted to distributing advisories through official channels — press releases, CDC alerts, and social media verified accounts — to ensure the delivery mechanism itself does not undermine credibility. The green onion warning is, in this sense, a case study in how the medium can poison the message, regardless of the message's truth value.

## 4. The Science in Depth

Hepatitis A is caused by the Hepatitis A virus (HAV), a non-enveloped, single-stranded RNA virus belonging to the Picornaviridae family. HAV is transmitted primarily through the fecal-oral route — the virus is shed in the feces of infected individuals and can be ingested through contaminated food, water, or surfaces. The incubation period ranges from 15 to 50 days, with a mean of approximately 28 days (Wasley, Fiore, & Bell, 2006, in Fields Virology, 5th ed., Lippincott Williams & Wilkins, pp. 1079–1102). This long incubation period is a critical epidemiological factor: infected individuals are shedding virus before they develop symptoms, making it extremely difficult to identify and contain outbreaks in real time. By the time a cluster of Hepatitis A cases is recognized, the contaminated food has typically already been consumed or discarded, and the supply chain may have moved on.

The reason green onions are particularly susceptible to HAV contamination lies in their morphology and agricultural practices. Green onions (Allium fistulosum or Allium cepa var. cepa, the latter grown for its immature form) have a layered, tubular structure. The layers — the concentric cylinders of the bulb and the hollow green shafts — create protected spaces where contaminated material can become physically trapped. If irrigation water contains even low levels of HAV, the virus can lodge between these layers and survive through harvesting, transport, and even rudimentary washing. Standard commercial washing and processing, which might remove surface contamination from smooth-skinned produce, is significantly less effective on green onions because the layers are not fully accessible to mechanical or chemical washing (Seymour & Appleton, 2001, "Food Safety and Quality: Market-based Approaches," in Proceedings of the International Conference on Food Safety, WHO/FAO).

The World Health Organization's guidelines on fresh produce safety emphasize that raw agricultural commodities, particularly those consumed without cooking, represent a significant vector for foodborne viral disease (WHO, 2002, "WHO Global Strategy for Food Safety: Safer Food for Better Health," World Health Organization, Geneva). The WHO notes that agricultural water — water used for irrigation, pesticide application, or post-harvest washing — is one of the most significant pre-harvest contamination points for produce. In regions where agricultural water is drawn from sources that may be contaminated with human or animal waste, the risk of viral contamination of produce is substantially elevated.

HAV's thermostability is another critical factor. Unlike many foodborne bacterial pathogens, which are reliably inactivated at standard cooking temperatures, HAV is among the more heat-resistant enteric viruses. Studies have shown that HAV can survive at 60°C (140°F) for up to 10–12 hours, and that temperatures of 85–90°C (185–194°F) sustained for at least 1 minute are required for reliable inactivation (Sattar et al., 2000, "Foodborne Viruses: An Under-recognized Problem," in Sattar, ed., The American Society for Microbiology, pp. 185–212). This means that "lightly cooked" green onions — those briefly sautéed, blanched, or used as a garnish on hot dishes — may retain viable virus. Only thorough cooking, in which the internal temperature of the vegetable reaches and maintains sufficient heat, provides reliable inactivation. This is why the FDA advisory specifically warned against both raw and "lightly cooked" green onions.

The immunological dimension is also relevant. Hepatitis A infection confers lifelong immunity in those who recover, and an effective vaccine has been available since 1995. However, vaccination rates in the United States have historically been uneven, and the 2003 outbreaks occurred before the vaccine was widely integrated into childhood immunization schedules. Adults who had never been vaccinated and who had not previously been exposed — a population that included many restaurant patrons in the affected states — were fully susceptible.

## 5. The Broader Pattern

The green onion warning is an instance of a broader pattern: legitimate FDA and USDA advisories, when circulated through informal channels, frequently take on the epistemic character of hoaxes. The pattern is structural rather than accidental. Official health advisories are, by nature, alarming — they describe a risk to public health and recommend action. When such advisories are extracted from their original context (an FDA press release, a CDC MMWR bulletin) and reformatted as forwarded emails, the framing changes from "institutional notification" to "friend-of-a-friend warning." The factual content may remain accurate, but the epistemic signal — the cue by which a recipient assesses the trustworthiness of the information — shifts.

The early-2000s email-forwarding culture was saturated with fabricated health scares: warnings about Costa Rican bananas carrying Necrotizing Fasciitis, claims that Sheryl Crow had developed breast cancer from water bottles left in hot cars, assertions that microwaving food in plastic containers released dioxins. Many of these were later catalogued and debunked by Snopes, and the very existence of such a catalogue trained a generation of internet users to treat any forwarded health warning as presumptively false. The green onion warning arrived in this environment, and was frequently filed alongside the hoaxes.

The USDA and FDA have invested substantially in advisory communication infrastructure since the early 2000s, partly in recognition of this problem. The FDA's current recall and safety alert system distributes through email subscriptions, RSS feeds, and social media accounts, reducing reliance on informal forwarding. The CDC's Foodborne Diseases Active Surveillance Network (FoodNet) provides real-time outbreak detection, and the CDC's integration with PulseNet — a national molecular subtyping network for foodborne bacterial pathogens — has dramatically improved outbreak investigation speed.

A parallel case is the 2018 romaine lettuce E. coli outbreak. In the spring and fall of 2018, multiple outbreaks of Shiga toxin-producing Escherichia coli O157:H7 were linked to romaine lettuce grown in the Yuma, Arizona region and later in the Central Coast region of California. The CDC issued advisories advising consumers to avoid all romaine lettuce during the active investigation periods (CDC, 2018, "Multistate Outbreak of E. coli O157:H7 Infections Linked to Romaine Lettuce," Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report). As with the green onion case, the advisories were genuine, but they too circulated through informal channels and generated confusion — some consumers were unsure whether the warning was current, whether it applied to all romaine or only specific sources, and whether it had been rescinded. The structural similarity to the green onion case is striking: an unfamiliar food vehicle, a serious pathogen, a broad consumer advisory, and significant confusion about scope and recency when the advisory reached consumers through non-official channels.

The romaine case also illustrates the evolution of public health communication since 2003. The CDC and FDA now use social media, press conferences, and direct retail partnerships to communicate advisories. Grocery chains can trigger checkout-screen notifications and pull product from shelves within hours of an advisory. This infrastructure was not yet in place in 2003, when the green onion advisory relied on press releases and the hope that local media and restaurant managers would relay the information.

## 6. Verdict and Gap Assessment

Snopes rated the green onion warning as "True" — the FDA did issue the advisory, the outbreaks occurred as described, and the deaths were real. This rating is correct and unambiguous. The factual claims in the original email, when traced back to their sources, are accurate.

Under the Observatory Almanac's EARC classification, this entry is rated **E — Empirically Established**. The core empirical claims (the FDA advisory, the outbreak magnitudes, the deaths, the epidemiological linkage to green onions) are documented in peer-reviewed literature, CDC surveillance reports, and contemporaneous press coverage. There is no serious dispute about the basic facts.

The gap category is **verified-public-health-alert** — a case where a circulated warning is not merely "based on a real event" but is itself an accurate reproduction of an official advisory. The gap, in this context, is not a gap in the factual record but a gap in the information ecosystem: the space between an accurate advisory and public reception of that advisory, a space that was filled with suspicion and disbelief rather than compliance.

What remains interesting about this case is the investigation outcome. The Tennessee outbreak was relatively well-traced to Mexican-origin green onions through molecular epidemiology and supply-chain analysis (Amon et al., 2005, Clinical Infectious Diseases, vol. 41, no. 6). But the Pennsylvania outbreak — the largest and most lethal — was never definitively traced to a single contamination source. The epidemiological evidence implicated green onions strongly: case-control studies showed that consumption of menu items containing green onions was significantly associated with illness, and the timing was consistent with a single contaminated batch. However, no HAV-positive green onion sample was ever recovered from the restaurant or its supply chain, and no farm-level contamination was identified. The outbreak thus stands as an example of a strong epidemiological case that falls short of definitive laboratory confirmation — a common outcome in foodborne disease investigations, where contaminated product is consumed or discarded before testing can be performed.

Ongoing food safety lessons from the green onion case include the importance of traceability in fresh produce supply chains. The Food Safety Modernization Act (FSMA), signed into law in 2011, was partly motivated by the failures of traceability exposed in outbreaks like the 2003 green onion event. The Produce Safety Rule under FSMA, finalized in 2015, established science-based standards for growing, harvesting, packing, and holding of produce. Requirements for agricultural water quality, worker hygiene, and equipment sanitation directly address the contamination pathways implicated in the green onion outbreaks. The question of whether these regulatory changes have meaningfully reduced the risk of produce-associated viral outbreaks remains empirically open — a question that surveillance data from the post-FSMA period is still accumulating the evidence to answer.

The green onion warning remains, in the end, a case study in a truth that was doubted because of how it was shared rather than because of what it said. It is a reminder that the epistemic quality of information is shaped not only by the content of the message but by the channel through which it arrives.
