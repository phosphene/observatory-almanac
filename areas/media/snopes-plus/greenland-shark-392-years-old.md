## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In August 2020, a widely circulated image purporting to show a 392-year-old Greenland shark (*Somniosus microcephalus*) resurfaced on social media, generating thousands of shares on both Facebook and Twitter. The image was accompanied by captions asserting that the individual shark photographed was approximately 392 years old—meaning the animal would have been alive since approximately 1628, predating the settlement of Boston, the founding of the Royal Society, and the publication of Newton's *Principia Mathematica* by more than half a century. Some versions of the claim escalated further, asserting the shark was 500 or even 600 years old.

Snopes investigator Madison Dapcevich (2020) rated this claim **Mixture**: the image genuinely shows a Greenland shark, a species known from peer-reviewed scientific research to live potentially hundreds of years; the claim of exceptional longevity for the species is scientifically grounded in a landmark 2016 study published in *Science*. However, the claim that the specific shark in the photograph is the 392-year-old individual described in that study—or that any individual specimen has been confirmed as exactly 392 years old—is not verifiable and arguably misleading. The age figure is an estimate with a confidence interval spanning more than two centuries, and the photographic subject's identity relative to the studied specimens remains unverified. The underlying science is robust; the specific claim about this specific photograph is unsupported.

This entry examines the 2016 *Science* study and its radiocarbon dating methodology, what the age estimates actually mean and what uncertainty they carry, the subsequent social media lifecycle of the image and the researcher's own correction attempts, and what this case reveals about the epistemics of scientific communication in the viral media environment.

## §2 The Source Study and What It Actually Found

The viral meme originated from a study published in *Science* in August 2016: Nielsen, J., et al., "Eye Lens Radiocarbon Reveals Centuries of Longevity in the Greenland Shark (*Somniosus microcephalus*)," *Science* 353(6300): 702–704. Julius Nielsen, then a Ph.D. student at the University of Copenhagen, led a team that used radiocarbon dating of eye lens proteins to estimate ages of 28 Greenland shark specimens that had been caught as bycatch over several years in the North Atlantic.

The methodology is ingenious and its validity well-understood. The crystalline lens of a vertebrate eye is metabolically inert—the proteins laid down during early embryonic development remain essentially unchanged throughout the animal's life. Radiocarbon (carbon-14) concentrations in those original proteins therefore reflect the atmospheric and oceanic radiocarbon levels at the time of the animal's birth. Because atmospheric radiocarbon was significantly elevated due to nuclear weapons testing in the 1950s and 1960s (the "bomb radiocarbon" signature), animals born after approximately 1955 have lenses with elevated C-14 values, while animals born before that period have lower values consistent with pre-bomb oceanic radiocarbon levels. By calibrating against known atmospheric and oceanic radiocarbon curves, the researchers could estimate birth years and thus ages.

The findings were striking. The 28 sharks ranged from about 2.5 feet to 16.5 feet in length. The smallest specimens showed bomb-radiocarbon signatures, placing their births after the 1950s testing. The largest specimen—approximately 16.5 feet (502 centimeters) in length—showed a radiocarbon signature consistent with a much older age, estimated at 392 years in 2016 (give or take 120 years, a 95.5% confidence interval). This means the study authors were 95.5% confident the shark was between 272 and 512 years old. The central point estimate of 392 was the median of the range, not a precise determination.

The study also showed that the species grows approximately 0.4 inches (1 centimeter) per year—an extraordinarily slow growth rate even by deep-sea standards—and that sexual maturity is not reached until approximately 150 years of age. This latter finding has significant implications for conservation, because it means that any individual Greenland shark killed before 150 years old has not yet reproduced.

As Dapcevich (2020) reported, when the meme recirculated in 2017 with claims of a 600-year-old shark, Nielsen took to Instagram to correct it directly: "We have not found any sharks to be 600 or 500 yr old... we have ESTIMATED (meaning that it has not been verified) that one shark was AT LEAST 272 yr old or in more detail that this shark was between 272-512 yr old with 95.5% certainty (the later also being an unverified estimate)."

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: What Radiocarbon Age Estimation Means, and What It Cannot Mean

The epistemic novelty in this case is the systematic misinterpretation of probabilistic age estimates as precise ages, and the collateral failure of science journalists and social media participants to preserve the uncertainty range in reporting. This is a foundational issue in science communication that this specific case illuminates with unusual clarity.

Radiocarbon dating does not produce a single number. It produces a probability distribution—in the Nielsen et al. (2016) case, a range of 272–512 years with 95.5% confidence for the largest specimen. The "392 years" figure that circulated virally was the central estimate, the median of the distribution, reported in the paper as the most likely value. In the paper itself, this was clearly accompanied by ±120 years. In all but the most careful science reporting and social media sharing, the ±120 was dropped, and "392" was treated as a precise measurement.

This is not a minor rounding error. A confidence interval of ±120 years means the shark could plausibly have been born in 1504 (Leonardo da Vinci was 52 years old, Copernicus had not yet published his heliocentric model) or in 1744 (the mid-point of the Enlightenment). The difference between a 272-year-old shark and a 512-year-old shark is not epistemically trivial—it spans an entire geological and cultural era of human history. The "392" figure confers a false precision that dramatically enhances the claim's social media appeal (a 272-to-512-year-old shark sounds plausible and boring; a precisely 392-year-old shark sounds almost mystical) while simultaneously misrepresenting the epistemological content of the finding.

Nielsen et al. (2016) explicitly discussed this uncertainty. The estimation methodology carries inherent limitations: the pre-bomb radiocarbon calibration curve for the North Atlantic has its own uncertainty, the assumption of constant growth rate (0.4 cm/year) is based on analysis of the 28-specimen dataset rather than longitudinal tracking of individual animals, and individual variation in growth within the species is not fully characterized. Campana (2016), writing a perspective piece in Science accompanying the Nielsen et al. paper ("Greenland Shark Lifespans: A Cautionary Tale for Age Estimation," *Science* 353(6300): 653), explicitly warned against overconfident interpretation of the specific age figures.

The epistemically novel point for fact-checking methodology is: when scientific claims involving probability distributions or confidence intervals are translated into single-number viral claims, the transformation is almost always a degradation of epistemic content. The Greenland shark case makes this degradation especially vivid because the confidence interval (±120 years) is more than half the central estimate (392 years)—the uncertainty is enormous relative to the estimate itself, a fact completely invisible in the viral framing.

Additional epistemic complexity: even if the specific age estimate were precise, the photograph in the meme is not identified with certainty as the largest (oldest) shark from the Nielsen et al. study. The meme's photograph is a Greenland shark image attributed to Julius Nielsen, University of Copenhagen—but Nielsen's research involved 28 specimens, and the iconic image widely circulated may or may not depict the specimen estimated to be 272–512 years old. Dapcevich (2020) reported that a reverse image search traced the photograph's earliest online appearance to 2018, two years after the study's publication, and Nielsen himself could not unambiguously confirm whether the circulating image showed the specific specimen from his study's upper age estimate.

This means the claim "this shark is 392 years old" combines (a) a species-level finding about maximum longevity in Greenland sharks, (b) a probabilistic age estimate for one individual, presented as a point estimate, and (c) a photograph that may or may not depict that individual. All three components have significant uncertainty, and the viral claim elides all three sources of uncertainty simultaneously.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Dapcevich's (2020) investigation is among the more technically thorough in the Snopes wildlife/science corpus, engaging directly with the Nielsen et al. (2016) study and representing the uncertainty structure of the age estimates reasonably well. Several threads merit extended analysis:

**The conservation significance of longevity and late sexual maturity**: The investigation discusses the remarkable longevity finding mainly as a curiosity. The conservation implications are severe and receive no attention. A species that reaches sexual maturity at approximately 150 years of age is extraordinarily vulnerable to population-level impacts from bycatch mortality. Every Greenland shark accidentally caught before age 150—a substantial proportion of any bycatch, given the age distribution of any large population—is an individual removed before contributing a single offspring to the next generation. Nielsen et al. (2016) noted this; the broader literature on CITES and North Atlantic fisheries management implications was not addressed in the fact-check.

**The status of the species post-study**: *Somniosus microcephalus* is assessed as Near Threatened by the IUCN Red List (Kulka et al., 2020, *IUCN Red List of Threatened Species*). Commercial and sport fishing for Greenland sharks has historically occurred in Greenland and Iceland. The relationship between the 2016 longevity findings and subsequent management discussions is scientifically and politically significant but not discussed.

**The bomb-radiocarbon methodology's limitations for very old specimens**: The investigation does not discuss that the bomb-radiocarbon method, while highly accurate for specimens born after approximately 1950, has substantially greater uncertainty for pre-bomb specimens. For animals born in the 17th or 18th century, the calibration curve uncertainty compounds the measurement uncertainty, and the method shifts from being primarily constraint-based (born before bomb peak vs. after) to being exclusively calibration-curve dependent. Campana (2016) flagged this limitation specifically.

**Subsequent research**: By 2020 when this article was published, no follow-on study had independently verified or extended the Nielsen et al. (2016) findings using alternative methods (otolith dating is not applicable to this species, but other biochemical approaches may be). The investigation does not address whether the 2016 findings remain the sole scientific basis for the longevity claims.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Greenland Shark Biology, Arctic Ecology, and the Science of Animal Longevity

The Greenland shark (*Somniosus microcephalus*) is one of the least studied large sharks on Earth, in part because its habitat—the deep cold waters of the North Atlantic and Arctic Ocean, typically between 200 and 2,200 meters depth—is extremely difficult to access. It can reach up to 7 meters (approximately 23 feet) in length and weigh over 1,000 kilograms, making it the largest fish native to Arctic waters (Compagno, 1984, *Sharks of the World*, FAO Species Catalogue, Vol. 4). Its flesh contains high concentrations of trimethylamine oxide (TMAO) and urea—cryoprotective compounds that prevent protein denaturation at near-freezing temperatures—rendering fresh Greenland shark meat acutely toxic to humans, dogs, and many other predators; traditional Icelandic *hákarl* (fermented shark) involves a months-long fermentation process specifically to break down these compounds (Baldursson & Kristbergsson, 2009, *Journal of the Science of Food and Agriculture*, Wiley).

Deep-sea sharks generally have slower metabolisms and longer lifespans than their shallow-water relatives. The underlying physiological basis of exceptional longevity in cold, slow-metabolism organisms has been extensively studied: low body temperature reduces the rate of molecular damage accumulation, particularly oxidative damage to DNA and proteins; the caloric restriction associated with food scarcity in deep-water environments may activate longevity-associated pathways analogous to those triggered by dietary restriction in laboratory models; and large body size is generally correlated with longer lifespan across vertebrate clades (de Magalhaes & Costa, 2009, *Journal of Evolutionary Biology*, Wiley).

The Greenland shark's maximum longevity, if confirmed at the range suggested by Nielsen et al. (2016), makes it the longest-lived vertebrate known to science—surpassing the Aldabra giant tortoise (*Aldabrachelys gigantea*, documented longevity ~255 years), the ocean quahog clam (*Arctica islandica*, documented longevity ~507 years, but an invertebrate), and the bowhead whale (*Balaena mysticetus*, estimated maximum longevity ~211 years based on ivory lance fragments found in living whales, as documented by George et al., 1999, in the *Canadian Journal of Zoology*).

The cultural fascination with exceptionally long-lived animals taps into deep human anxieties about mortality and the perceived injustice of biological age variation. This fascination explains the repeated viral spread of the Greenland shark image. As Dapcevich's (2020) investigation noted, reverse-image searches revealed recirculation dating back to at least 2018, with multiple distinct viral peaks correlating with different social media platform dynamics rather than any new scientific development.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: MIXTURE**

The circulating meme image genuinely depicts a Greenland shark (*Somniosus microcephalus*), a species whose maximum longevity has been scientifically estimated at potentially 400+ years based on radiocarbon dating of eye lens proteins in a landmark 2016 study (Nielsen et al., *Science*). The species' documented life history characteristics—extraordinarily slow growth (~1 cm/year), very late sexual maturity (~150 years), and deep-cold-water ecology—are consistent with extreme longevity. However, the specific claim that the shark in the photograph is the ~392-year-old individual from the study is unverified; the age "392" represents a central estimate with a ±120-year confidence interval (272–512 years at 95.5% confidence), not a precise measurement; and the photograph's subject has not been definitively tied to the study specimen. Claims of 500 or 600 years were explicitly corrected by the study's lead author.

**EARC Classification: E (Epistemic — probability/uncertainty representation gap)**

The primary analytical gap is epistemic: the claim requires not only verifying the image's provenance and the study's conclusions but understanding the uncertainty structure of radiocarbon age estimation and the difference between a central point estimate and a verified precise age. The systematic collapse of ±120 years of uncertainty into a single memorable number ("392 years old") represents a recurring pattern in science communication misinformation where statistical imprecision is stripped away in the translation from academic paper to viral claim.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Whether the specific photograph circulating virally depicts the study's largest/oldest specimen or another individual
- The current conservation status of *Somniosus microcephalus* and any management changes attributable to the longevity findings
- Independent verification of the Nielsen et al. (2016) age estimates using alternative dating methods
- The full uncertainty in pre-bomb radiocarbon calibration curves as they apply to ages in the 272–512-year range
