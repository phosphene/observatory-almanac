## §1 — Claim and Verdict

A viral social media image, widely circulated on Facebook, TikTok, and X from at least 2022 onward, claimed that cricket powder — ground-up house crickets (*Acheta domesticus*) — was being covertly added to common food products including chips, biscuits, grain bars, protein bars, some breads, and pastas, under the ingredient name "Acheta protein" or "Acheta powder," and that this practice was "being promoted as a good thing." The framing positioned consumers as unknowing participants in a food system that was substituting insect protein for conventional ingredients without adequate transparency.

Snopes fact-checker Jack Izzo investigated the claim and rated it **"Research In Progress"** in May 2024. The investigation confirmed three sub-claims and left a fourth open:

1. **TRUE**: Cricket powder exists and is sold and marketed as "Acheta protein" or "Acheta powder."
2. **TRUE (partially)**: Some specialty food brands openly use cricket powder in chips, protein bars, and related products.
3. **UNCONFIRMED**: The claim that cricket powder is "commonly" used in mainstream food products — i.e., in products from major manufacturers — could not be verified. Snopes contacted several large U.S. food manufacturers and had not received responses at time of publication.
4. **TRUE (with context)**: Cricket powder is positively promoted in sustainability and food innovation discourse.

This entry provides an extended analysis of the entomophagy movement, the regulatory and labelling framework for insect-derived food ingredients in the United States, the allergenicity implications, and the political economy of food conspiracy narratives.

## §2 — Viral Spread and Origin

The viral image traced by Snopes originated on iFunny (user @NotAGlobe) and propagated through Facebook, TikTok, and Twitter/X. The claim spread across multiple years and language communities — Snopes identifies posts from at least 2022–2024 as part of the same claim cluster.

The specific framing — "you don't know you're eating crickets unless you read the label" — activates two well-documented anxiety vectors: (1) loss of control over one's food inputs, and (2) cross-contamination of clean/natural food categories with insect matter, which carries strong disgust-based rejection in Western food cultures. The phrase "it's being promoted as a good thing" implicitly invokes a conspiratorial dynamic in which institutional promoters (presumably food corporations, governments, or international bodies) are advancing an agenda at odds with ordinary consumer preferences.

The claim's cultural context is significant. From approximately 2020 onward, a subset of Western political discourse — particularly in right-wing media — framed insect protein promotion as part of a broader elite agenda linked to the World Economic Forum's "eat bugs" discourse (connected to WEF sustainability recommendations), European Union regulatory approvals of insect foods, and the broader "Great Reset" conspiratorial narrative. The "Acheta powder" claim functions within this broader narrative framework, providing a specific, nameable ingredient that listeners can identify on labels as evidence of covert insect introduction into the food supply.

## §3 — Evidence and Epistemic Analysis

### What Acheta Powder Is and How It Is Used

*Acheta domesticus* is the house cricket, the most commercially farmed edible insect species globally. "Acheta powder" (also "Acheta protein") is the ingredient name for flour produced by grinding dried house crickets. The name derives from the genus *Acheta*.

The powder is commercially produced by companies including Aspire Food Group, All Things Bugs, and several Canadian producers, and is used as an ingredient in:

- Specialty protein bars (EXO Protein uses "Acheta protein" without consumer-facing explanation of its origin)
- Cricket-themed snack chips (Chirps brand openly markets cricket content)
- Specialty pasta and bread from artisanal producers
- Protein supplement powders

Critically, the brands that openly use Acheta powder — Chirps, and similar companies — are niche health-food and sustainability-focused brands with a transparent consumer proposition. These are not the large mainstream manufacturers (Frito-Lay, General Mills, Pepperidge Farm) that the viral claim implies.

Snopes contacted several large U.S. manufacturers directly to ask whether they used cricket powder and received no responses. This silence is ambiguous — it could reflect absence of such use, legal caution about disclosures, or simply slow corporate communications. The investigation leaves the "mainstream use" claim materially unresolved.

### FDA Regulatory Classification of Insect-Derived Ingredients

The FDA's regulatory framework for edible insects is structurally complex. Snopes' investigation outlines the functional situation:

- **Whole edible insects**: Must be raised explicitly for human consumption (analogous to food animals), under CGMP regulations and the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FD&C Act).
- **Cricket powder (Acheta powder)**: Classified as a **protein powder** or **dietary supplement ingredient** under FDA guidance, with slightly different regulatory requirements than whole insects or conventional foods.

The FDA spokesperson statement provided to Snopes affirms the FD&C Act applicability: all insect-derived food ingredients must be safe, not prepared under insanitary conditions, and labelled with their common or usual names. This last requirement is particularly important: ingredients in food must be identified by their "common or usual name" in the ingredient statement. The FDA's position therefore implies that "Acheta powder" or "Acheta protein" are acceptable ingredient designations — provided they accurately describe the ingredient.

However, the FDA does not currently have an explicit GRAS (Generally Recognised as Safe) determination for Acheta powder in the published GRAS database (as of the Snopes investigation). The absence of a formal GRAS determination means manufacturers are operating on a self-affirmed GRAS basis or under novel food ingredient frameworks — a common situation for novel food ingredients in the U.S. system.

The European Union, by contrast, has an explicit novel food authorisation process. The EU authorised *Acheta domesticus* as a food ingredient in November 2021 (Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/882), permitting use in specified food categories.

### Allergenicity: The Cross-Reactivity with Shellfish

The Snopes investigation identifies the shellfish cross-reactivity issue as the primary commercial disincentive for mainstream food manufacturers to adopt cricket powder. This is a critical point that deserves careful elaboration.

The exoskeleton of crickets — like the exoskeletons of crustaceans (shrimp, lobster, crab) — is composed primarily of **chitin**, a long-chain polysaccharide that is the primary structural material in arthropod exoskeletons. Additionally, both crickets and shellfish contain **tropomyosin**, a muscle protein that is a dominant allergen in shellfish-allergic individuals.

Shellfish is one of the FDA's "Big Nine" major food allergens (the others are milk, eggs, fish, tree nuts, peanuts, wheat, soybeans, and sesame), requiring mandatory label declaration under the Food Allergen Labeling and Consumer Protection Act of 2004 (FALCPA). Cricket-derived ingredients are not currently listed as major food allergens under FALCPA, but cross-reactivity creates a real safety risk for shellfish-allergic consumers.

The peer-reviewed immunology literature on cricket allergenicity includes:

- **Srinroch, C., Srisomsap, C., Chokchaichamnankit, D., Punyarit, P., & Dhepakson, P. (2015). Identification of novel allergen in edible insect, Gryllus bimaculatus and its cross-reactivity with Macrobrachium spp. allergens. *Food Chemistry*, 184, 160–166.** This study identified cross-reactive IgE-binding components between cricket species and shrimp in sensitised patients, providing direct laboratory evidence for the cross-reactivity hypothesis.

- **Broekman, H. C. H. P., Knulst, A. C., de Jong, G. A. H., Gaspari, M., den Hartog Jager, C. F., Houben, G. F., & Verhoeckx, K. C. M. (2017). Effect of thermal processing on mealworm allergenicity. *PLOS ONE*, 12(9), e0184163.** While focused on mealworms (*Tenebrio molitor*) rather than crickets, this study demonstrated that thermal processing reduces but does not eliminate edible insect allergenicity — directly relevant to risk assessment for cricket powder in processed foods.

- **Food Allergy Canada (2022)** — Mythbuster article confirmed by a Canadian allergy advocacy organisation that people with crustacean allergies may have elevated risk when eating crickets and other edible insects.

The allergy risk means that mainstream manufacturers have strong legal and liability incentives to avoid incorporating cricket powder into products not marketed to allergy-aware consumers. A mislabelling incident causing anaphylaxis in a shellfish-allergic consumer would be both a regulatory violation and a tort liability. This creates a structural commercial barrier to covert mainstream adoption — the opposite of what the viral claim implies.

### Epistemic Novelty: The Political Economy of Entomophobia and Food Conspiracy

The viral Acheta powder claim exists within a specific political and cultural frame that deserves analysis as a phenomenon in its own right. Western food cultures — particularly Northern European and North American — have historically maintained strong **neophobia** toward insect consumption, a pattern documented in ethnographic and behavioural nutrition literature.

Rozin, P., & Fallon, A. E. (1987). A perspective on disgust. *Psychological Review*, 94(1), 23–41 — the foundational empirical study of food disgust — demonstrated that insects occupy a "disgust" category in Western food psychology that is resistant to informational override. The disgust response to insects is rooted in evolved pathogen-avoidance mechanisms and is not substantially reduced by rational arguments about safety or nutrition. This means that insect food products face a perceptual barrier that is categorically different from, say, novel plant-based proteins.

The conspiracy framing of the Acheta powder claim activates disgust psychology in a politically directed way: rather than simply expressing aversion to eating bugs, the claim positions the consumer as a victim of institutional deception. This adds moral outrage to disgust, creating a more potent and shareable emotional cocktail.

More specifically, the claim resonates with post-2020 conspiracy narratives about the WEF and food system transformation. The WEF published a 2022 report noting that insects could play a role in sustainable food systems. In the populist media ecosystem, this was processed as an elite plan to force consumers to eat insects against their will. The "Acheta powder in your chips" claim serves as perceived evidence of this agenda being implemented covertly.

Research on how sustainability-oriented food innovation becomes politicised is a growing field. Hartmann, C., & Siegrist, M. (2017). Consumer perception and behaviour regarding sustainable protein consumption: A systematic review. *Trends in Food Science & Technology*, 61, 11–25 — reviews the literature on consumer resistance to novel protein sources and identifies disgust, naturalness concerns, and information transparency as key barriers.

## §4 — Entomophagy: The Science and the Case

### Nutritional Profile of Cricket Powder

The nutritional case for cricket powder is well-supported in peer-reviewed literature:

- **Zafar, A., Mughal, R., Naveed, M., Sajid, I., & Farooq, A. (2024). Unraveling the nutritional, biofunctional, and sustainable food application of edible crickets: A comprehensive review. *Trends in Food Science & Technology*, 143, 104254.** This comprehensive review documents that *Acheta domesticus* contains approximately 60–70% protein by dry weight, with a complete essential amino acid profile comparable to conventional animal proteins. It also contains omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids, vitamin B12, iron, zinc, and the prebiotic fibre chitin.

- **Larouche, J., et al. (2023). The edible insect sector in Canada and the United States. *Animal Frontiers*, 13(4), 16–25.** Documents the commercial landscape of edible insect production in North America, noting that the sector remains dominated by specialty and health-food channels rather than mainstream food manufacturing.

Additionally cited in the Snopes investigation: research linking chitin (the structural fibre in cricket exoskeletons) to anti-inflammatory properties and gut microbiome modulation. This benefit has been reported in emerging literature, though the clinical evidence base is still developing.

### Environmental Sustainability

Cricket farming is documented as significantly more resource-efficient than conventional livestock agriculture:

- Crickets require approximately 2 kg of feed per kg of body weight gain, compared to 8 kg for beef cattle (van Huis, A., et al., 2013, *Edible Insects: Future Prospects for Food and Feed Security*, FAO Report 171).
- Greenhouse gas emissions from cricket production are estimated at 1–2.7% of equivalent beef production emissions.
- Water use is estimated at less than 5% of beef water requirements.

These environmental advantages are directly relevant to understanding why cricket powder is "being promoted as a good thing" — as the viral claim notes. The promotion is not covert or ideologically motivated; it is grounded in documented resource efficiency comparison data.

## §6 — EARC Classification, Research Gap, and Implications

### EARC Classification

- **Empirical (E): YES** — The claim has multiple empirically testable sub-claims. The existence of Acheta powder is confirmed; the prevalence in mainstream foods is unconfirmed; the allergenicity profile is documented in peer-reviewed literature.
- **Amplified (A): YES** — The claim's viral spread is driven by disgust amplification, conspiratorial framing (covert elite food agenda), and the emotionally powerful combination of moral outrage and physical aversion.
- **Recurring (R): YES** — The claim has been circulating since at least 2022 and persists because its underlying cultural drivers — entomophobia, food sovereignty anxieties, WEF-adjacent conspiracy narratives — remain active and growing in the target demographics.
- **Compound (C): YES** — The claim contains three sub-claims with distinct truth values: (1) Acheta powder exists and is in some foods — *True*; (2) it is "commonly" in many mainstream foods — *Unverified/Probably False*; (3) labelling practices are deceptive — *Mixed* (EXO Protein labels accurately but without prominent disclosure; specialty brands are transparent; counterfeit or misleading labelling in mainstream products not documented).

### Research Gap

The central gap is the absence of market survey data on Acheta powder prevalence in mainstream retail food products. A systematic survey of U.S. grocery store products — checking ingredient lists of chip, cracker, and protein bar categories against cricket-derived ingredients — would resolve the "commonly added" claim with high confidence. This is a relatively straightforward empirical question that Snopes had neither the resources nor the access to answer as a journalism organisation.

The secondary gap is the immunology literature on cricket allergenicity risk, which Snopes references anecdotally (FSIS allergen overview, Food Allergy Canada) without citing primary allergenicity studies. This omission is significant because the allergen risk is the most practically important consumer safety point.

A tertiary gap is the political-cultural analysis of why insect food claims have become so virulent in the current media environment. Understanding the relationship between entomophagy promotion, WEF narrative constructs, and viral food conspiracy claims would be a valuable contribution to the political sociology of food.

### Implications

The Acheta powder claim illustrates how genuine food system innovation can be captured by conspiracy epistemology and reframed as covert corporate-institutional deception. The factual core of the claim — cricket powder exists, is nutritionally valuable, and is used in some products — is entirely true. The conspiracy framing — it's in everything, you're eating it without knowing, and powerful actors are pushing it against your will — is largely false or unverified. The gap between the factual core and the paranoid framing is precisely the space where media literacy interventions are most needed.

For consumers with shellfish allergies, the practical advice is clear: read ingredient labels, and be aware that cricket-derived ingredients (Acheta powder, Acheta protein) may trigger reactions. In the U.S. mainstream food supply, such ingredients are not known to appear in major brand products, and regulatory labelling requirements mean that any product containing them would be required to list them. The claim that consumers are being systematically deceived about insect ingredients is not supported by available evidence.
