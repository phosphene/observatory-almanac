# Teaching growth mindset significantly improves academic achievement

## 1. The Claim

Few ideas have moved from academic psychology into mass educational practice as rapidly and thoroughly as Carol Dweck's growth mindset theory. In the years since her 2006 book *Mindset: The New Psychology of Success* reached a popular audience, the theory has been adopted by school districts in dozens of countries, embedded in teacher training programs, featured in TED Talks watched by millions, and incorporated into corporate leadership development curricula worldwide.

The core claim, reduced to its most actionable form, is: if you teach students that intelligence is not a fixed trait but something that can be developed through effort and learning, they will perform better academically. Students with a "growth mindset" — the belief that their abilities can grow — outperform students with a "fixed mindset" — the belief that intelligence is innate and unchangeable. And crucially, interventions that shift students' mindsets from fixed to growth can produce measurable improvements in academic outcomes.

This claim was not only compelling in its logic but appeared, in early studies, to be supported by experimental evidence. The question that subsequent research has forced open is: how large are the effects, for whom do they appear, and does the intervention work at scale?

## 2. What's Actually True

The evidentiary landscape for growth mindset is more complicated than either enthusiastic advocates or dismissive critics have acknowledged.

**The foundational research was real:** Dweck's initial work, extending back to her early career research with Martin Seligman and continued through decades of her own studies, documented genuine and theoretically coherent findings. The distinction between mastery and performance goals, and between entity and incremental theories of intelligence, is well-supported by a substantial body of laboratory and classroom research. Blackwell, Trzesniewski, and Dweck (2007) conducted a longitudinal study of seventh-graders in which a growth mindset intervention improved mathematics grades relative to a control group. This was peer-reviewed, published in *Child Development*, and had a plausible theoretical mechanism. The foundation of the theory is not pseudoscience.

**Early enthusiasm outpaced evidence:** The translation from careful academic research to educational policy happened faster than the evidence warranted. The effect sizes in initial studies were often substantial by psychological standards, and the theory's intuitive appeal — hard work matters, effort is productive, students can grow — aligned with powerful cultural values in both the US and UK education contexts where it was most aggressively adopted. Districts and schools implemented growth mindset programs at scale without waiting for the kind of large, pre-registered replications that would establish whether effects were robust across contexts and populations.

**The replication results:** When researchers began conducting large-scale replications, the results were substantially more modest. Sisk, Burgoyne, Sun, Butler, and Macnamara (2018) conducted a meta-analysis of 43 studies examining growth mindset and academic achievement, finding an overall weighted effect size of *d* = 0.10 — extremely small by any standard, and smaller than the effect sizes in many of the original studies. The effect was near zero for students from high socioeconomic backgrounds and slightly larger for students facing socioeconomic disadvantage, but even there the effect was modest.

Li and Bates (2017, 2019) conducted large pre-registered studies specifically testing growth mindset interventions and found no significant effect on academic achievement in multiple samples. Their work attracted significant controversy, including a response from Dweck's lab, but the findings were robust within their design parameters.

**The Yeager et al. (2019) qualified validation:** A major study published in *Nature*, led by David Yeager and including Dweck, produced a more optimistic picture — but with important conditions. In a large, nationally representative US sample of ninth-graders, the growth mindset intervention had an average effect size of *d* = 0.10 on GPA (consistent with the Sisk meta-analysis), but the effect was substantially larger for lower-achieving students in schools with a peer norm supporting the growth mindset. The interaction with context was the key finding: effects were concentrated in students who were already academically struggling and in school environments where the intervention message was reinforced by the culture. For higher-achieving students in high-achievement contexts, effects were negligible.

**What the evidence actually supports:** Growth mindset interventions produce small average effects that are real but modest. Effects are concentrated among lower-achieving students and may require supportive institutional contexts to manifest. The broad claim that teaching growth mindset "significantly improves academic achievement" across the student population is not supported by the evidence. The more defensible claim — that targeted growth mindset interventions can modestly benefit some struggling students in supportive contexts — is accurate but considerably less sweeping.

## 3. Why People Believe This

Growth mindset theory's spread from carefully conducted psychology research to near-universal educational policy prescription is a case study in how ideas move from science to practice, and how that movement can distort findings in systematic ways.

**The theory is intuitively correct about something important:** The basic insight that students' beliefs about their own abilities affect their effort, persistence, and response to failure is well-supported. Students who believe failure is permanent and reflects fixed incapacity are less likely to persist after setbacks than students who believe failure is information about what to work on next. This is real and has genuine implications for educational practice. The problem is not that the insight is wrong but that the magnitude of the intervention effect is far smaller than the insight's intuitive importance would suggest.

**The mechanism story is compelling:** Growth mindset theory has a clear, communicable mechanism: fixed mindset → avoids challenges, gives up easily, threatened by others' success; growth mindset → embraces challenges, persists, learns from failure. This mechanism story is teachable, trainable, and fits into existing educational values about effort, resilience, and grit. It slots into a broader movement in education psychology emphasizing non-cognitive skills alongside academic content knowledge. The clarity of the mechanism made the theory extremely adoptable.

**Publication bias and the file drawer problem:** Early studies of growth mindset interventions were disproportionately likely to show positive effects, not necessarily because the effects didn't exist but because journals disproportionately publish positive findings and researchers with null results have less incentive to write them up. The published literature in the pre-replication-crisis era systematically overrepresented positive results. The studies that formed the initial evidence base for growth mindset's efficacy were likely biased toward success by these structural factors.

**The educational policy adoption cycle:** Educational interventions have a distinctive adoption cycle in which promising preliminary findings get adopted broadly before large-scale testing is complete. This is partly understandable — children are in school now, not in some future year when research is complete — and partly a product of the incentive structures facing district administrators, consultants, and publishers. Growth mindset became a commercial as well as a research enterprise. There are growth mindset curricula, professional development programs, books, and assessment tools. This commercial ecosystem has interests in the theory's success that are independent of the evidence.

**Dweck's public engagement:** Dweck became one of the most visible public-facing psychologists of her generation. *Mindset* was an accessible, well-written trade book that reached audiences far beyond the classroom. Her TED Talk (2014) has tens of millions of views. This public engagement substantially amplified the theory's reach, but it also meant that the nuanced caveats appropriate in academic discourse were often lost in translation to popular audiences. "Mindset matters" became "growth mindset interventions dramatically improve achievement," a simplification that crossed a threshold of accuracy.

**The appeal to educational equity:** One particularly powerful framing was that growth mindset interventions could close achievement gaps by changing beliefs rather than requiring expensive structural changes. The idea that a cheap, scalable psychological intervention could improve outcomes for disadvantaged students is enormously attractive to policymakers. This framing drove adoption even in contexts where evidence for broad efficacy was weak, because the hope of an inexpensive equity lever is compelling in resource-constrained educational systems.

## 4. Verdict

**CONTESTED** — but with a clear asymmetry.

The strong version of the claim — that teaching growth mindset significantly improves academic achievement broadly — is not supported by the evidence. Large-scale meta-analyses find small overall effect sizes, and pre-registered replications have found effects near zero in many samples. The widespread adoption of growth mindset programs as a broadly effective educational intervention has outpaced what the evidence actually supports.

The weaker, qualified version — that growth mindset interventions can produce modest benefits for some lower-achieving students in contextually supportive environments — has better evidentiary support, including the Yeager et al. (2019) *Nature* paper. This is meaningfully different from the broad claims in the popular literature.

The debate between Dweck's research group and critics like Li and Bates is technically unresolved in some particulars, and the field has not converged on a definitive final answer. What has emerged clearly from the replication period is that early enthusiasm for growth mindset as a broadly transformative educational tool was not warranted by the evidence available at the time, and that more careful and conditional claims are appropriate given what we now know.

This does not mean growth mindset theory is pseudoscience or that there is no value in helping students develop positive beliefs about their own capacity to learn. It means the effect sizes are smaller than commonly believed, the conditions for effects to appear are more specific than typically communicated, and the investment in growth mindset programs at scale has probably not produced returns proportional to the enthusiasm invested.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Growth mindset sits at the intersection of psychology's replication crisis, educational policy's tendency to adopt promising interventions prematurely, and the complex political economy of psychological research in the social media age.

**The replication crisis in educational psychology:** Psychology more broadly has experienced significant upheaval over findings that did not replicate. Growth mindset is far from the only casualty — power posing (Amy Cuddy), ego depletion (Baumeister), priming effects (Bargh), and stereotype threat (various) have all experienced substantial replication difficulties. Educational psychology is particularly vulnerable because the gap between laboratory conditions and real classrooms is large, sample sizes in school-based research are often constrained by practical and ethical limits, and effect sizes tend to shrink as research moves from controlled lab conditions to messy real-world implementation.

**The pre-registration movement:** One constructive response to the replication crisis has been the adoption of pre-registration — researchers publicly committing to hypotheses, sample sizes, and analysis plans before data collection. Pre-registered growth mindset studies have tended to show smaller effects than non-pre-registered studies, which is consistent with publication bias having inflated the earlier literature. The development of pre-registration norms in educational psychology represents genuine scientific progress.

**Implementation quality and theory:** One persistent question in the growth mindset debate is whether failed replications represent a failure of the underlying theory or a failure of implementation. Dweck and colleagues have argued that many interventions that "test" growth mindset are low-quality implementations of the theory — brief, poorly tailored to context, not embedded in a supportive school culture. Critics respond that this "no true scotsman" argument makes the theory unfalsifiable: positive results confirm the theory, negative results demonstrate poor implementation. This is a legitimate methodological tension that the field has not fully resolved.

**Grit, character, and non-cognitive skills:** Growth mindset is part of a broader movement in educational policy and psychology emphasizing "non-cognitive skills" — grit (Angela Duckworth), self-control, growth mindset, social-emotional learning. The replication record of this broader movement is mixed. Grit has faced many of the same replication challenges as growth mindset. This suggests that the appeal of simple, teachable psychological variables that predict achievement is generating a genre of research with systematic optimism bias, rather than being a property of any specific theory within the genre.

**What does work for educational achievement:** The educational research literature, while contested on the specifics of mindset interventions, is more settled on some broader points. Socioeconomic factors — family income, neighborhood poverty, parental education — are large predictors of academic outcomes and are not substantially addressable by psychological interventions alone. Within schools, evidence for high-quality tutoring, structured literacy instruction, and reduction of chronic absenteeism is considerably stronger than for mindset interventions. Teacher effectiveness is among the most powerful in-school predictors of student outcomes. The political appeal of mindset interventions is partly that they are cheap and avoid implicating structural or resource inequalities; but the evidence base for those interventions is weaker than for interventions that require more substantial investment.

**The irreducibility of belief and learning:** Despite the evidentiary difficulties, there is something genuinely right at the core of growth mindset theory that is worth preserving. How students think about their own intelligence and capacity does influence their behavior, particularly in the face of failure. Students who catastrophize failure as evidence of permanent incapacity do learn less than students who can process failure as information. Teachers who communicate to students that they are capable of learning difficult material, and who design assessments and feedback to support that message, are doing something that matters. The question is not whether this is true but whether structured growth mindset *interventions* are the mechanism by which it is best achieved — and on that question, the evidence is genuinely uncertain.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Growth mindset's status as a contested research question — not a clear hoax or a false claim but a scientific debate about effect sizes and conditions — places it in a difficult category for mainstream fact-checking operations.

**Why it resists standard fact-checking:** Growth mindset is not false information in the way that a fabricated quote or a misrepresented statistic is false. It is a scientific finding whose effect size is disputed and whose practical significance in educational settings is contested among experts with access to the same evidence. Fact-checkers are generally not equipped to adjudicate disputes between research groups about the magnitude of experimental effects. Standard true/false or rating-scale formats don't capture the epistemological structure of "small effects in specific conditions, not the broad transformation claimed in popular discourse."

**What responsible coverage looks like:** The best journalism on the growth mindset debate — including pieces in *The Atlantic*, *The Spectator*, and *Education Week* — has distinguished carefully between the core theoretical findings (real but modest), the popular discourse about those findings (substantially overstated), and the meta-analytic picture (effect sizes much smaller than initial studies suggested). This coverage has also been appropriately careful not to swing to the opposite error of claiming growth mindset is entirely without effect.

**The Dweck-critics exchange:** The back-and-forth between Dweck's lab and critics in the psychological literature has been more contentious than typical academic debate, with responses, rejoinders, and disputes about methodology playing out in a way that made the scientific disagreement visible to the public. This transparency is in some ways healthy — it models that science involves contestation — but it also created a media environment in which "growth mindset works" and "growth mindset is debunked" became available as competing headlines, making accurate contextualization harder.

**The "contested consensus" framing:** This entry's gap category — contested consensus — captures the key challenge. There is a loose consensus that some effect exists in some conditions; there is genuine dispute about effect size, conditions, and practical significance. Claims at either extreme (universally effective vs. completely ineffective) are both wrong. The honest characterization is that the evidence supports modest, conditional effects and does not support the strong popular claims about transformative impact on academic achievement. Observers should be skeptical of growth mindset programs sold as broad-spectrum academic improvement tools, while remaining open to the possibility that well-designed, context-sensitive implementations may offer real benefits to specific student populations.

**The broader lesson for educational policy:** Growth mindset's trajectory illustrates a recurrent pattern: a psychologically plausible, intuitively compelling theory generates promising early results, attracts enormous institutional investment and popular enthusiasm, and then runs into the replication wall. The appropriate policy lesson is not that educational psychology is useless but that the standard for widespread adoption should require large-scale, pre-registered, independently conducted replication before significant educational resources are committed. The current system — in which the gap between "promising finding in initial studies" and "adopted by thousands of schools" can be measured in months rather than years — systematically overinvests in interventions that turn out to have small or conditional effects.
