## §1 — The Claim and Its Social Context

In mid-March 2020, as COVID-19 spread globally and hand sanitizer became both a public health necessity and a scarce commodity, a Facebook post garnered more than 300,000 shares with the alarming assertion that hand sanitizer "has the same ingredients as antifreeze" and could therefore kill pets if they licked a recently sanitized hand. The post framed this as an urgent warning to pet owners who were increasing their use of alcohol-based hand sanitizers amid the pandemic.

The psychological conditions for viral misinformation were near-ideal. Hand sanitizer was suddenly ubiquitous in homes where it had previously been absent or rare. Concern for companion animal health was heightened — many people were spending more time at home with pets due to lockdowns and were therefore more attuned to potential hazards. And the claim had just enough surface plausibility to bypass quick skeptical assessment: antifreeze really does kill pets, ethylene glycol really is extremely dangerous to animals, and the word "antifreeze" carries potent negative connotations.

The Snopes fact-check by Alex Kasprak correctly identified the claim as False. The specific toxic mechanism claimed — ethylene glycol in hand sanitizer — does not exist. Modern hand sanitizers are formulated with either ethanol (ethyl alcohol) or isopropanol (isopropyl alcohol) as their active antimicrobial agents, not ethylene glycol. The fear was real; the chemistry was wrong.

This entry examines the toxicology in detail, the broader epidemiology of pet poisoning from household products, and the cognitive dynamics by which a genuine hazard (antifreeze) was mapped onto an irrelevant product (hand sanitizer).

## §2 — The Evidence Record

### 2.1 Hand Sanitizer Formulation: What It Actually Contains

Alcohol-based hand sanitizers registered with the FDA are formulated to contain either ethanol (60–95% concentration) or isopropanol (60–80% concentration) as the primary active ingredient (FDA, 2020, Temporary Policy for Preparation of Certain Alcohol-Based Hand Sanitizer Products During the Public Health Emergency). These alcohols kill bacteria and enveloped viruses — including SARS-CoV-2 — through protein denaturation and membrane disruption.

The inactive ingredients typically include humectants such as glycerol (glycerin), water, hydrogen peroxide (in WHO-recommended formulations), and fragrance compounds in commercial products. Ethylene glycol is not an ingredient in any FDA-registered hand sanitizer formulation. The American Journal of Health-System Pharmacy published a case study involving a hospitalized patient with alcohol use disorder who ingested hand sanitizer (Bookstaver et al., 2008), documenting the risks of ethanol toxicity from direct ingestion — entirely distinct from ambient exposure through hand contact.

### 2.2 Ethylene Glycol: The Actual Antifreeze Hazard

Ethylene glycol (*C₂H₆O₂*) is the primary active ingredient in most automotive antifreeze and coolant products. It is acutely toxic to mammals — including dogs, cats, and humans — through a metabolic pathway in which the liver converts ethylene glycol into glycolic acid and then oxalic acid metabolites. In cats, as few as 1.5 mL/kg constitutes a lethal dose; in dogs, the lethal dose is approximately 4.4 mL/kg (Connally et al., 2010, Journal of Veterinary Emergency and Critical Care). The mechanism is renal tubular obstruction by calcium oxalate crystal formation, leading to acute kidney failure within 24–72 hours of ingestion.

The U.S. Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry (ATSDR, 2010) has documented ethylene glycol as a serious public health concern particularly for companion animals, partly because the compound has a deceptively sweet taste that makes it palatable to pets and children who might encounter spilled antifreeze in driveways or garages.

The confusion in the viral post appears to have arisen from superficial category similarity: both antifreeze and hand sanitizer are clear liquids with chemical names that overlap in lay usage ("alcohol-based"), both are common household products, and both carry genuine toxicity risks if ingested in quantity. But these similarities are at the level of broad categorical association, not shared chemical composition.

### 2.3 The Actual Risk: Ethanol Toxicity from Hand Sanitizer

While the claimed mechanism (ethylene glycol poisoning) is chemically impossible, there is a real but categorically different risk from hand sanitizer: ethanol toxicity in pets who directly ingest the product. The ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center lists alcohol as one of the top household toxins for dogs (ASPCA, 2021, Top Pet Toxins of the Year). Isopropanol is approximately twice as toxic as ethanol on a per-weight basis.

However, the exposure pathway described in the viral warning — a pet licking the skin of a human who recently applied hand sanitizer — represents a vanishingly small dose. Standard hand sanitizer application involves approximately 1–3 mL of product. After 30 seconds of evaporation (which is what kills pathogens), the residual alcohol on the skin surface is minimal. A dog or cat licking such a surface would ingest a fraction of a milliliter of alcohol, far below any clinically significant threshold. For a 10 kg dog, lethal ethanol dose is approximately 5.5 g/kg (roughly 5.5 g × 10 = 55 g of pure alcohol, equivalent to several hundred milliliters of hand sanitizer). Hand-licking exposure represents essentially zero clinical risk.

The genuine risk scenario would be a pet directly accessing and consuming significant quantities of hand sanitizer from a bottle — which represents a distinct hazard for households with curious dogs, but is unrelated to the claimed mechanism of secondary contact via human skin.

### 2.4 Spread and Social Epidemiology of the Claim

The Facebook post that achieved 300,000+ shares circulated from approximately March 15, 2020 — the same week that U.S. demand for hand sanitizer surged to unprecedented levels and shortages were widely reported. The timing positioned the claim within an anxiety context that made pet-protective vigilance highly salient to recipients. VCA Hospitals published accessible documentation of ethylene glycol poisoning in dogs that remains widely referenced in pet owner communities (VCA Hospitals, 2019), providing what appeared to be confirmatory context when encountered in web searches by concerned owners who had seen the viral post.

## §3 — Scientific Context and Epistemic Novelty

### 3.1 The Chemistry Confusion: A Structural Analysis

The viral claim exemplifies what might be called "toxicological category collapse" — a misinformation pattern in which genuine toxicity knowledge from one compound is transferred to a different compound via superficial label similarity. In this case, the relevant nodes of false association were:

1. Both products are "chemical liquids used in everyday settings"
2. Both have colloquial descriptions involving "alcohol" in lay use (antifreeze is sometimes described as ethylene glycol, which contains hydroxyl -OH groups; alcohols have -OH groups; therefore, antifreeze "contains alcohol")
3. Both have documented toxicity risks to pets

This category collapse is distinct from standard misinformation generating a false claim from nothing — it represents misapplication of accurate factual knowledge to an incorrect target. Lewandowsky and Ecker (2011, Applied Cognitive Psychology) argue that this type of misinformation is particularly resistant to correction because the underlying factual knowledge nodes (antifreeze kills pets; alcohol products can be toxic) are correctly held and simply incorrectly linked.

### 3.2 Pet Toxicology: Gaps in Public Knowledge Infrastructure

The episode revealed structural inadequacies in how pet toxicology information reaches lay audiences. The ASPCA's Animal Poison Control Center receives approximately 400,000 calls annually (ASPCA, 2020 annual report), making companion animal poisoning a major public health adjacency. However, authoritative, searchable, rapidly accessible information about household product safety for pet owners remains fragmented — distributed across veterinary databases, website FAQ pages, and poison control hotlines rather than being consolidated in a form optimized for rapid public-facing retrieval during viral misinformation events.

**Epistemic novelty**: No prior systematic review has analyzed how viral pet-safety misinformation specifically exploits the cognitive distance between actual pet toxins (organically understood by pet owners) and claimed novel toxins introduced via social media. The 2020 hand sanitizer episode represents a case where the correction infrastructure — veterinary toxicology databases, poison control hotlines, ASPCA resources — existed but was not optimized for social-media-speed rebuttal. The Snopes fact-check served as a de facto rapid response mechanism that likely prevented veterinary care system burden from pet owners presenting animals exhibiting no actual symptoms. This is an underappreciated public health contribution of fact-checking during high-fear viral misinformation events.

### 3.3 Comparative Analysis: Other COVID-Era Pet Health Claims

The hand sanitizer claim was one of several pet-health warnings that circulated during the COVID-19 pandemic. Others included claims that household disinfectants containing quaternary ammonium compounds posed unique dangers to cats (partially true — certain concentrations of quats are toxic to felines; see Gershoff & Gottlieb, 1964, Journal of Nutrition), that pets could contract COVID-19 from human owners (confirmed possible in both dogs and cats via PCR testing in multiple countries; see Shi et al., 2020, Science), and that essential oil diffusers were acutely dangerous to birds (true — many essential oils are highly toxic to avian species at ambient inhalation levels; see Lightfoot, 2007, Proceedings of the Association of Avian Veterinarians). The hand sanitizer claim stands apart from these in having a completely false chemical mechanism, while many of the others had partial or contextual truth.

## §4 — Amplification Analysis

The claim achieved exceptional viral reach via several amplification pathways:

**Emotional salience**: The framing combined pet safety anxiety (a highly activating emotional topic) with pandemic anxiety (already at peak levels in mid-March 2020), producing a doubly charged shareable.

**Action prescription**: Posts that include a clear action directive ("if you use hand sanitizer, warn your friends") spread faster than descriptive posts because they give recipients a socially productive outlet for their concern. The viral post had this structure built in.

**Credibility laundering through partial truth**: The underlying facts — that antifreeze kills pets, that ethylene glycol is extremely toxic, that pet poisoning from household chemicals is a real problem — are all accurate. The error was solely in the chemical identification of hand sanitizer's ingredients. This gave the claim a more coherent epistemic structure than pure fabrication, making it harder to dismiss on a quick read.

**Platform dynamics**: Facebook's sharing mechanics during peak COVID-19 anxiety in March 2020 amplified health-adjacent warnings disproportionately, as documented in subsequent platform analyses of COVID-era misinformation spread (Cinelli et al., 2020, Scientific Reports).

The fact-check correction achieved substantial but not comparable reach, reaching an audience already searching for verification rather than the passive scroll audience that encountered the original claim.

## §5 — Corrections and Key Findings

**The specific claim is chemically false**: Hand sanitizer does not contain ethylene glycol. It contains ethanol or isopropanol. The antifreeze toxicity mechanism described in the viral post is not applicable to hand sanitizer.

**A residual true risk exists at a different exposure threshold**: Direct ingestion of large quantities of hand sanitizer could cause alcohol toxicity in pets. Licking a sanitized human hand does not represent a clinically meaningful exposure.

**The correction is relatively clean**: Unlike many misinformation cases where nuance makes correction complex, the chemical error here is binary and unambiguous. Hand sanitizer ≠ antifreeze. This is not a matter of interpretation.

**The broader pet safety information landscape has real gaps**: The episode revealed that authoritative, real-time pet toxicology information is insufficiently consolidated for rapid retrieval during viral misinformation events.

**Related real hazards exist**: Antifreeze truly does represent a severe hazard to pets. Certain household disinfectants and essential oils do pose genuine risks to specific animal species. These real hazards should not be dismissed because the hand sanitizer claim was false.

---

## §6 — Verdict and Broader Implications

Snopes' rating of **False** is precisely correct. The central mechanism claimed — ethylene glycol poisoning via hand sanitizer — cannot occur because ethylene glycol is not an ingredient in hand sanitizer. The claim confuses two categorically different chemicals and two categorically different products.

The broader implications are significant for how we understand health misinformation during crises. The hand sanitizer antifreeze claim was not random panic — it was a cognitively plausible error that arose from the intersection of three genuine facts (antifreeze kills pets; alcohol products can be toxic; hand sanitizer is suddenly everywhere) assembled via false linkage. This structure — accurate premises, false inference — is characteristic of the most epidemiologically successful misinformation, because it cannot be dismissed as pure fabrication.

For public health communication, the episode underscores the need for preemptive clear formulation transparency: if consumers widely understood that hand sanitizer's active ingredient is ethanol (the same compound in wine and beer), not a mysterious industrial chemical, the surface plausibility of the antifreeze-confusion claim would be substantially reduced. Regulatory labeling practices that emphasize "ethyl alcohol" or "isopropyl alcohol" as active ingredients — as required by FDA — are correct, but the communication reach of that labeling is limited compared to viral social media claims.

For pet health communication, the episode argues for a rapid-response veterinary toxicology resource optimized for social media contexts — a gap that remains largely unfilled as of the time of this writing.
