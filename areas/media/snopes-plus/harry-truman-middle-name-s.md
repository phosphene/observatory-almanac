# "Harry Truman's Middle Name 'S' Doesn't Stand for Anything"


## 1. The Claim

A persistent piece of presidential trivia maintains that Harry S. Truman's middle initial "S" is unique among American presidents because it doesn't stand for any particular name. According to this claim, Truman's parents chose the single letter "S" as his complete middle name to honor both of his grandfathers—Anderson Shipp Truman and Solomon Young—without showing favoritism to either family line. The story suggests that this diplomatic naming solution created an unusual situation where the middle initial is the actual name rather than an abbreviation.

This claim extends into questions of proper punctuation and style. Some versions assert that because the "S" doesn't abbreviate anything, it should not be followed by a period when written, leading to "Harry S Truman" rather than "Harry S. Truman." Others argue that conventional punctuation rules still apply regardless of the initial's origins, resulting in the more commonly seen "Harry S. Truman."

The naming story has become a standard element of Truman biographical writing and presidential trivia, often cited as an example of Missouri practicality and family diplomacy. It represents one of those peculiar historical details that seems to capture something essential about a historical figure's character and background.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim demonstrates strong evidential support in its core assertion while revealing interesting complications in its details. Primary source documentation from the Truman family and contemporary records confirm the basic naming story. Truman's birth certificate, family correspondence, and biographical materials consistently support the account that his parents, John Anderson Truman and Martha Ellen Young Truman, chose "S" as a compromise between honoring Anderson Shipp Truman (his paternal grandfather) and Solomon Young (his maternal grandfather).

The family dynamics underlying this decision are well-documented in Truman family papers and biographical research. Both grandfathers had been significant figures in young Harry's life, and the parents' decision reflects the practical diplomacy that would later characterize Truman's political approach. Anderson Shipp Truman had been a farmer and livestock dealer, while Solomon Young was a successful farmer and freight hauler who had made the overland journey to California during the gold rush era.

However, the punctuation question reveals the complexity of applying modern style conventions to historical naming practices. Truman's personal correspondence shows him using both "Harry S Truman" and "Harry S. Truman" at different periods of his life. His early letters often omit the period, while his presidential correspondence more frequently includes it. This inconsistency suggests that Truman himself may not have viewed the punctuation question as.

The documentary evidence shows that various official sources have adopted different conventions. The Government Printing Office Style Manual recommends using the period, and most official government documents from Truman's presidency follow this convention. However, the Truman Library has used both formats in different contexts, and scholarly works on Truman show similar variation.

Contemporary style guides and punctuation authorities have offered conflicting guidance on this specific case. Some argue that because the "S" is the complete name rather than an abbreviation, no period is needed. Others contend that conventional punctuation rules for initials should apply regardless of the initial's origin. Still others suggest that established usage patterns should take precedence over theoretical punctuation rules.


The scholarly consensus strongly supports the basic claim about Truman's middle name while acknowledging the unresolved punctuation question. The most authoritative source, the Harry S. Truman Library and Museum, maintains extensive documentation about the naming convention and has addressed the question multiple times in official communications and publications.

David McCullough's Pulitzer Prize-winning biography "Truman" provides detailed family history that confirms the naming story and explores its context within 19th-century Missouri family traditions. McCullough's research, based on extensive access to family papers and interviews with surviving relatives, establishes the factual foundation for the naming account while noting Truman's own inconsistency in punctuation usage.

The scholarly historical community has generally accepted the naming story as well-documented fact. Biographers, presidential historians, and Missouri history specialists consistently include this detail in their accounts, though they vary in their treatment of the punctuation question. The consensus view is that the basic naming facts are established beyond reasonable doubt, while the punctuation remains a matter of style preference rather than historical accuracy.

The U.S. Government Publishing Office (formerly the Government Printing Office) has maintained its style manual recommendation for including the period, and this has become the standard practice in most official government documents and publications. However, this represents an institutional decision about contemporary style rather than a historical determination about Truman's own preferences.

Academic style guides have generally followed the GPO recommendation while acknowledging the debate. The Chicago Manual of Style, widely used in historical writing, recommends the period while noting the unusual circumstances of Truman's name. The Associated Press Stylebook similarly recommends the period for consistency with general punctuation rules.

Recent scholarship has tended to focus on the broader implications of the naming story rather than resolving the punctuation question. Historians have used the naming convention as evidence of Truman's family background, Missouri cultural patterns, and the practical approach that characterized his later political career. The naming story has become part of the larger narrative about Truman's middle-class origins and pragmatic worldview.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The Truman naming case offers a fascinating window into how seemingly straightforward historical facts can become complicated when examined closely. The story demonstrates the intersection of family history, naming conventions, grammatical rules, and institutional practices in ways that resist simple resolution.

The epistemological complexity begins with the question of what constitutes authoritative evidence for naming conventions. While family testimony and birth records establish the basic facts about the name's origin, they don't necessarily resolve questions about proper contemporary usage. The fact that Truman himself was inconsistent suggests that the punctuation question may be more about modern editorial and institutional preferences than about historically accurate representation.

This case also illustrates how historical details become simplified in popular retelling. The basic story—"the S doesn't stand for anything"—is true but incomplete without the family context and the acknowledgment of ongoing punctuation debates. Popular accounts often present this as a quirky historical fact without exploring the underlying family dynamics or the practical implications for contemporary writing and documentation.

The persistence of debates about the punctuation reveals interesting patterns in how institutions handle historical ambiguity. Different organizations have adopted different conventions based on their interpretation of style rules, historical accuracy, and practical consistency. The Government Printing Office, the Truman Library, major newspapers, and academic publications have all made different choices, suggesting that there may not be a single "correct" answer.

The naming convention also reflects broader patterns in American naming practices and family dynamics. The compromise solution chosen by Truman's parents was not unique—other families have used similar approaches to honor multiple family members while avoiding favoritism. However, Truman's later prominence made this particular naming choice a matter of public interest and official documentation in ways that similar family decisions rarely become.
