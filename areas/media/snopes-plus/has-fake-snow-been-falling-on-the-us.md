# Fake "chemical" snow has been falling on the US


## 1. The Claim

Every year, when significant snowfall events hit American towns and cities — places that do not commonly experience heavy snow — a predictable cycle begins. Residents film themselves holding disposable lighters under snowballs gathered from their yards and driveways. The snow does not drip. It blackens and appears to shrink in place rather than melting into the familiar puddle of water one might expect. The conclusion drawn in these videos and in the accompanying social media commentary is stark: this is not real snow. It is fake. It is synthetic. It has been manufactured and deployed — by the government, by corporations, by shadowy geoengineers — as part of a programme of weather control, population manipulation, or some other undisclosed agenda.

The claim's most concentrated viral moment came in February 2021, when Winter Storm Uri delivered historic levels of snow to Texas, Oklahoma, Louisiana, and other southern states that had little infrastructure prepared for such conditions. Millions of Texans lost power. The event was catastrophic and, for many residents, genuinely traumatising. In the days that followed, hundreds of videos circulated on TikTok, Facebook, Instagram, and YouTube showing people performing the lighter test — holding flame to snowball, watching it char — and declaring the snow chemical, plastic, synthetic, or government-issued. The hashtag `#fakesnow` accumulated tens of millions of views.

In the broader version of the claim, the fake snow narrative connects to the longstanding geoengineering and chemtrail conspiracy ecosystem. According to this worldview, governments and agencies including NASA, NOAA, and the Department of Defense have been engaged in large-scale atmospheric manipulation for decades. Cloud seeding — a real, documented, and relatively mundane weather modification technology — is cited as evidence that atmospheric manipulation is occurring at far greater scale. The "fake snow" is presented as a visible, touchable piece of evidence that the general population can encounter directly: you don't need to look at contrails high in the sky; you can hold the proof in your hand and light it on fire.

Some versions of the claim have been more specific, alleging that the snow contains nanobots, bioengineered compounds, polymer fibres, or other exotic materials. Proponents have claimed that the snow "smells like chemicals," that it retains its shape anomalously, or that it "repels water" in ways inconsistent with natural snow. In others, the framing is softer: there is "something wrong" with this snow, it is "not normal," and the government is "hiding the truth" — a classic unfalsifiable structure.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Sublimation is a fundamental property of ice under common atmospheric conditions.** The core observation driving the fake snow videos — that snow held under a flame does not produce dripping liquid water — has a textbook explanation in basic thermodynamics. Water exists in three phases: solid (ice), liquid, and gas (vapour). Under standard atmospheric pressure, ice will melt into liquid water when heated above 0°C. However, under conditions of low relative humidity and with a source of direct heat such as a butane flame, ice can undergo sublimation: the direct transition from solid to vapour phase, bypassing the liquid phase entirely.

This is not exotic or unusual. Fierz et al. (2009), in the International Association of Cryospheric Sciences' definitive classification of seasonal snow, describe dry-snow metamorphism and the vapour-transport processes that govern snowpack evolution — processes fundamentally driven by the same sublimation thermodynamics (Fierz C, Armstrong RL, Durand Y et al., 2009, *The International Classification for Seasonal Snow on the Ground*, UNESCO-IHP Technical Documents in Hydrology No. 83, Paris). Pomeroy JW and Gray DM (1995), in their foundational monograph on snowcover accumulation and redistribution, document how sublimation losses from snowpacks routinely range from 10 to over 40 percent of seasonal snowfall in cold continental climates, attributable precisely to the vapour-phase transition that the lighter-test videos are accidentally capturing (Pomeroy JW and Gray DM, 1995, *Snow Accumulation, Relocation and Management*, NHRI Science Report No. 7, Environment Canada, Saskatoon).

In practical terms: when you hold a butane lighter under a snowball, you are introducing a flame of approximately 1,977°C — the adiabatic flame temperature of butane in air. The surface-adjacent snow is exposed to intense radiant heat. Because the flame is small, localised, and the snow is a poor thermal conductor with a large surface-area-to-mass ratio, the heated boundary layer of ice reaches vapour-phase transition conditions before the bulk of the snowball warms to the liquid-water melting point. You observe blackening and apparent shrinkage without visible dripping — exactly what the videos show. The snow is not fake. It is cold, dry, and obeying thermodynamic laws.

**The black residue is soot from combustion, not evidence of synthetic materials.** Butane (C₄H₁₀) combustion produces carbon dioxide and water under complete combustion conditions, but a disposable lighter held under a snowball does not produce complete combustion. The flame is oxygen-limited and the cold surface of the snowball quenches the combustion zone, producing incomplete combustion products including elemental carbon — soot. The black residue deposited on the snow surface is soot from the lighter, not a chemical signature of the snow itself. This has been independently confirmed by multiple analytical chemists and science communicators who performed the same test with distilled water ice and conventional tap-water ice and obtained identical results.

**Cloud seeding is real but is not "fake snow" at population scale.** The existence of atmospheric weather-modification technology is not disputed. Cloud seeding — the dispersal of silver iodide, potassium iodide, or other ice-nucleating agents into cloud formations to enhance precipitation — has been practiced commercially and experimentally since Bernard Vonnegut's original silver iodide experiments in 1947 (Vonnegut B, 1947, "The Nucleation of Ice Formation by Silver Iodide," *Journal of Applied Physics*, 18(7), 593–595). Cloud seeding operations are conducted by state water authorities in Colorado, Wyoming, Utah, and other western US states, primarily to augment mountain snowpack for water supply purposes. These programmes are documented, licensed, and their effects are measurable in the range of a few to perhaps 15 percent precipitation enhancement under optimal conditions.

Cloud seeding operations produce natural snow crystals via natural ice-crystal growth physics; they do not manufacture synthetic snow and deploy it from aircraft. The silver iodide concentrations used in cloud seeding are measured in grams per square kilometre of cloud coverage — amounts orders of magnitude too small to represent a public health concern, let alone to produce detectable quantities of foreign substance in snowfall at ground level. A person collecting snow after a cloud-seeded storm and testing it chemically would not be able to detect silver iodide above background environmental noise.

Weather modification is also geographically and meteorologically targeted. Commercial cloud seeding operations target orographic lift zones — mountain ranges — to enhance snowpack in specific drainage basins. Winter Storm Uri, the event most closely associated with the 2021 fake-snow discourse, was a synoptic-scale Arctic outbreak caused by polar vortex disruption — a documented atmospheric phenomenon resulting from the stratospheric interactions studied by Charlton A and Polvani L (2007, "A New Look at Stratospheric Sudden Warmings," *Journal of Climate*, 20(3), 449–469). No credible meteorological evidence suggests any anthropogenic contribution to the event's intensity or trajectory.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The visual evidence fallacy.** The fake snow claim derives much of its persuasive power from the apparent directness of its evidence. You are not being asked to trust a government agency, an expert institution, or a media organisation — all of which have been systematically delegitimised in the conspiratorial information environment. You are being asked to trust your own eyes. You picked up the snow yourself; you held the lighter yourself; you saw what happened. The subjective experience of "my own sensory data tells me this" carries epistemic weight that institutional claims labelled as debunking cannot easily overcome.

This is a specific instance of what cognitive psychologists call the "naïve realism" bias — the default assumption that perception gives unmediated access to objective reality (Ross L and Ward A, 1996, "Naive Realism in Everyday Life: Implications for Social Conflict and Misunderstanding," in Reed ES, Turiel E, Brown T (eds), *Values and Knowledge*, Lawrence Erlbaum Associates, Hillsdale). When naive realism meets incomplete physical knowledge, the result is confident misinterpretation. The lighter-test observer correctly perceives that the snow did not behave as expected; incorrectly concludes that the failure of expectation implies artificial origin; and has no accessible mechanism by which to distinguish sublimation from chemical contamination on the basis of visual inspection alone.

**The epistemic novelty problem in anomaly interpretation.** This case illustrates a general pattern in conspiracy belief formation: when a person encounters a genuine anomaly — something they did not expect and cannot immediately explain — and lacks access to the correct explanatory framework, they are epistemically vulnerable to the first plausible-sounding alternative explanation they encounter. Sublimation is real but unfamiliar. Very few people outside of physical chemistry, atmospheric science, or material science have direct working knowledge of phase transitions or their role in everyday snow physics. The gap between observation and explanation is filled by the most available narrative, which in the contemporary information environment is frequently supplied by conspiratorial content that has been algorithmically amplified for engagement.

What makes the fake snow case particularly instructive is that the conspiracy explanation is not even internally coherent: no proposed mechanism survives scrutiny. What would "synthetic snow" be made of? What substance would behave exactly like natural snow in all observable respects — forming snowflakes, accumulating in drifts, creating blizzard conditions — except that it would also not drip when a lighter is applied? Polymer aerogels? Expanded silica? Each proposed material either has clearly different observable properties from snow or cannot be aerosolised and distributed at weather-event scale by any plausible delivery system. The claim's immunity to this objection is maintained by its structural vagueness: it does not need to specify what the material is; it only needs to maintain that "something is wrong."

**Disaster as conspiratorial catalyst.** Winter Storm Uri's role as the trigger for the most intense cycle of fake-snow viral content is not coincidental. Disaster conditions prime conspiratorial cognition. The psychological literature on crisis conspiracy beliefs documents that large-scale unexpected events with significant harm — particularly events that disrupt normal social functioning and expose institutional failure — produce elevated baseline suspicion, reduced tolerance for uncertainty, and heightened appetite for explanatory narratives that assign intentional agency to suffering (van Prooijen JW and Douglas KM, 2017, "Conspiracy Theories as Part of History: The Role of Societal Crisis Situations," *Memory Studies*, 10(3), 323–333). Winter Storm Uri revealed catastrophic failures of Texas energy infrastructure. For millions of Texans who lost heat, water, and power in life-threatening conditions, the disaster demanded an explanation proportionate to its scale. "The polar vortex disrupted" is not an emotionally satisfying answer. "The government did this to us" closes the explanatory gap with an identifiable antagonist.


## 4. Verdict

**False.** The claim that fake, synthetic, or chemically manufactured snow has been deployed over the United States is false. The observations cited as evidence — particularly the lighter test showing snow that blackens without dripping — are explained entirely by the well-documented thermodynamics of ice sublimation and by soot production from incomplete butane combustion. Cloud seeding is a real and documented weather modification technology but it does not produce synthetic snow and does not operate at the scale, geography, or delivery mechanism that the conspiracy claim requires. No sample of alleged fake snow has ever been analysed and found to contain anomalous materials inconsistent with natural precipitation. The claim fails on its physical, chemical, and logistical premises.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The fake snow claim is not an isolated curiosity; it is one node in a broader network of atmospheric conspiracy beliefs that includes chemtrails, HAARP-based weather control, and geoengineering-as-depopulation narratives. These beliefs share a common deep structure: the atmosphere, which is publicly visible and directly experienced by everyone but whose physical mechanisms are opaque to non-specialists, is posited as a delivery system for covert interventions.

The epistemic structure of atmospheric conspiracy claims is particularly resilient because it is non-falsifiable by design. Contrails that persist in the sky become chemtrails; contrails that dissipate quickly become evidence of varying spray formulations; normal precipitation becomes evidence of weather warfare; unusual weather events become evidence of escalating operations. Disconfirming evidence is absorbed as further evidence of the cover-up. This structure means that standard empirical debunking — presenting physical chemistry, atmospheric science, or government programme documentation — does not reliably update belief, because the debunking institutions are themselves incorporated into the conspiracy.

The social function of atmospheric conspiracy beliefs in politically marginalised communities is worth examining seriously. Trust in government and institutional expertise in the United States has declined substantially over recent decades, and that decline is not uniformly irrational — there are genuine documented cases of government atmospheric experimentation (Project Popeye, the US military cloud-seeding programme during the Vietnam War, is a documented example), genuine cases of environmental contamination dismissed by regulatory agencies, and genuine failures of elite institutions to act in the public interest. The conspiratorial response overcorrects dramatically, but the suspicion from which it originates has real roots.

Understanding this context does not validate fake-snow claims, but it does suggest that effective response requires more than correction of the thermodynamic facts. The preconditions for conspiratorial belief — legitimate distrust, anomaly exposure, absence of accessible explanatory frameworks — must be addressed if correction is to be durable.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addressed the fake-snow claim as early as February 2014 and revisited it multiple times, most comprehensively in February 2021 following Winter Storm Uri. The Snopes verdict is rated **False**, with detailed explanation of sublimation physics and butane combustion residue. PolitiFact, USA Today Fact Check, and Reuters Fact Check all published similar assessments in February 2021 as the claim reached peak viral velocity.

The standard fact-check approach identifies three components: (1) demonstration of the sublimation explanation with reference to basic phase-transition chemistry; (2) documentation that multiple independent analysts replicated the lighter test with ordinary ice and obtained identical results; and (3) contextualisation of cloud seeding as a real but limited and entirely unrelated technology. These three moves are sufficient to establish the falsity of the substantive empirical claim.

The limitation of the standard approach is that identification of a video as false does not account for the belief architecture that produced the video and that ensures the next wave of fake-snow content after the next unusual snowfall. Fact-check organisations have increasingly recognised that their debunking uptake is disproportionately concentrated among audiences who were least likely to have believed the claim initially. Research by Nyhan B and Reifler J (2010, "When Corrections Fail: The Persistence of Political Misperceptions," *Political Behavior*, 32(2), 303–330) on the "backfire effect" — subsequently contested and refined in the literature — opened a debate about when corrections work and for whom that continues to shape fact-checking methodology. The current consensus suggests that corrections are generally effective in reducing misbelief at the population level, but that for strongly committed believers in a conspiratorial framework, corrections may have limited uptake. The practical implication is that fact-checking the fake-snow claim matters for the large majority of viewers who encountered it with scepticism or uncertainty; it matters less for the smaller group for whom the claim is structurally load-bearing in a broader conspiratorial worldview.
