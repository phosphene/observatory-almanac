# No, the State of Hawaii is Not Releasing Saltwater Crocodiles

**Snopes Verdict:** LABELED SATIRE  
**Original URL:** https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/hawaii-saltwater-crocodiles/  
**Snopes Author:** Madison Dapcevich  
**Snopes Published:** November 26, 2021

---

## §1 — Overview and Claim

On November 20, 2021, a Facebook page calling itself "The State of Hawaii" published a post that claimed the U.S. state of Hawaii was in the process of importing 174 adult saltwater crocodiles from Australia, with the stated goal of reducing shark attacks on Hawaiian beaches. The post was written in an authoritative, bureaucratic register that closely mimicked official government communications. It cited safety statistics, referenced a purported environmental justification, offered regulatory language about the animals' protected status, and even included a public safety advisory asking citizens not to "pet or feed the Salties."

The post rapidly circulated on social media, attracting widespread attention from users who encountered it in their feeds without the contextual cues necessary to identify it as satirical. The core claim — that an official state government was deploying large, apex-predator reptiles as a biological countermeasure against shark attacks — was extraordinary enough to attract curiosity, concern, and credulity in varying proportions from different readers.

Snopes reporter Madison Dapcevich investigated the claim and determined it was **labeled satire**: originating from a satirical Facebook page that did not make a genuine claim about real events and whose "about" section, while somewhat obscured by the platform's interface, indicated its content was humorous in nature.

This Truth Vault entry examines the post's anatomy, the ecological and zoological background that gives the claim its surface plausibility and ultimate absurdity, the social dynamics of satirical content misread as factual news, and the broader epistemic questions raised by state-impersonating satire pages on social media platforms.

---

## §2 — Snopes Investigation and Verdict

Madison Dapcevich traced the November 2021 post to its origin: the Facebook page "The State of Hawaii," a satirical page with more than 57,000 followers at the time of publication. Critically, this was not the first time the post had circulated. An identical or near-identical post had previously been shared by two Facebook pages: CnC Honolulu, on May 30, 2019, citing the import of 6 (not 174) saltwater crocodiles; and by the same State of Hawaii page approximately one year after that. The 2021 iteration cited 174 crocodiles — an escalation that itself suggests satirical intent, though one that was lost on many readers.

The "about" section of The State of Hawaii Facebook page contained multiple inconsistencies incompatible with an authentic government presence: it included structural typos; it called itself "The Official The State of Hawaii Page" — with the redundant article "the" constituting both a grammatical oddity and a subtle internal wink; and it used the construction "The State of Hawaii" rather than the official designation "State of Hawaii." The page had more than 57,000 followers at the time Snopes investigated.

The CnC Honolulu page, whose earlier version circulated in 2019, described itself explicitly as an "entertainment website" and stated in its about section that information available on the page is "NOT from the city and county of Honolulu." Dapcevich noted that CnC Honolulu had approximately 7,600 followers.

Snopes rates this content as **Labeled Satire**: the pages that originated and shared the content identified themselves, to varying degrees of visibility, as satirical in nature. This is distinct from outright fabricated content or from satire without disclosure labels — though the visibility of those disclosures, buried in "about" sections that most users never consult, is itself an epistemically significant issue discussed in §3.

The article also briefly addressed the shark-versus-crocodile zoological curiosity that forms the surface hook of the story: Dapcevich found documentation of saltwater crocodiles consuming sharks (though in most documented cases it was small or already-dead sharks), and cited that a National Geographic article confirmed American alligators attack and eat sharks in some contexts — acknowledging the grain of biological realism that makes the satirical premise entertaining rather than purely absurd.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis

The "State of Hawaii releasing saltwater crocodiles" case offers one of the most clearly articulated available examples of **contextual gap misinformation** — a category in which satire that is appropriately labeled in its original context loses that context as it migrates across platforms and communities. This constitutes the **epistemic novelty** of this case.

Satirical content often relies on what media scholars call *contextual cues* for correct interpretation: the name of the outlet, the tone of surrounding content, the community expectations of a shared information ecosystem. When a satirical post remains within its original community — readers who know the page posts jokes, who recognize the in-group register, who can identify exaggeration or parody cues — it functions normally. When the post is screenshot, shared, or linked across community boundaries into unfamiliar contexts, the contextual cues are stripped away, and what remains is formal factual language that may be processed as genuine.

This is precisely what the research of Pennycook et al. (2020, *Psychological Science*) identifies as the "familiarity backfire effect" in partisan misinformation contexts: repeated exposure to a claim increases its perceived accuracy, regardless of the claim's verifiability. Applied to satire-driven misinformation, when a satirical post circulates repeatedly — as this one did in 2019, 2020, and 2021 — iterations of exposure in unfamiliar contexts progressively launder its satirical framing into apparent factuality.

The additional epistemic complexity here is the impersonation element. "The State of Hawaii" is not merely a satire page; it is a satire page that appropriates the identity of a government entity. Research on parasocial authority — the tendency of audiences to attribute authority to information sources that carry the linguistic, visual, or structural markers of legitimate institutions — suggests that even mild institutional mimicry significantly increases belief compliance (Cialdini, 1984, *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*). A user who encounters "The State of Hawaii" page on Facebook and does not check the "about" section may reasonably, if incorrectly, interpret it as an official or quasi-official source.

This creates a structural problem for platforms like Facebook, which have historically relied on self-disclosure as their primary mechanism for distinguishing satire from news. If a page identifies itself as satirical in its "about" section but uses an official-sounding name and posts content in an authoritative register, the platform's disclosure system fails users who do not navigate to the about section. Amazeen and Muddiman (2018, *Journal of Communication*) found that corrections to misinformation are substantially less effective when the original claim was encountered in a high-authority context — and a page named after a government entity reads as high authority regardless of the small-print disclaimer.

## §4 — Scientific and Empirical Context

The ecological premises embedded in the satirical post are themselves worthy of careful examination, as they illuminate both how the satire works and how it fails.

**Saltwater Crocodile Range and Habitat:** Saltwater crocodiles (*Crocodylus porosus*) are, as Dapcevich notes, native to the coastal areas and brackish estuaries of eastern India through northern Australia and the western Pacific, including parts of Southeast Asia. They are the largest living reptilian species on Earth, with large males reaching lengths exceeding 6 meters and weights exceeding 1,000 kilograms (Webb and Manolis, 1989, *Freshwater Crocodiles*; Whitaker and Andrews, 2003, *Crocodilian Biology and Evolution*). Their habitat preferences include tropical and subtropical coastal environments with warm water temperatures.

Hawaii's water temperatures — typically 24–27°C year-round in shallow coastal areas — are within the thermal tolerance range of *C. porosus* (Seebacher, Elsworth, and Franklin, 2003, *Journal of Experimental Biology*). This is the grain of ecological plausibility in the satire. However, saltwater crocodiles are coastal and estuarine species; they do not typically inhabit open ocean environments. The premise of deploying them to patrol open-water beaches where shark attacks occur is biologically implausible. Sharks and crocodiles occupy largely non-overlapping spatial niches: sharks range through deep, open water, while crocodiles are primarily nearshore and riverine.

**Crocodile-Shark Interactions:** Documentation of saltwater crocodiles consuming sharks exists in contexts where species overlap occurs in shallow coastal habitats (Grigg, Seebacher, and Franklin, 2001, *Crocodilian Biology and Evolution*). However, these interactions typically involve small coastal shark species, not the bull sharks (*Carcharhinus leucas*) or tiger sharks (*Galeocerdo cuvier*) that are responsible for the majority of serious shark attacks in Hawaiian waters. Bull sharks and tiger sharks are large, aggressive species that are not systematically deterred by the presence of crocodiles in any documented ecological context.

**Hawaiian Shark Attacks:** The Hawaii Department of Land and Natural Resources tracks shark-human interactions in Hawaiian waters. Historically, incidents have involved tiger sharks as the most responsible species. The idea that saltwater crocodiles would displace or deter Hawaiian shark populations has no scientific basis; introduced apex predators would more likely restructure the coastal food web in unpredictable and potentially damaging ways — a well-documented phenomenon in invasion biology (Vitule et al., 2009, *Biological Conservation*).

**American Crocodile Range:** Dapcevich notes that American crocodiles (*Crocodylus acutus*) inhabit brackish or saltwater environments at the southern areas of Florida, constituting their most northern range, per the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission. This detail contextualizes the claim: even the closest native crocodilian to North American waters does not range to Hawaii, reinforcing the geographic and ecological implausibility of the satirical premise.

## §5 — EARC/Gap Classification and Misinformation Patterns

**EARC Classification: Contextual Gap (C)**

This case is classified under the **Contextual Gap** category. The claim is labeled satire at its source, but its satirical framing is stripped away as it migrates across contexts, creating a mismatch between what the content is and how it reads to audiences who encounter it without the original framing. There is no empirically defective claim at the level of individual propositions (the post is clearly not intended as a factual report), but the dissemination context creates conditions for misinterpretation that constitute a meaningful epistemic hazard.

The broader pattern here is **institutional impersonation satire** — a category of social media content that uses the identity of government entities, official bodies, or public institutions to frame satirical content. This category is particularly hazardous because it exploits the cognitive heuristic that well-known institutional names imply reliability and authenticity. Unlike obviously parodic outlets (The Onion, The Babylon Bee), which have sufficiently distinctive brand identities that most users can categorize them with exposure, a page named "The State of Hawaii" on Facebook requires research to identify as satire.

The **gap in public knowledge** this case illustrates is the *verification gap*: users who lack the habit of verifying that a source's name matches an actual institution, and who encounter content in a context stripped of satirical framing, have no readily available tool to distinguish between official and impersonating sources. This is particularly acute on Facebook, where a follower's feed may present content from both official government pages and impersonating satire pages with identical formatting and interface presentation.
