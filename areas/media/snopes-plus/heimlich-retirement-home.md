# The Truth Vault: Henry Heimlich Once Used His Maneuver to Save Fellow Resident in Retirement Home

## 1. The Claim

In March 2024, a post on the subreddit r/todayilearned claimed that Henry Heimlich, the inventor of the eponymous anti-choking technique, had finally used his own maneuver in a real-life situation — not in a medical conference, not in a training video, but in the dining room of his retirement home, at the age of ninety-six, to save an eighty-seven-year-old woman who was choking on a hamburger. The post accumulated over 23,000 upvotes and hundreds of comments, many expressing astonishment that the man who had given the world one of its most famous medical interventions had never actually used it himself until that moment.

The claim, as it circulated, had several layers. The core assertion — that Heimlich saved a fellow resident — was presented as a heartwarming fact, a kind of cosmic justice in which the inventor finally got to see his invention work in the most poignant possible setting. The subtext was that this was the first time Heimlich had ever performed the maneuver in a genuine emergency, despite having demonstrated it countless times over the forty-two years since he first described it in 1974. This "first time" framing was central to the story's emotional appeal: imagine inventing something that saves thousands of lives annually, but never needing to use it yourself until you are ninety-six and living in a retirement home, and then having it work perfectly when the moment finally arrives.

The claim was indeed true — the event happened, and it happened exactly as described. On May 26, 2016, at Deupree House, a senior living facility in Cincinnati, Ohio, Henry Heimlich saw fellow resident Patty Ris, eighty-seven, begin to choke on a hamburger in the dining room. He rose from his seat, positioned himself behind her, and performed the abdominal thrusts that bear his name. The obstruction was dislodged. Ris began breathing again. A dining room manager arrived to find the situation already under control — Dr. Heimlich had handled it.

But the "first time" framing, which gave the story much of its emotional resonance, turned out to be complicated. Conflicting reports from Heimlich himself over the years suggested that he had, in fact, used the maneuver on at least one prior occasion. The tension between these accounts — between the clean narrative of a first-time use at age ninety-six and the messier reality of a man who may have performed it earlier without fanfare — is where the deeper story lives.

## 2. What's Actually True

The incident at Deupree House is thoroughly documented. Multiple major news organizations covered it, including NPR, CNN, The Guardian, and The New York Times. Heimlich, Ris, and dining room manager Perry Gaines all appeared on Cincinnati's NBC affiliate WLWT to discuss the experience. The video of that interview remains available online and shows Heimlich, then ninety-six, describing the event with evident satisfaction. "That moment was very important to me," he told The Guardian. "I knew about all the lives my maneuver has saved over the years and I have demonstrated it so many times but here, for the first time, was someone sitting right next to me who was about to die."

The Heimlich maneuver itself is a well-established medical technique. First described by Heimlich in a June 1974 essay titled "Pop Goes the Café Coronary" in the journal Emergency Medicine, the maneuver involves standing behind a choking person, placing a fist just above the navel, and delivering firm upward thrusts to create the pressure needed to dislodge an obstruction in the airway. Before Heimlich's technique gained widespread acceptance, the standard response to choking was firm pats on the back — a method that can actually drive an obstruction deeper into the airway. Heimlich's innovation was simple, elegant, and has been estimated to have saved approximately 50,000 lives in the United States alone since its introduction.

The Heimlich maneuver is now taught in CPR and first aid courses worldwide. It is recognized by the American Heart Association, the American Red Cross, and medical authorities internationally. It has been performed by celebrities, by ordinary citizens, and even — according to lore — by Ronald Reagan, Cher, and Carrie Fisher, or on them. It is one of the few medical techniques that can be taught to schoolchildren and used by anyone in an emergency, requiring no equipment, no medication, and no specialized training beyond a few minutes of instruction.

The retirement home incident was not the first time Heimlich had been present at a choking emergency. The question was whether he had ever used his technique before. In 2003, Heimlich told the BBC that he had once performed the maneuver on a person at a restaurant in Cincinnati. In 2006, he told The New Yorker that he had used it on a man in a restaurant several years earlier. But in 2016, when the retirement home incident occurred, he told reporters — including The Guardian — that he did not recall it ever happening before. "I don't recall it ever happening," he said. "This was the first time."

This inconsistency is not necessarily evidence of deception. Heimlich was ninety-six years old at the time of the retirement home incident. Memory is unreliable at any age, and at ninety-six, it is entirely plausible that a prior use of the maneuver had faded from recollection. It is also possible that the earlier incidents were not as clear-cut as the retirement home rescue — that they involved partial choking, or that Heimlich's role was ambiguous, or that they simply did not register as significant enough to remember. The desire to frame the retirement home event as "the first time" may have been driven by reporters seeking a clean narrative rather than by Heimlich himself, who may simply have been uncertain about the details of his own past.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Joey Esposito's Snopes article, published December 10, 2024, does an excellent job of verifying the core claim and contextualizing the "first time" controversy. The article correctly identifies the event as true, provides thorough documentation from major news outlets, includes the video evidence, and thoughtfully addresses the conflicting reports about whether the retirement home incident was actually the first time Heimlich had used his maneuver. The Snopes rating of "True" is appropriate for the core claim — Heimlich did use his maneuver to save a fellow resident.

The article also does a good job capturing the human dimension of the story. The Reddit comments it quotes — "Can you imagine if he botched it?" and the personal story from a parent who used the maneuver on a child — add texture and demonstrate the story's emotional resonance for ordinary people. The inclusion of the WLWT interview, the dining room manager's account, and Heimlich's own words creates a rich documentary record.

Where the Snopes article falls short is in its treatment of the deeper questions surrounding the "first time" narrative. The article notes the conflicting reports — the 2003 BBC interview, the 2006 New Yorker interview, the 2016 Guardian interview — but does not interrogate what these contradictions mean for how we understand Heimlich's relationship with his own invention. Was he embellishing his history for the BBC and The New Yorker, or was he honestly forgetting in his interviews with The Guardian? Did it matter to him whether he had used it before? What does the changing story tell us about how inventors construct narratives about their own creations?

The article also does not explore the broader question of why the "first time" framing was so compelling to audiences. The idea that Heimlich had never used his own maneuver until age ninety-six is appealing because it fits a particular narrative structure: the inventor as theorist rather than practitioner, the technique as gift to humanity rather than personal tool. That Heimlich may have used it earlier, in less dramatic circumstances, would not detract from his achievement, but it would change the story's shape in ways that audiences apparently found less satisfying. The Snopes article does not analyze this narrative preference.

Furthermore, the article does not address the medical and ethical dimensions of the Heimlich maneuver's history. Heimlich was a controversial figure in some medical circles, advocating for his technique with a fervor that some considered excessive and opposing alternative approaches such as back blows in ways that were not always supported by evidence. The retirement home story is a heartwarming capstone to a career that included genuine contributions but also moments of controversy. Snopes presents the story as a simple verification without engaging with this complexity.

The article also misses an opportunity to discuss the epidemiology of choking and the public health impact of the Heimlich maneuver. How many lives has the technique actually saved? How does it compare to alternative approaches? What is the broader context of choking as a cause of death in elderly populations? These questions would add substantive depth to a story that is currently framed primarily as a human-interest anecdote.

## 4. Why This Matters

The story of Henry Heimlich using his own maneuver at age ninety-six matters for several reasons that extend beyond the heartwarming anecdote.

First, it illuminates the relationship between inventors and their inventions. Heimlich spent decades promoting his technique, demonstrating it at conferences, writing about it, and advocating for its adoption worldwide. Yet the question of whether he had ever used it in a real emergency became a subject of fascination because it speaks to a distinction we intuitively make between knowing and doing, between having a solution and being forced to apply it under pressure. The retirement home incident, whether or not it was actually the first time, satisfied a cultural desire to see the inventor become the practitioner, to close the loop between theory and application.

Second, the story raises questions about the reliability of personal narrative in constructing historical memory. Heimlich gave different accounts of his experience at different times. Which account was true? The answer is probably that all of them were true from his perspective at the time, which is itself an important lesson about how memory works — not as a perfect recording but as a reconstruction that shifts with context, audience, and the passage of time. The "first time" narrative was compelling, and it may have been accurate within Heimlich's own recollection in 2016, even if earlier recollections suggested something different.

Third, the story demonstrates the power of social media in reviving and reshaping historical narratives. The Reddit post that brought the story to renewed attention in March 2024 generated over 23,000 upvotes, which is a significant level of engagement for a piece of historical trivia. The comments section revealed not only astonishment at the story but also personal anecdotes from users who had used the Heimlich maneuver themselves, creating a kind of participatory commemoration that transcends the original event. Social media platforms have become the primary vector for the transmission of historical anecdotes, and the dynamics of these platforms — the emphasis on novelty, the preference for clean narratives, the pressure toward simplification — shape how history is remembered.

Fourth, the story touches on the relationship between aging and agency. Heimlich was ninety-six years old when he saved Patty Ris. He was living in a retirement home, the kind of setting where elderly people are often assumed to be recipients of care rather than providers of it. The image of a ninety-six-year-old man springing into action to perform a physical maneuver that requires strength and coordination challenges stereotypes about aging and capability. The story is, in this sense, not just about Heimlich but about the capacity of older adults to remain active, capable, and contributing members of their communities.

Fifth, the story matters for what it reveals about the sociology of medical innovation. The Heimlich maneuver succeeded not just because it was effective but because it was simple, memorable, and teachable. It could be performed by anyone, anywhere, without equipment. Its success is a case study in the importance of accessibility in medical technology — a lesson with implications for how we design and disseminate public health interventions.

Finally, the conflicting accounts of whether this was Heimlich's first use of the maneuver raise questions about how we handle ambiguity in historical narratives. The Snopes article correctly notes the inconsistency but does not attempt to resolve it, which is the right approach. Some historical questions cannot be definitively answered, and the responsible response is to acknowledge the uncertainty rather than impose a false clarity. The story is true in its essentials, and the ambiguity around the "first time" framing is a feature, not a bug — it is part of what makes the story interesting.

## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article by Joey Esposito, published December 10, 2024, is the primary reference for this Truth Vault entry. The article draws on extensive coverage from major news outlets including NPR (May 27, 2016), CNN (May 27, 2016), The Guardian (May 27, 2016), The New York Times (May 28, 2016), the Cincinnati Enquirer, and Mashable. The article also cites the WLWT television interview with Heimlich, Ris, and Gaines, which provides primary-source documentation of the event.

Heimlich's original 1974 article "Pop Goes the Café Coronary," published in Emergency Medicine and available via Gwern's digital archive, is the foundational document for the technique. The American Red Cross, the American Heart Association, and the Cleveland Clinic all provide current guidance on the Heimlich maneuver, reflecting its status as a standard emergency response technique.

The conflicting reports about whether the retirement home incident was Heimlich's first use of the maneuver come from a BBC interview from 2003 — "Heimlich: Still Saving Lives at 83" — and a New Yorker profile from 2006 titled "Choke Artist" by Lauren Collins. In both of these earlier sources, Heimlich indicated that he had performed the maneuver on at least one prior occasion. The 2016 Guardian interview and other contemporaneous reports quote Heimlich as saying this was the first time. The inconsistency is documented by Snopes but not resolved.

Notable gaps in the Snopes article include the absence of detailed discussion about the Heimlich maneuver's medical history, including controversies around its efficacy relative to alternative approaches. The article also does not explore the epidemiology of choking deaths in the elderly, a demographic that is disproportionately affected by this preventable cause of mortality.

The story's viral trajectory — from a 2016 news event to a March 2024 Reddit post to renewed public attention — would benefit from systematic analysis of the social media dynamics that drove its recirculation. The choice to frame the story as "today I learned" is significant: it positions the event as newly discovered knowledge rather than rediscovered history, which affects how audiences receive and interpret it.

## 6. Verdict

The claim that Henry Heimlich used his namesake maneuver to save a fellow resident in his retirement home is true. On May 26, 2016, at Deupree House in Cincinnati, the ninety-six-year-old Heimlich performed the abdominal thrust technique he invented forty-two years earlier to dislodge a piece of hamburger from the throat of eighty-seven-year-old Patty Ris. The event was witnessed, documented, and reported by multiple credible news organizations. The Snopes rating of "True" is correct.

However, the question of whether this was the first time Heimlich had ever used his maneuver in a real-life emergency is genuinely ambiguous. Heimlich gave conflicting accounts at different points in his life, claiming in 2003 and 2006 that he had used it before, but stating in 2016 that he did not recall any prior use. The retirement home incident may or may not have been his first real-world application of the technique; the evidence does not allow a definitive conclusion.

The Snopes article correctly identifies and documents this ambiguity, but it does not explore its deeper implications for how we understand the relationship between inventors and their inventions, the role of narrative in shaping historical memory, the dynamics of social media-driven historical rediscovery, or the broader medical and public health context. The story is richer and more interesting than the simple "true" rating suggests.

Beyond its factual verification, the story matters because it challenges assumptions about aging and agency, illustrates the power of simple and accessible medical interventions, demonstrates the complexity of personal memory and historical narrative, and offers a satisfying — if ambiguous — capstone to the life of a man whose name has become synonymous with a specific form of emergency care. The most important truth in this story is not that Heimlich used his maneuver at age ninety-six, but that a technique invented by a single individual in 1974 has saved tens of thousands of lives that might otherwise have been lost to a completely preventable cause of death.
