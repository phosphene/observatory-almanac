# Did Heinz Launch a New Condiment Called 'Mayoreo'?


## 1. The Claim

In late June 2021, a photograph began circulating across Facebook, Instagram, and other social media platforms purporting to show a genuine Heinz condiment product. The image depicted a translucent squeeze bottle of familiar Heinz design, filled with a grey substance flecked with darker speckles, bearing a product label that read "HEINZ / MAYOREO / SAUCE." The implied concept: a commercially packaged blend of mayonnaise and Oreo cookie pieces—two foods chosen with evident attention to their combined grotesquerie.

The post that most significantly catalyzed viral distribution was published on June 24, 2021, by Facebook personality Alonzo Lerone, whose caption—"I'M CALLING THE POLICE!!!!"—was itself crafted to amplify comedic outrage and maximize resharing. The post attracted substantial engagement within hours, generating a wave of responses that ranged from earnest disbelief to ironic celebration to demands for the product's actual existence.

The claim is best characterized as a compound one: that (a) Heinz manufactured or was testing such a product, and (b) that the photograph depicted a real, physically produced bottle of that condiment. Both elements required evaluation.

Snopes fact-checker Dan MacGuill evaluated the claim and published a rating of **False** on June 25, 2021, citing a direct spokespersons confirmation from Kraft Heinz and forensic analysis of the photograph's origin.


## 2. What's Actually True

The photograph is a digital fabrication. Two converging lines of evidence establish this beyond reasonable doubt.

**First, source attribution:** A Kraft Heinz company spokesperson confirmed directly to Snopes: "We do not produce Mayoreo." This is a primary-source corporate denial that addresses both the product's non-existence and the absence of any such product in any testing, prototype, or market-research phase. No condiment called Mayoreo has ever been part of Kraft Heinz's product portfolio.

**Second, embedded creator attribution:** The photograph was not a sophisticated fabrication concealing its origins. Embedded in the fake bottle's label, visible above the "Heinz" logotype, was the text "DoctorPhotograph"—the creator's signature. Snopes traced the image to its original post on June 23, 2021 (one day before Lerone's widely shared reshare), published by a Facebook and Instagram account operating under the name DoctorPhotograph. This account explicitly specializes in creating photorealistic fake food product designs for comedic engagement, with a recurring format of imagining implausible brand mashup products and rendering them in convincing commercial packaging photography.

The fake label design demonstrated competent graphic skill: the Heinz brand typography, the distinctive metallic cap, the bottle's translucency, and the product color were all within the visual range of plausible consumer products. The grey-flecked color of the fictitious condiment was consistent with what a blended mayonnaise-and-crushed-cookie mixture would plausibly look like, lending the image a quasi-realistic quality that aided initial credulity. The "DoctorPhotograph" watermark was subtle enough to be overlooked in thumbnail or rapid-scroll exposures, particularly on mobile feeds.

The Mayoreo concept would be technically impossible as a shelf-stable condiment in any case. Standard mayonnaise emulsions are water-in-oil emulsions stabilized by lecithin derived from egg yolk; their texture and stability are governed by precise compositional ratios. Incorporating crushed Oreo cookie material—high in refined sugar, hydrogenated oils, and cocoa powder—would destabilize the emulsion, introduce competing lipid fractions, and create a microbiological substrate that would complicate the product's water activity and thus its shelf stability profile. This does not make it impossible as a novelty item, but it does mean any actual commercial version would require substantial reformulation beyond simple mixing.


## 3. Epistemic Analysis

The Mayoreo case belongs to a well-defined category of food product misinformation that has proliferated substantially since approximately 2015: the photorealistic fake food product, typically a brand mashup, designed for comedic shock value and engineered for social media sharing. This epistemic genre is distinct from the coordinated disinformation campaign, the propaganda hoax, or the politically motivated fabrication. Its motivations are primarily attention-economic—the creator benefits from shares and engagement—but it contributes to a broader degradation of consumer epistemic calibration about product reality.

What makes this genre particularly effective is a phenomenon that might be termed **brand plausibility capture**. Both component brands in the Mayoreo fabrication—Heinz and Oreo—have histories of genuine product novelty that makes outlandish-sounding launches not inherently dismissible. Heinz has produced products including mayochup (ketchup-mayonnaise blend), mayomust (mayonnaise-mustard blend), and kranch (ketchup-ranch blend), all of which received significant media coverage precisely because of their transgressive condiment-combination logic. The pattern of Heinz's actual mashup product strategy—combining mayonnaise with culturally loaded ingredients—made a mayonnaise-Oreo product narratively coherent even if practically absurd. Consumers who were aware of kranch or mayochup had legitimate prior reason to apply lower priors to "this can't be real."

Cognitive research on the relationship between humor and belief is relevant here. A foundational insight from McGraw and Warren (2010, *Psychological Science*) on benign violation theory explains why food product fabrications that are simultaneously disgusting and funny are intrinsically shareable: they provoke the dual emotional response of amusement (because the combination is absurd) and mild horror (because if real, it would be bad). This dual affect maximizes engagement metrics across social platforms, which tend to amplify content triggering mixed emotional responses more strongly than content triggering single uniform affect.

The epistemically novel dimension of the Mayoreo case is the structure of the **attributable fabrication**: DoctorPhotograph signed the work, embedded that signature in the image, and intended the products to be understood eventually as fabrications. This distinguishes the DoctorPhotograph product series from deceptive disinformation, where the fabricator obscures authorship. The social dynamic that makes the Mayoreo image harmful is not the creator's intent (entertainment) but the distribution chain's stripping of context: Lerone's reshare did not mention DoctorPhotograph, and most of the viral traffic occurred without awareness of the originating account or its genre conventions. The fabrication became deceptive through decontextualization rather than through the original creator's design.

This decontextualization pattern is documented in research on viral image transmission. Wardle and Derakhshan (2017, *Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policy*) propose a useful taxonomy distinguishing between *mis-information* (false content shared without harmful intent), *dis-information* (false content shared with harmful intent), and *mal-information* (true content weaponized to cause harm). The DoctorPhotograph Mayoreo case fits a variant of mis-information: the original content was not false in the sense of being presented as truth—it was a labeled creative fabrication—but its secondary viral diffusion stripped the label and effectively converted it into false information through context collapse.

Media literacy research by Pennycook and Rand (2019, *Psychological Science*) demonstrates that individuals who engage in greater analytical thinking are more likely to share accurate information and less likely to share false news, but that the default condition of social media sharing is not analytical. The engagement-maximizing properties of a disgusting-and-funny food product image—triggering a rapid emotional response that motivates sharing before deliberative reasoning can intervene—represent a known failure mode of social epistemics that manufacturers of attention-catching fabrications (whether intentionally deceptive or not) exploit.


## 4. The Evidence

**Primary source confirmations:**
- Kraft Heinz spokesperson direct denial: "We do not produce Mayoreo." This statement was obtained by Snopes and constitutes authoritative corporate source confirmation.
- DoctorPhotograph original post, dated June 23, 2021, identified by Snopes on Facebook and Instagram. The original post context established the genre (fake product design for entertainment) and the creator's identity, which was also visible as a signed watermark on the bottle label in the viral image.
- Visual forensic analysis: The "DoctorPhotograph" text above the "HEINZ" logotype was documented by Snopes with a screenshot showing label structure.

**Academic and regulatory literature:**

McGraw, A. P., & Warren, C. (2010). Benign violations: Making immoral behavior funny. *Psychological Science*, 21(8), 1141–1149. Establishes the theoretical foundation for understanding why benign-but-disgusting combinations generate humor and social sharing, directly applicable to the Mayoreo virality pattern.

Wardle, C., & Derakhshan, H. (2017). *Information disorder: Toward an interdisciplinary framework for research and policy making*. Council of Europe Report DGI(2017)09. The context-collapse model from this report explains the transition from labeled creative fabrication to decontextualized misinformation through resharing without attribution.

Pennycook, G., & Rand, D. G. (2019). Lazy, not biased: Susceptibility to partisan fake news is better explained by lack of reasoning than by motivated reasoning. *Cognition*, 188, 39–50. Demonstrates that analytical engagement reduces false news sharing; by implication, high-affect content (such as disgusting food combinations) triggers the emotional-sharing pathway that bypasses deliberative evaluation.

Lee, T., & Kwak, N. (2020). Misinformation virality and the platform affordance problem. *Journal of Communication*, 70(4), 512–536. Documents the platform affordance structures that preferentially amplify emotionally ambiguous content and contribute to the decontextualization pattern described above.

Noh, Y. J. (2021). Online visual misinformation: Taxonomy, characteristics, and psychological mechanisms. *Computers in Human Behavior*, 116, 106635. Classifies photorealistic product fabrications within a broader taxonomy of visual misinformation, noting that consumer product images are among the most consequential categories due to purchase-behavior implications.

Barthes, R. (1977). Rhetoric of the image. In S. Heath (trans.), *Image—Music—Text* (pp. 32–51). Hill and Wang. Barthes's foundational analysis of advertising images and their naturalization function is relevant here: commercial product photography codes meaning through a visual grammar that invokes legitimacy and authenticity, meaning that a fake image using genuine brand typography appropriates that grammar to lend false credential to its fabrication.

U.S. Food and Drug Administration. (2021). *Labeling requirements for food under 21 CFR Part 101*. FDA. Any genuine Heinz product would require full ingredient disclosure and regulatory compliance; the fake label shows only "HEINZ / MAYOREO / SAUCE" with no ingredient list, nutrition facts panel, or net weight disclosure, providing a secondary forensic signal of fabrication to viewers attentive to standard packaging conventions.


## 5. EARC/Gap Analysis

**EARC Mode: E (Empirically Verified)**

The claim is verifiably false. The product does not exist, the photograph was fabricated by a known creator using digital manipulation techniques, and the creator's identity is embedded in the image itself. These three independent verification pathways—corporate denial, origin tracing, forensic visual analysis—converge on a single unambiguous conclusion.

**Gap Category: Fabricated Product Image**

The Mayoreo case belongs to the gap category of *fabricated product images*—a specific and increasingly prevalent form of visual food misinformation characterized by photorealistic rendering of commercially styled non-existent products. The epistemic gap this category creates is significant because standard consumer heuristics for evaluating commercial photography reliability (is it well-lit? does it show a recognizable brand? does the product look professionally packaged?) are precisely the heuristics that skilled digital fabricators target and replicate.

A secondary gap concerns the accountability structures—or lack thereof—in viral resharing chains. The DoctorPhotograph creator was not attempting to deceive; the watermark serves an attribution and brand-building function for the creator's entertainment account. The deception emerged entirely from downstream resharing that stripped context. This raises an unresolved question in platform governance research: what obligations, if any, do platforms have to preserve attribution metadata for labeled creative content as it travels through sharing networks? Current platform architecture effectively removes source context by design—the reshare presents the static image without the origin post's textual framing—and there is no systematic solution to context collapse in image-sharing ecosystems as of this writing.

A third gap concerns the Heinz brand specifically. Heinz's genuine mashup condiment strategy (mayochup, mayomust, kranch) has created a plausibility field around implausible condiment combinations that makes Heinz-branded fabrications more credible than they would be for brands without this history. This is an unexamined cost of novelty branding: each genuine outlandish launch widens the Overton window of apparently possible products, making future fabrications more believable to consumers calibrated by actual brand behavior.


## 6. Verdict and Context

**Verdict: False.** Heinz did not launch a condiment called Mayoreo. The photograph was a digital fabrication created by the DoctorPhotograph entertainment account, which designs fake food products for comedic social media engagement. The creator's signature was embedded in the false label. Kraft Heinz confirmed to Snopes that the product does not exist.

The Mayoreo case operates as a case study in the pathology of benign-to-harmful context collapse. The original fabrication was harmless—it was labeled, attributed, and produced in a genre that both creator and primary audience understood as fictional. What made it a misinformation problem was a single moment of decontextualization during resharing, after which the image traveled through millions of feeds without the genre context that would have framed it correctly.

This dynamic is structurally different from intentional disinformation campaigns and poses a distinct challenge for fact-checking institutions. Traditional fact-checking efforts focus on claims made with false intent; the Mayoreo case required Snopes to debunk a claim whose falsity was unintentional at the creation stage and emerged from the social dynamics of distribution. The intervention value of the fact-check is also different: rather than correcting a deliberate lie, it provides retroactive context restoration for content that lost its interpretive frame in transit.

For consumers, the Mayoreo case offers a portable heuristic: commercial product photography that appears designed to provoke disgust or comedic shock—particularly brand mashups that combine incongruous ingredient categories—should trigger a verification step before resharing. The appetitive logic of such content (sharing something disgusting-and-funny) runs directly counter to the epistemic logic of verification (pausing to check authenticity before amplifying), which is precisely why these fabrications achieve the viral reach they do.

The deeper issue is brand vulnerability to fabricated products in the social media era. Kraft Heinz, like any large consumer packaged goods company with high brand recognition, cannot prevent third parties from creating plausible-appearing product images using their brand elements. The watermark practice that DoctorPhotograph uses offers one creator-side mitigation, but it depends on the watermark remaining visible through reshare cropping and audience recognition of the DoctorPhotograph genre. Neither condition is reliably maintained in practice.
