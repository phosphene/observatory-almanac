## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In early August 2023, a tweet by user @josiahhughes was viewed nearly 5 million times, presenting what the poster called a "powerful visual" showing how much sugar is in 400 ml of Heinz tomato ketchup. The tweet featured two bottles of Heinz ketchup side by side: one filled with the actual condiment, the other filled with what the post claimed was the equivalent amount of sugar contained in that quantity of ketchup. The implied volume of sugar in a single bottle struck many viewers as alarming, generating substantial reply-thread discussion on health, dietary habits, and food industry practices.

Snopes investigator Jordan Liles (2023) assigned this claim the rating **True**. Through direct product purchase, nutrition label analysis, and a physical recreation of the demonstration, Liles confirmed that a standard 14-ounce bottle of Heinz Tomato Ketchup contains approximately 92 grams of sugar across 23 servings — an amount that, when poured as granulated sugar into an identical empty bottle, produces a visual result closely matching the comparison shown in the original tweet. The claim is, within reasonable margins attributable to the volume-versus-weight issue noted by Liles and the slight differences between a 14-ounce US bottle and the metric 400ml version in the original tweet, substantively accurate.

This entry examines the nutritional chemistry of Heinz ketchup and its sweetener profile, the broader context of sugar content in tomato-based condiments, the "volume versus weight" epistemological nuance that complicates but does not invalidate the claim, and what the virality of this visual demonstration reveals about audience understanding of food nutrition information.

## §2 The Verification: Label Analysis and Physical Replication

Liles' (2023) methodology for verifying the claim was admirably direct: he purchased two 14-ounce bottles of standard Heinz Tomato Ketchup at retail, confirmed the nutrition label details, weighed out 92 grams of granulated sugar, poured the sugar into an empty bottle, and photographed the result. The photograph produced by Snopes' independent replication "showed close to the same outcome as the comparison in the popular tweet" — validating the claim's central visual assertion through experimental demonstration rather than relying on secondary sources or the original poster's credibility.

The nutrition label arithmetic that validates the claim is straightforward. Per the standard 14-ounce (397g) US product label, as also confirmed on the Target product page for the item: one serving size is one tablespoon (17 grams), and there are 23 servings per container. Each serving contains 4 grams of sugar. Multiply 23 servings by 4 grams per serving and the total is 92 grams of sugar per bottle. The original tweet referenced 400ml, while Liles worked from the 14-ounce US bottle; these are close but not identical quantities (400ml of ketchup would be approximately 430–460g by mass depending on density, which is somewhat more than 397g). This minor discrepancy does not materially affect the visual demonstration's validity, as the sugar content scales roughly proportionally.

One substantive nuance acknowledged by Liles (2023) concerns the "volume versus weight" issue raised by a Reddit commenter he cited. Ninety-two grams of granulated white sugar occupies a volume of approximately 110–115 milliliters (roughly ½ cup by us measuring cup, or about 3.7 oz by volume). An empty 14-ounce ketchup bottle has a total interior volume of approximately 414ml. The sugar therefore partially fills the bottle — less than a third of the total bottle volume — in a visual representation that is accurate in gravimetric terms but would convey a different impression if viewers interpreted it as "this much volume of sugar" rather than "this massed quantity of sugar." The visual is essentially a mass-comparison, not a volume-comparison. Liles noted this without concluding it invalidated the claim, which is the correct analytical position: the claim is about total sugar content by weight, and the visual accurately represents that weight comparison.

Heinz Tomato Ketchup's ingredient list, as published on the official Heinz product page, lists both high fructose corn syrup and corn syrup as distinct sweetener ingredients. This dual-sweetener composition is the primary driver of the product's sugar content; both sweeteners are liquid at room temperature (whereas the visual demonstration uses granulated crystalline sucrose, which is solid). This is a second caveat: the sugar in the actual product is dissolved in solution with tomatoes, vinegar, and water, not present as discrete crystals. The granulated sugar demonstration is a representation of total sugar mass, not a physically accurate simulation of how that sugar is structurally present in the product.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Visual Nutrition Translation Problem and Consumer Innumeracy

The epistemic novelty in the Heinz ketchup sugar case lies in what the claim's viral reach reveals about a fundamental gap in consumer nutritional literacy: the difficulty of translating serving-size-fragment information on nutrition labels into total-product sugar content. The US Nutrition Facts label mandates disclosure of grams of total sugar and added sugar per serving, but does not require disclosure of total-package sugar content. For a product with 23 servings per container, translating per-serving to per-bottle requires multiplication that most consumers do not perform in practice.

This disclosure format exists for defensible regulatory reasons: portion size normalization allows comparison across products in different package sizes, and the serving size concept is meant to anchor nutrient disclosure to realistic consumption occasions rather than to total-package content, which varies with container size. However, the practical effect for high-use condiments like ketchup — which are consumed repeatedly across many meals, a bottle at a time over weeks — is that the per-serving framing creates a systematic underestimation of cumulative intake. A consumer who uses ketchup daily does not typically calculate that 23 daily tablespoon servings equal 92 grams of sugar over 23 meals.

Research on nutrition label comprehension has consistently documented this limitation. Cowburn and Stockley (2005), in a systematic review published in *Public Health Nutrition* (vol. 8, no. 5, pp. 694–706), found that while most consumers report that nutritional information is important to them in food selection, a significant fraction cannot accurately perform the serving-size multiplication required to determine actual daily or weekly intake from label data. The review noted that energy (calorie) totals were better understood than specific micronutrient or macronutrient figures, partly because energy provides an intuitive reference anchored in personal experience.

Block et al. (2010), in a study published in the *American Journal of Public Health* (vol. 100, no. 2, pp. 384–387), randomized grocery shoppers to different calorie-labeling formats and found that presenting calorie information in terms of minutes of exercise required to burn the stated amount significantly changed purchase behavior relative to standard calorie counts — suggesting that numerically accurate but perceptually inert label formats can be improved substantially by translational framing. The Heinz ketchup sugar demonstration works precisely by providing this translational framing: converting an abstract "4 grams per serving × 23 servings" calculation into a visceral bottle-full visual.

The epistemic novelty is therefore not in what the demonstration reveals about ketchup in particular, but in what the 5-million-view reach of the demonstration reveals about the scale of the translation gap: there is very large latent demand for visual nutrition translation that standard label formats are not providing. The tweet functioned as a public health communication in the way that nutrition labels were intended to function but do not — by making the aggregate quantity of a single-product nutrient intake viscerally comprehensible.

## §4 Heinz Ketchup's Sweetener Portfolio, US-Canada Formulation Differences, and Processing Context

The primary sweetener vehicle in Heinz US ketchup is distinct from those used in some of the brand's other markets. The standard US formulation, as documented by Liles (2023) and confirmed on the Heinz product page, uses high fructose corn syrup (HFCS) and corn syrup as twin sweeteners. Both are derived from processed corn starch; HFCS, as its name indicates, has been enzymatically treated to convert a portion of its glucose content to fructose, producing a sweetener approximately 1–1.7 times as sweet as crystalline sucrose (depending on the HFCS variant — HFCS-55, the most common in beverage applications, is 55% fructose; HFCS-42, the variant more common in non-beverage applications, is 42% fructose).

The Canadian Heinz ketchup formulation, reported by In The Know (2023) and noted by Liles, does not use HFCS; the Canadian version uses sucrose (cane or beet sugar) as its primary sweetener. This formulation difference, which became the subject of a viral TikTok comparing ingredients lists, reflects both the significantly lower price of HFCS in US commodity markets (a long-standing feature of US agricultural subsidy policy) and consumer preference differences between markets.

Malik et al. (2010), in a meta-analysis published in *Circulation* (vol. 121, no. 11, pp. 1356–1364) covering 88 prospective cohort studies, found a significant positive association between sugar-sweetened beverage consumption and weight gain and obesity in both children and adults. While ketchup is not classified as a beverage for nutritional epidemiology purposes, its HFCS content places it in the relevant category of HFCS-containing processed foods in the context of this research literature.

The distinction between sugar from natural tomato solids and added sugar is relevant here: tomatoes contain naturally occurring sugars (primarily glucose and fructose), and a ketchup product made from tomatoes alone would still contain some sugar. The 4 grams of added sugar per serving listed on the Heinz label represents the contribution beyond what comes from the tomato concentrate itself. The total sugar on the label (which was not separately reported in the Snopes article but would include both natural and added sources) is listed at 4 grams in the standard US formulation — in this case, the added sugar and total sugar appear to be equivalent (or the naturally-occurring tomato sugar is nutritionally negligible relative to added sweeteners). This detail does not change the calculation but affects the interpretation: most of the 92 grams of sugar in the bottle is added sweetener, not tomato-intrinsic carbohydrate.

Heinz also markets several alternative ketchup formulations for consumers seeking lower sugar options. The Tomato Ketchup with No Sugar Added uses stevia leaf extract as its sweetener (a non-caloric plant-derived sweetener), while Simply Tomato Ketchup with No Artificial Sweeteners uses cane sugar (sucrose) without corn syrups. These alternatives give consumers options across the sweetener-source spectrum but require active label-reading to select, which returns to the core problem identified in §3: most consumers do not perform detailed ingredient comparisons at the point of purchase.

## §5 The "Volume vs. Weight" Nuance and the Ethics of Simplified Health Visuals

The primary complication the Snopes investigation identified with the "powerful visual" claim — the volume-versus-weight issue — is worth exploring in more depth because it illustrates a broader challenge in health communication: the tension between accuracy and comprehensibility in simplified visual representations of nutritional data. The tweet showed a bottle filled with granulated sugar and implied it represented "the sugar in a bottle of ketchup." This is accurate in mass terms but potentially misleading in gestalt impression terms.

Granulated white sugar has a bulk density of approximately 800 grams per liter (or 0.8 grams per milliliter). Ninety-two grams of granulated sugar therefore occupies approximately 115 ml of volume — visible in the bottle, but filling it to only about 28% capacity. A viewer unfamiliar with the volume-vs-weight distinction might interpret the visual as showing that the bottle is approximately 28% sugar by volume, or alternatively might not notice the partially-filled bottle in the comparison image and simply read the visual as "bottle = sugar." Neither interpretation is quite right.

The Snopes investigation's independent replication photograph (published in the original article, described as showing "close to the same outcome") addresses this nuance by demonstrating exactly what the visual looks like when reproduced — confirming that the comparison bottle is indeed partially but not fully filled. This is the appropriate journalistic response: not to dismiss the claim as misleading but to verify it experimentally and document what the visual actually shows, while noting the conceptual nuance.

This tension between simplification and accuracy is endemic to health visualization. Research by Fagerlin et al. (2007) in *Medical Decision Making* (vol. 27, no. 5, pp. 606–617) found that different risk visualization formats (numerical, frequency, pictograph) produce systematically different emotional responses and comprehension outcomes among general audiences, and that simpler, more visceral formats consistently improve immediate comprehension at the potential cost of accuracy on secondary parameters. The ketchup sugar visual trades the secondary precision (it shows mass, not volume proportion) for an immediately comprehensible emotional impact (a full bottle is a lot of a thing). The trade-off is largely justified: the primary health communication goal — helping consumers understand the aggregate sugar content of a bottle of ketchup — is served by the visual, even if the precise volumetric interpretation requires the nuance Liles documented.

## §6 Research Gaps and Conclusion

**Verdict:** True. A standard 14-ounce bottle of Heinz Tomato Ketchup contains approximately 92 grams of sugar, and when that quantity of granulated sugar is poured into an identical bottle, the result closely matches the comparison photograph in the original tweet. Snopes independently verified the calculation from product label data and confirmed it through physical replication.

**EARC Gap — Replicability:** This is an unusual case where the EARC gap is about replicability rather than evidence quality — the claim is verifiable by any consumer who purchases the product and a scale. The deeper gap is in consumer nutrition literacy: the serving-size format of US nutrition labels systematically obscures total-container nutrient content, and a large fraction of consumers cannot perform the multiplication required to reconstruct it. The visual demonstration fills a real public health communication function.

**Research Gaps for Further Inquiry:**
1. The comparative sugar content of major ketchup brands in the US market — and the full range of Heinz's own alternative formulations — has not been visualized in a standardized format comparable to the tweet that generated 5 million views. A systematic comparative visualization across products might more effectively serve consumer education goals.
2. The behavioral impact of "sugar bottle" visualizations on actual purchasing behavior or consumption patterns has not been studied for ketchup or similar condiments. The Block et al. (2010, *American Journal of Public Health*) exercise-equivalent framing study is a partial model but does not apply directly to this visualization type or this product category.
3. The difference between the US and Canadian Heinz formulations — HFCS vs. sucrose — as a policy artifact of US corn subsidy programs has economic and public health implications that are not currently well synthesized in a single accessible source. The In The Know (2023) reporting is journalistic rather than scholarly.
4. The sensory and nutritional differences between ketchup sweetened with HFCS-55, HFCS-42, sucrose, and stevia are not comprehensively documented in peer-reviewed food science literature accessible to general audiences. Such a comparison would provide richer context for consumer choice.

The Heinz ketchup sugar case is ultimately a success story for fact-checking: a claim that generated enormous viral reach was submitted to direct experimental verification, found to be substantially accurate, and documented with the relevant caveats. The "powerful visual" it confirmed is, within the terms of the health communication literature, an example of translational visualization that successfully makes abstract label data perceptually accessible.
