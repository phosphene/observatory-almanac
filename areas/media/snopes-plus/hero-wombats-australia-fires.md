## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

As the 2019–2020 Australian bushfires generated global coverage and enormous emotional investment in the fate of native wildlife, a social media claim emerged in early January 2020 asserting that wombats were actively and deliberately shepherding other animals into their burrows to help those animals escape the flames. The claim, which circulated via Instagram, Twitter, Facebook, and was picked up briefly by news organizations, framed wombats as heroic agents performing an affirmative rescue function—not merely passive habitat providers, but active intercessors on behalf of imperiled species.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2020) assigned this claim the rating **Mixture**. The specifically heroic characterization is false: wombats are not herding, guiding, or otherwise actively inviting other animals into their burrows. Australian wildlife experts and ecologists directly rejected the shepherding claim. However, the underlying physical premise of the narrative carries genuine substance: wombat burrows are genuinely enormous underground tunnel systems, some extending up to 650 feet (approximately 200 meters) in length; wombats do periodically abandon burrows and dig new ones, leaving extensive underground refugia available to other species; and there is documented evidence, including camera trap footage, of non-wombat animals using wombat burrows. The claim is false as to animal intentionality but meaningfully true as to animal habitat utility.

This entry examines the behavioral biology of wombats, the documented ecology of wombat burrows as multispecies refugia, the social media dynamics that transform provisional habitat observations into heroic agency narratives, and the important epistemic distinction between what animals do and what animals intend—a distinction that is systematically collapsed in popular wildlife coverage, with downstream consequences for both fact-checking and conservation communication.

## §2 Wombat Burrow Biology and the Documented Evidence

The common wombat (*Vombatus ursinus*) and both species of hairy-nosed wombat (*Lasiorhinus latifrons* and *L. krefftii*) are among Australia's most accomplished burrowing mammals. The burrow systems they construct are engineering achievements by mammalian standards: single wombat burrow systems can encompass multiple tunnels, chambers, and entry points, with total linear tunnel length reaching up to 200 meters (approximately 650 feet) according to figures cited in general natural history literature (Bradford, 2015, *Live Science*). The dimensions of wombat tunnels—typically around 50 centimeters in diameter, sufficient for an adult wombat but also accessible by a wide range of smaller native fauna—make them structurally suitable shelters for other species.

Wombats add further habitat complexity by a behavioral characteristic that is biologically authentic: they periodically abandon burrows and dig new ones. Associate Professor Steve Johnston of the University of Queensland, interviewed by the Brisbane Times during the fires (Layt, 2020, *Brisbane Times*), noted that "a lot of those wombat burrows could be empty, so animals could easily make their way into those burrows to escape the fire... Wombats will often make a burrow system and then move on, so those empty burrows become open [to other animals]."

The camera trap evidence cited by Evon (2020) is particularly relevant: students at the University of Melbourne set up cameras near a wombat burrow in 2019 and recorded a koala and rabbits entering the tunnel, an event documented and explained by Dr. Kath Handasyde, an expert in native mammal ecology and physiology at the University of Melbourne. Handasyde's explanation was that the wombat was likely sleeping in a different chamber or not present when the other animals explored the burrow—not that the wombat invited them in. The Center for Biological Diversity subsequently shared video from the 2020 fires showing a common brown butterfly (*Heteronympha merope*, though species identification is uncertain from the cited footage) using a wombat burrow entrance during the fire, providing direct evidence of opportunistic use of wombat infrastructure during the fire crisis.

The experts who directly rejected the shepherding claim were clear and consistent. Jackie French, director of The Wombat Foundation, told IFLScience that wombats are "extremely short-sighted" and "focus mostly on food and dirt," and that it would be "hard for them to see well enough to shepherd" even if they were behaviorally inclined to do so. Scott Carver, a senior lecturer in wildlife ecology at the University of Tasmania, told the Associated Press (Dupuy, 2020) that no evidence exists that wombats were "sharing or encouraging other animals" to take refuge.

Greenpeace Australia's Instagram post, which appears to have been the primary viral vector for the shepherding claim, explicitly described "shepherding behavior" and "reports from Australia" supporting the hero narrative. After the story was investigated and challenged, Greenpeace Australia updated its post to remove the shepherding language—an acknowledgment that the most anthropomorphic framing was unsupported.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Animal Agency, Anthropomorphism, and the Conservation Communication Dilemma

The wombat hero story represents a case study in a broader epistemic problem in wildlife communication: the systematic conflation of animal habitat effects (what animals' structures or behaviors passively enable) with animal agency (what animals deliberately intend to do). This conflation is not merely an innocent error but reflects a specific and identifiable cognitive tendency—anthropomorphism in ecological contexts—that has a complex relationship with both accurate representation and conservation outcomes.

The specific claim at issue—active shepherding and inviting—requires attributing to wombats a mental model of fire danger, an awareness of other species' distress, and a motivational investment in other species' welfare that is entirely unsupported by comparative cognition research on wombats or closely related marsupials. Wombats are solitary, territorial animals that typically charge and chase intruding animals from their burrows (Evon, 2020); any incidental sharing of burrow space occurs despite, not because of, wombat social preferences.

The epistemic novelty lies in how this case illuminates the **conservation communication dilemma** identified in the literature on environmental communication: accurate representations of animal cognition and behavior are often less emotionally engaging than anthropomorphic framings, which in turn generate more public attention and fundraising traction for conservation causes. Dahlstrom (2014), writing in *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* (National Academy of Sciences), demonstrated across multiple studies that narrative formats are more persuasive than expository formats for communicating scientific information, and that emotionally engaging false narratives can out-compete accurate but less compelling true ones in public attention capture.

The wombat hero story is a paragon of this dynamic. A wombat burrow ecology story accurately framed—"wombat burrow systems are extensive enough that abandoned tunnels provide refuge to smaller native fauna during fires"—is biologically accurate, genuinely interesting, and reflects real ecological value. It is, however, considerably less emotionally compelling than "wombats are heroically leading fire victims to safety." The accurate version requires understanding passive habitat value; the inaccurate version requires only attributing intentional heroism to a recognizable animal.

This creates a documented tension in conservation NGO communication practice. Spitzer et al. (2021), writing in *Biological Conservation* (Elsevier), documented that conservation organizations face systematic incentives to amplify emotionally engaging content, including anthropomorphic animal narratives, because such content consistently outperforms technically accurate but less narratively compelling material in fundraising and public engagement metrics. The Greenpeace Australia post represents this dynamic: even an organization with sophisticated environmental communication capacity disseminated the anthropomorphic version of the story, suggesting that the viral pull of the hero narrative was sufficient to bypass normal fact-checking within the organization.

The important implication for fact-checking is that the **mixture** rating is not merely a diplomatic compromise between truth and falsehood. It reflects a genuine ontological distinction: the burrow-as-refuge claim is substantially true; the active-rescue-agency claim is false. These two components are separable, and fact-checking that treats them as a single claim requiring a single verdict misses the epistemically interesting structure—and misses the opportunity to reinforce the accurate version of the story while correcting the false one.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Evon's (2020) investigation identified the key expert commentators, distinguished the true from the false components of the claim, and represented the camera trap evidence accurately. Several threads merit additional exploration:

**The specific fire context**: The investigation does not specify which fire fronts were proximate to documented wombat habitat at the time the claim emerged. Wombat range across southeastern Australia overlaps significantly with heavily fire-affected areas in Victoria and New South Wales. Whether burrows in fire-affected areas were systematically surveyed afterward for evidence of refuge use—by researchers rather than social media observers—is not documented.

**Quantitative burrow ecology**: The claim that wombat burrows extend "up to 650 feet" is cited from general reference sources. The scientific peer-reviewed literature on wombat burrow dimensions is more nuanced: Taylor (1993), writing in *Wildlife Research* (CSIRO Publishing), documented that burrow length varies substantially by species, soil type, and individual history. Systematic surveys of burrow dimensions with zoological precision were not cited by the Snopes investigation.

**Species-specific differences**: The investigation discusses wombats as though they constitute a single behavioral type, but there are three extant wombat species with meaningfully different ecologies. The southern hairy-nosed wombat (*L. latifrons*), which lives in more arid conditions in South Australia, digs extensive shared warrens quite different from the solitary burrows of the common wombat. Whether the distinction between solitary and colonial burrowing wombats affects the plausibility of any incidental shelter hypothesis is not addressed.

**The Greenpeace correction**: Greenpeace Australia's acknowledgment that the shepherding claim was unsupported represented a notable instance of a major NGO correcting its own viral misinformation. How the correction was communicated relative to the original (the original remained visible with a text addendum; no separate correction post was documented) represents an underanalyzed question of whether platform-level correction mechanics are adequate for high-engagement posts.

## §5 Wombats in Fire Ecology: What We Actually Know

The 2019–2020 fires decimated populations of multiple native Australian species across vast swaths of habitat. Wombats themselves suffered significant mortality in fire-affected regions; they are not immune to the fires that their burrows may benefit other species in resisting. Understanding what wombat burrows actually contribute to post-fire ecological recovery requires situating them within the broader literature on burrow use in fire ecology.

There is an established scientific literature on underground refugia as survival mechanisms during fire events. Nimmo et al. (2020), writing in *Ecology Letters* (Wiley-Blackwell), examined how different animal communities use underground space during and after fire events in Australian landscapes, documenting that species with access to burrows enjoyed substantially higher survival rates than those without. This work does not specifically address wombat burrows as the mechanism, but establishes the general principle that the underground hypothesis is ecologically credible.

The behavioral literature on wombats in relation to territorial exclusion is relevant here too. Wombat territorial behavior is real and documented: common wombats (V. ursinus) are known to aggressively defend burrows against conspecific intruders and will push out or attack animals that invade active burrows (McIlroy, 1973, *Australian Journal of Zoology*, CSIRO Publishing). However, the key qualifier—"active burrows"—matters significantly: the camera evidence and expert testimony both suggest that incidental use of unoccupied or temporarily vacated burrows is where the true (non-hero) version of the story resides.

The popular account, when stripped of the anthropomorphic scaffolding, remains genuinely remarkable: Australia is home to animals that create some of the most extensive underground refugia in the mammalian world, and in an era of increased fire frequency and intensity associated with climate change (Williams et al., 2020, *Nature Climate Change*, Springer Nature), those burrows may represent a critical and underappreciated component of survival infrastructure for multiple species—none of which would be saved by wombat heroism, all of which could be saved by wombat engineering.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: MIXTURE**

Wombats are not actively herding animals or inviting them into their burrows to escape fires. This anthropomorphic agency claim is rejected by multiple Australian wildlife experts. Wombats are short-sighted, territorial, and solitary animals that typically exclude intruders from their burrows. However, wombat burrow systems are genuinely extensive (up to 200 meters of tunnel), wombats routinely abandon burrows and leave them as available habitat, and there is camera trap evidence of other species—including koalas and rabbits—using wombat burrows. It is plausible that animals fleeing the fires could have used empty wombat burrows opportunistically; this rescue occurred, if it occurred, without wombat intention or involvement.

**EARC Classification: R (Representational — anthropomorphism in ecological crisis communication)**

The gap is representational: the viral claim accurately identifies a real ecological phenomenon (extensive underground refugia created by wombat burrowing) but misrepresents the mechanisms at work by attributing intentional rescue agency to animals whose cognition and behavioral ecology are inconsistent with such agency. This is a case where debunking the false claim risks discarding the true substrate, making the mixture rating analytically essential.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Systematic post-fire surveys of wombat burrows in fire-affected areas to document actual use by non-wombat species
- Species-specific differences in burrow dimension and availability across common and hairy-nosed wombat populations
- Whether the Greenpeace Australia correction reached a comparable audience to the original post
- Quantitative data on how many wombat burrows in fire-affected areas were unoccupied at the time of peak fire intensity
