# "History Is Written by the Victors"


## 1. The Claim

"History is written by the victors" stands as one of the most frequently cited maxims about historical knowledge and power. Often misattributed to Winston Churchill (no reliable source exists for this attribution), the phrase suggests that those who win wars, conflicts, or power struggles control the subsequent narrative about what happened and why. According to this view, historical accounts systematically favor the perspective of the successful while marginalizing or erasing the voices of the defeated.

This understanding has become a default explanation for historical bias, suggesting that skepticism toward official histories is always warranted because they inevitably reflect the interests of those in power. The phrase functions as a thought-terminating cliché in discussions about contested historical events, implying that standard historical accounts should be dismissed as victor's propaganda while alternative narratives deserve credence precisely because they represent suppressed perspectives.

The maxim carries particular weight in contemporary debates about colonialism, war memory, and cultural narratives, where it's often invoked to challenge mainstream historical accounts and advocate for previously marginalized viewpoints. Its apparent wisdom and cynical realism make it rhetorically powerful across diverse political and cultural contexts.


## 2. What's Actually True

Examining the historical record reveals numerous counterexamples that challenge the "victors write history" generalization. Many of the most influential historical narratives have actually been shaped by defeated groups, marginalized voices, or perspectives that explicitly challenge dominant power structures.

**The Lost Cause mythology** provides perhaps the most striking counterexample in American history. Despite losing the Civil War, Confederate sympathizers and their descendants successfully dominated Southern historiography for nearly a century. The Lost Cause narrative — portraying the Confederacy as fighting for states' rights rather than slavery, depicting enslaved people as content, and romanticizing antebellum society — became entrenched in school textbooks, popular culture, and public monuments throughout the South and beyond.

This pro-Confederate interpretation wasn't simply preserved in private; it became the dominant public narrative taught in schools and universities. Historians like William Archibald Dunning at Columbia University institutionalized sympathetic interpretations of Reconstruction as a tragic mistake imposed on the defeated South. These views influenced scholarship, textbooks, and popular understanding well into the 20th century, demonstrating how defeated groups can successfully control historical narratives when they retain cultural and institutional power.

**Classical historiography** offers another counterexample. Thucydides wrote his influential account of the Peloponnesian War from the Athenian perspective, despite Athens ultimately losing to Sparta. His history became the foundational text of Western historical writing, ensuring that the defeated Athenian viewpoint would shape understanding of the conflict for millennia. Similarly, much of what we know about the fall of the Roman Empire comes from Christian authors who were critical of imperial power and often sympathetic to the "barbarian" groups that defeated Roman armies.

**Holocaust historiography** presents a particularly important case. The systematic murder of European Jews was documented and remembered primarily by survivors, victims' families, and their descendants — certainly not by the perpetrators who sought to destroy evidence. Nazi attempts to control the historical narrative failed completely; virtually all Holocaust scholarship, commemoration, and public understanding has been shaped by those who suffered rather than those who inflicted the suffering.

**Postcolonial historiography** demonstrates how formerly colonized peoples have successfully challenged and replaced imperial narratives. Works like Edward Said's "Orientalism" (1978) and a vast body of scholarship by historians from formerly colonized nations have reshaped understanding of empire, development, and cultural contact. Universities worldwide now teach perspectives that explicitly challenge European colonial narratives, often as standard curriculum rather than alternative viewpoints.

**Modern academic methodology** actively works against victor's bias through several mechanisms:

- **Subaltern studies** specifically seeks out marginalized voices and challenges elite narratives
- **Social history** focuses on ordinary people rather than political leaders
- **Oral history** preserves perspectives that might not appear in official documents
- **Interdisciplinary approaches** incorporate archaeology, anthropology, and other fields that reveal information beyond written sources
- **International scholarship** means historical events are studied by researchers from multiple national and cultural backgrounds

Professional historical training emphasizes source criticism, multiple perspectives, and skepticism toward official accounts. Modern historians are explicitly trained to question whose voices are preserved in the historical record and to seek out marginalized perspectives.


The persistence of "history is written by the victors" despite abundant counterevidence suggests that it serves psychological and rhetorical functions beyond its descriptive accuracy. The phrase offers several appealing features:

**Cynical sophistication**: It makes the speaker appear worldly and skeptical of naive acceptance of official accounts. This cynicism can feel more intellectually sophisticated than engaging with the actual complexity of historical methodology.

**Explanatory power**: It provides a simple explanation for why certain historical narratives might feel unsatisfying or incomplete. Rather than grappling with the genuine complexity of historical events, the phrase suggests that bias provides a sufficient explanation.

**Political utility**: It can be deployed to dismiss inconvenient historical evidence while lending credibility to preferred alternative narratives. If mainstream history is just "victor's propaganda," then any opposing view gains credibility simply by virtue of opposition.

**Moral clarity**: It transforms complex historical questions into simple moral categories of oppressor and oppressed, victor and victim. This can be emotionally satisfying but intellectually limiting.

The epistemological challenge involves maintaining appropriate skepticism toward historical claims while avoiding the nihilistic conclusion that all historical knowledge is equally unreliable. Good historical methodology involves constant attention to bias, power dynamics, and missing voices, but this sophisticated skepticism is different from the blanket dismissal implied by "victors write history."

The phrase also reflects a problematic understanding of how knowledge systems actually work. Historical understanding emerges from complex interactions between evidence, methodology, institutional structures, cultural values, and ongoing scholarly debate. Reducing this complexity to simple power dynamics obscures more than it illuminates.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The "victors write history" maxim reveals several problematic assumptions about how historical knowledge is actually produced and validated. While power undoubtedly influences historical narratives, the relationship is far more complex than the phrase suggests.

**The myth of monolithic victory**: Real historical situations rarely involve clear "victors" and "losers" with unified perspectives. The American Civil War, for example, involved multiple competing interests within both Union and Confederate sides, different regional perspectives, various ethnic and class positions, and evolving views over time. Reducing this complexity to "victor's narrative" obscures the actual dynamics of how different groups remembered and interpreted the conflict.

**Institutional vs. cultural power**: Political victory doesn't automatically translate into narrative control. Defeated groups may retain significant cultural institutions, educational systems, or social influence that allows them to shape historical understanding. The persistence of Lost Cause mythology demonstrates how cultural power can operate independently of political outcomes.

**Temporal complexity**: Historical narratives evolve over time as social values, methodological approaches, and available evidence change. What counts as "victor's history" in one era may be challenged and overturned in another. The changing interpretation of Columbus, from heroic explorer to symbol of colonial violence, illustrates how dominant narratives can be completely reversed as perspectives shift.

**The agency of historical actors**: The phrase tends to treat historical subjects as passive victims whose perspectives are simply erased by powerful elites. In reality, marginalized groups often actively work to preserve their own histories through oral traditions, cultural practices, and institutional memory, even under oppressive conditions.

**Methodological sophistication**: Modern historiography has developed sophisticated tools for dealing with bias, incomplete sources, and power dynamics. While these methods don't eliminate all forms of bias, they represent a significant advance over simplistic assumptions about whose voices get heard.

The epistemological problem with "history is written by the victors" is that it functions as an anti-epistemological device — it suggests that all historical claims are equally suspect without providing tools for distinguishing between more and less reliable accounts. This leads to a kind of historical nihilism where evidence-based scholarship is dismissed as mere propaganda while alternative narratives are accepted uncritically simply because they challenge mainstream accounts.


## 4. Verdict

**AUGMENTED**: "History is written by the victors" contains a grain of truth about power dynamics in historical narrative construction, but mischaracterizes how historical knowledge is actually produced, transmitted, and revised.

The maxim correctly identifies that power relationships can influence which voices are preserved and which perspectives dominate at particular moments. Political authorities, cultural elites, and institutional gatekeepers do shape historical narratives in ways that can favor their interests and perspectives.

However, the phrase substantially overstates victor control while understating the complexity of historical narrative formation:

**Counterexample abundance**: Many dominant historical narratives have been shaped by defeated groups (Lost Cause mythology), marginalized voices (Holocaust historiography), or explicit critics of power (postcolonial scholarship).

**Methodological evolution**: Modern historiography has developed sophisticated tools for identifying bias, recovering marginalized voices, and challenging dominant narratives. Professional historical practice explicitly works against victor's bias.

**Temporal dynamics**: Historical narratives evolve over time as values change, new evidence emerges, and different groups gain influence. Today's "victor's history" can become tomorrow's discredited propaganda.

**Institutional complexity**: Narrative control involves multiple competing institutions — universities, schools, media, popular culture, family traditions — rather than simple top-down victor dominance.

**Evidence-based standards**: Historical claims are evaluated based on evidence, methodology, and peer review, not simply on who tells the story. These standards are imperfect but represent genuine constraints on narrative construction.

The augmentation reveals how the maxim functions as a thought-terminating cliché that discourages engagement with actual historical evidence and methodology. While healthy skepticism toward official accounts is warranted, wholesale dismissal based on assumed victor bias can lead to historical nihilism or uncritical acceptance of alternative narratives simply because they oppose mainstream accounts.

The reality is more complex and more hopeful: historical understanding emerges from ongoing processes of evidence gathering, methodological refinement, and scholarly debate. These processes are influenced by power dynamics but not determined by them. Understanding this complexity is essential for developing the kind of sophisticated historical thinking that can distinguish between legitimate criticism of bias and simplistic rejection of evidence-based inquiry.

History is written by historians, using methods designed to identify and correct for various forms of bias including but not limited to victor's bias. The quality of historical understanding depends not on who wins political conflicts, but on how well these methods are applied and how seriously the historical community takes its responsibility to seek truth rather than serve power.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The "victors write history" phenomenon connects to broader patterns in how simplistic maxims can crowd out more sophisticated understanding of complex systems. Similar phrases — "follow the money," "power corrupts," "those who don't learn from history are doomed to repeat it" — offer apparent wisdom while actually discouraging deeper analysis.

These maxims share several characteristics:

**Surface plausibility**: They contain enough truth to feel insightful while obscuring important complexities and exceptions.

**Cynical realism**: They suggest that naive people accept official explanations while sophisticated people see through to underlying power dynamics.

**Analytical shortcuts**: They provide simple explanations for complex phenomena, reducing the cognitive work required to understand difficult topics.

**Rhetorical power**: They can be deployed to end rather than advance discussion, functioning as conversation-stoppers rather than analytical tools.

In the specific case of historical understanding, the "victors write history" maxim interacts with several contemporary trends that make it particularly appealing:

**Distrust of institutions**: Declining trust in educational institutions, media, and government makes people more receptive to explanations that portray official accounts as propaganda.

**Information proliferation**: The vast increase in available information makes it easier to find sources that support almost any historical interpretation, reducing the apparent authority of professional historians.

**Political polarization**: Contested contemporary events make people more aware of how different groups can interpret the same events differently, leading to skepticism about the possibility of objective historical accounts.

**Identity politics**: Growing attention to how marginalized groups have been excluded from historical narratives creates receptivity to claims about systematic bias in historical knowledge.

These trends aren't necessarily problematic — increased skepticism toward authority and attention to marginalized voices can improve historical understanding. But they become problematic when they lead to wholesale rejection of evidence-based methodology in favor of whatever narrative feels politically or emotionally satisfying.

The challenge for historical education involves helping people develop sophisticated skepticism that can distinguish between legitimate criticism of historical bias and simplistic rejection of historical methodology. This requires understanding how modern historiography actually works, what its limitations are, and how it has evolved to address earlier problems with elite-centered narratives.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed the accuracy of "history is written by the victors" as a general principle about historiography. The phrase exists in a difficult category for traditional fact-checking: it's not a specific factual claim about particular events, but rather a broad generalization about how historical knowledge is produced and transmitted.

This absence reflects limitations in current fact-checking approaches to evaluating meta-claims about knowledge systems. While Snopes excels at verifying discrete facts and debunking false attributions, it typically doesn't engage with epistemological claims about entire academic disciplines. The quote's misattribution to Churchill represents the kind of specific error Snopes might address, but the broader claim about historical methodology falls outside their usual scope.

The lack of fact-checking attention doesn't indicate the claim's validity — rather, it highlights how assumptions about knowledge production often escape systematic scrutiny despite their widespread influence on public discourse about history and truth.
