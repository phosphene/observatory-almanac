# 'Hocus Pocus' Prequel Series in Works at Disney?

## The Claim

In mid-2025, social media posts and several entertainment news outlets began circulating the claim that Disney was developing a *Hocus Pocus* prequel series for its streaming platform, Disney+. The claim typically appeared in headlines framed with a question mark — "Hocus Pocus Prequel Series in Works at Disney?" — a structure that simultaneously asserts and hedges, inviting the reader to fill in the affirmative. Posts frequently accompanied the claim with promotional imagery from the 1993 original film and the 2022 sequel, *Hocus Pocus 2*, implying continuity of cast, tone, and creative direction.

The viral formulation went something like this: "Disney has officially announced a Hocus Pocus prequel series coming to Disney+." The word "officially" did heavy lifting here, as did "prequel," which carries specific narrative implications — namely, that the story would be set before the events of the 1993 film, potentially exploring the origins of the Sanderson Sisters in colonial Salem. Some versions of the claim went further, suggesting that Bette Midler, Sarah Jessica Parker, and Kathy Najimy were "attached" or "in talks" to reprise their roles, either as narrators, flashback participants, or through de-aging technology. Still other iterations conflated the announcement with the development of *Hocus Pocus 3*, which had been separately rumored since the release of the 2022 sequel.

The Snopes fact-check evaluated the core claim — that a *Hocus Pocus* prequel series was "in the works" at Disney — and rated it as largely accurate, while noting that certain elements of the social media framing overstated the degree of certainty and the specificity of what had actually been announced. This entry examines the claim in detail, situates it within the broader context of Disney's intellectual property strategy and streaming ambitions, and offers a more granular analysis of what Snopes got right and where its assessment leaves room for deeper scrutiny.

## What's Actually True

Disney did, through official channels, confirm that a *Hocus Pocus*-related television project was in development. The confirmation came not via a flashy press release or a D23 Expo panel — the venues where Disney typically unveils major franchise announcements — but through more modest corporate channels: a programming update shared with industry trade publications and a brief mention in a Disney+ content slate briefing. This is an important distinction. There is a meaningful difference, in the entertainment industry, between a project that has received a straight-to-series order and one that is merely "in development," a status that can encompass everything from a signed writer's room to a single pitch document sitting on an executive's desk.

What Disney actually confirmed was that a television project set in the *Hocus Pocus* universe was in active development at Disney+. The project was described as exploring the backstory of the Sanderson Sisters — which is, definitionally, a prequel concept — but Disney's own language was careful and provisional. The word "prequel" appeared in some trade reporting but was not used in Disney's direct statement. Instead, the language was more along the lines of "a new series exploring the world of the Sanderson Sisters," which is capacious enough to include a prequel, a sidequel, a sequel-series, or even an anthology set in the same universe.

The creative team attached to the project, as reported by trade publications, included a writer with prior Disney+ credits and a production company with existing relationships at the studio. No cast announcements were made. No release date was specified. No episode count was confirmed. No production start date was announced. In the language of the industry, the project was at the "development deal" stage, meaning that Disney had committed resources to developing a script and bible, but had not yet greenlit the project for production. A development deal is not a greenlight. A greenlight is not a premiere date. And a premiere date is not a finished product on a screen.

It is also true that *Hocus Pocus 2*, released on Disney+ in September 2022, was a significant viewership success for the platform, drawing 2.7 billion streaming minutes in its opening weekend according to Nielsen data, and ranking among the most-watched streaming original films of that year. This commercial performance made a follow-up project commercially logical, if not creatively inevitable. Disney's incentive to extend the *Hocus Pocus* franchise into serial television is straightforward: the property has demonstrated audience demand, it fits the Halloween seasonal programming slot, and it leverages existing IP in a way that minimizes the marketing cost of launching a new title from scratch.

So: is a *Hocus Pocus* television project in development at Disney? Yes, confirmed. Is it a "prequel"? Probably, based on the described creative direction, but the word carries more narrative specificity than Disney has officially confirmed. Is it "coming to Disney+"? That is the intent, but development deals do not always result in produced shows. Projects are shelved, redeveloped, rewritten, and sometimes abandoned entirely. The claim, as circulated on social media, compressed several stages of the Hollywood pipeline into a single declarative announcement, and it is this compression that Snopes identified — correctly — as the site of potential misrepresentation.

## What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes rated the claim as "Mostly True," which is a reasonable assessment given the underlying facts. Disney did announce a development deal for a *Hocus Pocus*-related series. The series does appear to explore the Sanderson Sisters' backstory, which makes the "prequel" label substantively accurate even if Disney has not used that exact term. And the project is, genuinely, "in the works" — a phrase capacious enough to cover everything from a signed contract to a table read.

Where Snopes succeeds is in identifying the gap between the social media framing and the corporate reality. The viral posts presented the project as a fait accompli: a *Hocus Pocus* prequel series, confirmed, coming soon, with the original cast potentially involved. The reality is more provisional. Development is not production. An announced series is not a scheduled series. And the involvement of the original cast — which Snopes correctly notes was not confirmed — remains speculative, driven more by fan desire than by any official statement.

However, Snopes' analysis falls short in several respects that merit attention.

First, Snopes does not adequately contextualize the *Hocus Pocus* claim within Disney's broader pattern of franchise extension announcements. Disney has, over the past decade, made a strategic habit of announcing development deals as though they were premiere dates. The Disney+ launch in 2019 was accompanied by a slate of announced Marvel and Star Wars series, many of which were described in language that suggested imminent production. Several of these projects were subsequently delayed, retooled, or quietly shelved. The *Hocus Pocus* series announcement follows a familiar pattern: maximize the public relations value of an early-stage development deal by allowing the framing to imply more certainty than the deal itself warrants. Snopes treats this as a minor exaggeration in social media framing, when it is more accurately understood as a structural feature of how Disney and other studios manage public expectations around their content pipelines.

Second, Snopes does not interrogate the word "prequel" with sufficient rigor. The term has become a marketing shorthand that encompasses a wide range of narrative approaches. A prequel can be a direct origin story (e.g., *Rise of the Planet of the Apes*), a same-universe period piece (e.g., *Fantastic Beasts and Where to Find Them*), or a reimagination of existing events from a different perspective (e.g., *Wicked*). When social media posts claim "prequel," they imply a specific narrative promise — that viewers will see the Sanderson Sisters in their pre-1693 Salem years, learning magic, forming their coven, and perhaps confronting the events that led to their execution and resurrection. Disney has not confirmed any of this. The series could, for all we know, be set in the present day, exploring a new generation of Sandersons, or it could be an anthology format that moves across time periods. The assumption that "backstory" equals "prequel set in colonial Salem" is a fan projection, not a corporate commitment. Snopes accepts the prequel framing too readily, treating it as a reasonable interpretation rather than an unsupported inference.

Third, Snopes' treatment of the cast involvement question is correct in substance but incomplete in analysis. The fact-check correctly notes that no cast members have been confirmed for the series. But it does not explore why the rumor of original-cast involvement spread so readily, or what function it serves in the viral economy of entertainment news. The answer is that *Hocus Pocus* is, for a significant segment of its audience, inseparable from the performances of Midler, Parker, and Najimy. The 2022 sequel demonstrated this: the film was widely criticized for its screenplay and pacing, but the return of the three leads was uniformly celebrated. A *Hocus Pocus* project without the original Sanderson Sisters is, for many fans, not really *Hocus Pocus* at all. The rumor of cast involvement is therefore not a random embellishment but a structurally motivated inference: fans need the cast to return in order for the project to be legible as a continuation of the franchise. Snopes does not address this dynamic, treating the cast rumor as a discrete factual claim rather than as a symptom of the audience's relationship to the property.

Finally, Snopes could have more explicitly addressed the reliability of the entertainment trade press ecosystem in which such claims originate and circulate. Publications like *Deadline*, *Variety*, and *The Hollywood Reporter* operate on a combination of confirmed studio statements and sourced industry gossip. When *Deadline* reports that a project is "in development," that reporting is typically accurate — but the studio's strategic use of trade publications as amplification channels for development-stage announcements is itself a phenomenon worth examining. Studios leak development news to trades to test audience interest, to signal to talent representatives that a project is active (thereby attracting writers and actors), and to generate content for their own streaming platforms' marketing pipelines. The trades, for their part, have an incentive to report development deals as news because development news generates clicks, and clicks generate advertising revenue. This symbiotic ecosystem produces a steady stream of announcements that are factually accurate but contextually misleading — true in their specifics, but inflated in their implied significance. Snopes operates primarily at the level of factual verification, not systemic analysis, and this limitation is visible in its treatment of the *Hocus Pocus* claim.

## Why This Matters

The *Hocus Pocus* prequel claim matters not because of its specific subject matter — a Halloween comedy about three witches is, on its face, culturally lightweight — but because it is a representative case of how entertainment news is produced, circulated, and consumed in the contemporary media environment. The claim's trajectory, from trade report to social media amplification to fact-check, illustrates a pipeline in which each stage adds certainty and removes nuance.

At the origin point, a trade publication reports that Disney has a development deal for a *Hocus Pocus*-related series. This report is accurate. The trade uses careful language — "in development," "exploring," "attached to write" — that reflects the provisional nature of the deal. At the amplification stage, social media accounts and secondary news outlets pick up the trade report, strip the hedging language, and present the deal as a definitive announcement. Headlines deploy question marks to maintain plausible deniability while their framing implies confirmation. At the consumption stage, audiences encounter the claim in a format — a tweet, a TikTok, a listicle — that provides no access to the original trade reporting and its qualifiers. The fact-check, arriving late and at a different scale, can verify the core claim but cannot reverse the interpretive frame that has already been established.

This pipeline matters because it shapes audience expectations and, by extension, corporate behavior. When a development deal is received by the public as a confirmed series, the studio faces pressure to deliver on expectations it did not explicitly set. Projects that might have been quietly shelved are kept on life support because the public relations cost of cancellation is now higher than the cost of continued development. Conversely, projects that are genuinely proceeding toward production may be perceived as stalled if they do not produce the expected cadence of casting announcements, teaser trailers, and release date confirmations. The entertainment information ecosystem creates a feedback loop between audience expectation and corporate strategy, and the loop is tightened by the speed of social media circulation.

The *Hocus Pocus* case also matters because it illustrates the specific dynamics of Disney's intellectual property management. Disney is, at its core, an IP company. Its business model depends on the continuous extraction of value from a portfolio of owned properties — Marvel, Star Wars, Pixar, Disney Animation, and an expanding catalog of acquired franchises including *Hocus Pocus*. The streaming era has intensified this dynamic. Disney+ requires a constant supply of content to justify its subscription price, and the most cost-effective content, from Disney's perspective, is content derived from properties the audience already knows and loves. This is why Disney+ is saturated with series derived from existing IP: *Loki*, *The Mandalorian*, *Ahsoka*, *Percy Jackson*, *National Treasure: Edge of History*, and on and on. A *Hocus Pocus* series fits this pattern precisely — it is a recognizable property with a built-in audience, a seasonal marketing hook (Halloween), and a flexible enough narrative universe to support serial expansion.

Understanding this pattern is essential to media literacy in the streaming era. When a studio announces a development deal for an existing IP, the announcement is not merely informational — it is a strategic communication designed to signal commitment to the IP, to retain audience interest in the franchise between major releases, and to populate the streaming platform's content slate with recognizable titles. The *Hocus Pocus* announcement, in this reading, is less a promise to deliver a specific television series and more a signal that Disney intends to continue exploiting the property across media and formats for the foreseeable future. Whether the specific series currently in development becomes a produced show is, in a sense, secondary to the function the announcement serves in Disney's portfolio strategy.

This is not a cynical reading. It is a structural one. Disney's decision to develop a *Hocus Pocus* series is a rational response to market conditions: the property has proven commercial value, the streaming platform needs content, and the seasonal slot is commercially valuable. But understanding the structural logic of the announcement helps explain why development-stage deals are communicated with language that implies more certainty than they warrant, and why social media amplification of these announcements is not a bug in the system but a feature of it. The studios benefit from inflated audience expectations because expectations drive engagement, and engagement drives subscriptions. The social media ecosystem benefits because definitive-sounding announcements generate more engagement than provisional ones. And the audience — the party with the least agency in this dynamic — is left to navigate a landscape of technically-true but contextually-misleading claims.

## Research Notes

Research for this entry drew on several categories of source material. The primary trade reporting on the *Hocus Pocus* development deal was sourced from *Deadline* and *Variety*, both of which published initial reports based on information provided by Disney corporate communications and industry sources. These reports were supplemented by Disney's own investor relations materials and Disney+ content slate briefings, which mentioned the project in passing rather than as a headline announcement — a placement choice that itself reflects the project's developmental stage.

Box office and streaming performance data for *Hocus Pocus* (1993) and *Hocus Pocus 2* (2022) was sourced from Box Office Mojo, Nielsen Streaming Ratings, and Disney's quarterly earnings reports. The 1993 film's theatrical performance — a modest $39 million domestic gross on a $28 million budget, followed by robust home video and cable television performance — is relevant because it illustrates the property's unusual trajectory: a theatrical underperformer that became a cultural perennial through repeat viewing and seasonal programming. This trajectory is itself a factor in Disney's decision to extend the franchise; the property's value was not fully recognized at the point of original release but accrued over time through cultural osmosis.

Contextual data on Disney's streaming content strategy was drawn from Disney's annual reports (fiscal years 2020–2025), earnings call transcripts, and analysis from media industry research firms including MoffettNathanson and Antenna. These sources establish the broader economic context in which the *Hocus Pocus* series announcement should be read: Disney+ has faced persistent profitability challenges, and the platform's strategy has shifted from volume acquisition (the "content arms race" phase of 2019–2022) to selective franchise exploitation (the "quality over quantity" phase that began in 2023). A *Hocus Pocus* series, as a relatively low-cost, high-recognition property, fits the latter strategy more cleanly than it would have fit the former.

Snopes' fact-check, published approximately two weeks after the initial viral circulation of the claim, was assessed against the trade reporting and Disney's corporate communications. Snopes' sourcing methodology — which prioritizes primary documents and direct statements over secondary reporting — is appropriate for its function but, as noted above, limits its ability to address systemic patterns in entertainment industry communication. The fact-check's rating of "Mostly True" is defensible on the facts; this entry's critique is not that Snopes erred in its rating, but that the rating system itself does not capture the full dimension of the claim's relationship to truth.

Several aspects of this case remain genuinely uncertain and are flagged here for future monitoring. The creative direction of the series has not been publicly specified beyond the broad "Sanderson Sisters backstory" description. The involvement of the original cast has not been confirmed or denied. The production timeline has not been disclosed. The series' relationship to *Hocus Pocus 3*, which has been separately discussed by Disney executives in earnings contexts, is unclear — it is possible that the series and the third film are the same project, that they are distinct projects, or that one has been folded into the other during development. These uncertainties are not defects in the reporting; they are inherent features of the development stage, during which projects are actively being shaped and definitions are in flux.

## Verdict

The claim that a *Hocus Pocus* prequel series is "in the works" at Disney is **substantively true but contextually inflated**. Disney has confirmed the existence of a development deal for a *Hocus Pocus*-related television series for Disney+, and the described creative direction — exploring the Sanderson Sisters' backstory — makes the "prequel" label a reasonable, if not officially confirmed, characterization. Snopes' rating of "Mostly True" is appropriate at the level of factual verification.

However, the claim as circulated on social media implied a degree of certainty and specificity that the underlying facts do not support. The project is in development, not in production. No cast has been announced. No release date has been set. The word "prequel" carries narrative implications that Disney has not explicitly confirmed. And the viral framing — "officially announced," "coming to Disney+" — compressed the multi-stage Hollywood production pipeline into a single definitive statement, eliding the substantial gap between a development deal and a finished television series.

More importantly, the claim should be understood not merely as a factual proposition to be verified or debunked, but as a data point in a larger system of entertainment industry communication. Disney's strategic use of development-stage announcements to maintain audience engagement with its IP portfolio, the symbiotic relationship between studios and trade publications, and the amplification dynamics of social media all contribute to an information environment in which technically-true claims function as misleading promises. The *Hocus Pocus* prequel series may well materialize — the commercial logic is sound, the creative direction is promising, and Disney's track record of converting development deals into produced content for its streaming platform is reasonably strong. But the claim as circulated does not accurately represent the stage of the project, the specificity of the creative direction, or the inherent uncertainty of the development process. It is, in the language of this Truth Vault, a **distorted-but-grounded** claim: rooted in a genuine factual basis, but shaped by the communicative dynamics of the entertainment media ecosystem into something more certain, more specific, and more imminent than the reality warrants.

The lesson for media consumers is not that entertainment announcements should be disbelieved, but that they should be read with an understanding of what stage of the production pipeline they describe. "In development" is not "in production." "Exploring the backstory" is not "a prequel set in colonial Salem." And a development deal, however genuinely confirmed, is a promise to try — not a promise to deliver.
