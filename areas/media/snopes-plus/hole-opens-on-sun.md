# Yep, This Is a Real (and Stunning) Image of the Sun

## §1 — Claim & Verdict

In December 2023, a visually arresting image shared to the social platform X (formerly Twitter) accumulated more than two million views, accompanied by the caption "The Sun is NOT ok right now…" The image depicts the solar disk with a vast, dark expanse dominating roughly one face of the star — a feature that could easily strike a viewer as alarming, suggestive of apocalyptic solar failure, a mysterious void, or even CGI manipulation. The post was authored by Dr. Brian Keating, an astrophysicist and University of California San Diego professor, though even his framing implied something potentially abnormal.

Snopes contributor Madison Dapcevich investigated and rated the claim — that the image is a genuine photograph of the sun — **True**. The image was confirmed as captured on December 2, 2023, at 02:49:16 UTC by NASA's Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO), using its Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA) instrument at the 193-Ångström wavelength. Critically, Dapcevich established through source tracing a second important fact: **the image depicts something entirely normal**, specifically a coronal hole — a recurrent, well-understood feature of the solar corona that carries no threat of catastrophe.

This entry examines the verified source of the image, the physical nature of coronal holes, the role of the Solar Dynamics Observatory in continuous solar monitoring, the space-weather implications of coronal-hole activity, and the broader epistemic landscape of viral science imagery and misleading framing.

## §2 — Mechanism: What Is a Coronal Hole?

The dark region visible in the SDO image is a **coronal hole** — a large area of the solar corona where the magnetic field structure is fundamentally different from surrounding regions, producing a feature that is cooler, less dense, and visually darker in specific wavelength bands.

To understand this requires a brief primer on the Sun's structure. The Sun's corona — its outermost atmospheric layer — extends millions of kilometers into space and has a temperature that paradoxically exceeds the surface temperature by factors of hundreds to thousands (a phenomenon called the coronal heating problem, which remains an active area of solar physics research; see Klimchuk, J.A., 2006, "On solving the coronal heating problem," *Solar Physics*, 234(1), 41–77). The corona is a highly dynamic plasma environment permeated by complex magnetic field structures.

In most regions of the corona, closed magnetic field lines loop back to the solar surface, trapping plasma and creating the bright, dense structures visible in coronal imaging. In coronal holes, however, the magnetic field lines are **open** — they extend far outward into interplanetary space rather than looping back. This open topology allows the Sun's plasma to stream freely outward as the solar wind rather than being held in place. Because plasma escapes freely from coronal holes rather than being retained, these regions have lower density and lower temperature than surrounding closed-field regions, rendering them dark in the extreme ultraviolet (EUV) and X-ray wavelengths used by instruments like SDO's AIA (Phillips, K.J.H., Feldman, U., and Landi, E., 2008, *Ultraviolet and X-ray Spectroscopy of the Solar Atmosphere*, Cambridge University Press).

The 193-Å channel of the AIA instrument captures emission primarily from Fe XII ions — iron atoms that have lost eleven electrons, indicating a temperature of approximately 1.5 million Kelvin. In this channel, coronal holes appear as distinct dark regions precisely because they contain far less hot plasma per unit volume than the surrounding corona.

Coronal holes are normal features of the sun. They occur at all phases of the solar cycle, are most frequently found near the sun's poles (where they can persist for years during solar minimum), and can also appear at lower latitudes as transient structures. The December 2023 coronal hole visible in the viral image was a large but not unprecedented structure. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's Space Weather Prediction Center (NOAA/SWPC) routinely tracks and forecasts coronal hole activity as part of its operational space weather monitoring mission (NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center, 2023, "Coronal Holes," https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/phenomena/coronal-holes).

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The SDO Data Archive and Verification Methodology

A particularly instructive dimension of this fact-check is the verification methodology Dapcevich employed. Rather than simply accepting the image on face value or relying on the social media poster's identity, she traced the image back to its original source using metadata embedded in the image itself.

The image bore a watermark-style annotation reading "SDO/AIA- 193 2023-12-02 02:49:16" — a timestamp and instrument identifier. Dapcevich used these parameters to search the SDO data archive directly, locating not just the still image but the full 500-frame movie from which it was drawn. This is a model example of **forensic image provenance tracing**, a verification technique increasingly important as AI-generated imagery and manipulated media proliferate online.

The SDO archive serves as a critical public scientific resource here. The Solar Dynamics Observatory was launched on February 11, 2010, by NASA and placed into a geosynchronous orbit specifically designed to allow continuous, uninterrupted coverage of the sun. Its Atmospheric Imaging Assembly captures full-disk images of the solar corona and transition region across ten different EUV and UV wavelength channels every 12 seconds, producing what amounts to the highest-cadence continuous record of solar activity ever assembled. In its first decade of operation, SDO collected more than 20 million gigabytes of data (NASA Goddard Space Flight Center, 2022, "Solar Dynamics Observatory: 10 Years of Watching the Sun," public release video).

For science communicators and fact-checkers, the SDO archive's public accessibility represents a key resource: any image timestamped and attributed to SDO/AIA can be independently verified by querying the archive. That such an archive exists, is current to within seconds, and is publicly available at no cost is itself noteworthy. The broader epistemic point for Truth Vault analysis: **official, continuous, machine-generated scientific archives are qualitatively more reliable as verification targets than retrospective human-curated collections**, because the data volume and automated collection process make post-hoc insertion of fabricated images essentially impossible at scale.

The epistemic novelty of this entry is thus the verification chain itself: viral image → metadata → instrument archive → corroborating movie → physical explanation. This chain represents a repeatable fact-checking template for claims about astronomical imagery that should be more widely known among science communicators.

## §4 — Space Weather Implications of Coronal Holes

Though the December 2023 coronal hole posed no threat to life on Earth, coronal holes are not merely astronomical curiosities — they have practical, operationally important space weather consequences that justify the attention NOAA and NASA devote to tracking them.

When open magnetic field lines in a coronal hole allow solar wind plasma to stream outward unimpeded, the result is a high-speed solar wind stream traveling at velocities that can exceed 700–800 km/s, compared to the 400 km/s typical of the ambient solar wind (Cranmer, S.R., 2009, "Coronal holes," *Living Reviews in Solar Physics*, 6(1), 3). These high-speed streams do not produce the dramatic explosive energy releases of coronal mass ejections (CMEs), but they generate sustained enhancements in solar wind pressure and magnetic field strength that can trigger **geomagnetic storms** when they interact with Earth's magnetosphere.

Geomagnetic storms from high-speed wind streams — classified by NOAA's Kp geomagnetic index from G1 (minor) to G5 (extreme) — can produce measurable effects including: enhanced aurora visible at lower latitudes than usual; disruption to GPS navigation systems through ionospheric distortion; increased satellite drag from upper atmosphere density changes; and, in cases of severe storms, induced currents in long power transmission lines that can trip protective systems (Moldwin, M., 2008, *An Introduction to Space Weather*, Cambridge University Press). The December 2023 coronal hole was large enough that NOAA forecasters were monitoring it for potential G1–G2 storm conditions in the days following the SDO image.

This context is important for calibrating public communication about the image. The viral framing — "The Sun is NOT ok right now" — was technically false in its implication that something abnormal was occurring, yet the underlying concern about solar-terrestrial interaction is scientifically grounded. Coronal holes *do* have real effects; they simply are not the dramatic or catastrophic events implied by alarm-filled viral framing.

The pattern here is a common one in science communication: a real and interesting phenomenon (large coronal hole, high-speed solar wind stream, potential geomagnetic storm) becomes entangled with an inaccurate characterization (the sun is broken, the image shows something dangerous) through aggregative reposting. The scientifically literate correction is more nuanced than "nothing to see here" — it is "something real is happening, and here is what it actually is and what it actually might do."

## §5 — SDO, AIA, and the Architecture of Solar Monitoring

It is worth taking stock of what the Solar Dynamics Observatory represents as an instrument, because the architecture of its data collection is directly relevant to how images like this one can be verified.

The SDO spacecraft houses three science instruments: the Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA), the Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager (HMI), and the Extreme Ultraviolet Variability Experiment (EVE). Together, these instruments are designed to address fundamental questions about the solar dynamo, solar variability, and the Sun's influence on near-Earth space.

AIA, built by the Lockheed Martin Solar and Astrophysics Laboratory under contract to NASA, uses four independent telescope assemblies with different optical coatings to image the full solar disk simultaneously at seven EUV wavelengths spanning temperatures from ~5×10⁴ K (the chromosphere) to ~2×10⁷ K (flare-heated plasma), plus three UV/visible channels. Its spatial resolution of 0.6 arcseconds per pixel translates to approximately 435 km on the solar surface, and its 0.75-second cadence between full-disk images makes it the highest-combination time-and-space resolution solar EUV imager ever flown (Lemen, J.R. et al., 2012, "The Atmospheric Imaging Assembly (AIA) on the Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO)," *Solar Physics*, 275(1–2), 17–40). The 193-Å channel used in the viral image is sensitive primarily to the quiet corona and active-region plasma at ~1.5 MK and to hotter plasma (>20 MK) during flares.

HMI measures the Doppler shifts and intensities of a photospheric spectral line to produce continuous measurements of solar surface velocity fields and magnetic vectors — enabling helioseismology (the study of sound waves propagating through the solar interior) and magnetogram time series that track the Sun's magnetic field evolution throughout the solar cycle (Scherrer, P.H. et al., 2012, "The Helioseismic and Magnetic Imager (HMI) Investigation for the Solar Dynamics Observatory," *Solar Physics*, 275(1–2), 207–227).

EVE measures the EUV spectral irradiance of the Sun — the total energy output in extreme ultraviolet bands — at a 10-second cadence, providing the dataset needed to characterize how and why the Sun's radiative output varies over seconds (during flares) to months (over the solar cycle) to decades (Didkovsky, L. et al., 2012, "EUV SpectroPhotometer (ESP) in EVE on the Solar Dynamics Observatory," *Solar Physics*, 275(1–2), 179–205).

Together, these instruments provide a comprehensive, self-consistent picture of the Sun's state at any given moment, and their data have been used in thousands of peer-reviewed studies. The viral image from December 2023 is thus not an isolated frame from an amateur's telescope; it is a single snapshot from a scientific monitor that never blinks.

## §6 — Verdict Assessment, Evidence Classification, and Gaps

**EARC classification: E** (Empirical — the claim is evaluated against documented instrument data and archives, and the supporting mechanism is established solar physics).

**Snopes verdict: True.** This verdict is correct and well-supported. The image provenance is unambiguous: the SDO archive contains the original data, the metadata is consistent with the claimed timestamp, and the physical phenomenon depicted (a coronal hole) is textbook solar physics.

**The secondary implicit claim** — that what is depicted is dangerous or abnormal — is **False** as a categorical statement, though with the important nuance that large coronal holes do have real, forecasted space weather effects.

**Verification confidence: Very high.** The SDO archive is a continuous, publicly accessible, machine-generated data record. The metadata-to-archive traceability chain is reproducible by anyone with internet access to the SDO data browser.

**Epistemic gaps:**

1. **Contextual space weather impact reporting:** The Snopes article confirms the image is genuine and the coronal hole is normal, but does not report what geomagnetic storm conditions (if any) actually resulted from this specific coronal-hole high-speed stream arrival at Earth in the days following December 2. A complete account should note whether NOAA observed and reported a G1–G2 or stronger storm in the December 4–7, 2023 window, which would close the loop from image to real-world consequence.

2. **Coronal heating mechanism context:** The article correctly states that coronal holes are cooler and less dense than surrounding corona but does not engage with the still-unresolved question of why the corona is heated to millions of degrees at all. This is scientifically adjacent but not strictly necessary for verification; it is noted here as a gap for readers seeking deeper physical grounding.

3. **Scale communication:** The image does not include a scale bar or contextual size reference. A supplementary note on the actual physical scale of the December 2023 coronal hole relative to, say, the Earth–Sun distance would significantly improve public understanding of what is being depicted.

**Bottom line:** The image is real, verified, and depicts a normal solar phenomenon. The viral framing ("NOT ok") is misleading but not entirely baseless: coronal holes do emit enhanced solar wind with real space weather effects. The proper scientific correction is informative rather than dismissive — the sun has a large coronal hole, here is what it is, here is what it does.
