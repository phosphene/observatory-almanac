# Is This Really How Margarine Is Made?


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: A graphic produced and distributed by "Herbs Health Happiness" — a natural health advocacy page with a large social media following — and titled "Do People That Eat Margarine Really Know How It's Manufactured?" depicted the margarine production process in a sequence of steps designed to appear alarming. The graphic described margarine as originating from industrial oil extraction using chemical solvents, followed by chemical hydrogenation, saponification, bleaching, and artificial flavoring, with each step labeled with vocabulary chosen for its negative connotations: "rancid," "soapy mix," "bleach," "unnatural."

The implicit claim of the graphic is that margarine production involves chemical processes so extreme and artificial that informed consumers would reject it — and, by extension, that "natural" alternatives (primarily butter) are preferable because their production is not subject to the same industrial chemical treatments. The graphic exemplifies a widespread genre of viral health content that uses technically accurate process descriptions combined with misleading nomenclature to generate disgust responses toward industrially processed food.

**Publication and context**: The graphic circulated widely on social media platforms prior to its Snopes review, published August 1, 2018, authored by Alex Kasprak. Kasprak reviewed each step in the infographic against published food chemistry literature, specifically including the comprehensive "Fats and Fatty Oils" chapter by Thomas et al. in *Ullmann's Encyclopedia of Industrial Chemistry* (Wiley-VCH, 2003, ISBN 9783527303854) and authoritative regulatory documents from the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the Environmental Protection Agency. His conclusion was "mixture" — the basic process description is substantially accurate, but the framing and several specific claims are misleading.

**The naturalistic fallacy in food discourse**: The margarine infographic is an instance of a specific rhetorical move common in natural health content: describing chemically mediated processes in food production using vocabulary drawn from hazardous chemical contexts to equate "industrial" with "dangerous." The implicit contrast is with "natural" food processing, which the graphic implies involves no comparable chemical complexity. Kasprak's analysis directly addresses this implicit comparison: if the butter production process is described with equivalent chemical vocabulary starting from the same plant material that feeds the cow, the resulting "butter infographic" looks at least as alarming as the margarine one.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Margarine: definition and ingredients**: Margarine is legally defined in United States federal regulation (Code of Federal Regulations, Title 21, Section 166.110) as a food product composed of at least 80% fat, in a form that is solid or semi-solid at temperature of use. The fat component may be derived from vegetable oils, animal fats, or dairy products. Contemporary commercial margarines are predominantly vegetable oil-based, using soybean, palm, palm kernel, canola, sunflower, or corn oil as primary components. Land O'Lakes' standard margarine sticks, for example, use a mixture of soybean, palm, and palm kernel oils.

**Step 1 — Solvent extraction**: The oils in the seeds or kernel sources must be extracted from the plant material. The most common commercial method is solvent extraction using hexane. Food-grade hexane — a highly purified form of the hydrocarbon — dissolves the oil from crushed plant material and is then removed through evaporation and steam cleaning. The Thomas et al. *Ullmann's* chapter describes this process in detail; the EPA has classified hexane as a hazardous air pollutant at industrial occupational exposure levels (Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry, Toxicological Profile for n-Hexane, 1999). The infographic's implication that toxic solvent residues remain in the final product is, however, dubious: hexane has a boiling point of 69°C and is removed at temperatures well below the subsequent processing temperatures, which exceed 100°C. Food safety monitoring routinely verifies that residual hexane levels in edible oils fall below regulatory thresholds.

The infographic's characterization of the odor produced during extraction as "rancid" is technically inaccurate. Rancidity in the scientific sense refers to the degradation of fats and oils through oxidation or hydrolysis into shorter-chain volatile fatty acid compounds that produce off-flavors and odors (Rahman MS, *Handbook of Food Preservation*, CRC Press, 2007, ISBN 1574446061). The extraction process does not involve rancidity; it produces undesirable odors from other compounds in the plant material (phospholipids, carotenoids, waxes), which are addressed in the subsequent refining and deodorization steps. The word "rancid" in the infographic is used because it sounds alarming, not because it accurately describes the chemistry.

**Step 2 — Refinement/deodorization**: Extracted oils contain various non-triglyceride components — gums, waxes, free fatty acids, phospholipids, pigments, and trace contaminants — that are removed through a sequence of washing, neutralization, and steam distillation steps collectively called "refining." The deodorization step (steam stripping under vacuum) removes volatile compounds responsible for off-flavors and odors. This step is also employed in the production of edible oils for direct use, including the vegetable oils sold in supermarkets, and is not specific to margarine production.

**Steps 3 and 4 — Hydrogenation**: The central chemical transformation in traditional margarine production is hydrogenation: the addition of molecular hydrogen across double bonds in fatty acid chains in the presence of a nickel or palladium catalyst at elevated temperature and pressure. The Thomas et al. *Ullmann's* chapter provides a comprehensive technical description. This process converts unsaturated fatty acids (comprising carbon-carbon double bonds) to saturated fatty acids (comprising only single bonds) by adding hydrogen atoms at the double bond sites. The practical effect is to raise the melting point of the oils, converting them from liquid vegetable oils to solid or semi-solid fats.

The infographic correctly identifies hydrogenation as a step and correctly notes the use of a nickel catalyst and high temperature/pressure conditions. Its implicit criticism is that these conditions sound industrial and therefore potentially dangerous. The relevant food safety issue — which Kasprak addresses directly — is not the hydrogenation conditions per se but the potential for *partial* hydrogenation to produce *trans* fatty acids. In partial hydrogenation, not all double bonds are converted; some are geometrically isomerized from the natural *cis* configuration to the *trans* configuration. Trans fatty acids have been robustly associated with cardiovascular disease outcomes: increased LDL ("bad") cholesterol and decreased HDL ("good") cholesterol (Willett WC et al., 1993, *Lancet*, vol. 341, pp. 581–585). The FDA determined in 2015 that partially hydrogenated oils are not Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) and required their phase-out from the U.S. food supply by 2018 (FDA Final Determination Regarding Partially Hydrogenated Oils). Modern margarine production uses either full hydrogenation (which produces saturated fats without trans fats) or interesterification (a different process that modifies the distribution of fatty acids across glycerol without hydrogenation) to avoid trans fat production. Van Dujin (2005), in *OCL* (vol. 12, pp. 422–426), reviewed technical approaches to trans reduction in modified fats.

**Step 5 — Saponification**: After hydrogenation, the oil mixture is treated with an alkali — typically potassium hydroxide (lye) — to convert free fatty acids to soap (fatty acid salts) as a byproduct. The infographic describes the oil as being mixed with a "soapy mixture," which Kasprak identifies as technically inaccurate: the lye (potassium hydroxide) is added to the oil to cause the saponification reaction, and soap is a *byproduct* of this reaction. The soap and other unwanted compounds are then separated and removed. The goal of this step is not to add soap but to purify the triglyceride mixture by removing free fatty acids that would affect the final product's flavor and stability. The "soapy" descriptor is technically accurate in the sense that soap is produced, but the infographic implies that soap remains in the final product, which is incorrect.

**Step 6 — Bleaching**: The pigments remaining in vegetable oils after extraction and refining — primarily chlorophyll and carotenoids — give the oil a yellow-to-dark color. Bleaching removes these pigments to produce the pale color expected in refined vegetable oils and margarines. Critically, as Kasprak notes, bleaching in the food industry does not involve bleach (sodium or calcium hypochlorite, the household disinfectant). The process instead uses activated bleaching earth (modified clay) or activated carbon, which adsorbs pigmented compounds from the oil (Richardson LL, 1978, *Journal of the American Oil Chemists' Society*, vol. 55, pp. 777–781). The infographic's use of "bleach" is misleading: it implies the addition of a chemically harsh oxidizing agent when the actual process uses inert clay or carbon.

**Pesticide residues**: Kasprak acknowledges that pesticide residues from the original plant material are likely to persist at low levels through the production process. In most regulatory jurisdictions, these residues are monitored and must fall below established tolerances (U.S. EPA pesticide tolerance regulations, 40 CFR Part 180). The infographic's claim about pesticide residues is therefore the strongest of its technically accurate points.

**The butter comparison**: Kasprak's most rhetorically powerful analytical move is to turn the infographic's implicit logic against itself. If the same chemical vocabulary applied to the margarine production process — from oil extraction to hydrogenated fat — is applied to the butter production process starting from the same plant material (crops → cow digestion → milk → churning), the resulting description is at least as alarming. Cows digest plant material through fermentation and enzymatic degradation involving "myriad chemicals, enzymes, hormones, and feces," producing milk through processes involving "some 'horrible' smells." The chemical complexity of butter production is no less than that of margarine; it is merely invisible because it takes place inside an animal rather than inside a factory. The choice to describe one process in clinical chemical language and the other in agrarian pastoral language is a rhetorical choice, not a scientific one.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty: the "naturalistic fallacy" as cognitive default in food perception**

The margarine infographic works because it exploits a deep cognitive default in how humans evaluate food safety and quality. Psychologists have documented that people across cultures tend to evaluate foods along a natural-to-artificial continuum, and that this dimension predicts food acceptance preferences more powerfully than chemical composition or nutritional content in many consumer decisions (Rozin P et al., 2004, *Appetite*, vol. 43, pp. 147–154). "Natural" foods are perceived as inherently safer, more nutritious, and more trustworthy than "artificial" or "processed" foods — a judgment that persists even when people are told the nutritional content is identical.

This cognitive default — which might be called the naturalistic food heuristic — evolved reasonably. For most of human evolutionary history, chemically processed food did not exist, and food that "smells wrong" or is unfamiliar in composition really was riskier than familiar traditional food. Evolutionary psychologist Paul Rozin (1987, *Psychological Inquiry*, vol. 3, pp. 23–41) describes "disgust" as the primary emotion mediating the naturalistic food heuristic: stimuli that resemble or are associated with contamination, rot, or industrial toxins trigger disgust even when they are actually safe. The margarine infographic is specifically designed to exploit this disgust response: hexane, lye, nickel catalyst, bleach — these words are drawn from contexts associated with industrial hazard and are guaranteed to activate the same disgust-based wariness that would be adaptive when evaluating genuinely contaminated food.

The epistemic novelty is precisely this: the naturalistic food heuristic is a genuine cognitive tool that works well in its evolved context but produces systematic error when applied to food chemistry that is industrial but safe. Understanding that "food-grade hexane" evaporates completely before the final product is consumed requires knowledge of boiling points and process validation that is not available to consumers relying on the infographic. Understanding that "bleaching" in food chemistry refers to clay adsorption rather than hypochlorite addition requires specialized vocabulary knowledge. The infographic exploits the gap between the connotations of technical words and their specific technical meanings.


## 4. The Broader Pattern

**The viral food-fear genre**: The "how [food X] is really made" genre of viral content is a large and well-established category of natural health and food fear content. Related examples include viral content describing how chicken nuggets, hot dogs, and processed cheese are made, in each case using processing descriptions and imagery designed to generate disgust responses. These pieces share a common structure: accurate process description + alarming vocabulary + implicit naturalistic alternative = consumer rejection of industrial food.

The trans fat issue provides a cautionary note about dismissing all such concerns as merely rhetorical. The concern about partial hydrogenation and trans fat production in margarine was scientifically valid: there is robust evidence that dietary trans fat intake increases cardiovascular disease risk (Willett WC et al., 1993; Mozaffarian D et al., 2006, *New England Journal of Medicine*, vol. 354, pp. 1601–1613). The FDA's 2015 determination that partially hydrogenated oils were not GRAS was based on scientific evidence gathered over decades. In this case, the alarm raised about an aspect of margarine production was scientifically justified. Modern margarine formulations — which have largely eliminated trans fats — are therefore different products, from a health perspective, than the margarines that prompted justified concerns in the 1990s and 2000s.

The lesson is nuanced: the infographic's alarmist framing is misleading, but the underlying food chemistry is genuinely worth understanding. Consumers are better served by accurate process descriptions with appropriate risk calibration — solvent extraction with food-grade hexane is not a significant source of dietary hexane exposure; partial hydrogenation was a real health concern that has been largely addressed; pesticide residues in vegetable oils are regulated and monitored — than by either the infographic's alarmism or by dismissing all food chemistry concerns.

**The regulatory evolution of margarine**: Margarine's regulatory history is itself illuminating. Introduced in the 1870s as a cheaper substitute for butter, margarine was subject to discriminatory taxation and labeling requirements in the United States at the behest of the dairy industry — restrictions that persisted until the Federal Margarine Act of 1950 (Oleomargarine Act) removed most barriers. The product was reformulated multiple times: early animal-fat-based margarines gave way to vegetable oil-based products, which in turn went through the trans fat transition. Contemporary margarines, reformulated to eliminate partially hydrogenated oils, have substantially different cardiovascular profiles than their predecessors.


## 5. Evidentiary Assessment

**Factual accuracy of the infographic's process description**: Partially accurate. The basic sequence of industrial margarine production — solvent extraction, hydrogenation with catalyst, saponification, bleaching, flavoring — corresponds to real industrial processes. The specific chemical identities (hexane as solvent, potassium hydroxide as alkali, nickel as hydrogenation catalyst) are broadly correct.

**Accuracy of the risk characterizations**: Substantially misleading. "Rancid" is used incorrectly. The claim that hexane residues persist in the final product is dubious. "Bleach" implies hypochlorite when clay/carbon is actually used. The soap/lye distinction conflates a process agent with a byproduct. Pesticide residue presence is real but is regulated.

**The trans fat claim**: The most legitimately concerning aspect of margarine production historically — trans fat production during partial hydrogenation — is addressed in Kasprak's analysis but is partly outdated: modern margarine production has largely eliminated partially hydrogenated oils following FDA regulatory action.

**Known limitations**: Kasprak's analysis addresses the infographic's specific steps but does not comprehensively address all aspects of contemporary commercial margarine production, which varies by manufacturer and formulation. The butter comparison, while rhetorically powerful, is qualitatively argued rather than quantitatively analyzed.


## 6. Verdict and Implications

Snopes rates this claim a **Mixture**: the margarine production process described in the infographic is broadly real, but key claims are inaccurate and the framing is systematically misleading. The hexane solvent is food-grade and removed before consumption; "bleaching" uses clay rather than bleach; the "soapy mix" is a byproduct that is removed, not an ingredient. The implied comparison — that margarine is chemically contaminated while "natural" alternatives like butter are not — fails when applied consistently, because butter production involves equivalent chemical complexity when described from the same starting point.

The broader implication is for how consumers should engage with viral food chemistry content. The naturalistic food heuristic — the intuition that "industrial = unsafe" and "natural = safe" — is a cognitive default with evolutionary roots that does not reliably track actual food safety. Industrial food processes are subject to regulatory oversight, validated safety testing, and monitoring for contaminants; "natural" processes are not exempt from chemical complexity and may themselves produce or concentrate harmful compounds. Critical evaluation of viral food chemistry content requires distinguishing between accurate process description and misleading vocabulary choices — recognizing that "hexane" means something different in a food chemistry context than in a hazardous waste context, and that "bleaching" in an edible oil context does not mean the same thing as "bleach" in a home cleaning context. This vocabulary-gap exploitation is a standard technique in viral food fear content and can be identified and defused with basic food chemistry literacy.
