## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In March 2021, a video circulated widely on social media purporting to show the amount of sugar contained in a single Cadbury Creme Egg. The video, originally shared by Michael I. Goran — the Program Director for Diabetes and Obesity at The Saban Research Institute and author of the popular science book *Sugarproof* — depicted a demonstrator measuring out teaspoons of sugar to correspond with the stated sugar content of the product. The video's viral spread was accompanied by widespread claims from social media users that they were shocked or horrified by the amount of sugar in the iconic Easter confection.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2021) assigned this claim the rating **True**. The video accurately depicted the approximate sugar content of a Cadbury Creme Egg. According to Cadbury's own product information, a single Creme Egg contains 26.5 grams of sugar. The video's demonstrator measured approximately six and a half teaspoons, which at approximately 4.2 grams per teaspoon yields approximately 27 grams — just over the officially stated 26.5 grams, a negligible measurement variance. Additionally, the viral tweet's claim that the sugar content of a Creme Egg exceeds the recommended daily added sugar intake for children was evaluated as "generally accurate" against the American Heart Association's recommendation that children aged 2–18 should consume no more than 25 grams of added sugar per day.

This entry examines the product's sugar content in nutritional context, the cyclical nature of social media shock reactions to this specific claim, the scientific context of Michael Goran's work on sugar and children's health, the broader landscape of consumer attitudes toward sugar in processed foods, and the epistemics of nutritional information claims that are repeatedly "discovered" and re-discovered by different audience cohorts.

## §2 The Cadbury Creme Egg: Product Composition and Sugar Content

The Cadbury Creme Egg is a confection that has been manufactured by Cadbury (now a division of Mondelēz International in most markets) since 1963. The product consists of a chocolate shell — in its current formulation, milk chocolate — filled with a sweet fondant "yolk and white" center designed to mimic the appearance of a raw egg's interior. The filling is composed primarily of sugar, corn syrup, and inverted sugar syrup, with artificial flavoring and coloring to achieve the yellow yolk appearance.

The macronutrient composition of a standard UK Cadbury Creme Egg (40 grams), as listed on Cadbury's official website, is approximately: 177 calories, 5.5 grams of fat (of which 3.4 grams are saturated), 29.4 grams of carbohydrates, 26.5 grams of sugar, 1.7 grams of protein, and 0.07 grams of salt. This means that sugar constitutes approximately 66% of the total weight of the product and approximately 90% of its carbohydrate content. The fat-to-sugar ratio reflects the filling's composition: the chocolate shell provides the fat and milk proteins, while the fondant filling is essentially a sugar matrix with minimal fat or protein content.

The 26.5 grams of sugar figure cited in Evon's (2021) fact-check refers to the UK/European market product. The North American formulation of Cadbury Creme Eggs — manufactured under license by Hershey's in the United States — may differ slightly in composition due to recipe differences between Hershey's chocolate and original Cadbury chocolate, as well as differences in corn syrup versus sugar formulations between production regions. The Snopes fact-check used the Cadbury UK figure, which is the authoritative Cadbury source, and this is the appropriate reference for the product's canonical composition.

In terms of the video's measurement accuracy, six and a half teaspoons at 4.2 grams per teaspoon yields approximately 27.3 grams — slightly above the stated 26.5 grams, but within a reasonable margin for a demonstration using consumer measuring spoons with variable granule packing. Evon (2021) correctly characterized this as "just over" the official figure, acknowledging the trivial measurement variance without using it to contest the video's accuracy.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Perennial Re-Discovery Cycle and Mass Media Nutrition Claims

The most epistemically distinctive aspect of this fact-check, which Evon (2021) directly addresses with a touch of wry humor in his subheading ("And how many times can we be 'shocked' by this information?"), is the documented pattern of *cyclical re-discovery* of this exact claim. Evon catalogues an unbroken series of annual media cycles:

- 2021: Viral video widely reshared, social media users claim to be "shocked"
- 2020: The *Independent* reported that "chocolate lovers [were] shocked at sugar content of Easter treat"
- 2019: *Indy100* reported that "the amount of sugar in a Cadbury's Creme Egg is genuinely shocking"
- 2018: *Cosmopolitan* reported people "horrified" (not shocked) by the sugar content
- Earlier cycles are documented back at least to 2016 on various platforms

This cyclical re-discovery pattern — in which the same nutritional fact is repeatedly encountered as a novelty by successive cohorts of users who then experience and express shock — has significant epistemological and cognitive science implications that extend beyond the specific case.

The sugar content of Cadbury Creme Eggs is an objective, publicly available, static fact. The information has been printed on the product's packaging for decades, consistent with EU food labeling regulations and their predecessors. The information is available on Cadbury's corporate website. It has been reported in mainstream media repeatedly throughout the 2010s. And yet, year after year, substantial populations of social media users encounter the same information and report experiencing genuine surprise.

This phenomenon constitutes a case study in what Nickerson (1998), writing in *Review of General Psychology*, called the **curse of knowledge asymmetry**: the fact that certain types of practically available information remain effectively unknown to large populations not because the information is hidden but because the information is presented in forms — nutrition labels — that are systematically ignored, misread, or not consulted by a large proportion of consumers. Nutrition labels are legally required and publicly displayed, but research consistently shows that actual nutritional awareness among consumers is substantially lower than label availability would predict.

Wang et al. (2016), in a large population survey published in *Public Health Nutrition*, found that only 34% of American adults reported regularly reading nutritional information from food labels when making purchase or consumption decisions. Grunert and Wills (2007), reviewing European nutrition label usage data in *European Journal of Clinical Nutrition*, found that even consumers who reported reading labels frequently showed poor ability to correctly interpret or contextualize the information, with total sugar content being among the most poorly understood data points.

The epistemic novelty for Truth Vault purposes is therefore this: the Creme Egg sugar shock cycle is a recurring demonstration of a general law about nutritional consumer information — that factual availability does not equal effective awareness, and that repeated media "revelation" events serve a genuine social function of surfacing publicly available information to audiences who have not previously internalized it. The fact-checker's role in this case is accordingly not to prevent misinformation but to confirm accurate information that deserves to reach audiences who are genuinely encountering it for the first time in an attention-capturing format.

The virality of the demonstration video is itself a communication efficiency observation: a brief visual demonstration of sugar volume is far more cognitively effective at conveying "26.5 grams" than the same number printed on a nutrition label, because it converts an abstract quantity into a concrete, spatially visible representation. Wansink and Park (2002), writing in *Journal of Health Communication*, documented that visual representations of nutrition information significantly outperform numerical label data in terms of consumer recall and behavioral response.

## §4 Michael Goran, *Sugarproof*, and the Children's Health Context

The original viral tweet was posted by Michael I. Goran, whose institutional credentials are relevant to contextualizing the video's rhetorical intent. As Program Director for Diabetes and Obesity at The Saban Research Institute at Children's Hospital Los Angeles, and as a co-author of the popular science book *Sugarproof: Protect Your Family from the Hidden Dangers of Excess Sugar* (Avery/Penguin Random House, 2020, with Emily Ventura), Goran's professional focus is specifically on the relationship between sugar consumption in children and metabolic disease risk.

The *Sugarproof* framework, based on Goran and Ventura's (2020) research, argues that children are particularly vulnerable to the metabolic consequences of excess added sugar consumption for several reasons: (1) children consume more sugar relative to body weight than adults; (2) the developing liver processes fructose less efficiently than adult livers; (3) early life patterns of sugar consumption establish taste preferences and metabolic responses that persist into adulthood; and (4) added sugar is distributed across children's diets in quantities that exceed awareness because it appears in unexpected foods (breads, pasta sauces, flavored milks) in addition to obvious confections.

The claim in the viral tweet that a single Creme Egg contains "more sugar than is recommended for kids of most ages for a whole day" is therefore directly relevant to Goran's research focus. Evon's (2021) fact-check evaluated this sub-claim against the American Heart Association's (AHA) guidelines for children: "Kids ages 2-18 should have less than 25 grams or 6 teaspoons of added sugar daily for a healthy heart." A single Creme Egg at 26.5 grams therefore exceeds the AHA daily added sugar limit for all children in the 2-18 age bracket.

It is worth noting the precision issues involved in declaring this "generally accurate." The AHA guideline refers specifically to *added sugar* — sugars added during food processing, not naturally occurring sugars — while the 26.5 grams of sugar in a Creme Egg is the total sugar figure, which in the case of this product is overwhelmingly (effectively 100%) added sugar given that the product contains no fruit or dairy ingredients that would contribute significant naturally occurring sugars beyond trace amounts in the chocolate milk solids. The "generally accurate" characterization is therefore accurate without qualification for practical purposes.

Different guidelines from different health authorities would yield slightly different reference values. The World Health Organization recommends that free sugars (which includes both added sugars and naturally occurring sugars in honey, syrups, and fruit juices) constitute no more than 10% of total energy intake for all ages, with a target of below 5% for additional health benefits (WHO, 2015, *Guideline: Sugars Intake for Adults and Children*). For a child consuming 1,500 calories per day, 10% of energy from sugar would be approximately 37.5 grams — above the Creme Egg's 26.5 — while 5% would be approximately 18.75 grams, below the Creme Egg's content. The specific reference figure Evon uses (AHA's 25 grams) is therefore one of several applicable standards, and it happens to be the one that most clearly supports the "exceeds recommended daily intake" claim.

## §5 Consumer Sugar Awareness and the Processed Food Context

Evon (2021) makes a brief but important contextual observation in his fact-check: Cadbury Creme Eggs "are not the only treats that have a lot of sugar," noting that a standard 12-ounce can of Coca-Cola contains 39 grams of sugar — substantially more than a Creme Egg. This observation serves a calibration function, preventing the selective framing in which one product's sugar content is presented as exceptional when it is in fact representative of a broader category of highly sweetened processed foods.

The broader landscape of added sugar in the American and British diet has been extensively documented through large government dietary surveys. Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) indicates that added sugar accounts for approximately 13% of total caloric intake in the US adult diet, far above WHO recommendations (Ervin et al., 2012, *Advance Data from Vital and Health Statistics*). Major sources include sugar-sweetened beverages (the category where Coca-Cola falls), grain-based desserts, fruit drinks, dairy desserts, and candy — a category that includes Creme Eggs.

The confectionery industry's relationship to seasonal products like Cadbury Creme Eggs has a specific marketing dimension relevant to sugar content communication. Seasonal confections benefit from limited availability signaling that encourages consumption ("only around at Easter") and from the cultural framing of holiday consumption as categorically distinct from everyday consumption. Research on holiday eating behavior (Yanovski et al., 2000, *New England Journal of Medicine*) found that Americans gain an average of approximately 0.5 kilogram during the winter holiday period, with a subset gaining significantly more, and that the weight gain was attributable to increased intake of specific calorie-dense foods rather than generalized overeating.

The association of Easter specifically with confectionery consumption is culturally entrenched in both the UK and North America: UK confectionery sales data shows that approximately 10% of annual chocolate confectionery revenue is generated in the approximately four-week Easter season. The Creme Egg is the most recognizable single-item Easter confection in the UK and major Commonwealth markets, and its sales figures of approximately 500 million units per year (Mondelēz International, 2019, press materials) represent extraordinary market penetration.

## §6 The Annual Cycle as Policy Communication Failure

The annual re-discovery cycle of the Creme Egg sugar content is, at a systemic level, evidence of failure in nutrition policy communication. If consumers are genuinely surprised each year to learn that a confection consisting primarily of sugar and chocolate contains significant amounts of sugar, this represents a gap between the public health information environment that policy makers intended (one in which clear labeling and public health campaigns would create an informed consumer base) and the actual information environment consumers navigate.

Mhurchu et al. (2013), reviewing international evidence on front-of-pack nutrition labeling schemes in *BMC Public Health*, found that simplified front-of-pack labeling systems — such as the UK's traffic light system, which uses color coding to indicate high, medium, and low levels of fat, sugar, and salt — meaningfully outperformed standard panel labeling in consumer comprehension. The Creme Egg, under the UK traffic light system, would display a red ("high") indicator for sugar — a signal that should reduce the surprise effect at the population level. However, the persistence of the annual shock cycle suggests that even traffic light labeling is insufficient to create lasting population-level nutritional awareness about iconic seasonal treats, possibly because seasonal treats are cognitively exempted from nutritional scrutiny during holiday periods.

Evon's (2021) fact-check serves a genuine public health communication function by attaching a searchable, citable, trustworthy record to the accurate claim. It confirms the video's accuracy in a format that is indexed by search engines, shareable with permanence, and explicitly fact-checked against primary sources. In terms of marginal impact on consumer behavior, a single Snopes article is certainly modest — but at scale, the aggregation of trustworthy nutritional information across many such entries contributes to a searchable information commons that supports informed consumer choice.

The meta-story of the Creme Egg sugar claim — the fact that it requires annual re-verification because each year it surprises a new cohort of consumers — is itself the most significant finding of this fact-check, and one that deserves explicit framing: the claim is true, has always been true, is printed on the packaging, and will likely continue to require periodic re-verification for as long as the pattern of selective consumer attention and holiday-food cognitive exemption persists. This makes it a valuable entry in any taxonomy of "true claims that require repeated verification for structural rather than evidentiary reasons."
