# New Research Examines How People Conform to Their Pets' Traits


## 1. The Claim

A popular science article republished by Snopes from The Conversation in August 2022 reported findings from a series of 11 studies suggesting that people conform behaviorally to the stereotyped traits associated with their pets — specifically dogs and cats. The article, authored by Lei Jia (Assistant Professor of Marketing, Kent State University) and co-authored with marketing professors Xiaojing Yang and Yuwei Jiang, made two primary empirical claims:

First, that dog owners tend to take bigger risks and respond more positively to reward-oriented advertisements than cat owners do. Second, that cat owners are more cautious and more likely to react favorably to risk-averse advertising. The proposed mechanism is not that owning a pet changes one's personality directly, but rather that people form mental associations with the stereotyped personalities of their pets — dogs as eager and reward-oriented, cats as cautious and alert — and that activation of those associations (through direct exposure to, memory of, or even images of dogs or cats) influences decision-making behavior.

This claim sits at the intersection of consumer psychology, social priming research, and the broader cultural narrative about dogs and cats as personality proxies. It is interesting scientifically but also socially potent: the idea that your choice of pet might reflect and reinforce your personality traits resonates intuitively for many people, and the marketing applications of the research are immediately apparent. The claim was framed as a single coherent finding across a 11-study research program, which gives it an air of robustness that requires interrogation.

The article was classified as a news piece rather than a fact-check at Snopes, meaning the site treated it as science reporting rather than a claim requiring adjudication. However, the strength and reliability of the underlying research claims warrant careful examination.


## 2. What's Actually True

The research described in the Snopes/Conversation article is real. Lei Jia, Xiaojing Yang, and Yuwei Jiang conducted a multi-study research program examining whether pet-related stereotypes influence human behavior and decision-making. The studies span a range of methods, from state-level epidemiological correlations to experimental survey designs, and from observational correlational designs to causal experimental manipulations.

The first pair of studies described by Jia operated at the state level. The researchers examined pet ownership data across U.S. states and compared it with crude measures of risk-taking. One finding: states with higher proportions of dog owners, such as North Dakota, had higher rates of COVID-19 infections in early 2020 than states with higher proportions of cat owners, such as Vermont. Jia acknowledges this finding is correlational and subject to alternative interpretation — dog owners necessarily take their pets outside more frequently, which may increase exposure independent of any personality trait.

The subsequent experimental studies were more carefully designed to isolate the effect. In one study, 145 pet owners (either cat owners or dog owners, not both) were given a hypothetical $2,000 and asked to allocate it between a risky stock fund and a conservative mutual fund. Dog owners, who comprised 53% of the sample, were significantly more likely to invest in stocks and allocated more money to risky positions than cat owners.

In a further study, 225 participants viewed print advertisements featuring either a cat or a dog and then made the investment allocation decision. Exposure to dog imagery led participants to be more likely to invest more money in stocks. This is an important design feature: it moves from correlational pet-ownership data to experimental exposure manipulation, enabling stronger causal inference.

A third study recruited 283 undergraduate students and asked them to recall a past experience involving a cat or a dog, then exposed them to one of two versions of an advertisement for a massage business: one emphasizing metabolic and immune benefits (reward-framing) and one emphasizing relaxation and stress relief (risk-aversion framing). Students who recalled a dog interaction offered significantly higher bids when exposed to reward-oriented messaging. Students who recalled a cat interaction offered significantly higher bids when exposed to risk-aversion messaging.

These studies, taken together, suggest that stereotype activation — the mental priming of dog-associated or cat-associated personality characteristics — systematically influences financial risk-taking and advertising responsiveness. The proposed mechanism is not personality modification through pet ownership but rather situational activation of culturally shared stereotypes about animal temperament.

Jia, Yang, and Jiang situate this within established psychological theory about stereotype-congruence effects in consumer behavior. Research by Aaker et al. (2004, "When Good Brands Do Bad," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 31(1), 1–16) and numerous subsequent studies has documented that activating stereotype-related concepts influences behavior in stereotype-congruent directions — a framework sometimes called "concept accessibility" or stereotype priming. The specific application to animal stereotypes is relatively novel but theoretically grounded.

The research is genuine, but several important qualifications must be maintained. First, the sample sizes across many of the 11 studies are modest — 145 and 225 and 283 participants, respectively — which limits the statistical robustness of the findings and increases sensitivity to within-study variance. Second, the research has not, to the time of writing, been through the full cycle of independent replication that would allow high-confidence generalization. Third, the underlying stereotypes being activated are culturally contingent: the associations "dogs = eager/risk-taking" and "cats = cautious/risk-averse" are Western cultural constructs that may not hold across cultures with different relationships to these animals.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Handle Incompletely

The Snopes/Conversation article presents the research through a sympathetic and relatively uncritical lens, as expected from a piece written by one of the study's co-authors. The epistemic novelty that a more rigorous analysis would surface involves several dimensions that the article does not explore.

**The replication crisis context.** The research is situated within social psychology and consumer behavior, two fields that have been significantly affected by the replication crisis of the past fifteen years. Foundational findings in social priming — including the famous "Florida Man" study by Bargh et al. (1996, "Automaticity of Social Behavior: Direct Effects of Trait Construct and Stereotype Activation on Action," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 71(2), 230–244) and the "elderly priming" effects once treated as paradigmatic — have failed to replicate reliably in large-sample pre-registered follow-up studies (Doyen et al., 2012, "Behavioral Priming: It's All in the Mind, but Whose Mind?," *PLOS ONE*, 7(1), e29081). This does not mean that all priming effects are illusory, but it does mean that novel priming findings reported in single papers with modest samples require substantially more skepticism than the article's framing implies.

Open Science Collaboration (2015, "Estimating the Reproducibility of Psychological Science," *Science*, 349(6251), aac4716) found that of 100 psychological studies selected for replication, only approximately 36–39 could be confirmed at the same effect size and significance level. Consumer behavior and social psychology, the areas in which the pet stereotype research sits, were among the fields with the highest non-replication rates.

**The stereotype validity question.** The study assumes that "dogs are eager/risk-seeking" and "cats are cautious/risk-averse" are widely shared and internally consistent cultural stereotypes. This is likely true in broad strokes for U.S. samples, but the degree of stereotype uniformity matters for predicting effect sizes. If the stereotypes are held with widely varying strength across individuals, the population-level effect of stereotype activation on behavior will be correspondingly variable. The study does not appear to have measured stereotype endorsement as a covariate or moderator, which limits the mechanistic specificity of conclusions.

A related issue: the stereotype about dog owners taking more risks may reflect a genuine empirical regularity in personality-pet matching (anxious, risk-averse people may select cats; adventurous, risk-tolerant people may select dogs) rather than a causal influence of the pet on behavior. This self-selection hypothesis cannot be fully ruled out by the experimental studies, because the experiments activate cat and dog stereotypes rather than directly testing the personality difference between cat-owning and dog-owning populations. Gosling et al. (2010, "Personality of Self-Described Dog and Cat People," *Anthrozoös*, 23(3), 213–222) found modest but consistent personality differences between dog and cat owners in the Big Five model, with dog owners scoring higher on extraversion and agreeableness — consistent with the risk-seeking/risk-avoiding dichotomy — but the effects were small and the explanatory mechanism remains unclear.

**The marketing application framing.** The article emphasizes that these findings have commercial relevance for advertisers targeting pet owners. This framing is accurate but potentially misleading in scope: the study examined simplified, high-salience prime-and-measure designs under controlled lab conditions. Whether these effects persist in real-world marketing environments — where pet-related cues compete with dozens of other message elements, audience attention is partial, and stakes differ from hypothetical investment tasks — is a separate empirical question that the research program does not address.

The most genuinely novel insight that a careful reader should extract from this research program is methodological: the use of multi-study designs with progressively more experimental control to decompose a correlation (pet ownership and risk-taking) into its constituent causal structure (stereotype activation as mechanism) is exemplary in its aspirations, even if the individual studies are modest in scale. The research correctly recognizes that correlation without mechanism is epistemically incomplete and attempts to provide the mechanism. Whether it has succeeded requires replication evidence that does not yet exist.


## 4. Verdict

The research as described is real and the findings are internally consistent, but the Snopes/Conversation article's presentation significantly undersells the appropriate degree of uncertainty. The claim that "dog owners take more risks and cat owners are more cautious" is supported by the study's own data across several experimental designs — but these are findings from a single research group's program, conducted in modest-sample U.S. experiments, in a field with documented replication challenges.

The mechanistic claim — that stereotype activation by animal imagery influences financial decision-making — is theoretically coherent and experimentally supported within the studies themselves, but has not been independently replicated, and the effect sizes reported are not large enough to treat as firmly established findings.

Understanding this research well means holding two positions simultaneously: taking the findings seriously as a coherent and methodologically improving research program while maintaining appropriate skepticism about the generalizability and persistence of the observed effects until replicated by independent groups.

Snopes verdict: **Not addressed as a fact-check** (published as a news/science article). The underlying research claims are **credible but not firmly established**.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The question of whether humans are influenced by their pets sits within the broader interdisciplinary field of human-animal interaction (HAI) research, which has grown substantially since the 1980s and now encompasses not just personality and behavior effects but health outcomes, therapeutic applications, and child development. The field has moved from anecdotal observations to systematic empirical study, though it remains methodologically heterogeneous.

A related and partially overlapping literature examines the "pets resemble owners" effect — the empirical observation that people can match pets to their owners somewhat better than chance, and that owners and pets show some convergent characteristics in certain studies. Roy and Christenfeld (2004, "Do Dogs Resemble Their Owners?," *Psychological Science*, 15(5), 361–363) found that observers could match purebred dogs to their owners at above-chance levels, though the mechanism remains unclear. A subsequent study by Nakajima et al. (2009, "Dog and Owner Resemblance Revisited: The Role of Perceived Personality," *Anthrozoös*, 22(2), 145–157) found that owner-dog matching is stronger when personality traits rather than physical features are used, consistent with the type of self-selection effect discussed above.

The broader literature on pet companionship and human behavior includes well-established findings about health benefits of pet ownership — reduced stress cortisol levels, lower blood pressure, and enhanced social interaction — as well as more contested claims about personality development effects (Bao & Schreer, 2016, "Pets and Happiness: Examining the Association Between Pet Ownership and Wellbeing," *Anthrozoös*, 29(2), 283–296). The scale and nature of these effects depends significantly on the type of pet, the quality of the relationship, the owner's circumstances, and the outcome being measured.

What Jia and colleagues add to this literature is the specific cognitive mechanism of stereotype activation as a route through which pet-related associations influence behavior. This is a smaller and more specific claim than "pets make us healthier" or "owners resemble their pets," and it operates through a well-theorized psychological mechanism. The research program is genuinely novel in this specific contribution, even if the population-level effect sizes remain uncertain.

In the United States, approximately 70% of households own at least one pet, with dog ownership at approximately 50% and cat ownership at approximately 40% of households, according to industry surveys cited in the article. This means pet-related stereotypes are activated in contexts familiar to the large majority of the adult population, giving the stereotype activation mechanism potentially broad relevance.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes treatment of this research is unusual in structure: it was published not as a fact-check but as a reprinted Conversation article authored by one of the study's principal investigators, Lei Jia. This means the piece is simultaneously a piece of science communication and a promotional description of the author's own research, which creates a conflict-of-interest structure that readers should be aware of.

The Conversation, which produced the original article, specializes in academic-authored explainers of research findings and generally maintains high accuracy standards; however, its format does not include adversarial peer review of the claims made. Authors write about their own work and are expected to be accurate, but the self-serving framing risk is present.

From a fact-checking methodology standpoint, the appropriate approach to a claim of this type would involve: (1) confirming that the research exists and was peer-reviewed; (2) independent assessment of the study designs and statistical approach by a disinterested psychologist; (3) explicit quantification of the effect sizes and their practical significance; and (4) contextualizing the findings within the replication crisis literature for social priming research. None of these steps were taken in the Snopes/Conversation piece, which presented the findings as relatively established.

The result is a piece that is not inaccurate — the described studies are real and the findings are faithfully reported — but that is framed with less appropriate uncertainty than the current state of the evidence warrants. For a reader building an evidence-based understanding of how pets influence human psychology, the most important take-away is that this is genuinely interesting preliminary evidence, not a settled scientific conclusion.
