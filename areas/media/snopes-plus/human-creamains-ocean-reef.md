# Human cremated remains are being used to build ocean reefs


## 1. The Claim

The claim, widely shared in both news media and social media since approximately the early 2010s, holds that human cremated remains — colloquially "cremains" — are being incorporated into artificial ocean reef structures as an alternative burial practice. The fuller version of the claim describes a process in which a deceased person's ashes are mixed with a concrete or reef-safe material, cast into a form resembling natural reef structure, and deployed on the sea floor where the structure is intended to serve as a substrate for coral growth and marine life colonisation. The resulting reef formation thus simultaneously provides an ecological benefit and serves as a memorial to the deceased.

The claim circulates in several overlapping contexts: environmental burial advocacy, which promotes the practice as more ecologically beneficial and less wasteful of land and resources than conventional burial; grief and memorialisation discourse, where the practice is described as offering a meaningful way for families to honour environmentally conscious loved ones; and popular science communication about coral reef conservation, where memorial reefs are sometimes cited as innovative approaches to addressing reef degradation.

Specific companies are named in most versions of the claim, most prominently Eternal Reefs, Inc., which is based in Georgia and operates reef deployment sites primarily in waters off Florida, Georgia, South Carolina, North Carolina, Virginia, Maryland, New Jersey, and Texas. The company describes its process as mixing a person's cremains with a proprietary pH-balanced marine concrete mix to create "reef balls" that are deployed in permitted marine protected areas.

The implicit environmental argument in most coverage is twofold: first, that the practice diverts cremains from interment in land-based cemeteries, which occupy finite and increasingly expensive urban space; and second, that the reef structures provide positive ecological value by creating habitat for fish, invertebrates, and eventually coral growth, potentially contributing — in some coverage — to reef restoration efforts in areas where natural reefs have been degraded by bleaching, ocean acidification, or physical damage.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The companies are real and the practice is real.** Eternal Reefs, Inc. is a legitimate commercial operation founded in 1998, which has facilitated the deployment of several thousand reef balls incorporating human cremains in permitted US coastal waters. The company has permits from the US Army Corps of Engineers and complies with the Marine Protection, Research, and Sanctuaries Act, which regulates the disposal of materials in ocean waters. Under the Environmental Protection Agency's Ocean Dumping regulations (40 CFR Part 229), cremated human remains may be disposed of at sea provided they are scattered or placed beyond three nautical miles from shore. The incorporation of cremains into concrete reef structures operates under the same general permitting framework.

Additional companies offering similar services have proliferated since the early 2010s. Neptune Memorial Reef in North Palm Beach, Florida markets itself as a "life reef" designed specifically as a columbarium — an underwater cemetery structure — rather than an ecological reef restoration project, though it also deploys substrate that may attract marine life. Reef Innovations, a Florida-based company that manufactures the reef ball design used by Eternal Reefs, has been involved in reef restoration projects in numerous countries; while not a memorial company itself, its technology underpins much of the memorial reef industry.

International versions of memorial reef services have been established in Australia, the United Kingdom, and several other jurisdictions, sometimes under different regulatory frameworks. In Australia, regulatory requirements for sea burial including cremains vary by state. In the United Kingdom, the Environment Agency and Marine Management Organisation regulate sea disposal of human remains.

**The reef ecology.** The more ecologically nuanced dimension of the claim concerns whether incorporating cremains into reef structures does the marine environment any measurable ecological good — beyond simply deploying an artificial reef substrate, which would be beneficial regardless of whether it contained cremains.

Cremains consist primarily of calcium phosphate — the mineral component of bone — along with calcium carbonate, and smaller quantities of sodium, potassium, magnesium, and phosphate. Importantly, cremains are strongly alkaline: their pH when dissolved in water is typically above 10, and sometimes above 12. This high alkalinity is chemically significant in a marine context. Ocean water is normally slightly alkaline at approximately pH 8.1, and coral calcification — the process by which coral polyps build their calcium carbonate skeletons — is highly sensitive to carbonate chemistry. While coral settlement generally favours solid calcium carbonate substrates, extremely high local alkalinity around an uncured cremains-concrete mix can inhibit larval settlement and early coral growth by disrupting the seawater chemistry in the immediate vicinity of the structure.

Peer-reviewed research on artificial reef ecology by Burt, Van Lavieren and Feary (2011, *PLOS ONE*) examined colonisation dynamics on artificial reefs of differing substrate compositions, finding that substrate chemistry and surface texture are among the most important variables in early invertebrate and coral recruitment. Research by Walters and Banford (2002, *Gulf and Caribbean Research*) on reef ball deployments in the Caribbean found that concrete composition significantly affected colonisation rates in the first two years after deployment, with pH-neutral mixes outperforming higher-pH formulations in early coral recruitment.

Eternal Reefs and similar companies are aware of the pH issue and claim to address it by using "pH-balanced" concrete mixes that buffer the alkalinity of the cremains. The company's promotional materials assert that in practice their deployed structures do attract fish, invertebrates, and eventually some coral growth. Independent ecological studies of Eternal Reefs deployments are limited; most documentation comes from the company itself or from dive operators who visit sites and report on visible marine life. Ecological peer review of the specific claim that incorporating cremains into reef balls produces meaningfully better ecologic outcomes than equivalent reef balls without cremains has not been published in indexed scientific journals as of this writing.

**What memorial reefs actually do ecologically.** The more defensible ecological claim for memorial reef products is not that cremains are ecologically beneficial per se, but that the deployment of hard substrate in areas where reef structure has been reduced does provide genuine ecological value. Artificial reef research is extensive and generally positive: hard substrate in soft-bottom environments consistently increases local fish biomass, invertebrate diversity, and structural complexity in ways that benefit both ecology and small-scale fisheries. Bohnsack and Sutherland (1985, *Bulletin of Marine Science*) produced an early systematic review of artificial reef studies; subsequent decades have produced substantial confirmatory evidence that artificial reefs of various designs do increase local biodiversity relative to adjacent featureless substrate.

The memorial reef structures deployed by Eternal Reefs and similar companies are therefore doing genuine ecological work in providing substrate. Whether the cremains add, subtract, or are neutral to the ecological value of that substrate is a different and less-resolved question. The honest ecologic characterisation is that the hard structure is the primary driver of any ecological benefit, and that the cremains are an ingredient whose ultimate ecological effect depends heavily on the mix formulation, the degree of curing, the local oceanographic conditions, and the time elapsed since deployment.


## 3. Why People Believe This (And What It Illuminates)

**Epistemic novelty: the chemistry of grief.** The genuinely underappreciated dimension of the memorial reef story is that it sits at the intersection of two rapidly evolving cultural conversations — the green burial movement and coral reef conservation — in ways that popular coverage has not fully explored.

The green burial movement, which encompasses practices including natural burial without embalming, home funerals, biodegradable caskets, composting of human remains (legal in an increasing number of US states since Washington became the first in 2019), and sea burial, has grown substantially over the past two decades. Research by Harris (2007, *Sociology*) on death and disposal practices in the UK found increasing consumer interest in burial options perceived as environmentally beneficial, and American survey data from the National Funeral Directors Association (NFDA) annual reports show steadily increasing interest in cremation (now over 57% of US dispositions as of 2021 NFDA data) combined with growing interest in alternatives to nuclear-age burial options.

The memorial reef proposition offers a synthesis of several cultural desires: the wish to mark a death with a meaningful physical memorial, the desire to contribute something positive to a threatened ecosystem, and the appeal of permanent association between the deceased and the sea. This combination is culturally potent enough to motivate significant commercial development independent of its ecological merits. The popularity of the claim is therefore partly driven by its emotional and cultural resonance rather than by ecological argument, and fact-checkers approaching it from a purely ecological angle may miss the more significant cultural dynamics at work.

The ecologically novel dimension — less explored in popular media — is the question of what large-scale deployment of memorial reef structures would mean for reef ecology. If the practice scaled to millions of deployments, the aggregate effect of the pH disruption, the nitrogen and phosphorus loading from cremains, and the cumulative substrate provision would need to be assessed systematically. The current scale of the industry is small relative to natural reef acreage, and the EPA's permitting framework prevents deployments in proximity to sensitive living reef systems. But the implicit promise that memorial reefs contribute to reef restoration — made or implied in significant portions of the marketing for these services — invites scrutiny that the existing independent ecological literature has not yet fully provided.

**The media narrative structure.** Coverage of memorial reefs consistently emphasises the emotional and human-interest dimensions of the story: stories of individuals who loved the ocean choosing this disposition, families who scatter their loved one's ashes by attending the reef ball drop wearing wetsuits and diving to say goodbye. This narrative structure is compelling and not inaccurate, but it tends to foreground the human story and background the ecology. As a result, readers who have encountered this story feel confident that memorial reefs are both real and ecologically beneficial based on media consumption, when the evidence for the latter is stronger for the substrate effect than for the cremains-specific effect.


## 4. Verdict

**True.** Human cremated remains are genuinely being incorporated into artificial ocean reef structures. Companies including Eternal Reefs, Inc. have been doing this since 1998, with US Army Corps of Engineers permits, under EPA Ocean Dumping regulations. The structures are real, deployed in permitted US coastal waters, and do attract marine life. Thousands of individuals have chosen this disposition and had their cremains incorporated into reef balls that now sit on the sea floor off US coasts.

The nuances that matter are: (a) the ecological benefit of memorial reef structures derives primarily from the hard substrate effect of artificial reef deployment, which is well-supported by artificial reef science, rather than from any specific property of human cremains; (b) cremains are highly alkaline and their pH can, if not properly buffered, inhibit coral recruitment and early colonisation; (c) commercial operators claim to buffer this with pH-balanced concrete mixes, but independent peer-reviewed ecological studies of cremains-containing reef structures are limited; (d) the scale of current commercial memorial reef deployment is small relative to natural reef systems, and serious environmental concerns about cumulative impact have not arisen to date at current volumes.

The claim is true. The ecological framing in much popular coverage deserves more precision than it typically receives.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The memorial reef industry exists within a larger context of innovation in death care driven by growing cremation rates, land scarcity in conventional cemeteries, consumer interest in environmental alternatives, and the commercialisation of the grief industry. Cremation rates in the United States, which were below 25% in 1990, exceeded 57% by 2021 (NFDA, 2021), creating a large market for cremains disposition services beyond conventional scattering or home retention.

The green burial movement has advocated for a wide range of environmentally framed alternatives to conventional burial and cremation. Capsula Mundi, an Italian project, proposes burial in biodegradable pods that feed a memorial tree. Coeio offers mushroom-based burial suits designed to accelerate decomposition and reduce embalming chemical contamination. Human composting — marketed as natural organic reduction — is legal in Washington, Oregon, Colorado, Vermont, California, New York, and several other states as of 2024, and offers the prospect of genuinely beneficial soil enrichment from human remains (Spade, 2021, various public testimony and white paper materials). Memorial reefs are among the most established and commercially scaled of these alternatives, which is part of why they receive sustained media coverage.

The coral reef conservation context adds a layer of urgency and ethical weight to memorial reef marketing. Global coral reef systems have declined dramatically: Burke, Reytar, Spalding and Perry (2011, World Resources Institute, *Reefs at Risk Revisited*) estimated that approximately 75% of the world's coral reefs were threatened from human activity, a figure that subsequent bleaching events associated with record ocean temperatures have made more severe. The 2015–2016 and 2023 global bleaching events caused mass mortality on reefs worldwide (Hughes et al., 2017, *Nature*; 2018, *Science*). Against this backdrop, any service that claims ecological benefit for reefs carries emotional resonance.

Whether memorial reef services are making a meaningful contribution to reef conservation is a different question from whether they are real. The scale of current deployments is negligible relative to the scale of reef degradation. The structures are deployed in existing permitted areas away from sensitive living reef systems, so they do not replace damaged reef in the ecologically most significant way. They are best understood as providing genuine local habitat value — helping individual fish, invertebrates, and potentially some corals — rather than as contributing to reef-system conservation at any significant scale. That is still something; it is simply less than the more ambitious framing suggests.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim is unusual in the fact-checking landscape because it is both true and ecologically more complex than most coverage suggests. The basic verifiability of the claim — are there companies doing this? — is easily confirmed through a web search and examination of commercial operators' EPA permits and Army Corps of Engineers permits. Snopes and major fact-checking platforms have not formally addressed this claim, most likely because its basic truth is not seriously in doubt and it does not typically appear in politically charged misinformation contexts.

The more challenging fact-check task is examining the ecological claims embedded in memorial reef marketing and in popular coverage. Statements like "memorial reefs help restore dying coral reefs" or "incorporating cremains into reef structures benefits marine ecosystems" require engagement with marine ecology and artificial reef science at a level of specificity that most general-audience fact-checkers are not well-positioned to provide. The appropriate response is not to call such claims false but to contextualise them: artificial reef substrates do provide real ecological value; the specific effect of cremains on reef ecology is not independently peer-reviewed to a strong standard; and the scale of current commercial deployment is not sufficient to constitute reef restoration at ecologically meaningful scales.

For media literacy purposes, this case is a useful illustration of a claim that is true in its core factual assertion but that sits within a broader narrative — memorial reefs are good for the ocean — that requires disaggregation. The practice is real; the regulation is real; the ecological benefits are partially real (substrate) and partially marketing-inflated (cremains specifically, reef restoration at scale). Developing the habit of examining not just whether a claim is true but whether the significance attached to it is proportionate is a durable media literacy skill that this case exercises effectively.
