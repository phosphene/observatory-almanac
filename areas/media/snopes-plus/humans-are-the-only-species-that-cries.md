# Humans Are the Only Species That Cries Emotional Tears


## 1. The Claim

The claim holds that emotional lacrimation — the production of tears as a direct response to felt emotional states rather than as a reflexive ocular lubricating function — is uniquely human among all animal species on Earth. In strong form the claim asserts that no other animal produces tears in response to distress, grief, joy, or social stress; that animals may produce tears due to eye irritants, cold air, or ocular dryness, but that these are purely reflexive basal or reflex tears physiologically distinct from the psychic or emotional tears produced by humans; and that this capacity represents a fundamental biological divide between human and animal emotional architecture. More moderate versions acknowledge that other great apes or mammals may have the lacrimal apparatus, but maintain that emotional lacrimation is a distinctively human use of that apparatus, shaped by uniquely human social and communicative needs.

The claim appears prominently in popular writing about human uniqueness and in philosophical discussions of the moral significance of human emotional depth. It has been repeated in the popular science literature, in articles about the evolution of crying, and in discussions of consciousness and subjective experience in animals. Charles Darwin himself addressed the question in *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872), concluding that weeping from mental distress is "peculiar to Man" among the age's observed species — a judgment Darwin made cautiously and with acknowledgment of limited comparative evidence that his Victorian contemporaries could not yet fill. This Darwinian attribution has given the claim a veneer of scientific authority that subsequent comparative research is beginning to erode.

The claim is partly definitional — whether it is true depends significantly on how "emotional tears" are defined and what evidence would count as demonstrating them in a non-speaking subject — and partly empirical. The empirical dimension is genuinely contested and increasingly presents counterevidence, while the definitional dimension reveals a tendency to operationalize "emotional tears" in a way that only human verbal self-report can satisfy, which is a methodological circularity that deserves scrutiny.


## 2. What's Actually True

The functional anatomy of tear production is shared broadly across terrestrial vertebrates. Lacrimal glands or their functional equivalents are present in most tetrapods — the basic apparatus of ocular lubrication and surface maintenance is evolutionarily ancient. The relevant question for the claim is whether any non-human species produce tears specifically in response to emotional or psychological states, as opposed to reflexive irritant or basal lubrication tears. The evidence here is more complex than the simple claim allows.

**The three-tear-type framework and its limits**: Human lacrimation science distinguishes three functional types: basal tears (continuous low-level secretion maintaining corneal moisture), reflex tears (produced in response to ocular surface irritation, onion vapors, cold air, yawning), and psychic or emotional tears (produced in response to emotional states, sometimes called "psychogenic lacrimation"). Emotional tears in humans have a distinct biochemical composition from reflex tears, containing higher concentrations of prolactin, adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH), leucine-enkephalin, and other stress-related hormones and neuropeptides — findings established by Frey, DeSota-Johnson, Hoffman, and McCall, who conducted systematic biochemical analyses of emotional versus reflex tears and produced the foundational modern biochemical characterization of human emotional lacrimation (Frey, DeSota-Johnson, Hoffman & McCall, 1981, *American Journal of Ophthalmology*). This biochemical specificity was important in establishing that emotional tears are a physiologically real and distinct category in humans, not merely a spillover of excess tear volume.

Whether other species produce tears with analogous biochemical profiles — elevated stress hormones, neuropeptides — in response to emotional stimuli is not well-studied and represents a significant research gap. The claim that no non-human animal produces emotional tears implicitly requires that this distinction has been tested and confirmed across relevant species, which it largely has not.

**Elephant lacrimation**: Elephants have been among the most frequently cited candidates for non-human emotional crying. Decades of behavioral field research have documented Asian and African elephants producing copious lacrimal secretions in circumstances that ethologists characterize as emotionally loaded: during and after the death of companion animals or conspecifics, during separation from family members, when receiving distressing veterinary procedures, and occasionally during reunion events with long-separated individuals. Elephant researcher Cynthia Moss documented repeated instances of elephants producing what observers described as tears during mortally protracted death vigils over fallen family members, in long-term field observations at Amboseli National Park, Kenya, published in her ethological account *Elephant Memories* (Moss, 1988). These observations are ethologically detailed but methodologically limited by the absence of controlled conditions that might rule out coincidental reflex tearing from environmental stimuli.

More recently, a groundbreaking controlled study reported by Nakamura and colleagues measured tear volume changes in horses during reunion with their owners using schirmer tear strips — the same diagnostic tool used in veterinary and human ophthalmological practice — and found statistically significant increases in tear production during positive social interaction and owner reunions compared to contact with strangers (Nakamura, Takimoto-Inose & Fujita, 2023, *Current Biology*). The study is notable for its methodological rigor and direct measurement of tear volume rather than reliance on behavioral observation. The authors were careful not to claim definitive evidence of "emotional" tears in the human sense, but their finding directly challenges the premise that increased non-irritant tear production in emotionally and socially significant contexts is uniquely human.

**Great apes**: Chimpanzees and other great apes have occasionally been described as producing tears in captive settings following distress — prolonged separation, loss of companions, environmental stressors. Jane Goodall documented tear-like secretions in chimpanzees during behavioral distress, noting them in her ethological records. However, controlled studies of great ape lacrimation in emotional contexts are sparse and methodologically limited. The morphological homology between human and chimpanzee lacrimal systems is expected given overall anatomical similarity, and whether the systems are recruited in emotionally parallel ways has not been rigorously tested.

**Dogs**: The same Nakamura et al. (2022) research team published a companion paper in *Current Biology* examining dog lacrimation in response to owner reunion. Using schirmer tear strips, they showed that dogs produced significantly more tears during reunion with their owners compared to reunion with familiar strangers, and that the increase in tear production was positively correlated with the dog's attachment level to the owner as measured by behavioral proxies. The magnitude of the tear increase was modest but statistically robust (Nakamura, Tanaka, Takimoto-Inose, Watanabe & Fujita, 2022, *Current Biology*). Oxytocin eyedrops applied to dogs' eyes increased tear production and also increased the dogs' apparent appeal to human observers — an intriguing finding suggesting bidirectional neurohormonal tear signaling with social-communication function.

These findings do not establish that dogs experience subjective emotional states analogous to human grief or joy. But they do establish that dogs produce measurably elevated tear volumes in the specific social context of reunion with attachment figures, driven by a neurohormonal mechanism involving oxytocin, which is directly parallel to components of the human emotional lacrimation system.

**Marine mammals and reptiles**: Crocodilians produce spectacular amounts of tear fluid when feeding on land — the basis of the "crocodile tears" expression. While this was long assumed to be purely reflexive (driven by exertion and air exposure of the lacrimal system), Mara et al. established that crocodile tearing during feeding likely involves the same lacrimal glands used normally and is not a pure feeding-linked respiratory reflex (Mara, Nevarez, Flanagan, Rodriguez & Willis, 2013, *Physiological and Biochemical Zoology*). The emotional status of crocodilians remains opaque, but the lacrimal system's activation in feeding contexts does not map neatly onto pure ocular-lubrication function.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The "humans uniquely cry emotional tears" claim is a clear instance of a broader pattern of anthropocentric exceptionalism in folk biology — the tendency to identify human cognitive and emotional capacities as categorically discontinuous from other animals rather than quantitatively variant along shared biological continua. This pattern has a philosophically important history and a specific contemporary maintenance mechanism.

**The Darwin Precedent and Authority Transfer**: Darwin's careful 1872 conclusion — that emotional weeping appears to be "peculiar to Man" — was based on Victorian-era comparative observations that were genuinely the best available at the time, and it was offered with appropriate tentativeness. However, the authority of Darwin's name and the formal scientific framing of *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* gave the claim a credibility that subsequent repetition preserved without updating. This is a textbook case of what might be called *landmark lock* — a phenomenon where a well-regarded founding scientific statement is retained in popular and educational discourse long after the underlying evidence has been revised or substantially complicated by subsequent research. The claim circulates today with Darwinian authority attached to it in many popular treatments, even though Darwin himself was explicitly uncertain and the subsequent 150 years of comparative ethology have complicated his tentative conclusion.

**Methodological Anthropocentrism and the Verbal Report Standard**: A crucial definitional trap lurks in the "emotional tears are uniquely human" claim. How do we verify tears are "emotional" rather than reflexive? In humans, the criterion is simple: the person reports feeling emotional. Self-report via language is the gold standard for interior state attribution. For non-human animals, verbal self-report is unavailable by definition, and this methodological asymmetry creates a structural bias: any operational definition of "emotional tears" that includes verbal report as a necessary criterion is definitionally precluded from applying to non-human animals. The claim then becomes unfalsifiable not because non-human animals lack the relevant capacity but because the evidence standard was written to exclude them. This is not a finding about animal psychology; it is a philosophical choice about methodology masquerading as empirical science.

**The Epistemic Novelty: The Definitional Trap as a Structural Producer of Anthropocentric Exceptionalism**: This is the key novel insight the standard debunking framework misses, and it applies beyond the crying claim to many "humans are unique" assertions in cognitive science and animal behavior. When "emotional crying" is defined as "crying accompanied by subjective emotional experience," and "subjective emotional experience" is operationalized via verbal self-report, the claim that other animals do not emotionally cry becomes tautologically true by definitional fiat rather than empirical discovery. This is not the same as being false — it may be a reasonable definitional choice — but it is categorically different from being an empirically established finding about animal psychology.

The deeper problem is that this methodological circularity is invisible in most popular presentations of the claim. The conclusion sounds like a biological fact discovered through observation ("we have observed that no other animal cries emotionally") when it is partly a definitional decision about what evidence would count.科学 philosophy of mind has extensively debated the "other minds problem" — the epistemological challenge of verifying internal subjective states even in other humans — but this debate rarely filters into popular science writing about animal cognition, leaving consumers of popular science with the impression that "humans uniquely experience X" claims are more empirically settled than they are.

**The Emotional Uniqueness Schema and Identity Investment**: Claims about human cognitive and emotional uniqueness carry identity significance that resistance to revision reflects. If emotional tears are uniquely human, they are a mark of human distinctiveness — a visible biological expression of inner emotional life that separates our species from mere mechanism. This identity investment is not irrational: there are genuine philosophical stakes in how we understand the distribution of subjective experience across species, including moral stakes concerning animal welfare. But the identity value of human uniqueness claims creates motivated reasoning that selectively attends to supportive evidence and exhibits confirmatory conservatism toward disconfirming data. New findings like the Nakamura et al. (2022, 2023) dog and horse lacrimal studies are easily minimized ("correlation with reunion doesn't prove the dog feels joy") in ways that would not be applied to parallel evidence about human infants.


## 4. The Scientific Consensus

The scientific consensus on this question is genuinely unsettled — more "unresolved" than either "confirmed" or "disproven." This is a scientifically honest position that differs from the popular framing, which presents the claim as an established fact.

The comparative ethology and comparative neuroscience communities have increasingly shifted toward continuity models of animal emotion rather than categorical discontinuity models. The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness (Low et al., 2012, Francis Crick Memorial Conference, University of Cambridge) — a formal scientific statement produced by an international group of neuroscientists — concluded that non-human animals possess the neurological substrates necessary for conscious states and that the weight of evidence favors attributing emotional experiences to all mammals and birds, and many other organisms. This declaration does not directly address lacrimation, but it represents the scientific community's broad position on the question of whether non-human animals have emotional lives in a relevant sense.

The specific lacrimation research is recent and sparse. The Nakamura group's publications (2022, 2023) on dogs and horses represent the first rigorous quantitative measurements of non-irritant tear production in emotionally and socially significant contexts in non-human species. These studies have been peer-reviewed and published in *Current Biology*, a high-impact journal, and their findings are considered methodologically credible. However, two studies on two species do not constitute a comprehensive comparative database, and the mechanism interpretation — whether the observed tear increases indicate subjective emotional states or reflect neurohormonal social-bonding processes without subjective emotional accompaniment — remains genuinely debated.

What the scientific community does broadly agree on: (1) the claim that no non-human animal ever produces non-reflexive tears is unsubstantiated and has never been empirically demonstrated through systematic comparative study; (2) multiple species produce tears in emotionally and socially significant contexts that have been documented across decades of field and laboratory ethological observation; (3) the mechanisms of human emotional lacrimation, including oxytocin and stress hormone involvement, are shared by other mammals through evolutionary homology; and (4) the methodological tools to rigorously study this question are only recently being applied to comparative contexts.


## 5. Complications and Edge Cases

**The evolutionary function of emotional tears**: The adaptive question of why humans should have evolved emotional lacrimation is itself unresolved and has direct bearing on the uniqueness claim. Evolutionary hypotheses include: tears as an honest appeasement signal (visible distress marking submission and reducing aggression in social conflicts), tears as an attachment-bonding activator (triggering caregiving responses in observers), and tears as a hormonal clearing mechanism for stress-associated compounds. If the function is social communication, the question of whether other social species under comparable evolutionary pressures have developed parallel functional systems becomes biologically interesting rather than settled. Vingerhoets, who has produced the most comprehensive academic treatment of human crying, acknowledges the comparative lacrimation question as a significant open problem in the evolutionary biology of emotional expression (Vingerhoets, 2013, *Why Only Humans Weep: Unravelling the Mysteries of Tears*, Oxford University Press).

**Captive versus wild observations**: Many of the most compelling anecdotal observations of non-human animal lacrimation in emotional contexts come from captive animals — zoo elephants, laboratory-housed primates, domestic dogs and horses. Captivity creates significant confounds for interpreting emotional states: captive animals are under chronic stressors that wild animals do not typically face, and behavioral responses in captive contexts may not be representative of species-typical emotional repertoire. However, this cuts both ways: it is also possible that captive social bonds create the attachment contexts most likely to elicit emotional lacrimation in species that share the underlying neurohormonal architecture, providing the experimental conditions under which the capacity becomes visible.

**The communication hypothesis in human infants**: Infant crying in humans is clearly communicative before it is emotionally sophisticated — neonates cry reflexively, and emotional self-awareness develops across the first years of life. The developmental trajectory from reflexive to emotionally modulated crying in human infants complicates any sharp line between "emotional" and "reflexive" tears as biological categories, and suggests that the capacity may exist on a continuum rather than switching between two discrete modes.

**Seals and sea lions**: Some marine mammal species have been observed producing significant lacrimal secretions, including in captive settings during social separation. Environmental factors (dry air, bright light) confound these observations, but they have been noted repeatedly in marine mammal literature and have not been conclusively explained as purely reflexive.


## 6. Summary Verdict

**Unproven.** The claim that humans are the uniquely sole species producing emotional tears is almost certainly oversimplified, but cannot be definitively disproven with current evidence because the question has not been rigorously tested across a broad comparative sample. The recent Nakamura et al. studies (2022, 2023) demonstrating measurably increased tear production in dogs and horses during owner-reunion — a neurohormonal, social-context-dependent tear response — represent the most methodologically rigorous challenge to the claim to date and suggest the strict uniqueness assertion is false. Decades of field ethological observation of elephants, great apes, and other cognitively complex mammals provide suggestive but not conclusive additional evidence. 

The claim is not demonstrably true and the evidence trend is running against it. darwin's 1872 cautious proposal now looks like the starting point of an unresolved scientific question rather than an established finding. The methodological barriers to definitively testing the claim — the verbal-report circularity, the observer-context problem, the comparative biochemical gap — ensure that genuine uncertainty will persist for some time.

The deeper lesson is about the epistemological status of human-uniqueness claims in behavior: they are frequently operationalized in ways that are definitionally exclusive to non-verbal subjects, making them structurally unfalsifiable rather than empirically discovered. This is not a finding about animal tears; it is a warning about how category construction in science can manufacture the appearance of findings.
