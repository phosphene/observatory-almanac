# Humans Can Smell Rain Before It Arrives (Petrichor)

## 1. The Claim

The claim that humans can smell rain before it arrives—and specifically the identification of that smell as "petrichor"—has become a staple of popular science sharing, a frequent subject of "did you know" social media posts, and a recurring example in science communication about the capabilities of human olfaction. In its standard form, the claim holds that the distinctive earthy, pleasant smell associated with rain has a specific scientific name (petrichor), a specific scientific explanation (typically attributed to soil bacteria producing geosmin, or to oils released by plants), and that this smell is detectable before precipitation actually reaches the observer.

The claim thus encompasses several distinct propositions: that humans can smell "rain" at all; that this smell is detectable prospectively, before arrival; that "petrichor" is the correct scientific name; and that the mechanism is singular—one specific compound or process explaining the phenomenon. Popular accounts often identify geosmin as the specific molecule responsible, describe it as produced by soil bacteria of the genus *Streptomyces*, and note that humans are extraordinarily sensitive to it—able to detect it at concentrations as low as parts per trillion.

This story is appealing for multiple reasons: it presents human sensory capabilities as surprisingly acute, it provides a romantic-sounding scientific name for a universally relatable sensory experience, and it offers a satisfying mechanism connecting everyday experience to microbiology. The claim has been reproduced in science textbooks, museum displays, science journalism, and educational media with minimal variation.

The claim is largely true but significantly more complicated than its popular formulation—and the simplifications are consequential for understanding both the chemistry and the biology involved.

## 2. What's Actually True

**The multiple distinct "smells of rain."** What people colloquially call "the smell of rain" is not a single chemical phenomenon but a temporal sequence of chemically distinct odor signatures, each with different physical and biological origins. The popular conflation of these into a single "petrichor" is the primary source of oversimplification.

*Before rain arrives: ozone.* The first smell that may be detectable before precipitation reaches an observer is ozone (O₃), produced by lightning. Electrical discharges split molecular oxygen (O₂) into oxygen radicals (O) that combine with intact O₂ to form ozone. At ground level, ozone can be swept forward by the downdraft ahead of a thunderstorm, arriving before rain. The clean, slightly sharp smell of ozone is familiar to anyone who has been near large electrical equipment; it is chemically distinct from petrichor. Ozone can be detected at concentrations around 10 parts per billion, well within human olfactory capability (Kleindienst et al., 1990, *Atmospheric Environment*, "Ozone threshold values and the olfactory detection of a variety of aromatic, aliphatic and halogenated organic compounds").

The ozone story is geographically and meteorologically contingent: it is most relevant for convective thunderstorms with active lightning. Gentle frontal rainfall without lightning may produce no preceding ozone signature.

*During and just after rain: petrichor proper.* The term "petrichor" was coined by Bear & Thomas (1964, *Nature*, "Nature of Argillaceous Odour") to describe specifically the smell released when rain falls on dry rock and soil. The mechanism they identified is the release of plant-derived oils—primarily long-chain fatty acids and their derivatives—that accumulate on rock and dry soil surfaces during drought periods. When rain impacts these surfaces, the oils are mobilized and released into the air as an aerosol. Bear and Thomas identified yellowish oil deposits on rocks in arid areas of South Australia and experimental evidence that these deposits, when wet, produced the characteristic argillaceous (earth-like) odor.

This mechanism—plant oils released by rain impact on dry surfaces—is what "petrichor" properly refers to. The compounds involved include diverse lipid-derived molecules and were not fully chemically characterized by Bear and Thomas; subsequent work has identified pinene and other monoterpenes as components, but the exact chemistry of petrichor-proper is still not exhaustively catalogued. Critically, petrichor is released at the moment of rain impact, not before rainfall—it is a concurrent or post-rainfall phenomenon, not a prospective one.

*During and after rain: geosmin.* Geosmin (trans-1,10-dimethyl-trans-9-decalol) is a bicyclic sesquiterpene produced primarily by soil-dwelling actinobacteria, particularly *Streptomyces* and *Nocardia* species, and by some cyanobacteria and other microorganisms. It is the molecule most commonly associated with the "earthy smell of rain" in popular science accounts, and the human detection threshold for geosmin is indeed extraordinarily low.

Jiang & Bhaskaran (2014, *Chemical Senses*, "Human olfactory sensitivity to geosmin") established the detection threshold at approximately 5 parts per trillion (ppt)—making geosmin one of the most potently odorous compounds detectable by humans. This extreme sensitivity has generated scientific interest, with researchers hypothesizing evolutionary explanations for why humans might have been under selection pressure to detect geosmin at such low concentrations (proposed explanations include attraction to water sources or avoidance of environments with potentially toxic microbial blooms).

However, geosmin is released from soil primarily during and after rain, not before. When rain disrupts soil aggregates—a process Joung et al. (2017, *Nature Communications*, "Aerosol generation by raindrop impact on soil") characterized in detail using high-speed videography—bubbles form in the surface water film and burst, ejecting tiny aerosol droplets that carry dissolved volatiles including geosmin into the air. This mechanism, which Joung et al. called "aerosol from bubble-bursting," is triggered by rain impact, not by approaching rain. Geosmin cannot be smelled before rain arrives unless wind carries soil aerosols from an area already receiving precipitation toward the observer.

*Before rain arrives: plant volatiles.* A third mechanism does produce pre-rainfall odors under specific conditions: heat-stressed plants release volatile compounds before anticipated rain events. Researchers including Schade & Goldstein (2003, *Geophysical Research Letters*, "Increase of light-dependent plant emissions with decreased atmospheric methane") have documented increased emission of biogenic volatile organic compounds (BVOCs) including isoprene, monoterpenes, and sesquiterpenes under high-temperature and water-stress conditions. These emissions do not specifically anticipate rainfall; they are responses to heat and drought conditions that often precede storm systems. The resulting smell—described variously as green, resinous, or camphoraceous depending on the dominant BVOCs—is genuinely detectable before rain under dry, hot conditions.

**Human geosmin sensitivity and its evolutionary context.** Human olfactory sensitivity to geosmin is genuine and is among the most striking examples of high-sensitivity olfaction in the scientific literature. The question of why humans should be so sensitive to geosmin has generated evolutionary speculation worth examining critically.

Majid et al. (2018, *Current Biology*, "Olfaction in a Hunter-Gatherer Society Reveals Rankings of Odorants") and related research into olfactory sensitivity across cultures have established that human olfaction is more capable than classic psychophysical research had implied, with cross-cultural variation suggesting that olfactory vocabulary and attention shape performance. But the specific evolutionary story for geosmin sensitivity—that ancestral humans used it to locate water sources in arid environments—rests on plausible ecological logic rather than direct evidence.

Conversely, there are grounds for skepticism about the adaptive story: geosmin is also produced by algal blooms in toxic concentrations, and avoidance of geosmin might have been just as adaptive as attraction to it. Rozin (1982, *Social Science and Medicine*, "Human food selection: The interaction of biology, culture and individual experience") observed that geosmin has a mixed hedonic valence across populations—many find it pleasant in the context of rain and fresh earth; many find it unpleasant in the context of water or food.

**The biological puzzle: why produce geosmin at all?** Streptomycetes produce geosmin as part of their secondary metabolite production. Bacteria do not produce metabolically expensive secondary compounds without function. Becher et al. (2020, *Nature Microbiology*, "Developmentally regulated volatiles geosmin and 2-methylisoborneol attract soil arthropods to Streptomyces bacteria promoting spore dispersal") confirmed a compelling hypothesis: geosmin, along with co-produced 2-methylisoborneol (2-MIB), attracts collembolans (springtails)—soil arthropods that consume Streptomyces colonies, spreading the bacteria's spores in their gut contents. The bacteria's volatiles are not just incidental byproducts but signals in a mutualistic interaction with soil invertebrates.

This finding reframes the human detection of geosmin: humans are eavesdropping on a soil arthropod-bacteria communication system. The compounds were not "designed" by evolution for human detection; humans happen to have extraordinary sensitivity to molecules whose primary signaling function is in the soil micro-arthropod community. This is an epistemic novelty not captured in popular accounts that present geosmin as straightforwardly "the smell of rain."

## 3. Why People Believe This

The "humans can smell rain" claim, unlike many entries in this series, is substantially correct rather than false or distorted. The interesting epistemological question is not why people believe something wrong but why a largely true belief has become oversimplified in ways that conceal scientifically important complexity.

**The pleasantness of unified explanations.** Human cognition strongly prefers unified explanations over catalogues of distinct mechanisms. The smell of rain is a phenomenologically unified experience—most people have a single, coherent sensory memory of what rain smells like. Explaining this unified experience with a single mechanism and a single named molecule (geosmin) is satisfying in a way that "actually it's multiple distinct compounds from several unrelated biological and atmospheric processes, sequenced temporally as a storm approaches" is not. The latter is true; the former is memorable.

This is a case of what Fernbach & Sloman (2017, *The Knowledge Illusion: Why We Never Think Alone*, Riverhead Books) call the "illusion of explanatory depth": people feel they understand phenomena more deeply than they do, and crisp single-mechanism explanations produce confident feelings of understanding that discourage further inquiry. "Geosmin from soil bacteria" produces the illusion of complete explanation; the actual chemistry produces awareness of incomplete understanding.

**The "petrichor" naming effect.** The term "petrichor" has a particular cultural life. It is a latinate scientific-sounding name for a universally relatable experience, which makes it feel like a satisfying scientific discovery. The term sounds like it confers understanding; its use in social media contexts ("TIL the smell of rain is called petrichor") performs science literacy while conveying incomplete information. The naming does genuine cognitive work: once an experience has a name, it feels more fully explained, and the mechanism implied by the name ("rock fluid" from Greek petra + ichor) does partial work for the petrochemical (plant oil on rock) explanation but does not convey the full complexity.

Hirst (2021, *Cognition*, "The mnemonic effects of naming: known names enhance learning") demonstrated that novel vocabulary applied to familiar phenomena increases confidence in understanding without necessarily increasing actual understanding. Petrichor is an unusually clean example of this effect.

**The extraordinary sensitivity number.** The claim that humans can detect geosmin at "parts per trillion" is accurate and is one of the most striking facts in olfactory psychology. High-precision numbers attached to a familiar phenomenon are particularly effective at generating social transmission—they feel like evidence of genuine expert knowledge. The number 5 parts per trillion is four orders of magnitude below typical discussion of environmental chemical detection, making it feel like a revelation.

The number's success in social transmission may contribute to an implicit teleological narrative: evolution made humans exquisitely sensitive to geosmin because geosmin is the smell of rain, and detecting rain is important for survival. This narrative is intuitive but goes beyond the evidence. The actual evolutionary story—humans may be sensitive to geosmin because geosmin-sensitivity co-evolved with responses to other soil microbes, or because the olfactory system's architecture produces sensitivity to structurally similar compounds—is more complex and less teleologically satisfying.

**Multisensory conflation.** The "smell of rain" experience is itself multisensory: it includes olfactory signals but also the change in air temperature and humidity, the visual darkening of the sky, the sound of distant thunder, and the reduction of ambient noise as wind drops before a storm. The olfactory component is a real part of the experience, but the experience as colloquially described encompasses several sensory channels. Experiments isolating the olfactory component from these contextual cues are methodologically challenging; most laboratory studies of geosmin detection use isolated chemical stimuli that may not capture the full ecological context that makes rain-smell recognizable in the field.

## 4. Verdict

**TRUE, WITH IMPORTANT QUALIFICATIONS**: Humans can smell rain—or more precisely, multiple distinct rain-associated chemical signals—and the claim is well-supported by sensory biology and atmospheric chemistry research.

The qualifications are:

1. "Petrichor" specifically refers to plant oils released by rain impact on dry surfaces, not to geosmin or ozone. These are chemically distinct phenomena with different temporal profiles.

2. Geosmin—the earthy compound from soil bacteria most commonly cited in popular accounts—is released during and after rainfall, not before it. Pre-rain smell detection depends on ozone (from lightning), plant BVOCs (under heat stress), or wind-transported geosmin from already-wet areas.

3. The evolutionary story for extreme human geosmin sensitivity is underspecified. Geosmin is likely primarily a chemical signal in the *Streptomyces*-springtail mutualism that humans happen to be exquisitely sensitive to, not a signal that evolved specifically for human detection.

4. The singular "smell of rain" is a phenomenologically unified experience that maps onto a chemically heterogeneous set of compounds and processes.

The core claim—that humans can perceive olfactory signals associated with rain and approaching storms—is true and interesting. The popular formulation that attributes this to a single compound called petrichor is oversimplified in ways that conceal the richer chemistry and biology.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The petrichor story is an unusual entry in the Truth Vault series because the primary claim is correct. Its interest lies in what it reveals about how true-but-incomplete scientific explanations circulate and what is lost in the simplification.

The complex, multi-compound reality of rain's odors connects to a large domain of atmospheric chemistry research. Biogenic volatile organic compounds—released by plants, soil bacteria, fungi, and algae—play important roles in atmospheric chemistry beyond their role in human olfaction. Monoterpenes from conifer forests contribute to aerosol formation and cloud condensation nuclei; isoprene from tropical vegetation affects ozone and hydroxyl radical concentrations in the troposphere. The chemically complex "smell of a forest" is simultaneously a human sensory experience, a regional atmospheric chemistry signal, and a component of the climate system.

Laothawornkitkul et al. (2009, *New Phytologist*, "Biogenic volatile organic compounds in the Earth system") reviewed the ecological and atmospheric chemistry of BVOCs, noting that global BVOC emissions from vegetation are estimated at approximately 1,000 teragrams of carbon per year—roughly comparable in atmospheric chemistry significance to human fossil fuel emissions in terms of reactivity. The human capacity to detect a tiny subset of these compounds is a window into a chemical communication system of global importance.

The geosmin story specifically illustrates a broader theme in chemical ecology: secondary metabolites produced for intraspecific or interspecific signaling in microbial systems are detectable by—and may have behavioral relevance for—much larger organisms that were never the intended signal recipients. Eavesdropping on chemical signals is a widespread phenomenon in ecology (Wyatt, 2003, *Pheromones and Animal Behaviour: Communication by Smell and Taste*, Cambridge University Press), and human detection of geosmin represents a case where an important agricultural and ecological microbe's chemical vocabulary has been incorporated into human sensory experience and culture.

The petrichor myth's broader cultural resonance also illuminates how scientific concepts enter popular culture. The coinage of "petrichor" by Bear and Thomas in 1964 was a routine scientific naming act; the term's emergence into popular culture—apparently gradually, with a sharp increase in online mentions after a 2010 BBC documentary feature and subsequent social media amplification—represents a 46-year gestation from scientific coinage to cultural meme. The cultural appeal of the term shows that naming phenomena in accessible but scientifically accurate language can produce lasting public science engagement—but also that the engagement may not accurately transmit the underlying complexity.

Olfaction more broadly is an underappreciated sense in popular scientific consciousness, which tends to privilege vision and hearing. Human olfactory capabilities are more extensive than classic psychophysics implied: Bushdid et al. (2014, *Science*, "Humans can discriminate more than 1 trillion olfactory stimuli") estimated human odor discrimination capacity at over one trillion distinguishable odors, dramatically higher than the often-cited figure of 10,000. The olfactory system's involvement in memory (Proust's madeleine has a neuroscience basis: the olfactory bulb has direct connections to the hippocampus and amygdala with fewer synaptic intermediates than other sensory systems) and in emotional processing suggests that the rain-smell story is a small window into a scientifically rich and underexplored sensory domain.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not produced a dedicated fact-check on the petrichor/rain-smell claim, and its absence makes sense: the claim is not false—it is a mostly accurate popular science story that has been oversimplified. Standard fact-checking is calibrated to evaluate truth-value; it is not well-designed to capture "true but incomplete in specific instructive ways."

Science journalism has covered the petrichor story extensively, with pieces in outlets including *Scientific American*, *National Geographic*, *The Conversation*, and *BBC Earth*. The coverage quality is variable: some pieces accurately distinguish geosmin from petrichor and ozone, and note that different compounds have different sources and temporal profiles; others reproduce the simplified single-compound narrative.

The Joung et al. (2017) *Nature Communications* paper on aerosol generation by raindrop impact generated accurate popular coverage of the aerosol mechanism—this was genuinely novel science that hadn't been visualized before, and the high-speed camera imagery of bubble formation and bursting on soil surfaces was striking enough to penetrate general media. But this coverage was temporally isolated; it did not retroactively correct earlier simplified versions of the petrichor story.

The appropriate fact-checking contribution for this claim would be not a simple true/false verdict but a "true but requires precision" analysis that distinguishes the three (at least) distinct chemical phenomena involved, clarifies the temporal profile of each (before/during/after rain), and identifies what specific claims about petrichor are accurate versus oversimplified. This kind of nuanced evaluation is difficult within the binary or ternary verdict systems used by most fact-checking platforms but represents exactly the standard of rigor that science communication should aspire to.
