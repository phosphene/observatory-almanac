# The human body has more bacteria than human cells


## 1. The Claim

Among the most widely cited facts in popular and professional science communication is the assertion that the human body contains more bacterial cells than human cells — typically presented with the additional precision of a 10:1 ratio. In this formulation, for every human cell in the body, ten bacterial cells coexist: the human being is, from a cellular counting perspective, nine-tenths microbial and only one-tenth genuinely human. The claim appears in TED talks viewed hundreds of millions of times, in the opening paragraphs of microbiome review papers, in NIH educational materials, in high-school and university biology textbooks, and in virtually every popular science article written about the gut microbiome since at least the early 2000s.

The rhetorical function of the 10:1 ratio is to dramatise the importance of the microbiome as a field of study and as a subject of public interest. The claim operates as a hook: it is counterintuitive, precise-sounding, and evocative enough to be memorable. It licenses the follow-up conclusion that "you are more bacterium than human" — a claim that sounds profound and destabilising, suggesting that the concept of individual biological identity is more porous than we had assumed. Researchers studying the microbiome, science journalists writing about it, and public communicators advocating for greater diet-and-health awareness all had reasons to repeat and amplify the claim without close scrutiny.

The claim emerged from legitimate scientific engagement with very real and important biological facts. The human gastrointestinal tract does harbour extraordinary numbers of microbial cells. The microbial genetic diversity in the human body does vastly exceed the human genome's genetic diversity. The microbiome does have profound and still-incompletely-understood consequences for immune development, metabolism, and neurological function. None of these important ideas depends on the 10:1 ratio being precisely correct. But the ratio became the standard entry point into the subject, and its eventual correction generated considerable attention precisely because of how widely it had been accepted as established fact.

More recent scrutiny has found that the specific 10:1 figure is substantially wrong. The actual best-estimate ratio is closer to 1.3:1, meaning that, for a reference adult, bacterial cells modestly outnumber human cells rather than dominating by a factor of ten. The correction does not diminish the microbiome's importance, but it does repudiate a precise quantitative claim that was treated as verified when it was, in origin, a rough estimate that was never rigorously checked.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The original estimate and its provenance.** The 10:1 figure derives most directly from work by Thomas D. Luckey, whose 1972 paper "Introduction to intestinal microecology," published in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* (25(12): 1292–1294), provided an early estimate of the total number of bacterial cells in the human gut. Luckey's calculation was a rough approximation based on the best available data at the time about bacterial concentrations in fecal matter and estimates of gut volume. He arrived at a figure of approximately 10^14 bacterial cells associated with the human body. Prior estimates of total human cell number, circulating at the time, put the human cell count at around 10^13. The ratio of approximately 10:1 emerged from dividing one figure by the other.

Neither figure was the product of rigorous measurement. The bacterial estimate assumed relatively uniform bacterial density throughout the gut, which significantly overestimates total bacterial count because the large intestine harbours orders of magnitude more bacteria than the small intestine. The human cell count was itself a rough order-of-magnitude estimate. The ratio of these two imprecise numbers, treated as approximate by those who produced them, was subsequently transmitted through the scientific literature as a fact — losing its caveats at each citation link.

**The correction: Sender, Fuchs, and Milo (2016).** The definitive revised calculation was published by Ron Sender, Shai Fuchs, and Ron Milo of the Weizmann Institute of Science in *Cell* (164(3): 337–340) in January 2016, with an earlier preprint available in 2015. The paper is titled "Revised Estimates for the Number of Human and Bacteria Cells in the Body." It is a methodological paper in the sense that it does not present new biological experiments but instead carefully re-derives both the bacterial cell count and the human cell count using updated knowledge of human anatomy, cell size distributions, and bacterial density distributions across gut segments.

For bacterial cell numbers, Sender et al. focused on the colon, which contains the vast majority of human-associated bacteria. Using microbiological data on bacterial density in the colon and updated estimates of colonic volume and content, they calculated approximately 3.8 × 10^13 bacterial cells for a reference 70 kg adult male. Other body sites (skin, oral cavity, respiratory tract) contribute minor additional numbers. The earlier estimate of 10^14 was too high primarily because it overestimated bacterial density in regions of the gut that are in fact sparsely populated and because it used an overestimate of fecal bacterial concentration to represent the entire gut.

For human cell numbers, the calculation is more complex because different cell types vary enormously in size and number. Red blood cells are small and extraordinarily numerous — approximately 25 trillion, representing roughly 70 percent of all human cells by count. Platelets number around 1.5 trillion. Nucleated cells of various types make up the remainder. Sender et al. estimated total human cell count at approximately 3.72 × 10^13 for the same reference individual. Dividing the bacterial estimate (3.8 × 10^13) by the human cell estimate (3.72 × 10^13) yields a ratio of approximately 1.3:1.

**Individual variation and temporal fluctuation.** Sender et al. emphasise that the 1.3:1 ratio is an estimate for a reference individual, not a universal constant. The ratio is sensitive to individual body composition (larger individuals have more human cells without proportionally more bacteria), recent bowel movements (which can remove 25–50 percent of total gut bacteria), dietary patterns, antibiotic exposure history, age, and health status. The confidence interval on the estimate spans roughly an order of magnitude in either direction once individual variation is considered. The ratio can temporarily favour human cells following defecation or antibiotic treatment; it can temporarily favour bacteria in certain gastrointestinal disease states. A 10:1 ratio would be at the extreme high end of individual variation but is not physiologically impossible for some individuals at some times.

**What remains genuinely remarkable.** The corrected ratio does not diminish the biological significance of the microbiome. Even at 1.3:1, the fact that a comparable number of bacterial cells coexists with human cells within and on the body is genuinely extraordinary, and no other organism of comparable size harbours a comparable microbial community in terms of ecological complexity. Furthermore, the genetic dimension of the comparison still favours bacteria dramatically: the human genome contains approximately 20,000 protein-coding genes, while gut bacteria collectively carry an estimated 3 to 5 million unique genes. In terms of functional genetic diversity, the microbiome genuinely dwarfs the human genome by a factor of 100–250. The 10:1 ratio was the wrong dramatic entry point into an important subject; better ones were available.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Citogenesis: citations as a self-referential amplification system.** The term "citogenesis" — the process by which citations of citations create the appearance of independent confirmation where none exists — was coined in a 2011 XKCD webcomic but describes a phenomenon well-documented in the academic literature under various headings. Greenberg (2009), writing in *BMJ* (339: b2680) about the persistence of a false claim in orthopaedic surgery literature, traced a parallel citation cascade and identified the same structural dynamics: an original paper makes a claim; subsequent papers cite it; papers citing the subsequent papers now have multiple citations supporting the claim; the multiple citations create the appearance of convergent evidence; and no one in the chain ever returns to verify the original claim or ask whether the citations actually confirm anything independent of each other.

The 10:1 bacterial ratio case is arguably the most comprehensively documented example of scientific citogenesis. Sender et al. (2016) note that the ratio had been cited in over 3,000 scientific papers by the time of their correction. Many of these citations did not cite Luckey (1972) directly but instead cited later papers that had cited still earlier papers, creating a citation telephone game spanning 44 years in which the original caveats progressively disappeared. By the time the claim appeared in introductory textbooks, it had been cited in enough review papers to seem like established knowledge rather than a transmitted estimate.

**The rhetorical incentive structure.** No fraud was involved. But there was a systematic incentive structure that rewarded amplifying the dramatic claim rather than interrogating it. Microbiome researchers introducing their field to general audiences benefited from the 10:1 figure's rhetorical power: it made their subject seem immediately significant and counterintuitive, justifying the research investment and capturing public attention. Science journalists covering the microbiome could open articles with a striking fact. The NIH could use the figure to motivate support for the Human Microbiome Project. Public health communicators could invoke it to emphasise the importance of diet and antibiotic stewardship. Every use reinforced the claim's credibility through the illusion of consensus.

The constituency for correction was correspondingly small. The revised ratio is real but less memorable. "You have approximately equal numbers of bacterial cells and human cells" captures the same biological reality but generates less astonishment. There was no obvious actor whose interests were served by correcting a claim that served almost everyone's interests in its false form.

**The plausibility scaffold.** The 10:1 figure also benefited from being plausibly consistent with other things people knew to be true. The gut does contain enormous numbers of bacteria — the large intestine really does harbour concentrations that approach the physical limits of cellular packing. The microbiome really does have hundreds of times more genetic diversity than the human genome. A claim that bacteria vastly outnumber human cells was not inconsistent with prior experience of handling fecal matter, understanding gut physiology, or reading about microbiome research. The figure slotted into a pre-existing cognitive structure that made it feel right, reducing the likelihood that anyone would invest effort in checking it.

**The epistemological blind spot of institutional science.** One underappreciated aspect of this case is that it reveals a particular kind of blind spot in scientific epistemology. The 10:1 ratio was accepted as established fact not because it passed rigorous scrutiny but because it circulated within the scientific community itself. External fact-checkers, who were willing to debunk urban legends and folk myths, typically deferred to claims with extensive scientific citation. The claim's scientific provenance immunised it against the kind of debunking applied to claims with no academic backing. Science journalists assumed scientists had vetted it. Scientists assumed the textbooks had. Textbook authors assumed the review papers had. The review papers assumed the original citations had. No one had.

The correction required not an external debunker but an internal one: researchers with sufficient expertise and motivation to actually reconstruct the calculation from first principles, rather than simply citing prior sources. Sender et al. (2016) performed a technically straightforward calculation that would have been feasible at any point in the preceding four decades; it simply required someone to decide that verifying the estimate was worth doing. The 44-year gap suggests that the perceived epistemic cost of questioning a well-cited claim is substantial even in academic contexts.


## 4. Verdict

**CONTESTED CONSENSUS (C).** The directional claim — that the human body harbours a significant number of bacterial cells — is fully supported. The specific 10:1 ratio is substantially false for the average person. The best current estimate places the ratio at approximately 1.3:1, meaning bacterial cells modestly outnumber human cells rather than dominating by an order of magnitude. This revised ratio is itself variable enough that the original claim is not biologically impossible for some individuals at some times, but it cannot be stated as a universal or approximate fact.

**On the original claim's origins.** The 10:1 figure was a back-of-the-envelope estimate from Thomas D. Luckey (1972) in the *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* that was never rigorously verified. It propagated through the scientific literature for 44 years via citation cascades that gave the appearance of independent confirmation while actually tracing back to a single preliminary calculation.

**On the corrected figure.** Sender, Fuchs, and Milo (2016) in *Cell* provide the most carefully derived estimate currently available. Their 1.3:1 ratio for a 70 kg reference adult carries significant individual variability: bowel movements, antibiotic history, diet, age, and body composition all affect both terms of the ratio substantially.

**On what the correction does not touch.** The microbiome remains a biologically and clinically important system regardless of the precise ratio. Bacterial genetic diversity in the gut still vastly exceeds human genomic diversity by a factor of 100–250. Microbiome composition has been linked to immune development, metabolic function, neurological outcomes, and numerous disease states. This significance does not depend on the 10:1 figure being correct, and correcting the figure does not diminish the case for microbiome research funding or public health attention.

**On what the case illustrates.** The 10:1 myth is the canonical modern example of scientific citogenesis: a preliminary estimate treated as established fact through citation without verification, immunised against correction by its institutional provenance. The correction required internal expertise rather than external debunking, arriving nearly half a century after the original publication.

**Snopes gap.** Snopes has not addressed this specific claim. The technical nature of the correction — requiring engagement with cell biology methodology and citation analysis — may explain the absence.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The Human Microbiome Project and the field it founded.** The false 10:1 ratio helped catalyse enormous investment in microbiome research that has produced genuinely important findings, creating the paradox of a scientific myth that contributed to correcting itself. The Human Microbiome Project (launched 2007, culminating 2013 and in a second phase) produced the foundational characterisation of microbial communities across diverse body sites, demographic groups, and health states that made subsequent microbiome science possible. The dramatised version of the bacterial ratio played a role in building public and institutional support for this investment, even though the ratio it asserted was wrong.

The project's outputs — including the identification of core microbiome compositions, the characterisation of inter-individual variation, and the development of reference datasets for subsequent research — represent scientific achievements that will persist after the 10:1 figure is universally corrected. Relman (2012), writing in *Science* (336(6086): 1261–1262) about microbiome research priorities in the project's early years, characterised the microbiome as "our other genome," a framing that accurately captures functional significance without depending on cell count ratios.

**Functional significance beyond cell counting.** Modern microbiome science has moved substantially beyond simple enumeration toward functional analysis of what microbial communities do. Metabolomics studies characterise the chemical outputs of microbial metabolism — including short-chain fatty acids, vitamins, bile acid metabolites, and neuroactive compounds — that influence host physiology through well-characterised molecular pathways. Turnbaugh et al. (2006), studying germ-free mice colonised with microbiota from obese donors in *Nature* (444: 1027–1031), demonstrated that microbiota composition transfers energy-harvesting efficiency, establishing a causal link between gut bacteria and host metabolism that depends on microbial function, not microbial number.

The gut-brain axis — the bidirectional communication system linking gut microbial communities to central nervous system function — has emerged as a particularly active research area. Mayer et al. (2015), reviewing gut-brain axis signalling in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* (16(8): 461–474), document multiple neural, endocrine, and immune pathways through which gut bacteria influence mood, cognition, and stress responses. These functional connections are significant regardless of whether bacterial cells outnumber human cells by 10:1, 1.3:1, or any other factor.

**Clinical translation: FMT and beyond.** Fecal microbiota transplantation for recurrent *Clostridioides difficile* infection represents the most mature clinical application of microbiome science, with cure rates exceeding 90 percent documented in multiple randomised controlled trials, including van Nood et al. (2013) published in the *New England Journal of Medicine* (368: 407–415). The mechanism is microbiome ecosystem restoration: transplanting a healthy donor microbiota reconstitutes the competitive exclusion dynamics that normally prevent *C. difficile* overgrowth. This clinical success does not depend on bacterial cells outnumbering human cells; it depends on microbial community ecology.

Pharmaceutical development increasingly incorporates defined bacterial consortia intended to modulate microbiome composition for therapeutic purposes. Live biotherapeutic products — regulatory-approved preparations of specific bacterial strains intended to modify host microbiome composition — represent a rapidly growing segment of clinical-stage drug development. These approaches depend on understanding microbial function in the host context, a scientific framework that the corrected cell ratio does not affect.

**The citogenesis lesson for science communication.** The 10:1 case has become a standard teaching example in science communication courses and philosophy of science discussions for good reason: it demonstrates the failure mode of citation-based epistemology at scale with unusually clear documentation. The case is exceptional in that the correction came from within the scientific community in the form of an explicit published reanalysis, rather than through informal correction or popular debunking. Sender et al. (2016) chose to frame their paper not as a criticism of prior researchers but as an opportunity to improve the evidentiary basis for a widely used quantity — a model of how internal scientific correction can function.

The broader lesson is that citation density is not evidence of empirical verification. A claim cited in 3,000 papers may have been independently verified zero times. The appearance of consensus produced by citation cascades is epistemically distinct from the consensus produced by independent attempts at replication. For quantitative biological facts, especially those used as rhetorical entry points in scientific communication, periodic return to primary measurement rather than reliance on transmitted citation is epistemically necessary.

The 10:1 ratio also illustrates the specific vulnerability created by the rhetorical needs of a scientific field in its early stages of public justification. Emerging fields often need dramatic, memorable claims to establish public salience and secure funding. The pressure to communicate importance may create a selective advantage for inflated precision that does not reflect the actual state of knowledge. Awareness of this dynamic among both researchers and science communicators would not eliminate the problem, but it would provide a productive frame for interrogating claims whose rhetorical utility seems suspiciously convenient.

**Global microbiome variation and the limits of any single ratio.** Any estimate of "the" bacterial-to-human-cell ratio involves a reference individual drawn from a specific demographic and geographic context. Yatsunenko et al. (2012), publishing in *Nature* (486: 222–227), characterised gut microbiomes from populations in the United States, Malawi, and Venezuela and found dramatic compositional differences reflecting diet, environment, and cultural practices. The specific ratio of bacterial to human cells varying across these populations with different body compositions, gut microbial community structures, and dietary patterns would be unlikely to converge on a single universal number. The 1.3:1 estimate for a reference 70 kg adult male is a useful corrective to the 10:1 figure but is itself an abstraction that should not be treated as universally applicable any more than its predecessor was.

**Personhood, identity, and the "who are we really?" dimension.** One dimension of the 10:1 claim's cultural resonance that deserves explicit attention is its philosophical freight. The assertion that nine-tenths of your cells are not genetically human carries implicit implications about personal identity, the boundaries of the self, and what it means to be an individual organism. It invites a kind of philosophical vertigo that is rhetorically productive in scientific communication. The correction to 1.3:1 substantially deflates this rhetorical register. A claim that roughly half of your cells are bacterial does not generate the same philosophical drama as a claim that nine-tenths are.

This suggests that part of what was being transmitted in the 10:1 claim was not just a number but a philosophical orientation toward the microbiome — a desire to reconceive the human organism as a radically entangled composite. That orientation may be scientifically valuable even if the specific ratio supporting it was wrong. The functional interdependence between human and microbial biology, the co-evolutionary history of human immune systems and microbiome communities, and the vertical transmission of microbial populations from parent to offspring all support viewing humans as human-microbial systems rather than individuals with attached microorganisms. This framing survives the numerical correction, which is perhaps the most important thing to communicate about what the Sender et al. (2016) revision actually changed.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not addressed the 10:1 bacteria-to-human-cells claim. The absence is notable given the claim's extraordinary reach in popular and scientific communication. The most plausible explanation is the barrier posed by the claim's academic provenance: the 10:1 figure circulated within peer-reviewed scientific papers and institutional materials, giving it a level of apparent authority that standard fact-checking practice typically respects as outside its remit. Snopes debunks folk myths, urban legends, and political misinformation; a claim that has appeared in thousands of peer-reviewed papers occupies uncertain territory for a platform whose authority claim is grounded in opposing low-credibility sources.

This gap is significant because the 10:1 claim illustrates a distinct category of misinformation: scientific misinformation that emerges from within science rather than against it. The standard fact-checking model, premised on distinguishing expert consensus from folk error, has difficulty adjudicating cases where scientific institutions themselves generated and transmitted the false claim. Correcting it requires engaging with the original methodology — evaluating what Luckey (1972) actually measured versus what was claimed, and what Sender et al. (2016) actually demonstrated — rather than simply contrasting authoritative sources against lay confusion.

There is an argument that this kind of intra-scientific correction is precisely where platforms like the Truth Vault provide distinctive value. The expert consensus on the 10:1 figure was, for decades, that it was correct — but that consensus was based on citation rather than measurement. An entry that explains why a widely cited scientific figure is wrong, and what the correct figure is and why, adds something that neither peer-reviewed science (which corrected it in 2016) nor general-audience fact-checking (which has not substantially engaged it) has fully provided in accessible form.

The Truth Vault entry exists to supply that account: to trace the figure's origins, explain the citogenesis mechanism, present the corrected calculation and its uncertainty range, and specify what the correction does and does not affect for the broader question of the microbiome's biological importance.
