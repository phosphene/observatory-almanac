# Humans Swallow 8 Spiders Per Year While Sleeping

## 1. The Claim

Every year, the claim goes, the average person swallows approximately eight spiders while sleeping. The spiders wander into open mouths, presumably drawn by warmth or moisture, and are unknowingly ingested. The figure is sometimes cited as four, sometimes eight, sometimes a range — but the basic claim is consistent: sleeping humans regularly and involuntarily consume live arachnids.

The claim circulates in a register of confident, specific statistical assertion — the kind that seems too precise to be fabricated. "Eight spiders per year." Not "some" or "a few" — *eight*. This specificity is part of the claim's staying power. It sounds like someone measured it.

Nobody measured it. The claim is false. And its origin is one of the most instructive case studies in the history of internet misinformation — not despite being a relatively trivial myth, but precisely because of it.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

Humans do not regularly swallow spiders while sleeping. The number of documented cases of a person swallowing a spider during sleep is, as far as the scientific literature is concerned, effectively zero. No study has ever produced the figure "8 spiders per year" through any empirical methodology. The claim is not a distortion of real data — it is invented data.

Several independent reasons explain why the scenario is implausible from an arachnological perspective:

**Spiders avoid sleeping humans.** Spiders are vibration-sensitive animals. The human body during sleep produces a continuous array of signals that spiders register as danger: breathing moves the chest cavity rhythmically, a heartbeat sends vibrations through any surface the sleeper contacts, and even quiet sleep involves occasional movement, exhalation, snoring, and the sounds of internal processes. To a spider, a sleeping human is not a quiet environment — it is a low-frequency seismic event.

**Open mouths are not attractive environments for spiders.** The claim implies that spiders are drawn to sleeping human mouths. There is no documented reason why a spider would be. Open mouths produce exhalation — warm, humid air moving outward — which does not resemble any prey signal or habitat preference for common household spiders. The dark, moist interior of a mouth is not an environment that spiders preferentially seek out.

**Spiders' threat response would prevent entry.** Even if a spider wandered near a sleeping face — which does occasionally happen, as spiders move through living spaces including bedrooms — the presence of a large, breathing, heat-producing body would almost certainly trigger the spider's threat-avoidance behavior before any contact occurred. Spiders that wander into areas associated with large organisms typically retreat rapidly.

**The specific number was never measured.** No study has placed recording equipment in bedrooms, tracked spider movement near sleeping subjects, and produced a figure anywhere near eight annual swallowing events. No methodology for producing such a figure exists. The precision of "8 spiders per year" is invented — not derived from sampling, not from probability modeling, not from even rough estimation based on real data.

**The origin is documented and artificial.**

In 1993, a writer named Lisa Holst published an article in *PC Professional*, a computing trade magazine. The article's argument was about how credulous internet users — this was the early commercial internet — were with false information passed along in forwarded emails. To illustrate the point, Holst deliberately compiled a list of bizarre, specific, confident-sounding "facts" that were entirely fabricated. The spider-swallowing claim was one of them.

Holst's article was, in other words, a demonstration project in misinformation. She made up compelling-sounding statistics to show how easily they would spread. The article worked as intended as an argument. It also worked, unintentionally, as a vector. The facts she invented were spread as genuine facts, stripped of their original context. The proof became the myth.

This origin has been verified by multiple investigators, including Snopes's co-founder David Mikkelson, who traced the documented history of the claim. The *PC Professional* article is the terminus of the paper trail.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The spider myth is unusually instructive because it combines several classic misinformation amplifiers simultaneously:

**False precision.** "Eight spiders per year" is specific in a way that implies measurement. When a number is specific, we instinctively assume it was produced by someone counting. This heuristic — precision as evidence of rigor — is exploited constantly in misinformation. Vague claims invite scrutiny; precise claims do not. The spider figure of "8" (or "4" in some versions) has exactly the feel of a carefully arrived-at statistic.

**The gross-out factor.** Claims that are viscerally unpleasant, transgressive, or disturbing tend to spread faster than neutral ones. The psychology of disgust makes disgusting claims more memorable and more shareable. We tell people about the spider-swallowing claim because it produces a strong reaction — that reaction is both the signal of the myth's sticking power and the mechanism of its transmission.

**No immediate falsifiability.** You cannot check whether you swallowed a spider last night. The claim is about something that happens during unconsciousness, produces no wake-time evidence, and leaves no trace. This makes it unfalsifiable through personal experience in a way that most claims are not. You cannot say "I watched myself sleep and saw no spiders."

**Authority by obscurity.** The claim has the quality of something a biologist might know. It concerns animal behavior, sleep physiology, probability — all legitimate scientific domains. Most people are not arachnologists and cannot evaluate the claim from first principles. It occupies a zone of expertise that most individuals will never enter.

**The internet-era contagion mechanism.** The claim emerged at exactly the moment — 1993 — when forwarded email was becoming a major content distribution channel. Early-internet forwarded content was not fact-checked; it was shared because it was interesting. The spider claim was perfectly formatted for this medium: short, specific, memorable, slightly gross, and carrying no internal markers of falsity.

**The meta-myth amplification.** After the Holst origin story became widely known, the claim developed a second layer: "did you know that the spider thing is false, AND that it was invented as an example of misinformation?" This meta-version is, if anything, more widely shared than the original. The corrective spread the claim further. The myth about the myth created another vector.

## 4. Verdict

**False — emphatically and documentably so.** The claim that humans swallow approximately eight spiders per year while sleeping has no empirical basis, was deliberately invented as an example of fabricated misinformation in 1993, and is inconsistent with what is known about spider behavior. Spiders avoid sleeping humans for documented physiological reasons. There are zero documented cases of the event the claim describes.

The earc_mode is **E (Evidentially Empty)** — the appropriate classification when a claim has no empirical support and a documented fabricated origin.

This is one of the very few popular myths with a specific, traceable, documented origin point. Most myths have murky origins; this one does not. That makes it more interesting, not less: it demonstrates in a clean experiment how false information travels once its origin context is stripped away.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The spider myth's documented origin makes it an unusually clean case study for understanding how misinformation propagates, and what conditions allow it to persist even after the origin is known.

**The Holst experiment and its lesson.** Lisa Holst was making a specific argument in 1993: that the internet's forwarding culture creates a credibility vacuum in which invented information circulates as freely as true information. Her demonstration worked. The facts she invented — including the spider claim — spread and persisted for decades after the article in which they appeared as deliberate fabrications.

The lesson is uncomfortable because it suggests that even knowing where misinformation comes from does not reliably stop its spread. The spider claim continues to circulate more than 30 years after its fabrication, despite being debunked by every major fact-checking organization, discussed in countless school lessons about internet literacy, and directly disproved by basic arachnology. Knowing something is false is not sufficient to prevent its circulation.

**The "meta-myth" propagation effect.** After Snopes debunked the claim and the Holst origin became widely known, many articles about the myth's origin took the form: "Did you know that the spider swallowing thing is a myth — invented by someone to prove fake facts spread?" These articles are themselves further propagation events. A significant percentage of people who read them remember the claim and not the refutation. The memorable hook (8 spiders per year) outlasts the memorable correction (it was invented) because the hook was designed for memorability and the correction was not.

**What makes a claim "sticky."** The spider claim has the features that Richard Dawkins might call memetic fitness: it is short, vivid, counterintuitive, emotionally impactful, and attached to universal human experience (sleep). These properties are independent of truth value. Myths with all these properties will spread faster than truths that lack them. This is not a moral failing of individuals who spread the claim — it reflects how human memory and attention work.

**The false-precision signature.** "Eight spiders" is an illustration of what might be called statistical misinformation's false-precision signature: a specific number that implies measurement where none occurred. Similar false-precision claims include "we use only 10% of our brains" (false, but sounds like a finding), "it takes 21 days to form a habit" (an oversimplification of one study finding, turned into a false universal), and "the human body replaces itself every seven years" (imprecise to the point of meaninglessness, stated with false exactitude). The false-precision pattern is one of the most reliable surface markers of misinformation in popular science.

**The arachnological reality.** It is worth noting, in closing, that spiders are not our adversaries. The dramatic framing of spiders invading sleeping mouths plays on widespread arachnophobia — one of the most common phobias, affecting an estimated 3.5–6% of the population. In reality, household spiders are typically beneficial: they prey on insects, rarely bite humans, and avoid human contact where possible. The spider myth is partly sustained by phobia-adjacent disgust responses that exaggerate the likelihood of spider-human contact. The actual relationship between humans and household spiders is considerably more peaceful than the myth suggests.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The spider-swallowing claim is one of the most comprehensively debunked myths in fact-checking history, and it serves as a reference case in media literacy education.

**Snopes.** David Mikkelson's Snopes analysis of the spider claim is one of the site's most-visited articles. It correctly identifies the Holst origin, explains the arachnological implausibility, and rates the claim False. Mikkelson's research traced the documented paper trail to the 1993 *PC Professional* article and verified the Holst attribution. The Snopes article has been cited in academic papers on misinformation as a model of myth-tracing.

**The origin verification is itself complicated.** Some fact-checkers have noted that while the Holst origin is the best-documented explanation, the primary source — the original 1993 *PC Professional* article — is difficult to access. The magazine is no longer published; physical copies are rare; digital archives are incomplete. Snopes's verification relies on secondary and tertiary sources as much as on the primary text. This limitation is worth acknowledging: the Holst origin is well-attested but not as directly verifiable as one would ideally want.

**Media literacy curriculum.** The spider claim has entered media literacy and internet literacy curricula as a case study. This is appropriate. Its clean origin story, documented propagation, and persistence after debunking make it a pedagogically useful example of misinformation lifecycle dynamics: origin → decontextualization → spread → persistence despite correction.

**The claim's unusual status.** Most misinformation fact-checks are trying to establish: "this thing that people believe is not true." The spider myth goes further: "this thing that people believe is not true, AND we know it was deliberately invented to demonstrate that false things spread, AND it has spread anyway, which has somewhat proven its creator's point at scale." The self-referential quality makes it uniquely interesting as a cultural artifact.

**What fact-checkers sometimes overclaim.** Occasionally, fact-checking treatments of the spider myth state that it would be "impossible" for a spider to enter a sleeping person's mouth. This is an overclaim. What is true is that it is highly unlikely, that spiders are strongly deterred by the signals sleeping humans emit, and that the event is effectively undocumented. "Impossible" is stronger than the evidence warrants; "effectively never happens and there is no scientific basis for the 8-spider figure" is accurate.

**Rating:** False. One of the highest-confidence fact-check verdicts in the popular myth category. The claim has no empirical basis, a documented artificial origin, and is inconsistent with spider behavioral biology.
