## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In October 2020, a short video began circulating widely across social media platforms showing what appeared to be an enormous humpback whale surfacing in extremely close proximity to a kayak during an apparent breach. The viral spread was rapid: one posting on Twitter by user @Zoe_Guwop accumulated more than one million views accompanied by the caption "Yo a whale really ate this lady." Numerous other versions circulated with similar framings—that the whale had swallowed, engulfed, or devoured the occupant of the kayak.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2020) assigned this viral claim a rating of **Miscaptioned**. The video is genuine, depicting a real event that occurred on November 2, 2020, off the coast of Avila Beach in California. Two kayakers—Julie McSorley and Liz Cottriel—were whale watching when a humpback whale breached extraordinarily close to McSorley's kayak, capsizing it. Both kayakers survived the encounter entirely unharmed. The whale did not consume, swallow, or attempt to eat either person. The claim of a whale "eating" a kayaker is therefore a serious mischaracterization of a real, visually dramatic, and genuinely dangerous-looking event.

This entry investigates the details of the incident, the biology that makes the claim implausible on anatomical and behavioral grounds, the epistemics of dramatic wildlife video misinterpretation, and what the pattern of recurring "whale eating humans" claims reveals about human cognition, social media dynamics, and the failure modes of eyewitness interpretation in extreme close-encounter contexts.

## §2 The Incident at Avila Beach: What the Video Actually Shows

The incident occurred on November 2, 2020, off Avila Beach, a coastal community in San Luis Obispo County, California. Julie McSorley and Liz Cottriel were in kayaks and actively engaged in whale watching when a humpback whale (*Megaptera novaeangliae*) surfaced from below with striking speed and proximity. McSorley later told CNN affiliate KMPH that she saw what she described as a large pool of fish—a bait ball—emerge at the surface, and recognized that the whale was rising beneath or near her: "I saw the big pool of fish, the big bait ball come up out of the water. I saw the whale come up. I thought, 'Oh, no! It's too close.'"

The whale surfaced underneath or directly beside McSorley's kayak, causing both kayakers to capsize into the Pacific Ocean. McSorley stated: "All of a sudden, I lifted up, and I was in the water." Both women were subsequently recovered. Neither sustained serious injuries. The event was captured on video, and at least two distinct video angles were published—a fact that additionally confirms this was a significant public incident and not, as some skeptics briefly suggested, a fabrication or simulation.

CNN (2020) reported the confirmed details: the kayakers were unharmed, the whale appeared to be feeding—lunging at a bait ball near the surface—and the proximity of the humans to the whale was incidental rather than predatory. California news outlet KSBY's reporter Melissa Newman published both video angles to Twitter shortly after the event, establishing the verified two-camera record.

The visual appearance of the video—in which the whale's open mouth, the kayak's apparent disappearance into or beneath the enormous animal, and the turbulent water combine in a single chaotic frame—creates a strong impression of consumption. The mouth of an adult humpback whale is genuinely enormous, capable of engulfing thousands of gallons of water during a lunge-feeding event. The impression created by the footage is therefore not entirely irrational. Nevertheless, both the context and the physical reality are clear: the kayaker was capsized, not consumed.

It is worth noting that Evon (2020) documented this incident as part of a broader pattern at Snopes. In 2016, a photoshopped image showed humpback whales breaching dangerously close to a small boat—this was fabricated. In 2010, Snopes addressed a photoshopped image showing a kayak inside the mouth of a breaching humpback. The November 2020 event is, to Snopes's documented knowledge, the first genuinely real video of a humpback whale nearly engulfing a human kayaker—making it both more dramatic and more verifiable than its predecessors.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Anatomy of "Consumption" Misinterpretation and Lunge-Feeding Mechanics

The epistemic novelty in this case arises from the intersection of genuinely accurate visual information (a large whale's open mouth near a small boat) with the fundamentally incorrect inference that naturally follows from naive visual interpretation (the whale consumed the kayaker).

This misinterpretation is not arbitrary. It is grounded in a specific cognitive shortcut: humans interpret apparent engulfment as predatory consumption, because in almost all vertebrate fauna that humans encounter, an open-mouthed lunge toward a smaller object and the apparent disappearance of that object constitutes an eat event. This heuristic is generally well-calibrated. For baleen whales, it is systematically wrong in a way that is non-obvious without biological knowledge.

Humpback whales are obligate filter feeders. They belong to the suborder Mysticeti—baleen whales—and are incapable of swallowing a human being due to both anatomical and behavioral constraints. Their feeding strategy is lunge feeding: the whale accelerates toward a concentrated aggregation of prey (small schooling fish such as herring or anchovies, or krill), opens its mouth to engulf an enormous volume of water containing the prey, and then forces the water out through its baleen plates while retaining the prey inside. The esophagus of a humpback whale is anatomically small—typically cited in biological literature as roughly the diameter of a human fist, approximately 10–15 centimeters in diameter (Slijper, 1962, *Whales*, Hutchinson & Co., London). A human being could not be swallowed by a humpback even if the whale attempted to do so.

Goldbogen et al. (2011), writing in *The Journal of Experimental Biology* (The Company of Biologists), provided the most thorough biomechanical analysis of humpback lunge feeding, documenting that a single lunge can engulf a volume of water exceeding the whale's own body mass—upwards of 80,000 liters during a maximal lunge in large individuals. The speed of such a lunge, and the vast scale of the oral cavity at full engulfment, creates the visual appearance of total consumption of anything in proximity to the open mouth. This is precisely what the viral video appeared to show.

The epistemically novel claim here is that the video's apparent evidence—a whale's open mouth, a kayak in proximity, turbulent water—is genuine evidence of a real event while simultaneously being maximally misleading evidence for the claimed conclusion (consumption). The video does not show fake footage. It shows real footage from which a false conclusion is drawn, and the false conclusion is drawn because the real event, viewed naively, is visually indistinguishable from the false version.

This case represents what might be called an "accurate evidence, false inference" pattern of misinformation—distinct from fabricated evidence, manipulated evidence, or decontextualized evidence. The corrective is not to question the footage but to supply the biological context that makes "consumption" impossible. Without that context, the footage is genuinely ambiguous to a naive viewer, and no amount of visual scrutiny alone would resolve the ambiguity.

Goldbogen et al. (2007), also in *The Journal of Experimental Biology*, had previously documented that humpback whales during suction and lunge feeding can execute extraordinarily rapid body movements—accelerating from low speed to high speed in under a second—which contributes to the visual chaos in close-proximity footage, making accurate interpretation of the sequence of events extremely difficult without slow-motion analysis.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Evon's (2020) investigation correctly identified the event, the victims, the location, and the fact of survival, and appropriately rated the claim Miscaptioned. Several lines of inquiry receive limited treatment:

**The kayakers' perspective and decision-making**: The investigation briefly quotes McSorley from a local news source but does not examine why two kayakers were in close proximity to actively lunge-feeding humpbacks. Humpback feeding near bait balls is not unpredictable; bait balls themselves are visually obvious—dark masses at the surface, often accompanied by diving seabirds and surface boils. While no criticism of the kayakers is warranted, the question of what conditions led to the close proximity (drift, intentional approach, surprise) has safety-communication value.

**The whale's experience and condition post-event**: The investigation does not address whether the whale showed any signs of distress after the collision with the kayak (for example, behavioral changes observable in the video or in subsequent monitoring). Whale-human collisions during lunge feeding are not common, and the literature on whether such events affect whale behavior is sparse. Researchers tracking individual whales in the Central California feeding area may have recorded this individual before or after the incident.

**Regulatory and safety context**: California boating and whale-watching regulations specify minimum approach distances to whales—the National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS) recommends a minimum 100-yard (approximately 91 meters) approach distance under the Marine Mammal Protection Act. Whether the kayakers were in compliance at the time of the incident, and whether the incident prompted any regulatory review, is not discussed. This context is relevant for readers seeking to understand the risks of kayak-based whale watching.

**Prior "whale engulfs human" incidents**: Beyond the prior photoshopped imagery discussed at Snopes, the investigation does not examine whether there are documented historical cases of humans being temporarily taken into a whale's mouth and surviving. In fact, there is at least one: in January 2021, a lobster diver named Michael Packard was momentarily inside the mouth of a feeding humpback whale off Provincetown, Massachusetts, before being expelled unharmed (Packard, interviewed by Cape Cod Times, 2021). Packard's account specifically noted that the whale expelled him almost immediately, consistent with the anatomy described above. This incident, occurring a few months after the Avila Beach incident, provides powerful verification of the biological conclusion.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Humpback Whale Ecology, Human Risk, and the Sociology of "Monster Swallows Person" Narratives

Humpback whales (*Megaptera novaeangliae*) are found in all major ocean basins and are among the most studied and publicly recognizable large cetaceans. Adults typically range from 13 to 17 meters in length and weigh between 25,000 and 36,000 kilograms (Clapham, 2018, "Humpback Whale," in *Encyclopedia of Marine Mammals*, 3rd ed., Academic Press). They underwent a dramatic population collapse during the commercial whaling era and have since made substantial recoveries in multiple ocean basins following international protections; the North Pacific population, which includes the Central California feeding aggregation where this incident occurred, has recovered to approximately 21,000–28,000 individuals (Wade et al., 2016, *Royal Society Open Science*).

Humpback whales in the Central California waters are summer and fall feeding aggregations, targeting forage fish such as northern anchovy (*Engraulis mordax*), Pacific sardine (*Sardinops sagax*), and market squid (*Doryteuthis opalescens*). The bait ball observed by McSorley is a characteristic aggregation of schooling prey driven to the surface by predators from below and the air above (diving birds). These bait balls are sites of intense predatory activity by multiple species simultaneously; kayakers and small boat operators in these areas face meaningful risks of close proximity encounters with lunging whales, and wildlife safety organizations routinely warn of these hazards.

The narrative template of "creature swallows person whole" is one of the oldest and most culturally widespread in human storytelling—from the biblical story of Jonah to folk tales across Melanesia, West Africa, and Indigenous North America (Levi-Strauss, 1969, *The Raw and the Cooked*, University of Chicago Press). The narrative has an obvious evolutionary logic: large aquatic creatures represent genuine predation risk to early humans at shorelines and in shallow water, and memorable cautionary narratives about such risks would have high survival value. Modern viral media exploits precisely this narrative template, making "whale eats kayaker" a compelling claim even (or especially) when the footage is genuine:  the real footage is close enough to the narrative template that the misinterpretation requires almost no additional work.

This underscores a fundamental challenge for wildlife-related misinformation: the most dramatic and attention-capturing footage of real animals often most closely resembles the fictional or exaggerated claims. The very qualities that make a video "shareable"—dramatic close proximity, apparent danger, visible creature scale—are also the qualities most likely to trigger misinterpretation rather than accurate analysis. Fact-checkers face a consistent information environment in which real footage of real events constitutes highly misleading evidence for false conclusions, because the dramatic visual content drives sharing behavior while the biological or contextual knowledge that would enable correct interpretation is not embedded in the visual information itself.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: MISCAPTIONED**

The video is genuine and depicts a real November 2, 2020 incident off Avila Beach, California, in which a humpback whale surfaced during a lunge-feeding episode in close proximity to, and beneath, a kayak occupied by Julie McSorley. Both McSorley and her companion Liz Cottriel were capsized but survived entirely unharmed. The whale did not "eat" either kayaker—the claim is anatomically impossible for a humpback whale (*Megaptera novaeangliae*), a baleen whale with an esophagus approximately 10–15 centimeters in diameter, incapable of swallowing a human. The video's dramatic visual appearance, capturing an enormous open mouth in proximity to a small watercraft, creates the appearance of consumption without its reality.

**EARC Classification: C (Context — biological and situational context required for accurate interpretation)**

The analytical gap is contextual: the footage is genuine but can only be correctly interpreted with knowledge of humpback whale feeding anatomy and behavior. Without the contextual knowledge that humpback whales are anatomically incapable of swallowing humans and are lunge-feeding rather than predating large prey, the footage is visually consistent with the false claim. This is a context gap rather than an evidence gap or attribution gap.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- The full circumstances that led the kayakers into close proximity to the feeding whale (approach behavior vs. drift)
- Whether the responsible whale was subsequently tracked or behaviorally monitored
- The specific Marine Mammal Protection Act compliance status of the kayakers at the time
- Whether the incident attracted official NMFS review or any regulatory response
