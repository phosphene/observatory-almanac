# Hyenas are more closely related to cats than to dogs


## 1. The Claim

The claim that hyenas are more closely related to cats than to dogs is a popular zoological fact that appears regularly in informal natural history writing, social media wildlife posts, and science communication aimed at a general audience. The phrasing varies: sometimes it is presented as "hyenas are actually cats, not dogs," or "hyenas are members of the cat family," or the comparative form "more closely related to cats than to dogs." In all versions, the point is to challenge the intuitive assumption that hyenas — which look, hunt, and behave in ways that superficially resemble large dog-like predators — are phylogenetically aligned with dogs rather than with cats.

The claim is used as a striking example of how morphological intuitions can mislead about actual evolutionary relationships, and as a hook for teaching the principle that convergent evolution can produce similar body forms and behaviours in distantly related lineages. Dog-like characteristics in hyenas — the elongated muzzle, the hunting pack behaviour, the bone-cracking jaws adapted to a scavenging-inclusive lifestyle — are frequently cited as examples of convergent evolution, features shaped by similar ecological pressures rather than by shared descent from a dog-like ancestor.

The claim functions effectively as a "wow, did you know?" item: it is counter-intuitive enough to be interesting, it has a basis in genuine phylogenetic classification, and it can be used to introduce the broader concept of convergent evolution in accessible terms. Wildlife documentaries, natural history educators, and popular science outlets have deployed it for these purposes. But the claim as commonly stated contains implicit assumptions about what "more closely related" means and what level of phylogenetic resolution is relevant that significantly shape whether the statement is meaningful or merely technically true in an unhelpfully narrow sense.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The phylogenetic basis of the claim.** Modern molecular phylogenetics has confirmed that the order Carnivora — the clade containing all land carnivorous mammals except marsupials — is divided into two major suborders: Caniformia and Feliformia (Flynn & Wesley-Hunt, 2005, *Systematic Biology*, 54(2), 317–337). This division is consistently recovered in both morphological and molecular analyses and represents one of the better-resolved splits in mammalian systematics.

Caniformia ("dog-like carnivores") includes the family Canidae (dogs, wolves, foxes, jackals), as well as bears (Ursidae), mustelids (weasels, otters, badgers), raccoons (Procyonidae), seals, sea lions, walruses (the pinnipeds), and the red panda (Ailuridae). Feliformia ("cat-like carnivores") includes cats (Felidae), hyenas (Hyaenidae), mongooses and meerkats (Herpestidae), civets and genets (Viverridae), fossa and related Malagasy carnivores (Eupleridae), and the African palm civet (Nandiniidae). The hyenas' placement in Feliformia alongside the cats is not disputed; it is consistently recovered by the best available molecular and morphological evidence.

The clade Hyaenidae currently comprises four living species: the spotted hyena (*Crocuta crocuta*), the striped hyena (*Hyaena hyaena*), the brown hyena (*Parahyaena brunnea*), and the aardwolf (*Proteles cristatus*), which is notably insectivorous and morphologically distinct from its carnivorous relatives. Hyenas are closely related within Feliformia to the mongooses (Herpestidae) and the Malagasy carnivores (Eupleridae); the Hyaenidae and Herpestidae form a clade called Herpetoidea or Hyaenoidea depending on the analysis (Gaubert & Veron, 2003, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, 270(1532), 2523–2530).

So in the technically correct sense of the statement — hyenas share a more recent common ancestor with any Feliformia member (including cats) than with any Caniformia member (including dogs) — the claim is accurate. Hyenas are indeed in the same suborder as cats and not in the same suborder as dogs.

**Where the claim becomes misleading.** The problem with the "more closely related to cats" framing is that Feliformia is a large, diverse, and ancient clade with numerous distinct lineages, and the statement glides over the degree of divergence within Feliformia as if "being in the same suborder" translated straightforwardly into close relationship.

The estimated divergence time of Caniformia from Feliformia is approximately 42 to 54 million years ago in the early Eocene (Nyakatura & Bininda-Emonds, 2012, *BMC Biology*, 10, 12). The divergence of hyenas (Hyaenidae) from cats (Felidae) within Feliformia is estimated at approximately 50 to 60 million years ago, near the base of Feliformia. This means that while hyenas and cats are in the same suborder, they diverged from their last common ancestor very close in time to when Feliformia diverged from Caniformia. The practical effect is that hyenas are similar distances in evolutionary time from cats and from dogs — both represent ancient divergences deep in carnivore evolutionary history.

Molecular clock analyses using calibrated genomic datasets find that the divergence between hyenas and cats is roughly equivalent to the divergence between hyenas and dogs on large time-scale comparisons, because the hyena lineage is one of the older splits within Feliformia (Eizirik et al., 2010, *Molecular Phylogenetics and Evolution*, 56(1), 49–63). When specific gene trees or protein-coding sequence distances are used, the "closer to cats than dogs" claim may be formally supportable, but the margin is small and depends heavily on which measure and which genetic loci are used.

**Convergent evolution and morphological confusion.** The reason the claim is surprising is that spotted hyenas in particular exhibit numerous morphological and behavioural features that superficially resemble members of Canidae: digitigrade locomotion with non-retractile claws, a large and elongated muzzle adapted for pursuit predation, social hunting in coordinated groups, and a generally "dog-shaped" body plan with proportionally shorter forelimbs than hindlimbs. These features are convergently evolved adaptations to a similar ecological niche — cursorial pack hunter / scavenger on open plains — rather than indicators of shared descent from a Canidae-like ancestor.

The cat family Felidae, by contrast, features retractile claws in most species, an ambush rather than cursorial hunting strategy, largely solitary lifestyles (with notable exceptions like lions), and a body plan with proportionally longer and more flexible forelimbs. The phenotypic distance between hyenas and felids is large and immediately visible to any observer: hyenas look nothing like cats. The claim that they are "more closely related to cats" therefore has an almost paradoxical quality — the organisms that hyenas look like (dogs) are the ones they are not related to, and the organisms they are more closely related to (cats, mongooses, civets) show no obvious resemblance.

**The epistemic novelty: what the claim teaches about taxonomy.** The deeper point illustrated by the hyena case is one of the most important lessons in systematic biology: phenotypic similarity is not a reliable guide to phylogenetic relationship, because evolution can produce similar morphologies from different ancestry through convergence and can maintain highly divergent morphologies among closely related lineages. This principle — which was articulated by Darwin and formalised in cladistic methodology in the twentieth century — is not intuitively obvious to most people and runs counter to folk-biological classification systems that group organisms by perceived similarity (Atran, 1998, *Behavioral and Brain Sciences*, 21(4), 547–569).

The hyena case makes this principle vivid precisely because the convergence is so thorough. Spotted hyenas and domestic dogs share no recent common ancestor yet have converged so strongly on similar morphological solutions to similar ecological problems that untrained observers cannot reliably distinguish the evolutionary origin of the relevant traits. This is a more important and durable lesson than the specific clade assignment of hyenas.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The claim is intuitively wrong and therefore memorable.** The apparent paradox — these dog-looking animals are phylogenetically with the cats — is exactly the kind of surprising factoid that spreads effectively through informal science communication. It has the structure of a gotcha: what you thought you knew is wrong, and the truth is counter-intuitive. This makes it eminently shareable and memorable in a way that more nuanced statements about the ambiguities of phylogenetic distance do not achieve. The fact that the claim is approximately true — and certainly more true than "hyenas are more closely related to dogs" — means that people who have independently checked it will have found it confirmed, reinforcing confidence in the simple version.

**Visual classification heuristics.** Human folk biology relies heavily on perceptual gestalt. Hyenas look like dogs: they run like dogs, they have dog-like muzzles, and outside of nature documentaries most people's intuitions about animals are formed by visual pattern recognition rather than by phylogenetic reading. The surprise that hyenas share a suborder with cats is a direct consequence of this gap between perception-based classification and phylogenetic classification. People believed the opposite because they've been using the wrong classification tool, and this realisation is genuine and valuable — but it can be overcorrected into an equally misleading simple statement in the other direction.

**Science education framing.** Popular science educators have found the hyena-cat relationship to be an effective teaching hook for cladistics and convergent evolution. This legitimate pedagogical use has shaped the way the claim is framed: it is typically presented with the drama of the paradox front-loaded and the qualifying detail — that feliformia is a large and ancient clade and that "more closely related" has specific technical meaning — relegated to later or omitted entirely. In formats optimised for engagement and retention, the paradox tends to survive while the precision gets cut.

**Snopes gap and authority vacuum.** The claim does not have a widely-read formal fact-check assessment, partly because it is not attached to any politically contentious context and partly because it does not make an explicitly false claim that would trigger a "false" verdict — it is more accurately described as a misleadingly framed technically accurate statement. This means there is no authoritative easily-googled correction to the oversimplified version, and curious people who verify it typically find sources confirming "yes, hyenas are feliformia" without sources explaining why that fact does not mean what the memorable version implies.

**The "trick question" genre.** The hyena claim belongs to a specific genre of zoological "trick question" facts — including "pandas are technically not bears" (false: giant pandas are bears, in family Ursidae within Caniformia), "starfish are not fish," and "guinea pigs are not pigs" — that function as status markers in informal natural history knowledge communities. Knowing the supposedly counter-intuitive phylogenetic fact signals sophistication over folk-biological intuition. This social function reinforces the claim's circulation and reduces motivation for the additional step of asking whether "more closely related to cats than to dogs" actually means what it sounds like.


## 4. Verdict

**Misleading framing — technically accurate but easily over-interpreted.** It is correct that hyenas belong to suborder Feliformia and that dogs/canids belong to suborder Caniformia, meaning that within the standard structure of Carnivora systematics, hyenas share a more recent common ancestor with all Feliformia members (including cats) than with any Caniformia member (including dogs). The claim is technically true in this sense.

However, the claim is routinely interpreted to mean that hyenas are substantially similar, in evolutionary terms, to cats — more closely related in a manner that implies meaningful phenotypic or functional convergence with cats rather than dogs. This interpretation is not supported by the evidence. The divergence between hyenas and cats within Feliformia is one of the oldest in the clade, occurring near the base of Feliformia, and the practical evolutionary distance between hyenas and cats is comparable to or only marginally less than the distance between hyenas and dogs. The morphological and behavioural convergence of hyenas with canids is real, extensive, and the product of genuine convergent evolution that the clade assignment does not override.

The most accurate framing of the underlying phylogenetic fact is something like: "Hyenas and dogs look similar but evolved their similarities independently; hyenas belong to the same major grouping as cats, but this doesn't mean they are particularly cat-like, since they diverged from cats nearly as long ago as they diverged from dogs." This is considerably less punchy than the memorable version but substantially more accurate.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The hyena phylogenetic claim is a useful entry point into understanding what taxonomic classification systems do and do not tell us about evolutionary relationships. Biological classification is a human-constructed system that maps onto the tree of life at a chosen level of resolution, and the level of resolution matters enormously for interpreting statements about relatedness.

Saying that hyenas are in the same suborder as cats is true at the level of suborder. Saying they are "more closely related to cats than to dogs" is true in the formal cladistic sense — within Carnivora, any two Feliformia members are more closely related to each other than either is to any Caniformia member. But this formal truth obscures the fact that Feliformia contains a very wide diversity of forms that have been evolutionarily diverging for 40 to 60 million years. Cats, hyenas, mongooses, civets, the fossa, the binturong, the African palm civet, and the linsang are all Feliformia, but they are enormously diverse in form, ecology, and life history. "More closely related to cats than to dogs" in the clade-membership sense is analogous to saying that a remipedia crustacean is "more closely related to insects than to lobsters" — technically true at the right taxonomic level but potentially more confusing than illuminating.

The broader lesson for systematic biology education is that phylogenetic statements require specification of the metric being used. Is the comparison clade membership? Molecular sequence divergence? Shared derived characters? Time since common ancestor? Different metrics can produce different orderings, particularly when multiple divergence events are closely spaced in geological time, as is the case near the base of Feliformia.

Research on carnivore evolutionary history by Viranta (1996, *Acta Zoologica Fennica*, 204, 1–108) and subsequent molecular work has illuminated the Miocene radiation of hyenas, which produced a much more diverse and lion-like group of species than the four living species suggest. Fossil hyenas include large bone-crushing machairodonts and cursorial hunting forms that were apex predators in Eurasian and African ecosystems. The modern hyena fauna is a remnant of a much larger past diversity, and placing them in Feliformia is a statement about ancient common ancestry, not about close functional or ecological relationship with modern cats.

The convergent evolution angle also deserves elaboration beyond the sound bite. The evolutionary convergence between hyenas and canids in locomotor morphology, skull and jaw structure, social behaviour, and scavenging ecology is one of the better-documented examples of convergence in the mammalian fossil record and has attracted substantive research attention (Van Valkenburgh, 2007, *Annual Review of Earth and Planetary Sciences*, 35, 357–392). The convergence suggests that the ecological niche of large cursorial pack hunter / bone-cracking scavenger on open grasslands has a fairly constrained optimal morphological solution, and that both the lineage leading to modern canids and the lineage leading to modern hyenas converged on similar solutions from different starting points. This is mechanistically interesting and has implications for understanding the constraints on adaptive evolution in large-bodied terrestrial carnivores.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The hyena-cat relatedness claim has not been the subject of major formal fact-check assessments, as it does not typically appear in politically controversial contexts and does not make a claim that is simply false. It occupies the "misleading framing" category that is more common in science communication accuracy assessment than in political fact-checking, and mainstream fact-checking platforms are primarily oriented toward the political context.

Science journalism correction typically flags this claim when it appears in the "more closely related to cats" formulation, noting the need for phylogenetic context. Natural history publications that have addressed it tend to present the correct feliformia/caniformia classification information while adding the distance and divergence time context that the memorable version omits. Biology educators who use the claim as a teaching example about convergent evolution and cladistics generally present it more completely than the social-media version.

For fact-checkers and science communicators, the appropriate handling involves: (1) confirming the phylogenetic classification (Hyaenidae in Feliformia) as accurate; (2) noting that the popular interpretation — "hyenas are basically cats not dogs" or "hyenas are closely related to cats as we know them" — is significantly overstated; (3) explaining that Feliformia is a large and ancient clade and the hyena-cat divergence is among the oldest within it; (4) describing the convergent evolution of hyena-canid similarities as the substantively important biological fact that the popular claim obscures; and (5) specifying the divergence time context that makes the evolutionary distance concrete rather than abstract.

The teaching value of the claim is real and should be preserved: the hyena case genuinely does illustrate that morphological intuition is unreliable for phylogenetic inference, and that is a lesson worth communicating. The accurate version of the lesson is slightly more complex than the "gotcha" version but not substantially harder to understand with a brief additional step of explanation.
