## The Claim

In mid-June 2025, as U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) was conducting active operations in communities across Southern California — including, according to reporting Snopes linked to during this period, operations near Disneyland — and protests were growing in Los Angeles in response, an image began spreading across Facebook, TikTok, and X (formerly Twitter). The image depicted what appeared to be a sign mounted on the door or window of an In-N-Out Burger restaurant. The sign read: "Free burgers for all ICE agents in uniform, thank you for your service."

The claim spread in a politically charged environment in which it was immediately legible to audiences on both sides of the immigration debate. Supporters of ICE operations shared it with approval, some calling for supporters to visit In-N-Out Burger in solidarity. Critics called for a boycott of the chain. The combination — a beloved regional brand, a politically polarising law enforcement agency, a simple and quotable message of support — was designed combustibly for social media engagement. Regardless of political position, the image prompted users to react and share.

The claim has a surface plausibility. In-N-Out Burger is a family-owned West Coast chain with a number of conservative cultural associations. The chain's cups and packaging include discrete Biblical references. The company has donated to Republican Party organisations. It was therefore not implausible in structural terms that In-N-Out might have made a symbolic gesture of support for federal law enforcement. The sign looked, in its cropped versions, like something that might be placed on a restaurant door — plain sans-serif text on a white background, standard sign dimensions, recognisable context.

The image was fake.

## What's Actually True

In-N-Out Burger confirmed directly to Snopes journalist Joey Esposito via email that the sign circulating on social media was AI-generated, had not been posted in any of the company's restaurants, and did not represent any offer, promotion, or company policy. The spokesperson's statement was unambiguous: "I can confirm that the sign circulating on social media is an AI-generated image. It is not accurate nor is it posted in our restaurants."

The company also addressed the claim through its official social media accounts. On Instagram, responding to a user who asked "Is it true you guys are giving out free food to ICE agents?" in the comments of an unrelated post, the In-N-Out account replied: "We'd like to clarify that the information shown on the sign that is currently circulating on social media is untrue. In-N-Out Burger is not affiliated with the sign depicted, and we apologize for any misunderstanding."

The origin of the image was traceable through a reverse-image search on Google. The earliest appearance was a post from 18 June 2025 by the X account @lalovestrump, identified by Snopes as a popular conservative account. Crucially, the original poster immediately followed the image post with a second post confirming that the image had been made using AI. A community note on the original post also identified it as AI-generated. The image bore a Grok watermark in the bottom right corner of the uncropped version — Grok being the X platform's integrated artificial intelligence assistant, capable of generating both text and images.

The mechanism of spread followed a pattern that has become standard in AI-generated political disinformation: an AI-generated image is posted to a high-engagement account, possibly with a disclosure in a follow-up post or metadata that many subsequent sharers never see; the image is cropped to remove identifiable watermarks; and the cropped version spreads through platforms and contexts where the AI origin is no longer visible. By the time the image reached Facebook at scale, the Grok watermark that would have immediately identified its artificial origin had been removed from most widely shared versions.

## The Epistemic Architecture of AI-Generated Political Disinformation

The epistemic novelty of this entry lies in the specific mechanisms by which AI-generated images enter political discourse and what they reveal about the evolving informational challenges of the current media environment.

The traditional taxonomy of misinformation distinguishes between disinformation (deliberately false content spread to deceive), malinformation (true content spread to cause harm), and misinformation (false content spread without deliberate intent to deceive). The @lalovestrump case is instructive because it involves a post that is clearly identified as AI-generated by its original poster — both through the platform community notes system and through an immediate follow-up post — yet spreads as if it were genuine because the disclosure is structurally decoupled from the image's circulation path. The original poster may or may not have intended the image to deceive (the follow-up disclosure is ambiguous in intent — it might be competitive provocation, satire, or genuine disclosure), but many of the subsequent sharers treated it as a genuine photograph.

Wardle and Derakhshan (2017, *Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policymaking*, Council of Europe Report) proposed a framework for information disorder that emphasises the distinction between intent and effect. Even in cases where original posters act without deceptive intent, the downstream spread of false content produces the same informational harms as deliberate disinformation. The @lalovestrump case is a near-perfect illustration: the original post was followed immediately by an AI disclosure, yet that follow-up was structurally insufficient to prevent the image from spreading as genuine to audiences who encountered it through sharing, reposting, or screenshot circulation.

Jerit and Zhao (2020, *Annual Review of Political Science*, "Political Misinformation") reviewed experimental evidence on the persistence of false beliefs after correction and found that initial exposure to false claims creates a memory trace that persists even after correction, with corrections producing smaller belief updates for politically congruent false information (that is, false information that aligns with the recipient's political beliefs) than for politically neutral false information. In the ICE sign case, politically congruent receivers — those who supported ICE's operations and found the sign emotionally resonant — were likely to experience less updating from the company's denial than politically neutral receivers. The correction arrived into an already-polarised informational environment.

The specific technology implicated — Grok's image generation — is an important detail of this case that distinguishes it from earlier generations of digitally manipulated political imagery. Grok's image generation capability, integrated directly into the X platform, significantly lowers the technical barrier to AI image production and collapses the pathway between the desire to create a fake political image and the ability to distribute it to a large audience on the same platform. The Grok watermark present in uncropped versions of the image is the primary forensic provenance marker, but it is trivially removed by cropping.

Farid (2022, *Forensic Science International: Digital Investigation*, "Creating, Using, Misusing, and Detecting Deep Fakes") examined the forensic detection landscape for AI-generated images and found that while watermarking is the most widely deployed detection mechanism integrated into generative AI platforms, the watermark is only effective as a provenance signal when it remains on the image throughout its circulation life. Cropping, screenshotting, and re-uploading are sufficient to defeat watermark-based detection, and these operations are trivially performed by any user sharing content on social media. The system depends on the original poster having no motivation to remove the watermark, which is structurally unreliable.

## The Context: ICE Operations and the Los Angeles Protests

The claim did not emerge in a vacuum. Mid-June 2025 was a period of significant political tension in Southern California around immigration enforcement, and understanding why the image gained the traction it did requires situating it in that context.

ICE had been conducting active enforcement operations in Southern California communities, which prompted large-scale public protests in Los Angeles. Snopes' reporting from this period noted operations near major public facilities and a corresponding public controversy. The debacle of ICE officers wearing masks or face coverings during arrests — a specific publicly debated topic at the time, with proposed California legislation to criminalise the practice under a misdemeanour provision — was a parallel controversy that kept ICE visibility high in public discourse.

In this environment, a sign promising free food to ICE officers in uniform functioned as a precise positioning statement, directly engaging the central controversy: did In-N-Out Burger, as a culturally conservative California company, support the federal enforcement operations that many Californians were actively protesting? The image was not a generic political symbol but a targeted claim about a specific institution's stance on a specific contested issue. This specificity amplified its shareability — it was directly actionable information for both supporters (who might choose to patronise In-N-Out in response) and opponents (who might choose to boycott).

Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018, *Science*, "The Spread of True and False News Online") famously demonstrated that false news spreads faster, wider, and deeper on social media than true news across categories, with political content showing the most pronounced false-true speed differential. They attribute this primarily to novelty — false news items tend to be more novel than true news, producing greater emotional arousal and therefore greater sharing motivation. The ICE burger sign is a borderline case: false content that is emotionally and politically novel in exactly the ways the Vosoughi et al. analysis would predict would drive exceptional viral spread.

The brand choice is also analytically significant. In-N-Out Burger occupies an unusual position in American food culture — beloved by a notably broad fan base that includes both progressive and conservative consumers, particularly in California and the Western United States. The chain's quality and price-value reputation means that it functions as culturally neutral terrain in most contexts. Using In-N-Out as the imagined venue for the pro-ICE gesture was therefore more effective disinformation than, say, attributing the sign to a chain that already carried strong partisan associations. The implausibility of In-N-Out taking a side produced both credulous sharing from those who wanted to believe it and incredulous sharing from those who were scandalised by the possibility — both categories amplifying the image's reach.

## What the Evidence Shows About AI Image Detection in Practice

The ICE burger sign case is a practical illustration of what is and is not achievable in the AI image detection ecosystem as it currently exists.

The Grok watermark was the primary forensic signal available in this case. It appears in the uncropped original image shared by @lalovestrump. For users who encountered the original post on X before it was cropped and reshared, the watermark was visible and meaningful. Community notes on X further flagged the AI origin, adding an additional disclosure layer within the platform's own ecosystem. These mechanisms worked as designed — within X.

The problem is that X is not the ecosystem in which most of the image's disinformation impact was generated. The image spread primarily on Facebook, TikTok, and through messaging applications — contexts in which the X-platform community notes had no presence and in which the watermark had been cropped away in almost all widely shared versions. The in-platform detection tools are structurally limited to the platform on which the original was posted, while the harm generalises across platforms.

The C2PA (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) standard — an industry initiative adopted by some AI image generation platforms to embed cryptographic provenance metadata into AI-generated images — would, if applied to Grok's image outputs, provide a machine-readable provenance signal that cannot be cropped away (C2PA, 2023, *C2PA Technical Specification v1.3*, online). However, as of mid-2025, X's Grok image generation had not consistently implemented C2PA-compliant provenance metadata in a way that verification tools could reliably access in shared images. The gap between the conceptual availability of a solution and its operational deployment is the practical failure point.

## Verdict and Epistemic Standing

The claim that an image shared online authentically depicts a sign inside an In-N-Out Burger restaurant promising free food to ICE officers in uniform is FAKE. The image was generated by Grok, X's artificial intelligence image tool, and bears a visible Grok watermark in the uncropped original. In-N-Out Burger confirmed directly to Snopes that the sign is AI-generated, was never posted in any of their restaurants, and does not represent any company offer or policy. The original poster disclosed the AI origin in an immediate follow-up post.

The case illustrates a specific and increasingly common disinformation pathway: AI-generated imagery of political specificity, produced on platforms that integrate image generation, spreads as apparent documentary evidence with insufficient platform-consistent provenance signals to prevent its misinterpretation by audiences encountering it through secondary and tertiary sharing. The political context — ICE enforcement operations, protests, and a culturally legible national brand — provided ideal conditions for rapid viral spread across ideological lines, with both supporters and opponents of the depicted action amplifying the image before its AI origin was widely understood.

The correct response to encountering politically specific images of this kind is not simply to label them "AI-generated" after the fact but to develop the prior expectation that politically convenient images of surprising specificity — especially those involving well-known brands or institutions making unexpected partisan gestures — warrant provenance verification before sharing. The image was exactly as suspicious as a careful audience should have found it.
