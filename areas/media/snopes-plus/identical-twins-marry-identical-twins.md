# "After Two Sets of Identical Twins Married Each Other, a Doctor Told Them Their Children Would Be Genetic Siblings"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: When two sets of identical twins each marry one another — that is, twin A₁ marries twin B₁, and twin A₂ marries twin B₂ — any children produced by the two couples are genetic siblings rather than merely cousins. Their shared DNA percentage is the same as full biological siblings, even though the children are raised in different households by different (if genetically identical) parents.

**The specific couples involved**: The claim became a major viral talking point following the marriage of Brittany and Briana Deane (identical twin sisters) to Jeremy and Josh Salyers (identical twin brothers) at the Twins Days Festival in Twinsburg, Ohio, in 2018. The couples subsequently had children — Jax (born to Briana and Josh) and Jett (born to Brittany and Jeremy) — and publicly identified the boys as genetic siblings and "quaternary multiples," a term they used on social media, generating further widespread coverage (Salyers, B. & Salyers, Br., Instagram posts, 2021).

**The clickbait variant**: Versions of the claim circulating as online advertisements appended a more dramatic element: that a doctor warned the couples to "stop having children" or counseled against further reproduction because of the genetic implications. This amplification was the specific element targeted by Snopes' review of the claim. The medically alarming addendum — a "doctor says stop" framing — is what converted an already unusual-but-benign genetics story into a sensationalized claim with implicit medical horror.

**The genetic mechanism**: The claim about genetic siblinghood rests on the fact that identical (monozygotic) twins are genetically near-identical: they share approximately 99.9–100% of their DNA, having derived from a single fertilized egg that divided into two embryos. Because each member of a twin pair contributing genetic material to a child is genetically interchangeable with the other, children born to Twin A₁ × Twin B₁ and Twin A₂ × Twin B₂ receive essentially the same genetic contributions as children born to the same pair of parents.

**Related terminology**: The genetics literature uses the term "quaternary multiples" to describe children born to two sets of identical twin parents — four genetic contributors who are, in pairs, genetically identical to each other. This is a recognized if rare genetic kinship category. The Salyers-Deane children are among the most publicly documented examples of quaternary multiples.

**Scope of the fact-check**: The compound claim has two verifiable components: (a) the genetic siblinghood claim, which is largely scientifically accurate; and (b) the "doctor says stop" addendum, which is unsubstantiated tabloid amplification. Snopes rated the overall claim as a "mixture" — the genetics are real, the alarmist medical framing is false.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Identical twins and DNA identity**: Monozygotic (identical) twins originate from a single fertilized egg (zygote) that undergoes complete division into two cell masses, each developing into a separate individual. Because both individuals derive from the same original zygote, they carry the same genomic sequence, barring somatic mutations that accumulate post-division. The genetic identity of identical twins has been quantified by multiple whole-genome sequencing studies. A landmark study by Jonsson et al. (2021, *Nature Genetics*) analyzing 381 monozygotic twin pairs found that most pairs differed by an average of 5.2 early somatic mutations, but these represent a trivially small fraction of the 3-billion-base-pair human genome. For practical genetics purposes — including the calculation of kinship coefficients — identical twins are treated as genetically equivalent.

**The mechanism of genetic siblinghood**: In standard reproduction, a child inherits approximately 50% of its nuclear DNA from each parent. Two full siblings born to the same biological parents share, on average, 50% of their DNA with each other (with variation around this mean due to random Mendelian segregation). Now consider the cross-twin marriage scenario. Child C₁ born to Twin A₁ × Twin B₁ inherits DNA from A₁ (≈100% identical to A₂) and from B₁ (≈100% identical to B₂). Child C₂ born to Twin A₂ × Twin B₂ inherits DNA from A₂ (≈100% identical to A₁) and from B₂ (≈100% identical to B₁). Because A₁ is genetically equivalent to A₂ and B₁ is genetically equivalent to B₂, the genetic contributors to C₁ and C₂ are effectively identical. C₁ and C₂ therefore share the same proportion of DNA as full biological siblings — approximately 50% (Carey, G., 2003, *Human Genetics for the Social Sciences*; Segal, N. L., 2000, *Entwined Lives: Twins and What They Tell Us About Human Behavior*). This is four times the DNA sharing of typical first cousins (approximately 12.5%).

**Quaternary multiples as a recognized category**: The term "quaternary multiples" has appeared in discussions of this kinship structure in popular science literature and twin studies research, though it is not a standard clinical genetics term in routine diagnostic use. Twin-studies researchers and population geneticists recognize the category as a theoretically meaningful one. Nancy Segal, a psychologist specializing in twin research at California State University, Fullerton, has commented publicly on the Salyers-Deane case and confirmed the accuracy of the "genetic siblings" characterization (Segal, N. L., referenced in media interviews, 2021).

**The DNA sharing comparison table**: Regular first cousins share approximately 12.5% of their DNA through having one pair of grandparents in common. Half-siblings (shared one biological parent) share approximately 25%. Full siblings (shared both biological parents) share approximately 50%. The children of two identical twin pairs share approximately 50% — the full-sibling coefficient — because their respective parents are genomic equivalents of each other. The formal kinship coefficient calculation confirms this: the expected identical-by-descent fraction for cross-twin children is 0.25, which is equivalent to full siblings in terms of phenotypic trait correlation (Lynch, M., & Walsh, B., 1998, *Genetics and Analysis of Quantitative Traits*, Sinauer Associates).

**The legal reality vs. the genetic reality**: Legally and socially, the children of two different married couples — even identical twin couples — are cousins, not siblings. They have different legal parents, different birth certificates, and live in separate households. The genetic overlap does not create legal kinship obligations, inheritance rights, or any formal juridical relationship beyond the cousin designation. This gap between the genetic and legal categories is the source of much public fascination with the Salyers-Deane case: how can children who are legally cousins be genetically siblings? The answer is that genetics and law track different things — law tracks lineage and household; genetics tracks sequence identity.

**The "doctor says stop" element**: Snopes found no evidence that any physician or genetic counselor advised either the Salyers or Deane twins to cease having children on account of genetic risk to their offspring. The genetic relationship between the children produces no elevation in genetic disease risk compared to ordinary full-sibling families. Unlike consanguineous reproduction (between biologically related individuals), cross-twin reproduction does not increase the probability of homozygous recessive allele pairing in offspring because the parents of each child are not related to each other — the DNA overlap is between the parallel couples, not within any single couple. There is therefore no genetic counseling rationale for advising reduced reproduction in this context. The "doctor says stop" framing appears to be a tabloid fabrication that exploited the scientifically unusual nature of the genetic relationship to imply unspecified medical danger.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the kinship category collapse between genetics and law**:

The Salyers-Deane case is epistemically interesting because it exposes a fundamental disjunction between two conceptual systems that most people treat as unified: biological kinship (measured by shared genetic material) and social-legal kinship (measured by lineage, household, and legal designation). In ordinary cases, these systems coincide almost perfectly. Your biological siblings share roughly 50% of your DNA and are also legally designated your siblings, raised in the same household, recognized as such by inheritance law, described as such on legal documents. The coincidence between these two senses of "sibling" is so robust in ordinary experience that most people do not realize they are two distinct concepts that happen to track — rather than define — the same category.

The cross-twin marriage case surgically separates the two criteria. The children of two sets of identical twins are legally cousins (their respective parents are siblings to each other, not their own parents) but genomically siblings (their respective parents are near-identical in sequence, making the genetic contribution to each child equivalent). Either way of categorizing the relationship — legal cousin or genetic sibling — is internally consistent. The claim is surprising precisely because it forces the recognition that two independently coherent conceptual frameworks yield different verdicts on the same question, and that "sibling" is being used in both claims to mean something different.

This conceptual collision generates genuine puzzlement and fascination independent of the factual question of whether the claim is true. Even readers who verify the genetics and confirm the accuracy of the "genetic siblings" characterization are left with a residual phenomenological strangeness: the two boys Jax and Jett live in different houses, have different parents listed on their birth certificates, and will likely be raised as cousins. Yet at the level of DNA sequence — the chemical message that encodes biological inheritance in its most fundamental form — they are as close as brothers. The fact that there is no formal social institution or legal status for "genetic siblings who are legally cousins" means there is no framework into which to place the relationship, which sustains the sense that something genuinely unusual is happening.

A secondary epistemic mechanism is the "doctor says stop" amplification's exploitation of the template of incest-risk anxiety. Human incest taboos have deep evolutionary and cultural roots, and the psychological architecture is sensitive to warnings about reproduction between closely related individuals. The tabloid framing imports this anxiety — mapping "genetic siblings" onto the medical alarm mode associated with consanguineous reproduction — even though the actual mechanism of genetic risk in consanguinity (increased homozygosity in offspring) is completely absent in the cross-twin case. The children are not the products of genetic relatives reproducing together; they are the products of two separate, genetically unrelated couples. The alarm is unfounded, but it leverages a well-worn cognitive pathway.


## 4. Verdict

**MIXTURE**: The core genetic claim — that the children of two sets of married identical twin parents are genetic siblings with approximately 50% shared DNA — is scientifically accurate and confirmed by geneticists and twin-studies researchers. The tabloid addendum — that a doctor warned the couples to stop having children — is unsupported by evidence and appears to be fabricated for sensational effect.

**What the genetics actually shows**: Children born to parallel identical-twin couples inherit DNA from genomically equivalent sources, producing a DNA-sharing coefficient between the children that is equivalent to full biological siblings. This is confirmed by population genetics formalism (Lynch & Walsh, 1998, *Genetics and Analysis of Quantitative Traits*) and by the testimony of researchers in twin studies including Segal (2021).

**What the legal situation actually shows**: The children are legally first cousins. They have different legal parents, different households, and no special legal kinship designation. The genetic overlap creates no legal rights, obligations, or formal family bonds beyond the standard cousin relationship.

**What the medical situation actually shows**: There is no elevated genetic risk for the offspring of cross-twin couples compared to ordinary full-sibling families. The mechanism that creates elevated genetic risk in consanguineous reproduction — increased probability of offspring carrying two copies of rare recessive alleles — does not apply here. No responsible medical or genetic professional would advise against reproduction on these grounds in this scenario.

**Summary verdict**: The biological claim underlying the story is real and interesting. The "doctor says stop" framing is tabloid manufacture. The claim as it circulates online packages a genuine genetic curiosity inside a fabricated medical alarm — a common structural pattern in health-adjacent misinformation.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**Identical twinning rates and the prevalence of this scenario**: Monozygotic (identical) twinning occurs at a relatively stable background rate of approximately 3–4 per 1,000 births worldwide, largely independent of genetic factors, maternal age, and fertility treatments (in contrast to dizygotic/fraternal twinning, which is influenced by these factors) (Hall, J. G., 2003, *American Journal of Human Genetics*). The probability that two sets of identical twins would independently encounter and marry each other is extremely low — making the Salyers-Deane case a genuinely rare event of the type that, by the law of very large numbers, nevertheless occurs somewhere in a global population of 8 billion. Twinsburg, Ohio's annual Twins Days Festival — which draws thousands of twin attendees — substantially elevates the probability of twin-twin romantic encounters relative to the background base rate, suggesting that venue selection is not a coincidence in this particular case.

**The twin-studies research tradition**: The genetic near-identity of identical twins has been the foundation of one of behavioral genetics' most productive research methodologies. The classical twin study design compares trait similarity between identical twins (who share ~100% of their DNA) and fraternal twins (who share ~50% on average) reared together and apart, allowing researchers to partition variance in observed traits into genetic, shared environmental, and non-shared environmental components. This tradition has generated substantial evidence for heritability across a wide range of traits including personality, intelligence, mental health risk, and even political preferences (Bouchard, T. J. Jr., & McGue, M., 2003, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*). The existence of this research tradition ensured that the genetics of the cross-twin kinship question were not controversial — the framework for thinking about it was already well-established.

**Historical examples of cross-twin marriages**: Well-documented historical cases of both sets of twins marrying each other have been recorded since at least the 19th century, and their unusual kinship structures were noted by contemporaries. Before modern genetics provided precise language for describing the DNA-sharing relationship, such arrangements were discussed in terms of "confusion of paternity" or similar premodern kinship vocabulary. The genetics now makes precise what was previously described only descriptively or legally.

**The social experience of quaternary multiples**: What is less documented in the genetics literature is the subjective experience of the children themselves growing up as quaternary multiples. Jax and Jett Salyers-Deane are young children at time of writing; their own experience of a kinship structure that is difficult to explain to peers and teachers, and that has no formal template in Western social institutions, remains to be understood. Research in twin psychology and behavioral genetics has documented extensively how identical twins navigate their unusual kinship (Segal, N. L., 2000, *Entwined Lives*), but the quaternary-multiple experience is so rare that no systematic psychological study has been possible.

**The role of celebrity and media in genetics education**: The Salyers-Deane case attracted enormous media attention partly because of the couples' willingness to document their unusual lives on social media, including a reality television program appearance. This public visibility served — accidentally but genuinely — as a vehicle for popular education about genetics concepts that would otherwise remain confined to academic literature. Many people who encountered the story and pursued understanding of the "genetic siblings" terminology were exposed to concepts like kinship coefficients, identical-by-descent fractions, and the distinction between monozygotic and dizygotic twinning in the context of a compelling, emotionally engaging human story. The tabloid amplification appended to the case was epistemically harmful, but the underlying story was epistemically useful.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes' compound analysis**: Snopes' approach to this claim recognized that it had two distinct components requiring separate analysis. The genetics claim could be evaluated by consulting the scientific literature and experts in twin research and population genetics — it did not require verification of any specific event, only confirmation that the described genetic mechanism was real. This component was confirmed as accurate. The "doctor says stop" claim required verification that any such medical advice had actually been given — finding no evidence that it had, Snopes classified that component as false. The overall "mixture" rating reflects the compound structure of the claim.

**The challenge of mixture verdicts**: "Mixture" verdicts are among the more complex fact-check outcomes to communicate, because they resist the clean true/false binary that makes fact-checks memorable and shareable. A claim that is true in its factual core but false in its sensationalist addendum can be plausibly shared as either "confirmed" or "debunked" depending on which element the sharer emphasizes, making the nuanced verdict difficult to sustain in the viral information environment. This structural challenge is a general problem in fact-checking compound claims.

**Scientific credentialing**: A key element of fact-checking the genetics claim is identifying researchers with relevant expertise. Twin studies researchers, behavioral geneticists, and molecular biologists with knowledge of kinship coefficients all represent appropriate expertise for the genetics component. The absence of controversy within the genetics community about the basic mechanism — unlike factual claims in actively contested scientific areas — made the expert consultation relatively clearcut.

**Comparison to other genetics myths**: The genetics of cross-twin families sits adjacent to a broader category of genetics myths and misconceptions that fact-checkers encounter periodically, including claims about genetic determinism, misunderstandings of heritability, and confusion about the inheritance of complex traits. The cross-twin case is unusual in that it involves a claim that is largely accurate — making the fact-checking role one of contextualization and addendum-correction rather than straightforward debunking.

**Media framing and tabloid incentives**: The attachment of "doctor says stop" to an otherwise accurate genetics story reflects the structural incentives of clickbait media, which are rewarded for emotional engagement rather than accuracy. A story about a genuine but obscure genetics curiosity generates modest engagement; the same story plus an implied medical prohibition generates substantially more. Fact-checking organizations that engage with such claims play a role in disrupting the engagement-reward cycle, though the economic incentives that created the sensationalism persist.

**The genetics education opportunity**: Several science communicators and educators have used the Salyers-Deane case as a vehicle for explaining kinship coefficients, DNA-sharing percentages, and the difference between legal and biological kinship — concepts that are central to genetics, forensic science, and evolutionary biology. The fact-checking of this claim opens a pathway to genuine science education that is more broadly engaging than the typical genetics textbook introduction to these concepts.
