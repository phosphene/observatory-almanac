## The Claim

In November 2020, a highly detailed image was shared on Twitter by Mahjabin Noroozi, a researcher at the Stanford Cancer Clinical Trials Office, with the description that it represented "the most detailed model of a human cell to date." The image subsequently spread to Reddit and other social media platforms, generating millions of views and considerable discussion about its nature. Some users were uncertain whether the image was a real microscopy image, a digital artwork, or something in between. A subset of viewers compared it to an aerial nighttime photograph of India during Diwali — the visual density of lit points in the rendering resembling a satellite view of a city grid. Snopes fact-checker Madison Dapcevich, writing on April 18, 2021, investigated the image's origins and rated the claim True: the image does show an animal cell, specifically a 3D rendering of a eukaryotic cell cross-section created by Evan Ingersoll and Gael McGill for Cell Signaling Technology, Inc. This entry examines the methodology behind the visualisation, the cellular structures it depicts, and the scientific significance of attempting to render the full molecular complexity of an animal cell.

## What's Actually True

The image in question — titled "Cellular landscape cross-section through a eukaryotic cell" — was created by Evan Ingersoll and Gael McGill for Cell Signaling Technology, Inc. (CST), a private biomedical company specialising in antibodies and assays for research into cell signaling pathways. McGill, a molecular sculptor and developer of the Digizyme molecular visualisation platform, described the image on his ArtStation portfolio page as "an attempt to recapitulate the myriad pathways involved in signal transduction, protein synthesis, endocytosis, vesicular transport, cell-cell adhesion, apoptosis, and other processes."

The rendering was produced using structural data from three primary experimental sources: X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). All three techniques produce atomic-resolution structural data that describes the three-dimensional arrangement of atoms within biological macromolecules, and all three deposit their results in the Protein Data Bank (PDB), a global open-access repository of biological macromolecular structure data established in 1971 and maintained by the Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics (Berman et al., 2000, *Nucleic Acids Research*). As of 2021, the PDB contained over 170,000 structures of proteins, nucleic acids, and their complexes determined across decades of structural biology research.

The rendering is therefore not an artist's impression of what a cell might look like, nor is it speculative fiction about cellular organisation. It is, in Gael McGill's words, a "data-driven visualisation" that directly represents the structural data deposited in the PDB, scaled and positioned according to the known biology of eukaryotic cell organisation. The sizes and shapes of individual molecular components — ribosomes, mitochondrial membranes, actin filaments, microtubules, nuclear pore complexes, vesicle coats, and plasma membrane proteins — are derived from experimental data rather than artistic inference. The relative positioning of these components within the cell reflects the extensive characterised biology of eukaryotic intracellular organisation.

The distinction between a rendering of this type and a direct microscopy image is real but should not be interpreted as a criticism of its veracity. Direct light microscopy reaches a resolution limit (the Abbe diffraction limit, approximately 200 nanometres) that is insufficient to resolve individual protein molecules. Electron microscopy (EM), particularly cryo-EM in its modern super-resolution form, can resolve individual protein complexes at near-atomic resolution in their native cellular context, but producing full-cell views at this resolution across the entire volume of a mammalian cell is not currently technically feasible — cells are typically too thick for standard transmission EM, and cryogenic focused ion beam milling combined with scanning transmission EM (cryo-FIB-SEM) can image small volumes at high resolution but cannot yet produce whole-cell images of the type depicted here.

The Ingersoll-McGill rendering therefore does something that no current experimental technique can do directly: it presents the full molecular-scale complexity of a eukaryotic cell as a unified, navigable, visually coherent image. The information content of the image is real; its representation is synthesised. This is precisely analogous to the production of geological maps, architectural blueprints, or chemical structural formulae — all of which are representations that synthesise and communicate empirical knowledge in forms that the sensing apparatus cannot directly perceive.

## Epistemic Novelty: The Rendering as Scientific Argument

The distinction between "real image" and "visualisation" is more philosophically interesting than the simple True/False verdict implies. What does it mean for an image to show something accurately, when the object in question cannot be directly photographed?

This is a question with deep roots in the history and philosophy of science. Bruno Latour and Steve Woolgar, in *Laboratory Life: The Social Construction of Scientific Facts* (1979, Beverly Hills: Sage), documented how scientific "inscriptions" — experimental traces, graphs, autoradiographs, crystallo­graphic electron density maps — mediate between the natural world and scientific knowledge claims. All scientific images are, in some sense, representations rather than direct perceptions: even a conventional microscopy photograph is produced by an optical system that transforms a particular interaction between light and specimen into a two-dimensional pattern of intensity values that the scientist then interprets as "showing" cellular structure. The philosophical question of what it means to "see" a cellular component through a microscope is not trivially different from the question of what it means to "see" it through a data-driven rendering.

The more practically significant point is that the Ingersoll-McGill rendering distilled into a single image the cumulative structural knowledge of a century of cell biology and structural biochemistry. The development of techniques for the study of cellular structure traces from Robert Hooke's first observation of cells using a compound microscope in 1665 (Hooke, 1665, *Micrographia*) through the development of electron microscopy by Ernst Ruska and Max Knoll in the 1930s (Ruska, 1986, Nobel Lecture), the establishment of the biochemistry of cell organelles through differential centrifugation by Albert Claude, Christian de Duve, and George Palade during the 1940s–1960s (Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 1974), through the cryo-EM revolution marked by Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank, and Richard Henderson's contributions in the 1980s–2010s (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 2017).

What the rendering represents, therefore, is not one researcher's image of one particular cell at one particular moment, but the synthesised knowledge of what a typical eukaryotic animal cell contains and how its components are organised — knowledge accumulated by thousands of researchers across decades of work. This makes it more informationally representative of cellular biology in general than any single microscopy image of a specific cell would be. A direct cryo-EM image of a specific mammalian cell would show precisely what was in that particular cell at the moment of freezing, with all the stochastic variation in organelle position, local density, and molecular state that characterises any single biological specimen. The rendering shows a principled synthesis of what eukaryotic cells are typically found to contain. Both kinds of representation are valuable; they answer different questions.

## Why People Struggle to Interpret This Image Correctly

The initial viral claim that the image was "the most detailed model of a human cell to date" introduced an ambiguity that subsequent discussion frequently did not resolve clearly. The word "model" is used in at least two senses in everyday language: a simplified or schematic representation (as in "a scale model of the solar system"), and a detailed, faithful replica (as in "a painstaking model of the Titanic"). The image is emphatically the second kind of model, not the first. Its extraordinary visual density and complexity communicate the genuine extraordinary density and complexity of the biological structure it represents.

The Diwali comparison that appeared in some social media discussions is a useful indicator of what the image actually communicates. Viewers who spontaneously compared the rendering to a nighttime cityscape were — without knowing it — responding to the correct biological reality: an animal cell is, at the molecular scale, something like a city. It contains billions of molecules engaged in thousands of distinct biochemical processes simultaneously, with traffic management (cytoskeletal transport), energy generation (mitochondria), waste processing (lysosomes), manufacturing (ribosomes), administrative coordination (nucleus), communication (receptor-mediated cell signaling), and physical infrastructure (cytoskeleton, plasma membrane). The density and interconnection evident in the image is not artistic exaggeration — it is the biological fact.

There is also a common misapprehension — sometimes promoted by simplified diagrams in textbooks — that cells are mostly empty inside, with organelles floating in a fluid cytoplasm. This is the impression conveyed by the standard schematic diagrams that appear in educational materials because such diagrams need to be legible and cannot depict the actual density of the cellular interior. The reality, depicted accurately in the Ingersoll-McGill rendering, is that the cytoplasm of a typical mammalian cell is extremely dense: the concentration of macromolecules (proteins, RNA, lipids, polysaccharides) in cytoplasm is approximately 200–400 mg/mL (Ellis, 2001, *Trends in Biochemical Sciences*), meaning that the "empty" space between organelles is extensively occupied by a dense, crowded solution of macromolecules that is nothing like the dilute aqueous environments in which most biochemistry is studied in test tubes.

This phenomenon, called "macromolecular crowding," is a significant complicating factor in the quantitative biochemistry of cellular processes and has been recognised as an important determinant of reaction rates, equilibria, and protein structural stability within cells (Zimmerman & Minton, 1993, *Annual Review of Biophysics and Biomolecular Structure*). The visual density of the Ingersoll-McGill rendering faithfully captures this crowded reality.

## The Broader Context: Scientific Visualisation as Communication and Epistemology

The creation of high-fidelity scientific visualisations of molecular and cellular structures has accelerated dramatically in the past two decades with the combination of cryo-EM structural data, computational power sufficient to render complex three-dimensional structures, and visual design expertise. Gael McGill's Digizyme platform and the earlier work of David Goodsell (whose hand-painted molecular paintings for the RCSB Protein Data Bank are among the most widely reproduced science illustrations in existence) represent an approach to scientific communication that operates at the boundary between art and rigorous empirical representation (Goodsell, 1991, *Trends in Biochemical Sciences*).

Goodsell's paintings — which follow the same data-driven compositional approach as the Ingersoll-McGill rendering, populating cellular cross-sections with proteins and nucleic acids drawn to scale from crystallographic data — have been described by the biologist Robyn Williams as "the most honest pictures in all of science," because they refuse the temptation to simplify or schematise and instead confront the viewer with the actual congested complexity of living systems.

The development of cryo-EM to near-atomic resolution, which accelerated rapidly following the development of direct electron detectors and improved reconstruction algorithms in the 2010s, has transformed structural biology by making the detailed structures of large, flexible protein complexes accessible to determination (Nogales, 2016, *Nature Methods*). The implications for cell visualisation are profound: as cryo-EM datasets populate the PDB with increasingly detailed structures of the molecules that populate cells, renderings of the type created by Ingersoll and McGill become progressively more accurate in their molecular-scale detail. The image that went viral in 2020 will, over time, be superseded by renderings that incorporate even more structurally detailed components — not because the 2020 image was wrong, but because the experimental knowledge base is growing.

An emerging frontier is correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM), and its extension into "integrative structural cell biology," in which cryo-EM of isolated components is combined with whole-cell imaging approaches (including cryo-FIB-SEM) to produce models of cellular organisation that are simultaneously faithful to both whole-cell context and molecular-scale detail (Bharat & Bharat, 2019, *Science*). These methods represent the experimental frontier of the enterprise that the Ingersoll-McGill rendering represents computationally.

## What This Tells Us

The Snopes True verdict is correct on both the factual question (the image does accurately depict an animal cell) and the implicit contextual question (the image is not a direct photograph but a data-driven scientific visualisation, which is a different and in some ways more informationally rich thing). The appropriateness of calling the rendering a true depiction of an animal cell is directly comparable to the appropriateness of calling a detailed anatomical diagram a true depiction of the human heart: everyone understands that anatomical diagrams are representations, but their accuracy as representations is precisely what makes them valuable.

What the image most powerfully communicates — to anyone who takes the time to understand what they are looking at — is the staggering molecular complexity of the most fundamental unit of life. The crowded, dense, intimately organised interior of the eukaryotic cell, with its thousands of distinct molecular species engaged in thousands of simultaneous biochemical processes, is as far from the sanitised diagram in the introductory biology textbook as a real city is from a map notation. The image went viral for the comparison to Diwali night lights; it deserves attention for what it actually shows.
