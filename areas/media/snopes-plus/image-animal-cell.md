# Does This Image Show an Animal Cell?

## 1. The Claim

In November 2020, a highly detailed, richly colored three-dimensional rendering depicting the interior of a eukaryotic cell circulated widely on social media, shared on Reddit's r/interestingasfuck and Twitter by Mahjabin Noroozi, a researcher with the Stanford Cancer Clinical Trials Office, who described it as "the most detailed model of a human cell to date." The image prompted immediate reactions combining astonishment and skepticism: viewers found it simultaneously beautiful and implausible, with some comparing its jewel-toned, light-studded complexity to an aerial photograph of India on Diwali night—a visual metaphor that itself went viral alongside the image.

The implicit question the image raised—"Is this real?"—is actually several distinct questions bundled together. It is simultaneously a question about authenticity (is this a genuine scientific product rather than artistic invention?), about accuracy (does it correctly represent the structural biology of animal cells?), about methodology (what data sources were used to construct the representation?), and about epistemology (in what sense does a 3D model "show" something that cannot be directly photographed at the scale in question?). The Snopes claim statement—"A highly detailed image showed the many microscopic components that make up an animal cell"—simplifies this bundle to a question about authenticity and accuracy, which it then confirms as True.

The image was created by Evan Ingersoll and Gael McGill for Cell Signaling Technology, Inc. (CST), a private biomedical research company based in Danvers, Massachusetts. McGill is a molecular visualization expert and Director of Molecular Graphics at Digizyme, Inc., a scientific visualization company that specializes in building structural molecular models from experimental structural biology data. The methodology employed—constructing a 3D scene by assembling experimentally determined molecular structures from X-ray crystallography, nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, and cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) datasets into a spatially accurate representation of a cellular cross-section—is a recognized and scientifically valuable approach to making molecular and cellular structural biology accessible to non-specialist audiences.

The claim connects to broader questions about how scientific knowledge is communicated through images, what standards of accuracy apply to scientific visualizations, and how the line between scientific illustration, scientific model, and scientific data is drawn. These questions have become increasingly prominent as cryo-EM, super-resolution light microscopy, and computational structural biology have produced increasingly detailed data about molecular structures that are below the resolution limit of conventional optical microscopy and cannot be "photographed" in any simple sense.

## 2. What's Actually True

The image is genuine, the creators are real and have documented professional credentials in molecular visualization, and the methodological description is accurate. Gael McGill has published peer-reviewed work on molecular visualization methodology (McGill, 2008, *Structure*) and the Digizyme visualization approach has been applied to multiple educational and research communication projects for academic and commercial clients.

The cellular structures represented in the image are scientifically accurate at the level of molecular architecture. The components depicted—including microtubules (tubulin polymer networks), the nuclear envelope and nuclear pore complexes, mitochondria with their characteristic double-membrane structure and internal cristae, ribosomes (both free and endoplasmic-reticulum-associated), vesicle membranes, actin filaments, clathrin-coated endocytic vesicles, and various signaling proteins—are all real structures that have been characterized through experimental structural biology. The spatial relationships and relative abundances are based on quantitative cell biology data, not artistic invention.

The data types that underlie the individual molecular components are standard structural biology tools. X-ray crystallography provides atomic-resolution structures of purified proteins and complexes crystallized under controlled conditions, with results deposited in the Protein Data Bank (PDB), which as of 2021 contained approximately 180,000 experimentally determined macromolecular structures (Berman et al., 2000, *Nucleic Acids Research*). NMR spectroscopy provides solution-phase structural information particularly useful for smaller proteins and dynamic systems. Cryo-electron microscopy—which earned Jacques Dubochet, Joachim Frank, and Richard Henderson the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry—allows direct visualization of biological macromolecules and complexes in near-native aqueous conditions, without the need for crystallization (Callaway, 2015, *Nature*).

The resulting visualization represents the state of structural knowledge about eukaryotic cell components as of the time of its creation. Individual molecular structures are placed at their correct relative sizes (determined by structural biology), and their spatial organization within the cell reflects current understanding of cell biology as established through decades of fluorescence microscopy, transmission electron microscopy, and biochemical fractionation studies.

The characterization of the image as showing "processes that occur in animal cells" rather than "an animal cell directly" is an important factual nuance that Snopes includes and that deserves emphasis. The image is a composite representation, not a snapshot of a single cell at a single moment. Different molecular structures depicted were determined from different organisms, at different purification states, under different experimental conditions. The cell shown does not correspond to a specific cell type from a specific organism at a specific moment; it is a composite model representing the general eukaryotic cellular landscape as understood from multiple independent lines of structural and cell biological evidence.

## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Framing Obscures

The Snopes verdict correctly confirms the image's authenticity and general accuracy, but frames the epistemic issue as a simple binary—is the image real or fake?—without engaging the more interesting and instructive questions about what kind of scientific image this is and what it takes to move from experimental data to image.

**The image is a model, not a measurement.** This distinction is crucial for understanding what standards of accuracy apply. Conventional microscopic images are measurements: they record light or electrons scattered from a physical sample under specific conditions and processed through specific optics and detectors. A 3D visualization constructed from composite structural biology data is a model: it synthesizes multiple independent measurements into an explicit representation of something that cannot be directly measured in its assembled form. Models and measurements have different epistemic statuses, different sources of error, and are assessed against different standards of accuracy.

The gold standard for direct cellular imaging at molecular resolution is cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET), which produces 3D reconstructions of specific regions within frozen cells at approximately 2–5 nanometer resolution. Cryo-ET studies of specific cell types, including synaptic terminals (Fernandez-Busnadiego et al., 2011, *Journal of Cell Biology*), nuclear pores (Beck et al., 2004, *Science*), and mitotic spindles (Kiewisz et al., 2022, *eLife*), have validated many aspects of the general cellular architecture depicted in scientific visualizations. The Ingersoll/McGill visualization is generally consistent with these direct empirical observations but depicts structures at a level of idealized clarity and isolation that direct cellular imaging does not achieve—actual cryoelectron tomograms show a much denser, more entangled, and more ambiguous molecular milieu.

**Molecular crowding is systematically underrepresented.** One of the most important findings of quantitative cell biology is that the cytoplasm is extraordinarily crowded. As famously documented by Ellis (2001, *Trends in Biochemical Sciences*), the total concentration of macromolecules in the cytoplasm is approximately 200–300 mg/mL, corresponding to volume occupancy by macromolecules in the range of 20–30%. At this level of molecular density, individual protein molecules are separated from their nearest neighbors by distances of only a few nanometers, equivalent to only a few molecular diameters. This level of molecular crowding has major consequences for reaction kinetics, protein-protein interaction probabilities, diffusion rates, and enzymatic function that are not apparent from "dilute" representations of cellular architectures.

The Ingersoll/McGill visualization explicitly acknowledges this limitation in the artist's statement: "Although dilute in its concentration relative to a real cell, this rendering is also an attempt to visualize the great complexity and beauty of the cell's molecular choreography." This crucial epistemic caveat—present in the creator's own documentation but absent from the viral social media circulation—means that the image systematically understates the density and complexity of the real cellular interior in the interest of visual parseability. Accurately representing true molecular crowding would produce an image in which almost no individual structures would be distinguishable—a visually impenetrable mass rather than an intelligible landscape.

**The question "what does a cell look like?" is philosophically complex.** Cells at the molecular scale are not objects with fixed appearances in the way that macroscopic objects are. The appearance of any molecular structure in an image depends on the specific imaging modality (X-ray crystallography resolves atomic positions; fluorescence microscopy resolves sub-cellular compartments; cryo-ET resolves mesoscale architecture), the specific preparation method (thin section vs. freeze-fracture vs. whole-cell imaging), the specific staining or labeling approach, and the specific computational processing pipeline applied to raw data. Each imaging approach effectively "sees" different aspects of the same underlying reality, producing complementary rather than redundant information.

A transmission electron microscopy image of a thin-sectioned cell presents cells as a landscape of electron-dense membranes, organelles, and chromatin, with proteins appearing as amorphous density rather than as individually resolved molecules. Super-resolution fluorescence microscopy using structured illumination or stimulated emission depletion (STED) achieves approximately 50–100 nm resolution, sufficient to resolve individual organelles and some large complexes but not individual protein molecules. Single-molecule localization microscopy (SMLM) achieves approximately 20 nm resolution under ideal conditions, beginning to reveal individual protein complex arrangements at the plasma membrane. Cryo-ET approaches 2–5 nm resolution in specific tomographic volumes but is limited to small fields of view. No single imaging modality captures the full cellular reality depicted in the Ingersoll/McGill visualization, which is why such composite modeling representations remain scientifically valuable while being epistemically distinct from direct imaging data.

**The significance of the scientific visualization practice deserves its own treatment.** McGill's work at Digizyme is part of a broader methodological tradition in structural biology visualization that includes pioneering work by David S. Goodsell, whose watercolor illustrations of cellular landscapes have been published in *PNAS*, *Biophysical Journal*, and through the Protein Data Bank Molecule of the Month series since the early 1990s (Goodsell et al., 2019, *Journal of Integrative Bioinformatics*). Goodsell's visualizations, like McGill's, are composites that synthesize quantitative structural biology data with artistic judgment about color, scale, and composition to produce images that communicate scientific knowledge while acknowledging the gap between model and measurement.

The broader practice of molecular visualization has been analyzed as a field of epistemic practice in its own right (Francoeur, 1997, *Social Studies of Science*), with scholars examining how choices about rendering style, color assignment, surface representation, and compositional framing encode specific scientific commitments, communicate uncertainty, and shape the intuitions of scientists and public audiences about molecular reality.

**The viral comparison to Diwali night lights** is epistemically interesting in its own right. The observation that a molecular landscape model resembles an aerial photograph of city lights at night reflects a genuine structural similarity: both represent approximately isotropic distributions of discrete luminous objects against a dark or deep-colored background at widely separated scales. This kind of cross-scale visual analogy has been noted by physicists studying fractal self-similarity and by complexity theorists examining scale-invariant network structures. The aesthetic resonance is not accidental—similar physical organizational principles (network connectivity, power-law distributions of connection frequency, scale-free clustering) may govern both the organization of city infrastructure and the organization of cellular signal transduction networks (Barabási & Oltvai, 2004, *Nature Reviews Genetics*).

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** True (with crucial epistemic qualification about model vs. measurement status)

The image is genuine, created by credentialed molecular visualization professionals using published structural biology data, and accurately represents the general molecular architecture of eukaryotic cells as understood from convergent experimental evidence. The Snopes verdict of True is warranted.

**EARC Classification: R (Representation gap)**

The classification reflects the central epistemic issue: the fact-check verifies authenticity without adequately addressing the image's epistemological status as a model/composite visualization rather than direct cellular imaging, and without communicating the systematic distortions (particularly molecular crowding) that this representational approach introduces.

**Gap Category: Representation Gap**

The primary analytical gap is the failure to educate readers about the distinction between molecular models built from structural data and direct measurements of cellular architecture. This distinction matters because it changes how the image should be interpreted: not as "what cells literally look like," but as "the best current scientific synthesis of what we know about cellular molecular architecture, rendered in a format that prioritizes communicability over completeness." This is a different and more sophisticated truth, and communicating it would constitute genuine public education rather than merely fact-checking.

## 5. Cross-Connections

The animal cell visualization claim connects to a cluster of Truth Vault themes about scientific imaging, the epistemology of models, and the public communication of invisible biological realities.

The methodological discussion of cryo-electron microscopy as the source of many individual component structures connects to the broader revolution in structural biology enabled by the "resolution revolution" in cryo-EM documented by Kühlbrandt (2014, *Science*), which lowered the barrier to high-resolution structure determination and has been transformative for drug discovery, immunology (particularly understanding viral spike proteins during COVID-19 vaccine development), and fundamental cell biology.

The molecular crowding discussion connects to entries on colloidal chemistry, the physical chemistry of the cellular interior, and the ways in which the "dilute biochemistry" model taught in introductory cell biology understates the true complexity of intracellular biochemistry. The macromolecular crowding effect has been shown to influence protein folding kinetics (van den Berg et al., 1999, *EMBO Journal*), protein aggregation (Minton, 2001, *Journal of Biological Chemistry*), and enzymatic reaction rates in ways that differ substantially from predictions based on dilute solution biochemistry.

The scientific visualization practice connects to broader discussions of scientific image manipulation, the reproducibility crisis, and the epistemology of visual evidence in science (Gross, 2009, *Quarterly Review of Biology*). High-impact journals including Nature, Science, and Cell have established increasingly detailed image integrity policies specifically in response to concerns about unacknowledged image manipulation that crosses from legitimate enhancement and composition into misrepresentation of experimental results.

The Diwali lights comparison and the aesthetic experience of the cellular image connect to discussions of scientific aesthetics, the relationship between mathematical structure and visual beauty, and the role of aesthetic response in scientific intuition—a topic explored by Dirac (who famously preferred mathematically beautiful theories), by the physicist Frank Wilczek in *A Beautiful Question* (2015, Penguin), and by science studies scholars examining beauty as an epistemic value in structural biology (Daston & Galison, 2007, *Objectivity*, Zone Books).
