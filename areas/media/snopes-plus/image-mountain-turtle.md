## The Claim

An image circulating on social media since at least 2018 depicts what appears to be an extraordinary natural formation: a large mountain or rocky prominence rising from a scenic lake landscape, its summit bearing an unmistakable resemblance to the head and neck of a giant turtle. The formation appears to rise from calm blue water against a backdrop of forested slopes and mountain ridges. The "turtle head" — formed by the rounded knob of the peak — is rendered in convincing geological detail, with what appears to be rock texture and vegetation suggesting an aged, weathered surface.

Posts sharing the image span multiple platforms and multiple years. When the image reappeared in 2025 on Threads, Instagram, and Facebook, it was presented in various captions as a real natural formation — a mountain that genuinely resembles a turtle. One 2019 iteration, from a Facebook group called *Gardening Humour*, identified it as "a tree in Cambodia" that resembled "a giant turtle." A Czech-language blog called *Rouming* posted it in January 2019 as a natural curiosity. A meme-generation website called Imgflip appears to be among the earliest documented hosts of the image, from 2018. The image resurfaced again on Pinterest, Instagram, and TikTok in 2023 and 2024 before achieving another wave of circulation in 2025.

Snopes rated this claim **Fake** in a September 2025 fact-check by Nick Hardinges — updating and republishing an original debunking from January 2019 by Dan Evon. The verdict is unambiguous: the image is digital artwork produced by combining altered versions of at least two distinct real photographs, neither of which shows a turtle-shaped mountain.

## What's Actually True

The "Turtle Mountain" image is a composite. It is not a photograph of any single real location. Snopes' 2019 investigation identified the component images.

The background of the composite — the lake, the forested slopes, and the distant mountain ridges — derives from a photograph taken by Jacob Frank, a National Park Service ranger and photographer, in August 2015. Frank's original image, hosted on the National Park Service's official Flickr account (user glaciernps), depicts Wild Goose Island in Glacier National Park, Saint Mary Lake, in the state of Montana. Wild Goose Island is a tiny rocky island in Saint Mary Lake, one of the most photographed landscapes in Glacier National Park, with a visual profile instantly recognisable to anyone familiar with the park. Frank's photograph was taken in hazy conditions that give the image a soft, atmospheric quality, and it shows the distinctive profile of the island against the lake's blue water with the park's characteristic mountain backdrop.

The turtle-head formation superimposed onto that background corresponds to a photograph of Pilot Mountain in North Carolina. Pilot Mountain is a quartzite monadnock — an isolated hill or mountain rising from a relatively flat surrounding terrain — located in Surry County, North Carolina, in the Sauratown Mountain range. Its summit consists of a rounded rocky knob, known locally as the "Big Pinnacle," which rises approximately 1,400 metres above sea level. From certain angles, the rounded knob protrudes above the surrounding treeline in a manner that can, particularly with some photographic manipulation, suggest a large rounded head emerging from a body-like rocky mass below. Pilot Mountain is a North Carolina state park and is a genuinely distinctive and attractive natural feature — it has been a landmark for indigenous peoples, travellers, and settlers for centuries.

In the composite "Turtle Mountain" image, Pilot Mountain's distinctive rocky knob has been extracted from its original North Carolina context, altered to suggest additional "neck" and "body" features, and superimposed onto Frank's Glacier National Park background. The resulting image — Pilot Mountain's rounded knob floating above a Montana lake landscape it has never overlooked — reads convincingly as a single location to viewers who are unfamiliar with both original sources.

Snopes' 2019 investigation produced a three-part collage placing the composite image alongside the two source photographs, demonstrating the matching of landscape features. The Wild Goose Island/Saint Mary Lake landscape in the background of the composite corresponds precisely to Frank's photograph — the island shape, waterline, and mountain background are the same. The rocky knob superimposed as the turtle head corresponds precisely to photographs of Pilot Mountain available on Pixabay, a stock photography and free image repository. The Australian Associated Press conducted its own investigation and debunked the image independently in January 2022.

No real natural formation corresponding to the composite image exists. There is no "Turtle Mountain" in Glacier National Park, Montana, Cambodia, or anywhere else that matches the image's specific visual features.

## Why People Believe This

The "Turtle Mountain" composite achieves its effect through several intersecting factors, some perceptual and some cultural, that make the image persuasive even to careful viewers on initial encounter.

**The pareidolia response.** The most fundamental mechanism is pareidolia — the perceptual tendency to detect meaningful patterns, particularly faces and animals, in ambiguous or random visual stimuli. Pareidolia is a well-documented feature of human visual processing, understood as a consequence of the brain's object-recognition systems being calibrated toward high sensitivity rather than high specificity: it is more evolutionarily costly to fail to see a face (or predator, or competitor) than to see one where none exists. Research by Kovic, Plunkett, and Westermann (2010, *Neuropsychologia*, vol. 48, pp. 3406–3415) demonstrated that face-detection in non-face stimuli is automatic and occurs very rapidly, before conscious evaluation. More broadly, the tendency to see animals and faces in clouds, rock formations, tree bark, and other natural textures is universal across cultures.

Natural rock formations that genuinely resemble animals are not rare — they include well-documented examples such as the Old Man of the Mountain in New Hampshire (a rock profile that resembled a human face, which collapsed in 2003), the rock formations of the Valley of the Gods in Utah, and numerous named geological features worldwide. The pareidolia response is therefore calibrated to genuinely fire at natural landscapes, because natural landscapes occasionally produce genuine animal-resembling shapes. Viewers encountering the "Turtle Mountain" image are responding entirely normally to the image's visual profile — they are seeing something that genuinely looks like a turtle, because the image has been constructed to look like a turtle. The response is not naive credulity but normal perceptual processing.

**The photographic realism of the composite.** The composite is well-constructed for its apparent purpose. Both source photographs are high-resolution, professionally taken images with matching tonal qualities — the hazy, soft atmospheric quality of Frank's Glacier National Park photograph works with rather than against the inserted mountain element. The colour grading and perspective of the inserted Pilot Mountain element are broadly consistent with the background landscape. Viewers with no prior familiarity with either Wild Goose Island or Pilot Mountain have no perceptual cue — no mismatched colour profile, no obvious scaling error — that would trigger visual suspicion. The image looks like a real photograph.

**The "nature is amazing" content category.** The image circulates primarily within content categories devoted to natural wonders, curious wildlife, geological oddities, and landscape photography. These categories have well-established audiences across Facebook, Instagram, Pinterest, and similar platforms, and they have a strong norm of sharing genuinely extraordinary real-world images: real formations that do resemble faces or animals, real landscapes with unusual geometry, real photographs of unusual events. The "Turtle Mountain" image fits seamlessly into this content stream because it looks exactly like the kind of image that genuinely circulates in these categories when a real remarkable formation is photographed. Viewers in these communities are oriented toward sharing extraordinary images, not toward verification.

**The lack of claimed specific location.** Several versions of the circulating image offered no specific location claim — just the image and a caption about a turtle-shaped mountain or tree. The absence of a verifiable location claim paradoxically reduces the opportunity for scepticism. If the claim were "this is located at coordinates X, in park Y, in country Z," a motivated viewer could verify or falsify the claim by consulting maps, satellite imagery, or geological databases. Without a specific location claim, there is nothing specific to check. The image is presented as a generalised wonder rather than a specific verifiable assertion.

**The Cambodia attribution.** One early version of the image attributed it to Cambodia — identifying the feature as "a tree in Cambodia." Cambodia is not a country most Western social media users are familiar with at the level of detailed landscape knowledge, and the country does contain genuinely remarkable natural and architectural features (Angkor Wat, the Cardamom Mountains, the Mekong River Delta). The Cambodia attribution, while false, was not immediately implausible to many of the audiences it reached, and it provided just enough specific-seeming detail to give the image's claims a surface of credibility without providing enough detail to enable verification.

---

## Verdict

The image is **Fake**. It is a composite, not a photograph. No real mountain matching its visual profile exists. The background derives from Jacob Frank's 2015 photograph of Wild Goose Island in Glacier National Park, Montana; the "turtle head" derives from a photograph of Pilot Mountain in North Carolina. The two elements were combined and manipulated to produce the composite. Snopes first debunked the image in January 2019 and re-confirmed the debunking in September 2025.

Pilot Mountain is a real and genuinely distinctive natural feature. Wild Goose Island is a real and genuinely beautiful natural feature. Neither, in their actual location or form, shows a turtle-shaped mountain.

## The Wider Picture

The "Turtle Mountain" image is a good case study in the mechanics of fabricated natural-wonder content and the visual culture that sustains it, as well as in the perceptual and epistemic gaps it exploits.

**Pareidolia, pattern recognition, and the natural world.** Pareidolia as a perceptual phenomenon has been studied extensively in psychological and neurological research contexts. It is particularly well-documented for face perception — the tendency to see faces in non-face stimuli — but extends to other biologically and evolutionarily salient categories including animals, eyes, and hands. The mechanism involves overlapping neural systems for object recognition and categorical classification: when perceptual stimuli are ambiguous, the brain resolves the ambiguity in the direction of biologically significant categories rather than toward the null hypothesis.

Research by Liu, Li, Feng, Li, Tian, and Lee (2014, *Cortex*, vol. 58, pp. 56–67) using fMRI demonstrated that the fusiform face area (FFA), a brain region specifically associated with face perception, activates in response to pareidolic face stimuli — objects that look like faces — with patterns comparable to those produced by viewing actual faces. This neural evidence suggests that pareidolia is not a "mistake" in any simple sense but rather a systematic consequence of a perceptual system that is calibrated to face-recognition rather than to accurate statistical classification of visual stimuli.

The cultural dimension of pareidolia is also significant. The same tendency toward face and animal perception in natural features underlies numerous traditions of identifying significant or sacred features in landscapes — "Old Man" mountains, turtle-shaped rocks in indigenous cosmologies, the "Man in the Moon," the constellations of the night sky. These traditions represent pareidolia operating within cultural frameworks that give perceptual pattern-matching specific meaning and social function. The secular viral version — sharing an amusing "natural resemblance" image — is a stripped-down, socially-network-adapted version of the same fundamental perceptual dynamic.

**Composite image fabrication in the social media era.** The "Turtle Mountain" image was created and began circulating in approximately 2018 — a period in which image editing tools had become widely accessible but AI image generation was not yet available to most users. The image appears to have been produced with standard photo editing software capable of layer compositing and colour matching. It represents the kind of intentional fabrication that requires modest but non-negligible skill: selecting compatible source images, extracting elements, adjusting scale and lighting, and blending seams convincingly.

This entry point — competent but not extraordinary digital fabrication — represents a tier of image manipulation that predates AI-generated imagery and that fact-checkers have long had to address using reverse image search and source photograph identification. The tools to detect this kind of composite are available and have been systematically applied by organisations including Snopes, AAP Fact Check, and Reuters Fact Check. The tools to create such composites are also widely available — which means the supply of fabricated natural-wonder images shows no sign of decreasing.

**The geological reality of Pilot Mountain.** Pilot Mountain itself deserves far more attention than it receives when it appears in this story, because it is a genuinely remarkable geological formation with a rich cultural history.

Pilot Mountain is a quartzite monadnock — a geological term for an isolated hill or mountain that has survived the erosion that levelled the surrounding terrain, typically because its rock is harder or more resistant than the material that surrounded it. Quartzite is among the hardest common rock types, and Pilot Mountain's survival as a prominent feature in an otherwise relatively flat section of the North Carolina Piedmont is a direct consequence of its exceptional resistance to weathering. The mountain's "Big Pinnacle" — the rounded rocky knob that appears in the "Turtle Mountain" composite — is composed of quartzite and rises approximately 120 metres above the surrounding forested shoulders of the mountain.

Pilot Mountain was a landmark for the Saura Indigenous people, who called it *Jomeokee*, meaning approximately "great guide" or "pilot" — a geographic reference point used for navigation across the Piedmont. European settlers adopted the "Pilot" name for the same navigational reason. The mountain was acquired by North Carolina as a state park in 1968 and is today a popular hiking and rock-climbing destination. The Big Pinnacle's rounded quartzite summit does genuinely produce pareidolic impressions in some viewers — comparisons to a turtle's head are not the only animal association that has been made, and its distinctive silhouette makes it an unusually recognisable landmark.

Understanding Pilot Mountain's actual geological and cultural context enriches rather than diminishes the interest of the image: the composite is fake, but the raw material — an extraordinary quartzite monadnock with a long human history — is real.

**The Wild Goose Island context.** Jacob Frank's 2015 photograph, the source of the "Turtle Mountain" background, is itself a genuinely beautiful image of one of Glacier National Park's most iconic landscapes. Saint Mary Lake and Wild Goose Island have been photographed millions of times and are among the most recognised landscape images in the American West. Frank's image, taken in hazy atmospheric conditions that gold the landscape, is a high-quality example of the genre. It appeared in the composite because it provides a convincing and beautiful natural background — but its origin in a specific, recognisable National Park landscape also turns out to be a key to debunking the composite, since the topographic and reflective details of Saint Mary Lake and its surrounding mountains are well-documented enough to allow identification.

**The content moderation and verification challenge.** The multi-year persistence of the "Turtle Mountain" image — circulating across at least seven years and a dozen or more platforms, surviving multiple debunkings — illustrates a fundamental asymmetry in the information environment: false or fabricated content is easy to produce and easy to reshare, while effective debunking requires specific effort (source identification, comparison imagery, authoritative publication) and must be repeatedly renewed as the image resurfaces in new contexts to reach new audiences.

Platform content moderation does not systematically identify and label known-false images beyond narrow categories (health misinformation, election misinformation). Fabricated landscape images like "Turtle Mountain" fall outside these categories, which means they are largely unchecked by platform-level intervention and depend on fact-checking organisations, individual users' media literacy, and algorithmic systems for their limitation. None of these mechanisms completely suppresses a viral false image, which is why the same image can resurface in 2025 after having been debunked in 2019 and 2022.

**Implications for visual media literacy.** The "Turtle Mountain" image is a useful illustration for visual media literacy education because it is visually convincing, factually falsifiable with available public evidence, and representative of a broad category of fabricated natural-wonder images. The verification methodology it exemplifies — reverse image search to identify source images, comparison of landscape features against documented source photographs, identification of geographic inconsistencies — is a transferable skill applicable to a wide range of fabricated image claims.

It also illustrates the value of first-instance debunking resources: Snopes' 2019 investigation by Dan Evon was the definitive first-pass debunking, and subsequent fact-checks by AAP in 2022 and Hardinges in 2025 built on that foundation rather than starting from scratch. This layered, iterative fact-checking approach — initial investigation followed by periodic republication as the claim resurfaces — is an efficient model for managing the long tail of persistent image misinformation.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes debunkings of the "Turtle Mountain" image — in 2019 and again in 2025 — represent a methodologically clean example of reverse image investigation applied to fabricated landscape photography.

**The reverse image search methodology.** The core investigative tool for this kind of claim is reverse image search: using a known image as the query to identify other instances of the same or similar image online and, from those, tracing back to source images. This tool converts the general question "is this real?" into the specific question "where did this image come from?" — a question that has a definitive answer when the source photographs can be identified.

In the "Turtle Mountain" case, the investigation identified both the background landscape (Frank's glaciernps Flickr image) and the turtle-head element (the Pixabay image of Pilot Mountain). The match is demonstrated through the visual comparison collage Snopes published in 2019, which allows direct inspection of the correspondence between the composite and its sources. This is evidence presented in a directly verifiable form — readers can assess the match for themselves rather than taking the fact-checker's word for it.

**The "Fake" verdict mechanics.** The "Fake" rating is Snopes' clearest and most definitive negative verdict — used for claims that are not merely false but actively fabricated. Its use here is appropriate: the question is not whether the original claim is exaggerated or partially true, but whether the image is authentic or fabricated. The answer is unambiguous. A "False" verdict might suggest that the claim is incorrect but the image is real; "Fake" correctly captures that the image itself is not what it purports to be.

**What the fact-check establishes beyond the verdict.** The 2025 fact-check, following from the 2019 original, establishes the timeline of the image's circulation back to 2018, identifies the specific source photographs, and provides the visual comparison evidence. This completeness serves multiple purposes: it gives readers the evidence to understand the debunking, it provides future fact-checkers with a citable resource, and it documents the circulation history in a way that is itself valuable for understanding the dynamics of this kind of image misinformation.

The "Turtle Mountain" story is ultimately about the perennial appeal of apparent natural wonders, the ease with which compelling composites can be produced from real photographs, and the way that pareidolia — our deeply wired tendency to see meaningful shapes in the world — makes us receptive to images that confirm the world's capacity for surprising resemblances. There is no "Turtle Mountain." But there is a real mountain in North Carolina with a remarkable rounded quartzite knob, a real lake in Montana with a tiny island in its centre, and a human perceptual system that, given a well-constructed composite, will read them together as a single natural marvel without a moment's hesitation.
