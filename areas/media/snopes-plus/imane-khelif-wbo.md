# Imane Khelif Was Banned from Boxing or Failed a Gender Test at the Olympics


## 1. The Claim

The claims about Imane Khelif, the Algerian boxer who won gold in the women's welterweight division at the Paris 2024 Olympics, proliferated in multiple variants during and after the Games. The most widely circulated version held simply that a woman competing in women's boxing at the Olympics had "failed a gender test" or had been "proven not to be a woman" and been "banned from boxing" — yet was permitted to compete by the IOC anyway. More specific variants claimed she had failed testing administered by the World Boxing Organization (WBO); others attributed the ban to the International Boxing Association (IBA); yet others claimed she had been "banned from all boxing" globally.

The claims spread with unusual velocity in July and August 2024, triggered partly by first-round coverage of her opening bout against Italian boxer Angela Carini, who withdrew from the match after 46 seconds following punch contact that left her visibly distressed. Video of Carini's withdrawal was widely shared, and commentary framing the bout as evidence of fundamental biological unfairness spread rapidly. High-profile amplifiers including public figures, journalists, and politicians across multiple countries used the episode to advance arguments about transgender athletes and sex categories in sport — despite the fact that Khelif is not transgender. She was assigned female at birth and has lived and competed as a woman throughout her life.

The simplest version of the claim — that she was "banned from the Olympics" or "banned from boxing" — is false as stated. What exists, however, is a genuine and unresolved institutional dispute involving competing sporting bodies, contested testing methodologies, undisclosed test results, and ongoing legal proceedings that make this one of the more epistemologically complex cases in the Truth Vault.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The IBA disqualification.** Imane Khelif was disqualified from the 2023 IBA Women's World Boxing Championships in New Delhi by the International Boxing Association. The IBA, under president Umar Kremlev, stated that Khelif had failed "gender eligibility tests." The IBA's stated basis was that blood tests showed chromosomes inconsistent with the XX chromosomal pattern it had adopted as its definition of female eligibility, following a rule change in May 2023 that explicitly defined "Women/Female/Girl" as individuals with XX chromosomes. The IBA claimed two rounds of testing produced "identical findings" — one in May 2022 (after the 2022 World Championships had already concluded) and one in March 2023 before the 2023 championships.

This is verifiably true. The IBA disqualification happened. Khelif's removal from the 2023 World Championships was real.

**What the tests actually showed is not publicly known.** The IBA's full testing methodology, the specific lab conducting the analysis, the complete results, and the precise biological markers assessed have never been publicly released. The IBA's president referred to "XY chromosomes" in public statements. The IBA also initially mentioned elevated testosterone levels, then denied that testosterone was the basis for the disqualification. No independent verification of the test results has been conducted. Khelif herself has not publicly confirmed or denied her chromosomal status, characterising the IBA decision as a "big conspiracy."

**The IOC's position and governance context.** The International Olympic Committee did not accept the IBA's findings. The IOC had, prior to the 2024 Games, stripped the IBA of its status as the Olympic boxing governing body — a decision driven primarily by concerns about the IBA's governance, financial transparency, and the integrity of referee decisions at the Tokyo 2020 Olympics rather than specifically about eligibility rulings. The IOC operated Paris 2024 boxing through the Paris Boxing Unit, its own oversight body. The Paris Boxing Unit cleared Khelif to compete, citing existing IOC eligibility criteria under which athletes' eligibility in women's events is determined by their legal documentation — passport gender — unless specific physiological evidence meeting IOC standards establishes a relevant advantage.

The IOC has stated that Khelif had been eligible to compete in women's boxing at previous Olympics, including Tokyo 2020, without incident. The IOC characterised the IBA's last-minute disqualification from the 2023 World Championships as "sudden and arbitrary" and "lacking any due process."

**The WBO claim is specifically false.** Snopes investigated the specific claim that Khelif had been banned by the World Boxing Organization (WBO). The WBO confirmed to Snopes that it does not sanction amateur boxing events and has no regulatory authority over Olympic boxing. The WBO had issued no ban against Khelif. The WBO claim migrated into the information environment because the institutional abbreviations — WBO, IBA, IOC, World Boxing — were sufficiently similar that non-specialist audiences frequently conflated them.

**Khelif is not transgender.** This point was stated with unusual clarity by multiple authoritative sources during the controversy. Khelif was assigned female at birth, raised in Algeria as a girl, and has competed as a woman throughout her amateur boxing career. She does not identify as transgender and has never claimed trans identity. The popular conflation of her case with the separate debate over transgender women in sports rested on a categorical error. The biological question at issue in her case — if any such issue exists — concerns intersex traits, differences of sexual development (DSD), or chromosomal variation, not gender transition. These are related but distinct categories in both medical and sporting governance terms.

**The Carini bout context.** Angela Carini's withdrawal after 46 seconds was genuine and distressing. However, both Carini herself and subsequent reporting provided important context that the initial social media response lacked. Carini later stated she had been instructed by her coach to stop the fight due to a nose injury; she retracted initial statements suggesting unfairness and apologised to Khelif. Carini's injury history and the technical circumstances of the stoppage were relevant to understanding the bout's outcome, and boxing analyses noted that Khelif had not been unusually dominant in terms of punch landed before the stoppage. The visual drama of the withdrawal was real; the inference that it demonstrated unfair biological advantage was not established by the available evidence.

**Khelif's testosterone disclosure.** In February 2026, Khelif disclosed in an interview that she has elevated testosterone levels that she is reducing under medical supervision, and that she was willing to undergo IOC-mandated sex verification tests. This disclosure, coming nearly two years after the Olympics, added texture to the factual record. Elevated testosterone in female athletes is a documented phenomenon with significant debate about its competitive implications. Research by Handelsman, Hirschberg, and Bermon (2018, *British Journal of Sports Medicine*) on testosterone, athletic performance, and sport eligibility concluded that testosterone is associated with performance differences and that the relationship varies substantially by sport and event, with effects measured as meaningful in some domains and less clearly significant in others. Khelif's voluntary disclosure and willingness to be tested shifted her public posture, though it also raised retrospective questions about the basis of the IBA's actions and what the still-undisclosed test results showed.

**World Boxing's 2025 ruling.** World Boxing — a different organisation from the IBA — announced in May 2025 that Khelif would not be permitted to compete in its events until undergoing a mandatory genetic sex-screening test. World Boxing's president subsequently apologised for naming Khelif specifically in the announcement, characterising the personalised naming as a violation of her privacy. Khelif challenged the mandate at the Court of Arbitration for Sport (CAS); her request for a suspension of the testing requirement pending her appeal was denied in September 2025, and proceedings were ongoing as of 2026.


## 3. Why People Believe This — and the Epistemic Complexity

The Khelif case is structurally different from most Truth Vault entries. Most entries address claims that are simply false. This one addresses claims in a spectrum ranging from clearly false (banned from the Olympics; banned by the WBO) to genuinely contested and unresolved (what her biological markers show; whether they confer competitive advantage; which governing body had legitimate authority to rule).

**Institutional opacity and the vacuum it creates.** The IBA never publicly released its test results, methodology, or the chain of custody for its analysis. This opacity was not neutral: it simultaneously made the disqualification impossible to independently verify and impossible to definitively refute. Research on institutional trust in scientific and regulatory contexts — notably work by Jasanoff (2009, *Harvard University Press*) on co-production of knowledge and legitimacy — suggests that institutional opacity in contested decisions predictably generates competing explanatory narratives that fill the information vacuum. In the absence of the actual test data, audiences with different priors read the available fragments to confirm opposed conclusions. Those who believed the IBA saw the disqualification as confirming their prior expectations. Those who trusted the IOC saw the reversal as confirming IBA governance failures. Neither interpretation could be definitively tested against evidence because the evidence was never shared.

**Categorical conflation.** The Khelif controversy merged multiple distinct conceptual categories in popular discourse: transgender athletes, intersex athletes, athletes with DSD conditions, chromosomal variation, and testosterone-related eligibility rules. These categories have distinct biological, medical, legal, and sporting implications, but they were flattened into a single undifferentiated claim in most popular discussion. The sliding between categories was not entirely accidental: political actors invested in a general argument about biological sex in sports had incentives to treat all cases as instances of the same phenomenon, regardless of categorical differences. Research by Clayton, Davis, Drury, Haslam, Stott, and Reicher (2021, *British Journal of Social Psychology*) on intergroup stereotype generalisation demonstrates that politically motivated cognition substantially increases the rate of categorical extension from specific instances to general group claims.

**Epistemic novelty — governance credibility as fact-making.** The most epistemically interesting dimension of this case is the question of what it means for a claim to be "true" or "false" when competing authoritative bodies have reached opposite conclusions using undisclosed evidence and applying incompatible methodologies. When the IBA says Khelif failed its test, and the IOC says she meets its eligibility criteria, and both are formally recognised international sporting organisations, the factual question — is Khelif eligible — is partly a question about which institution's epistemic authority and methodological standards should govern. This is not a standard fact-checking problem. It is an instance of what Sheila Jasanoff describes as "regulatory science" — science produced in the context of governance decisions, where the boundary between fact and policy is blurred by institutional interest.

The popular claim that Khelif "wasn't a woman" or "failed a gender test" treats this contested institutional dispute as if it had a settled factual resolution. It didn't — and still doesn't. The IBA tests show something; we don't know what. The IOC cleared her; by standards that are articually more permissive than the IBA's amended standards. The Court of Arbitration for Sport has not ruled on the merits. Khelif has disclosed elevated testosterone and is receiving treatment. What all of this means in terms of competitive eligibility depends on normative and empirical questions about the biological basis of the male/female performance gap, the relationship between testosterone and performance by event, and the appropriate trade-off between inclusion, fairness, and the definitional stability of sex categories in sport. These are genuinely hard questions. The flat "she failed a gender test" claim papers over all of them.

**The non-trans misattribution.** The mass misidentification of Khelif as transgender — despite extensive reporting clarifying she is not — illustrates a phenomenon documented in research on motivated reasoning: Kunda (1990, *Psychological Review*) showed that individuals construct reasoning chains that reach their preferred conclusions while maintaining subjective experience of objectivity. For audiences engaged in the trans-athletes debate, Khelif's case was functionally equivalent to a transgender woman in sport because it served the same narrative purpose, regardless of the actual categorical difference. The correction — "she's not transgender" — failed to stick in many contexts because the audience's interpretive goal was not accurate categorical assignment but symbolic deployment of a case that confirmed an existing argument.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE in most circulated forms; CONTESTED in underlying factual dispute.**

The specific claim that Khelif was "banned from the Olympics" is false: she competed at Paris 2024 and won a gold medal. The claim that she was banned by the WBO is false: the WBO confirmed it has no jurisdiction over Olympic amateur boxing and issued no ban. The claim that she "failed a gender test at the Olympics" is false: no such test was administered at Paris 2024 by the Paris Boxing Unit, and the IOC cleared her to compete under its eligibility framework.

**What is true:** Khelif was disqualified from the 2023 IBA Women's World Championships under the IBA's revised eligibility standards based on undisclosed test results the IBA has characterised as showing chromosomal patterns inconsistent with its female eligibility definition. The IBA's test methodology and results have not been made public. The IOC rejected the IBA's action as procedurally deficient and cleared Khelif to compete in Paris. World Boxing (distinct from the IBA and the WBO) subsequently imposed a testing mandate that Khelif is challenging through CAS.

**She is not transgender.** This distinction is factually clear and matters for accurate description of the dispute, even where questions about her biological markers and competitive eligibility remain genuinely unresolved.

**Epistemic note.** This case cannot be fully resolved through available public evidence. The IBA's test results remain undisclosed. The legitimate authority question between the IBA and IOC involves governance considerations that go beyond biology. Khelif's February 2026 disclosure about elevated testosterone adds new information that complicates prior framings without resolving them. Honest fact-checking requires acknowledging that this is a genuinely complex case with false claims at its surface and genuinely contested questions underneath.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**Competing international sporting bodies.** The governance structure of international boxing is unusually fragmented. The IBA (formerly AIBA) was historically the dominant governing body but lost IOC recognition following persistent governance failures, financial opacity, and controversies at Tokyo 2020. World Boxing was established as a competing body seeking to replace the IBA and restore boxing's Olympic standing. The WBO is primarily a professional boxing sanctioning body with no jurisdiction over amateur sport. This three-body landscape — with incompatible rules, incompatible test methodologies, and competing claims to legitimacy — created the precise conditions under which the Khelif controversy could be exploited and misrepresented. Individuals wishing to claim authority for the claim that "boxing banned her" could select whichever institution's ruling served their claim while knowing that most audiences would not distinguish between them.

**DSD eligibility rules across sports.** The eligibility of athletes with differences of sexual development is an active and contested area of sports governance. World Athletics introduced Eligibility Regulations for the Female Classification for DSD athletes in 2019, requiring athletes with naturally elevated testosterone above a threshold to reduce their levels to compete in certain events. The regulations were challenged by Caster Semenya, a South African middle-distance runner who had been subject to similar eligibility disputes since 2009 and whose challenges to World Athletics' rules at the CAS and European Court of Human Rights generated landmark rulings. The European Court of Human Rights ruled in 2023 that Switzerland had violated Semenya's rights under the European Convention on Human Rights in its handling of her CAS case, in a judgment that added legal complexity to the regulatory terrain. Research by Harper, Martinez-Patino, Pigozzi, and Pitsiladis (2018, *International Journal of Sports Physiology and Performance*) reviewed DSD eligibility policies across sports governing bodies and identified persistent inconsistencies in both the empirical bases for the rules and their procedural implementation. The Khelif case exists within this broader context of evolving and contested sports governance frameworks.

**Misinformation about women athletes.** The Khelif episode is part of a pattern of heightened scrutiny and misinformation targeting women athletes who do not conform to particular aesthetic or performance stereotypes. Research by Cooky, Messner, and Musto (2015, *Gender & Society*) documented long-standing structural patterns in sports media coverage that treat women's athleticism with more ambivalence than men's, particularly when women athletes exhibit physical characteristics or performance levels that deviate from normative femininity. The specific deployment of gender eligibility claims against Khelif — escalated by social media velocity that earlier athletes like Caster Semenya did not face in 2009 — illustrates how existing media asymmetries operate in amplified form in the social media environment. Carini's later retraction and apology to Khelif received substantially less social media attention than the original bout imagery, a disparity consistent with well-documented negativity bias in news sharing behaviour (Trussler & Soroka, 2014, *Political Communication*).

**Algeria and international sports politics.** The geopolitical dimension of the Khelif case — involving an Algerian athlete, a Russian-led international body (the IBA under Kremlev), and a Western-led Olympic framework (the IOC) — was noted by multiple international observers, some of whom suggested the IBA's eligibility action was politically motivated. Algeria publicly defended Khelif throughout the controversy. These geopolitical dimensions do not resolve the underlying factual questions but contextualise the institutional dispute in ways that complicate any account premised purely on neutral technical assessment.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes' verdict on the specific WBO claim is **"False"**: the WBO confirmed it had no authority over Olympic amateur boxing and had issued no ban against Khelif. Snopes' broader coverage of the Khelif controversy, including a detailed news article published on August 2, 2024, assessed multiple specific claims — that she is transgender, that she had XY chromosomes, that she was banned from the Olympics — and consistently found them either false or unverified, while acknowledging the genuine and unresolved underlying dispute about the IBA's testing.

Snopes' approach to this case illustrated an important methodological evolution in fact-checking: rather than issuing a single verdict on a complex institutional dispute, the organisation fact-checked specific individual claims and gave each its appropriate assessment, while contextualising all of them within the broader governance dispute. This claim-by-claim approach is more accurate than a binary verdict would allow and is particularly appropriate for cases where some sub-claims are clearly false, others are genuinely contested, and the underlying questions remain unresolved in governing bodies and courts.

PolitiFact, Reuters Fact Check, and BBC Reality Check all addressed aspects of the Khelif controversy. The convergent methodology focused on: (1) establishing what the IBA actually said and what authority it had; (2) distinguishing the IBA disqualification from Olympic eligibility; (3) affirming Khelif's non-transgender status; and (4) acknowledging the contested underlying question about biological markers while refusing to treat the IBA's undisclosed, disputed test results as established fact. This layered approach — distinguishing the false claim from the contested underlying reality — represents a model for fact-checking cases in which misinformation is layered over genuinely unresolved empirical disputes.
