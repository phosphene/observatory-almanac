# "Immigration Increases Crime Rates"


## 1. The Claim

"Immigration increases crime." The claim appears in multiple forms: that immigrants commit more crimes than native-born citizens, that undocumented immigrants in particular are disproportionately criminal, that high-immigration areas have higher crime rates, or — in its most loaded register — that the cultural and demographic changes brought by immigration erode the social norms that keep crime in check.

The assertion has been a recurring feature of political discourse in the United States, Europe, and Australia. In the United States, it became a central organizing principle of immigration restriction advocacy in the 2010s, amplified by high-profile cases in which undocumented immigrants committed serious crimes, and by political rhetoric that treated individual cases as statistically representative. "Illegal aliens" and "criminal aliens" became near-synonyms in certain media registers. The Trump campaign of 2016 opened with a speech characterizing Mexican immigrants as bringing drugs, crime, and rapists — a formulation that, however extreme, was not invented in a vacuum but rather represented the distilled form of a widely held folk belief.

In Europe, the claim gained similar traction in the context of refugee flows from Syria, Afghanistan, and sub-Saharan Africa. In Sweden, Germany, and the United Kingdom, politicians and media outlets pointed to crime statistics involving foreign-born individuals as evidence that immigration policy had failed. In Australia, media coverage of crimes committed by Sudanese-born Australians generated a political crisis in 2018 that was disproportionate to the underlying statistics.

The claim appeals to a particular structure of intuition: if you are unfamiliar with a group, if that group is associated in the media with criminality, and if crime itself is a salient concern, the association feels like evidence. Its persistence reflects not stupidity but rather a predictable failure mode in how human cognition handles base rates, media selection effects, and politically charged information.

What makes this entry in the Truth Vault unusual is not that the claim is simply false — it is, by the research consensus, largely false. What makes it complex is that the political context in which the claim lives has distorted both sides of the debate. Advocates of immigration restriction motivated-reason toward exaggeration; advocates of immigration liberalization motivated-reason toward minimization. The task here is to report what the research actually shows.


## 2. What's Actually True

The research literature on immigration and crime is large, multi-method, and remarkably consistent in its direction, even if not in its precise magnitudes.

**The Cato Institute findings:** The Cato Institute — a libertarian think tank with strong pro-immigration views on policy grounds, but also a commitment to rigorous empirical analysis — has produced among the most comprehensive analyses of immigration and crime in the United States. Their studies, drawing on U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey data, Texas Department of Public Safety conviction data, and other sources, have consistently found that both legal immigrants and undocumented immigrants have substantially lower rates of criminal conviction and incarceration than native-born citizens.

Their 2020 analysis found that undocumented immigrants were 45 percent less likely to be incarcerated than native-born citizens; legal immigrants were 78 percent less likely. These figures held across property crimes, violent crimes, and drug offenses. The differences were not marginal. They were large, consistent, and replicable.

**The academic literature:** Cato's findings are broadly consistent with the academic literature. A 2020 meta-analysis by Ousey and Kubrin, reviewing 51 studies published between 1994 and 2014, found that the majority of studies showed no significant relationship or a negative relationship between immigration and crime — meaning more immigration was associated with the same or lower crime rates. Only a minority of studies found positive associations, and those tended to involve specific crime types, contexts, or methodological choices.

Robert Sampson's landmark work on Chicago neighborhoods found that the most heavily immigrant neighborhoods had substantially lower rates of violent crime than similarly impoverished native-born neighborhoods — a finding he attributed to higher social cohesion and informal social control in immigrant communities. Tom Wong's 2017 analysis of sanctuary city policies found that counties with sanctuary policies (which limit local law enforcement cooperation with federal immigration authorities) had lower crime rates than comparable non-sanctuary counties.

The international evidence is similar. Studies in Europe find mixed results — more variation than in the U.S. literature — but the consistent pattern is that controlling for socioeconomic status, country of origin, and legal status substantially reduces or eliminates any positive association between immigration and crime. The raw statistics that generate political alarm typically reflect the socioeconomic disadvantage that accompanies recent immigration more than any characteristics of immigrants themselves.

**The aggregate analysis:** Studies examining whether crime rates rise or fall with immigration at the metropolitan area or state level have generally found neutral or negative associations. Cities and states that received the largest immigration inflows in the 1990s and 2000s typically saw crime fall, along with the rest of the country — and often faster. This does not prove a causal relationship in the other direction (immigration reducing crime), but it decisively falsifies the simple claim that more immigration produces more crime.

**The mechanics of lower crime rates:** Researchers have proposed several explanations for why immigrants commit crimes at lower rates. Self-selection is one: people who undertake the difficult, risky, often expensive process of emigrating are selecting for ambition, future-orientation, and risk tolerance — traits that are negatively associated with crime. Legal status creates deterrence: for undocumented immigrants, any interaction with law enforcement carries the risk of deportation, making crime uniquely costly. Family and community ties: recent immigrants typically arrive with strong social networks and obligations. And there is a generational pattern — first-generation immigrants have lower crime rates than second-generation, who have lower rates than third-generation. Assimilation into American culture appears to raise crime rates, not lower them.

**Where it gets complicated:** The research is clearest about aggregate rates and least certain about specific subpopulations, crime types, and local contexts. Some studies find elevated rates of certain crimes (particularly drug trafficking) in areas near international borders. Some European studies find elevated rates of specific violent crimes among certain refugee populations, though these findings are sensitive to how controls are specified. Gang activity associated with specific immigration waves (MS-13 emerging from Central American immigration, for example) is real — though whether the baseline for comparison is other immigrant populations or native-born populations matters enormously for the interpretation.

The honest summary: by virtually every rigorous measure, immigrants commit crimes at lower rates than native-born citizens. The claim that immigration increases crime is not supported by the weight of the evidence. But the relationship is not zero, is not identical across all crime types and populations, and is entangled with enough methodological complexity that it can be selectively quoted to support almost any conclusion.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Availability heuristic and media selection:** The most powerful driver of the belief is not crime statistics but crime coverage. When an undocumented immigrant commits a serious crime — a murder, a rape, a fatal drunk driving accident — it becomes national news. The same crime committed by a native-born citizen typically does not. This selection effect creates a vivid, available mental model of the immigrant criminal that is systematically unrepresentative of the underlying population. The individual cases are real. The inference from them to population-level rates is a statistical error that the media structure makes almost inevitable.

**The "stranger danger" heuristic:** Human psychology is predisposed toward threat detection involving unfamiliar groups. Immigrants are, by definition, from somewhere else — they look different, speak differently, have different customs. The association between unfamiliarity and threat is not learned but evolved. Societies have always constructed the stranger as dangerous; immigration just gives this tendency a contemporary target.

**Political amplification:** From Pat Buchanan in the 1990s to Donald Trump in the 2010s to Viktor Orbán in Europe, immigration restriction as a political project has required a threat narrative. Crime is one of the most emotionally resonant threats available. The political infrastructure for amplifying individual immigrant crimes — congressional hearings featuring crime victims' families, "Angel Families" given platforms at rallies — is substantial and deliberate. Political communication does not create the belief but does sustain, amplify, and normalize it.

**The Mariel boatlift mythology:** The 1980 Mariel boatlift, in which Cuba released approximately 125,000 people to emigrate to the United States, is a reference point in both the academic literature and popular discourse. The popular account holds that Castro deliberately emptied Cuban prisons into the boats. Research on whether the boatlift actually raised Miami crime rates is disputed — a paper by Harvard economist George Borjas argued it did; other researchers, including David Card, found no effect. The public memory of Mariel as a crime wave has persisted regardless of the research outcome.

**Conflation of legal status and criminal propensity:** A significant portion of the belief derives from treating unauthorized immigration itself as evidence of criminal character. "They broke the law to get here" is a common formulation. This conflates a civil violation (unauthorized entry) with violent or property crime — a category error that is rhetorically powerful precisely because it requires no additional evidence. Under this logic, any undocumented immigrant is a criminal by definition.

**Real cases with real victims:** High-profile cases like the murder of Kate Steinle in San Francisco in 2015 (by an undocumented immigrant with a prior deportation record) generate genuine grief and genuine rage. The victims are real. The political mobilization around such cases exploits that grief — but the existence of exploitation does not mean the underlying concern is illegitimate. People who believe immigration increases crime are not simply dupes of political propaganda; many encountered real events that were narrated to them in particular ways.

**Motivated reasoning on the restriction side:** Advocates of immigration restriction are subject to motivated reasoning that biases them toward data points supporting their position. But it is worth noting that motivated reasoning operates on both sides. Advocates of immigration liberalization have sometimes minimized, dismissed, or failed to engage with evidence of elevated crime rates among specific subpopulations or in specific contexts. The political salience of the topic makes dispassionate analysis genuinely difficult for everyone involved.


## 4. Verdict

**Contested — research clearly points in one direction, but politically contested enough to generate motivated reasoning on all sides.**

The research consensus is substantially against the claim. Immigrants, including undocumented immigrants, commit crimes at lower rates than native-born citizens by virtually every measure that has been carefully examined. The claim that immigration increases crime rates is not supported by the weight of the evidence.

However, this verdict requires several qualifications. The relationship between immigration and crime is not zero; context, crime type, and population subgroup matter. The evidence is clearest in the aggregate and murkier at the margins. The methodological choices in any given study can affect the conclusion, and the literature is not perfectly consistent. Claims of near-zero crime among immigrants can be as misleading as claims of dramatically elevated crime.

The EARC rating of C — Contested — reflects not a failure of the evidence but the political context in which the claim lives. In a world without motivated reasoning, this would be a straightforward R (Refuted). In the actual world, the dispute is not primarily empirical but ideological, and the evidence is selectively deployed by both sides in ways that make simple verdicts feel dishonest.

What is not contested: the claim in its strong form — that immigration significantly raises overall crime rates — is false. The claim in its folk register — that immigrants are more likely to be criminals than native-born citizens — is false. The claim that undocumented immigrants are disproportionately criminal, relative to the native-born population, is false.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The immigration-crime relationship sits at the intersection of several broader questions about how empirical research survives contact with political incentives.

**The sociology of immigrant communities:** The research on lower immigrant crime rates connects to a larger body of work on what makes communities safe. Robert Sampson's work on collective efficacy suggests that social cohesion — people looking out for each other, informal social control, investment in shared norms — is a powerful predictor of low crime rates, independent of poverty. Immigrant communities often exhibit high collective efficacy precisely because they are communities: networks of people who moved together, maintain ties to a place of origin, and have strong incentives to protect their community's reputation. The "immigration increases crime" narrative gets the causality exactly wrong in these communities.

**Second-generation divergence:** The finding that second-generation immigrants have higher crime rates than first-generation, and third-generation higher still, has implications that cut against simple assimilation narratives. Integration into American society — access to mainstream cultural norms, distance from immigrant community networks, exposure to the structural disadvantages faced by minorities — appears to increase criminal involvement. This is a finding that neither immigration restrictionists nor liberals find comfortable, because it implicates American culture and structural inequality rather than immigrant characteristics.

**The sanctuary city evidence:** The research on sanctuary cities has been particularly robust and politically consequential. Sanctuary policies — which limit local law enforcement cooperation with ICE — were attacked on the grounds that they make communities less safe by allowing criminal immigrants to remain. The evidence consistently shows the opposite: sanctuary counties have lower crime rates than comparable non-sanctuary counties. The mechanism appears to be that when immigrant communities trust local police (because they won't be referred to ICE for routine encounters), they are more willing to report crimes — making communities safer for everyone.

**The European context:** European data on immigration and crime is more mixed than American data, reflecting both different patterns of immigration (more recent, more from conflict zones, with higher rates of trauma and displacement) and different welfare state structures (more likely to create dependency traps that reduce labor market integration). Studies in Sweden, Germany, and Denmark have found elevated crime rates among certain refugee populations in certain crime categories. These findings are real and should be engaged honestly. But they are typically interpreted without adequate controls for age distribution (refugee populations skew young and male), socioeconomic integration, and time — recent arrivals have elevated crime rates that decrease with time in country.

**The measurement problem:** Crime statistics are not objective counts of crime but counts of crime that comes to official attention. Populations that are more policed — and immigrant communities, particularly communities of color, are heavily policed in many U.S. cities — generate more recorded crime regardless of underlying behavior. Incarceration rates are similarly shaped by policing patterns, prosecutorial discretion, and plea bargaining dynamics in ways that make them imperfect proxies for actual criminal behavior. The finding that immigrants have lower incarceration rates is less clean than it appears, because incarceration rates are not pure measures of crime commission.

**What "immigration increases crime" does politically:** The claim functions politically not merely as an empirical assertion but as a bundling device — linking immigration to danger, illegality, and threat in ways that make restriction feel like simple prudence. Understanding the claim requires understanding what work it does in political discourse, which is partly independent of its truth value. Even if definitively refuted by the research, the claim would survive as long as the political incentives for it remain.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Mainstream fact-checkers including PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, and The Washington Post Fact Checker have repeatedly addressed specific formulations of the immigration-crime claim, typically rating strong versions as False or Mostly False. PolitiFact rated Trump's 2015 claim that undocumented immigrants are "bringing crime" as Mostly False. The Washington Post's fact-checker gave several related Trump claims Three or Four Pinocchios.

Snopes has addressed specific viral claims about immigrant crime (particular statistics, particular incidents) but has not published a comprehensive treatment of the general claim.

The fact-checking literature on this topic illustrates a recurring challenge: political claims about immigration and crime often combine a false general claim with a true specific claim. An undocumented immigrant did commit a specific murder. The invocation of that case to support a general claim about immigrant criminality is a statistical error. Fact-checkers are better at debunking the specific false statistics than at addressing the underlying inferential error — the move from vivid case to population-level generalization.

The claim also poses challenges for fact-checkers because it is genuinely complicated: the research is clear in aggregate but messier in specifics, the political context introduces motivated reasoning from all sides, and the claim can be restated in narrower forms that are harder to definitively refute. "Undocumented immigrants commit crimes at lower rates than native-born citizens" is a clean factual claim that can be assessed against research. "Immigration from conflict zones increases rates of sexual assault" is a claim with more empirical complexity that resists simple verdicts.

The most honest rendering of the research: the claim in its common form is false. Immigrants are not more criminal than native-born Americans. But honest engagement with the evidence requires acknowledging the complexity of European data, the measurement problems in American data, and the ways in which motivated reasoning has distorted the discussion on all sides.
