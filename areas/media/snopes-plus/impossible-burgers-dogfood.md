# Are Ingredients in 'Impossible' Burgers Indistinguishable from Dog Food?

**Verdict: Mostly False — some ingredient overlap exists, overstated as "indistinguishable," and the comparison reflects rhetorical strategy rather than scientific analysis.**

In the summer of 2019, plant-based meat products were at peak hype. Beyond Meat's IPO in May had been the most successful debut for a US company since 2008. Impossible Foods was in the midst of a high-profile Burger King rollout. The cultural politics of meat consumption, sustainability, and health were generating intense media attention. Into this environment, a tweet from a UC-Davis professor placed three ingredient lists side by side and invited readers to guess which was dog food. The immediate response was viral spread and significant concern among consumers. The underlying argument was considerably weaker than its rhetorical impact suggested.

---

## 1. The Claim

On approximately June 24, 2019, Frank Mitloehner — a professor and air quality specialist at the University of California, Davis, who studies chemical emissions in animal agriculture — tweeted three cropped images of ingredient labels. The tweet showed, side by side, the ingredient list for a Beyond Meat Burger, an Impossible Burger, and "premium dog food" (subsequently identified as "Walk About Dog Food" grain-free vegan).

Mitloehner subsequently tweeted that the products were "indistinguishable from dog food," later arguing in a follow-up tweet that the plant-based burgers "fully qualify for the NOVA definition of 'ultra-processed foods.'"

The claim as received by consumers was approximately this: Impossible and Beyond Meat burgers contain essentially the same ingredients as dog food and are therefore, on some axis, equivalent to or no better than pet food — with the implication that consumers who buy them for health or environmental reasons have been deceived.

Snopes rated this claim **Mostly False**.

---

## 2. What the Ingredient Comparison Actually Shows

### The Impossible Burger vs. vegan dog food

Snopes' investigation worked through the ingredient lists systematically. Taking the Impossible Burger's 20 ingredients and the vegan grain-free dog food's 28 ingredients:

**Shared ingredients (approximately 10):**
- Modified food starch / Pea starch
- Mixed Tocopherols (Vitamin E) / Vitamin E Supplement
- Niacin / Niacin
- Pyridoxine Hydrochloride (Vitamin B6) / Pyridoxine Hydrochloride
- Riboflavin (Vitamin B2) / Riboflavin Supplement
- Salt / Salt
- Sunflower Oil / Sunflower Oil
- Thiamine Hydrochloride (Vitamin B1) / Thiamine Mononitrate (Vitamin B1)
- Vitamin B12 / Vitamin B12 Supplement
- Zinc Gluconate / Zinc Proteinate

The first thing to observe about this list is its composition: it is almost entirely vitamins and mineral supplements. Niacin, riboflavin, thiamine, B6, B12, vitamin E, and zinc are nutritional micronutrients that appear in virtually any fortified food product — breakfast cereals, energy bars, nutritional supplements, infant formula, and yes, pet food. Their presence in both a plant-based burger and a nutritionally balanced vegan dog food is not evidence of compositional similarity; it is evidence that both products are fortified with the same micronutrients required for health.

The Impossible Burger's primary and differentiating ingredients — soy protein concentrate, sunflower oil, natural flavors, methylcellulose, yeast extract, and the proprietary soy leghemoglobin (heme) that gives the burger its characteristic flavour and appearance — are not in the dog food.

### The Beyond Meat comparison is weaker

For the Beyond Meat Burger, the overlap with the vegan dog food was just two ingredients from the current ingredient list:
- Pea Protein Isolate / Pea Protein
- Salt / Salt

Both products happen to use pea protein as a protein source and both contain salt. This level of "overlap" would also link Beyond Meat burgers to vegetable soup, hummus, protein shakes, and most other food products on the market. Two shared ingredients from 18 and 28 ingredients respectively represents a minimal connection.

### What "indistinguishable" requires

The claim that the products are "indistinguishable" is falsified by the actual ingredient lists. "Indistinguishable" in this context would require the lists to be nearly identical — same primary ingredients, same relative proportions, same overall nutritional profile. They are not. The vegan dog food includes chicory root inulin, pumpkin, sweet potato, carrots, and spinach as substantive ingredients — a markedly different composition from either plant-based burger. The Impossible Burger's soy leghemoglobin (heme), the technology at the heart of the product's development, is not present in the dog food at all.

Mitloehner himself, when contacted by Snopes, framed his point differently from the initial tweet: his concern was with how the plant-based burgers were marketed as healthier than real meat, when in his view they qualify as "ultra-processed foods" — a NOVA classification he considered relevant to their health claims. This is a substantially more defensible argument than "indistinguishable from dog food," but it is also not what his original tweet appeared to say.

## 3. The Ultra-Processed Food Framework and Its Limitations

### NOVA classification

The NOVA food classification system, developed by Carlos Monteiro and colleagues at the University of São Paulo (Monteiro et al., 2019, *Public Health Nutrition*), divides foods into four groups based on the degree and purpose of processing: unprocessed/minimally processed foods, processed culinary ingredients, processed foods, and ultra-processed foods. Group 4 (ultra-processed) is defined as "formulations of ingredients, mostly of exclusive industrial use, that result from a series of industrial processes," and includes soft drinks, flavoured cereals, reconstituted meat products, and packaged snacks.

Under this classification, plant-based burgers do appear to qualify as ultra-processed. They contain industrial ingredients (methylcellulose, soy protein concentrate, yeast extract) not typically present in home cooking. Monteiro et al. (2019) and related NOVA research (e.g., Srour et al., 2019, *BMJ*) have documented associations between ultra-processed food consumption and adverse health outcomes including increased cancer risk and cardiovascular mortality.

Mitloehner's application of NOVA to plant-based burgers is, as a framing argument, not unreasonable. But several complications arise:

**Comparative ultra-processing.** Real beef burgers consumed in the same fast-food context — with processed buns, condiment packets, and added sodium — are themselves at minimum "processed foods" and arguably approach ultra-processed status in their commercial form. A comparison of a fast-casual Impossible Burger with a raw grass-fed sirloin would be comparing different categories of food; a comparison with a corresponding conventional beef patty at the same restaurant is more appropriate.

**Causal questions.** NOVA-based epidemiological research documents correlations between ultra-processed food consumption and health outcomes but faces inherent challenges in isolating ultra-processing per se from confounders (diets high in ultra-processed foods also tend to be high in calories, sodium, and saturated fat, and low in vegetables and fibre). Polsky and Campbell (2020), reviewing NOVA-based research in *Current Nutrition Reports*, noted that the causal mechanism — whether ultra-processing itself causes harm versus the nutritional profile of typical ultra-processed foods — remains incompletely resolved.

**Environmental claims vs. health claims.** The original conceit of the Impossible Burger was explicitly environmental: producing a product that tastes like meat using processes that could scale sustainably. Mitloehner notes this in his statement to Snopes. Critiquing a product marketed on environmental grounds by applying health-oriented NOVA criteria is not an invalid argument, but it involves conflating distinct claims.

## 4. The Conflict of Interest Question

Snopes' investigation noted Mitloehner's professional position: his research focuses on reducing greenhouse gas emissions from animal agriculture, and he has testified as an expert witness before the U.S. Senate defending the agricultural industry. He has served as a consultant to livestock and dairy industry bodies. His Twitter feed regularly engages with debates over plant-based meat and alternative protein in ways that tend to favour conventional animal agriculture.

This does not make his argument wrong. Ad hominem fallacies are real fallacies. But conflict of interest is epistemologically relevant information: it allows a reader to understand the prior probabilities and rhetorical motivation of a speaker. A researcher primarily funded by and academically aligned with animal agriculture interests who tweets ingredient comparisons positioning plant-based meat as equivalent to dog food is not presenting a disinterested scientific analysis — and the "indistinguishable" framing has characteristics (the challenge to guess which is dog food, the implied indignation, the viral format) more consistent with rhetorical advocacy than with scholarly communication.

Disclosure norms in academic science — as articulated, for example, in the ICMJE (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors) conflict of interest reporting standards — exist precisely because researchers with financial or institutional interests in a topic are more likely, unconsciously or consciously, to frame their findings in ways that advance those interests. A tweet is not a journal article, but the same disclosure norm is intellectually relevant.

### The broader framing war

The "plant-based burger is dog food" claim is one instance of a sustained rhetorical campaign against alternative protein conducted by livestock industry interests. Other instances have included:

- Claims that plant-based burgers contain genetically modified organisms (true, and disclosed as such)
- Claims that plant-based burgers are not actually healthier than real meat (a complex empirical question)
- Lobbying to restrict the use of the word "meat" for plant-based products (legal battles in multiple US states and EU jurisdictions)
- Claims about environmental harms from soy cultivation used in plant-based products (partially true, partially framed to deflect from comparative analysis of livestock soy consumption)

These claims range from accurate to misleading to outright false, but they share an industry-aligned rhetorical purpose. Contextualising any single claim within this broader campaign does not invalidate specific factual points but does reveal the selective use of evidence.

## 5. What the Episode Reveals About Ingredient-List Comparisons

The deeper epistemological question raised by the dog-food tweet is: what can ingredient-list comparisons legitimately demonstrate?

Ingredient lists, presented as a single flat list without proportions, do not adequately represent a food product's nutritional profile. Two products can share an ingredient — salt, say — with one containing 2,000 mg per serving and the other 100 mg. The list-level comparison treats them as equivalent. Medical nutritionist Marion Nestle (2006, in *What to Eat*, North Point Press) has argued repeatedly that raw ingredient lists are among the least informative dietary tools available to consumers, precisely because they present ingredients without quantitative weighting.

The specific rhetorical move in Mitloehner's tweet exemplifies what philosopher of science Erik Angner (2012, in *A Course in Behavioral Economics*, Palgrave Macmillan) calls **framing effects in decision contexts**: presenting the same information in a different frame changes its perceived significance. Three ingredient lists presented side by side with the challenge "which one is dog food?" primes the reader to perceive the shared ingredients as damning. Three ingredient lists presented as "which one is a multivitamin?" would emphasise the same shared inputs (niacin, riboflavin, B12) in a completely different light.

The scientifically valid version of Mitloehner's concern — that heavily processed plant-based burgers may not deliver the health benefits their marketing implies — is a legitimate research question that merits investigation on its own terms. The dog-food framing does not advance that investigation; it shortcuts the analytical work in favour of a visceral rhetorical impact.

---

## 6. Verdict

**Mostly False.** Impossible Burgers and Beyond Meat burgers do share some ingredients with a specific brand of vegan grain-free dog food. Those shared ingredients are primarily vitamins and mineral supplements common to all fortified food products. The products are not "indistinguishable" — they differ in primary protein sources, proprietary ingredients, flavour additives, and overall composition. The comparison was framed rhetorically to imply equivalence that does not exist and was made by a researcher with documented industry ties to conventional animal agriculture.

The legitimate underlying concern — that plant-based burgers are ultra-processed foods whose health claims deserve scrutiny — is real and worth examining. But the dog-food framing is not an appropriate vehicle for that examination: it generates memorable outrage at the cost of analytical accuracy.
