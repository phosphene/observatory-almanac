# Sleep training methods like controlled crying cause long-term psychological harm to infants

## The Claim

Sleep training methods — particularly "controlled crying" (also called graduated extinction or the Ferber method) — cause lasting psychological damage to infants. Proponents of this claim argue that when babies are left to cry themselves to sleep, even for short intervals, they experience profound stress that disrupts attachment bonds, damages the parent-child relationship, and produces long-term anxiety, behavioral problems, or emotional dysregulation. The claim is widely circulated in parenting communities and attachment parenting literature, and has generated sustained controversy in pediatric and developmental psychology circles.

The specific mechanism cited is the elevated cortisol response observed in crying infants — stress hormones, the argument goes, flood the developing brain during sleep training and leave permanent neurological damage. Some advocates go further, suggesting that controlled crying is a form of neglect, that it trains babies to "give up" rather than self-soothe, and that the apparent success of the method merely reflects learned helplessness rather than genuine sleep development.

This is a claim with emotional weight, real scientific texture, and a significant evidence base that has accumulated over the past two decades. It deserves careful parsing.

## What's Actually True

The best available clinical evidence does not support the conclusion that sleep training methods like controlled crying cause long-term psychological harm to infants. The most rigorous long-term follow-up study, conducted by Harriet Hiscock, Melissa Wake, and colleagues at the Murdoch Children's Research Institute in Australia, found no adverse outcomes from behavioral sleep interventions at 5-year follow-up.

The landmark study in question is Price et al. (2012), published in *Pediatrics*. In this randomized controlled trial, 326 infants aged 8 months were assigned to either a behavioral sleep intervention group (which included graduated extinction and camping-out methods) or a control group. Crucially, the researchers followed up with the children at age 5 and assessed outcomes across multiple dimensions: emotional and behavioral functioning, sleep patterns, cortisol stress response, parent-child attachment, and overall child and family well-being. On every measured outcome, there were no statistically significant differences between the sleep training group and the control group. The authors concluded that behavioral sleep techniques "have no adverse effects on child emotional and behavioral development, sleeping, and stress response at 5 years."

This finding has been substantially replicated. A 2016 randomized controlled trial by Gradisar et al., also published in *Pediatrics*, assigned infants to graduated extinction, bedtime fading, or a control condition. After 12 months, no differences emerged in infant stress, emotional and behavioral regulation, parent-child attachment security, or parenting stress. The researchers also tracked cortisol levels in saliva and found no evidence of elevated chronic stress in sleep-trained infants.

The American Academy of Pediatrics, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, and the Australian Psychological Society have all reviewed the evidence and concluded that behavioral sleep interventions, implemented appropriately, are safe and effective. There is no clinical consensus that sleep training harms children.

On the attachment question specifically: attachment security in children is assessed using standardized instruments like the Strange Situation procedure and the Attachment Q-Sort. Studies using these instruments have consistently found no significant differences in attachment security between infants who underwent sleep training and those who did not.

Where does the complexity lie? Primarily in the cortisol findings, which are real but contested in their interpretation. Wendy Middlemiss and colleagues (2012) published a study in *Early Human Development* that tracked cortisol in both mothers and infants during sleep training. They found that while infant cortisol elevated during the initial crying episodes, it dropped to baseline after a few days — but crucially, they observed a "desynchrony" between maternal and infant cortisol responses: by day 3, infants were no longer crying and their observable distress had diminished, but their cortisol levels remained elevated, while mothers' cortisol had fallen. Middlemiss interpreted this as evidence that infants had learned to suppress behavioral distress signals while still experiencing physiological stress — a potential concern about what the absence of crying actually means.

This finding has been contested on methodological grounds. The Middlemiss study was small (25 mother-infant dyads), lacked a control group, and the cortisol measurement timing was not ideal. Critics noted that the study could not establish whether the elevated cortisol represented harm or simply a normal adaptive physiological adjustment. Moreover, transient cortisol elevation is not the same as chronic stress, and short-term elevated cortisol does not predict long-term neurological outcomes.

The honest summary: behavioral sleep training methods, when properly implemented, are not associated with measurable long-term psychological harm in the available clinical literature. The cortisol question raises legitimate scientific questions that remain actively researched. The claim that sleep training *causes* long-term harm is not currently supported by the evidence.

## Why People Believe This

The belief that sleep training harms infants is understandable, emotionally coherent, and draws on real features of developmental science — even when it overstates the evidence.

**The evolutionary argument feels persuasive.** Across most of human evolutionary history, infants did not sleep separately from their caregivers. The assumption that a crying infant separated from its parents is experiencing something that evolution has marked as dangerous has intuitive appeal. For much of human prehistory, an infant left alone at night faced real predatory risk. This framing makes it easy to believe that the crying infant's distress is not merely behavioral but deeply biological.

**Attachment theory has broad cultural penetration, but is often misapplied.** John Bowlby's foundational work on attachment established that the quality of early caregiver responsiveness shapes internal working models of relationships. This is a robust finding with extensive empirical support. But the popular version of attachment theory — particularly as it filtered into "attachment parenting" movements popularized by figures like Dr. William Sears — often extends the science beyond its evidence base, treating any non-immediate response to infant crying as a potential attachment disruption. This is not what the empirical attachment literature supports, but the popular framing is sticky.

**The Middlemiss cortisol study was widely covered.** The finding that infants show cortisol elevation even after they have stopped crying created significant media attention. The headlines were striking: "Babies' stress doesn't go away just because they stop crying." The limitations of the study — its small size, the absence of a control group, the contested interpretation of cortisol as harm — received far less coverage. Dramatic findings travel; methodological caveats do not.

**Parental guilt is a powerful amplifier.** Sleep deprivation is one of the most acute challenges of early parenthood, and the decision to use behavioral sleep training is frequently accompanied by distress and uncertainty. When a parent hears that sleep training might harm their child, the claim lands in soil already prepared by anxiety. Confirmation of worry is easier to accept than reassurance.

**The absence of long-term follow-up was historically exploitable.** For much of the period when sleep training debates were most active, the long-term follow-up studies had not yet been published. Critics could legitimately argue that the absence of evidence of harm was not the same as evidence of absence. Since the publication of Price et al. (2012) and the Gradisar et al. (2016) studies, this epistemic gap has substantially closed — but older arguments circulated through parenting communities continue to be shared as if the evidence gap persists.

**Expert credentialing amplifies fringe positions.** Figures in the attachment parenting space who hold clinical credentials can make claims that, to lay audiences, appear to carry equal weight to the consensus literature. When a developmental psychologist or pediatrician argues that sleep training is harmful, parents reasonably assume this reflects scientific consensus rather than a minority position.

## Verdict

**Contested consensus.** The consensus among pediatric sleep researchers and major clinical bodies (AAP, AASM, Australian Psychological Society) is that behavioral sleep training methods, including graduated extinction, do not cause long-term psychological harm to infants when appropriately implemented. The best-designed randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up find no adverse outcomes on attachment, behavior, stress regulation, or emotional development.

The minority scientific position — supported primarily by the Middlemiss cortisol desynchrony finding — raises legitimate questions about the interpretation of short-term cortisol elevation and what behavioral quieting after sleep training actually means physiologically. These are genuine scientific questions. But the extrapolation from "cortisol elevation during a few nights of training" to "lasting psychological harm" goes substantially beyond what the evidence supports.

The claim as typically stated — that controlled crying *causes* long-term psychological harm — is not supported by the current evidence base. Parents should make decisions informed by their own values, their infant's temperament, and their family context, but they need not fear that evidence-based behavioral sleep interventions carry documented long-term risks.

## The Wider Picture

The sleep training debate is a useful window into how contested parenting science actually functions in public culture — and how the gap between research findings and popular claims widens over time.

Sleep medicine as a clinical specialty has developed substantially over the past two decades. The behavioral sleep interventions studied in the clinical literature are considerably more nuanced than the popular image of "leaving babies to cry alone." Graduated extinction (the Ferber method) involves systematic parental check-ins at progressively longer intervals. Camping-out involves parental presence during the sleep transition. Bedtime fading involves shifting the sleep schedule to match the infant's actual sleep pressure. These are not monolithic approaches, and the evidence base varies across them.

The cultural context of sleep training debates also matters. Sleep expectations for infants and young children vary dramatically across cultures. Co-sleeping remains the norm across much of the world; the expectation that infants should sleep independently in their own space is primarily a feature of certain Northern European and North American contexts. This does not make behavioral sleep interventions harmful, but it does suggest that the intensity of Western debates about sleep training is partly a product of cultural specificity — what counts as "normal" infant sleep behavior varies enormously depending on context.

There is also an important distinction between how sleep training is discussed in clinical literature versus how it circulates in parenting communities. Clinical protocols involve parent education, individualized guidance about timing and age-appropriateness, and follow-up support. The do-it-yourself application of sleep training methods without professional guidance introduces variables that clinical studies control for. A well-supported behavioral sleep intervention at the appropriate developmental stage is not equivalent to simply ignoring an infant's nighttime crying indefinitely.

The question of what crying means in infants — developmentally, physiologically, and in terms of caregiver relationship — remains an active research area. There is genuine scientific uncertainty about the mechanisms of infant self-soothing, the developmental windows during which sleep training is most appropriate, and the moderating factors that might make some infants more or less suited to particular interventions. These are real questions that researchers are working to answer. The problem is when uncertainty about mechanism is converted into claims about harm that the evidence does not support.

It is worth noting that untreated infant sleep problems also have documented costs. Pediatric insomnia and frequent nighttime waking are associated with maternal depression, parental sleep deprivation, marital stress, and in some studies with child behavioral difficulties. A complete evidence-based analysis of infant sleep intervention must weigh the documented costs of non-intervention as well as the theorized harms of intervention.

The Truth Vault recommendation: when evaluating parenting science claims, ask specifically about the quality and design of the studies cited, the size and follow-up duration of the evidence base, whether the claim is endorsed by major clinical bodies, and whether the claim distinguishes between short-term and long-term outcomes. Apply the same evidential standards to claims that confirm parental fears as to those that challenge them.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This specific claim — that sleep training causes long-term harm — has not been directly addressed by Snopes, but the general terrain of infant sleep debates has generated substantial fact-checking adjacent coverage.

The challenge for traditional fact-checkers is that the sleep training harm claim operates in the "contested science" register rather than the "false claim" register. It is not simply false in the way that, say, vaccine-autism claims are false. It draws on real studies, involves genuine scientific debate, and is advocated by credentialed experts. This makes it harder to debunk cleanly and easier for advocates to maintain the claim's apparent credibility.

What distinguishes rigorous science communication from advocacy in this space is attention to study design. The Middlemiss cortisol study, frequently cited to support the harm claim, had significant methodological limitations that are almost never mentioned in popular coverage. Conversely, the Price et al. (2012) and Gradisar et al. (2016) randomized controlled trials — the strongest evidence available — are often absent from advocacy materials that argue against sleep training.

The responsible fact-checking approach to claims like this involves several moves that are worth making explicit:

**Distinguish claim types.** "Sleep training elevates cortisol in some studies" is a factual claim that is true. "Sleep training causes lasting psychological harm" is a causal claim about long-term outcomes that requires a different and more demanding evidence standard.

**Assess the evidence hierarchy.** Anecdotes, case studies, and small observational studies sit lower in the evidence hierarchy than well-designed randomized controlled trials with long-term follow-up. The best evidence on long-term outcomes does not support the harm claim.

**Identify clinical consensus.** When major pediatric and sleep medicine organizations have reviewed the evidence and concluded that behavioral sleep interventions are safe, that consensus position should be weighted against minority dissenting views.

**Note what has changed over time.** The evidentiary landscape for sleep training changed materially with the publication of long-term follow-up studies. Claims about sleep training harm that circulated before 2012 were operating in a genuine evidence gap. Claims that circulate today are operating in the face of contrary evidence.

**Check for value-laden framing.** The sleep training debate is entangled with ideological positions about attachment parenting, maternal labor, and what "good" parenting looks like. Arguments that mix empirical claims with normative positions about what parents should do are often misread as purely scientific. Factual accuracy and value alignment are separate questions.

The bottom line for anyone assessing this claim: the empirical question (does sleep training cause long-term psychological harm?) has a reasonably clear answer in the clinical literature — no, not according to the best available evidence. The normative question (should parents use sleep training?) involves values and individual circumstances that the empirical literature cannot settle. These two questions are frequently conflated, and much of the heat in this debate is generated by that conflation.
