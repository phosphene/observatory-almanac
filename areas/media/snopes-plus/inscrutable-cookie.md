## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

The claim that fortune cookies originated in China is one of the most persistent misattributions in the cultural geography of American food. Its premise is simple and intuitive: fortune cookies appear on the tables of Chinese restaurants across the United States, are handed out following Chinese meals, and are widely associated in popular imagination with Chinese culture, wisdom, and aphorism. Therefore, the logic goes, they come from China.

Snopes investigator Barbara Mikkelson (2008) rated this claim **False**. Fortune cookies did not originate in China. They originated in California, almost certainly in the early decades of the twentieth century, and their precise inventor and origin location remain contested — but what does not remain disputed is that China is not where they came from. Prior to Americanization of the confection in and after World War II, fortune cookies were virtually unknown in China, and they only reached Hong Kong in earnest in the late 1980s when a Hong Kong entrepreneur began importing them and marketing them as luxury novelties explicitly described as "Genuine American Fortune Cookies." A Brooklyn-based American fortune cookie company that attempted to establish a fortune cookie factory in China in 1992 found the confection too alien to succeed there.

This entry traces the genuine, contested, and surprisingly Japanese history of fortune cookies; surveys the documented origin claimants and the 1983 mock trial that attempted to adjudicate them; examines why the Chinese-origin belief is so durable despite being straightforwardly false; and situates the fortune cookie in the broader scholarship on Chinese-American culinary synthesis as a site of identity, performance, and cross-cultural projection.

## §2 What's Actually True: California Origins and the Japanese Connection

Fortune cookies are an American invention, and their closest known ancestor is a Japanese confectionary form, not a Chinese one. Mikkelson (2008) summarized the documentary and oral-history record as identifying multiple origin claimants, all in California, almost all tracing their families' histories to an Asian immigrant introducing the cookie somewhere in California prior to World War I.

The two principal claimants adjudicated in a 1983 mock trial held in a San Francisco federal courthouse were:

**Makoto Hagiwara** and his family, representing the San Francisco claim. Hagiwara hailed from the Yamanashi region of central Honshu, Japan, and his family asserted that today's fortune cookie descended from the Japanese *senbei* — a rice cracker that contains a slip of paper. The Hagiwara family claimed that Makoto first served fortune cookies in the Japanese Tea Garden in San Francisco's Golden Gate Park in the early 1900s.

**David Jung**, founder of the Los Angeles-based Hong Kong Noodle Company, representing the Los Angeles claim. Jung was from Canton, China, and his family asserted that he created the fortune cookie in the 1910s to give to poor people on the streets around his noodle company.

The 1983 mock trial was held before a federal judge and was referred to in subsequent coverage as ruling for the San Francisco claimant — a decision that reportedly prompted the Los Angeles contingent to announce they were seceding from the union. Mikkelson (2008) noted that the decision turned in part on one of the claimants' producing an aged set of round black iron grills said to have been used by the San Francisco family in making the cookies.

The evidentiary quality of this mock trial was, of course, not that of a formal judicial proceeding. But the historical record beyond the trial supports the Japanese genealogy. Jennifer 8. Lee, in her 2008 book *The Fortune Cookie Chronicles* (New York: Twelve, ISBN 0-446-58007-6), conducted extensive research into fortune cookie origins and found the Japanese connection compelling. Lee's account, cited by Mikkelson (2008), documented the relationship between fortune cookies and Japanese *tsujiura senbei* — fortune-containing crackers made in the Kyoto region, sold near temples — which predate California fortune cookies by at least decades and exhibit the same fundamental form: a thin, folded wafer containing a paper slip with a written message. The *tsujiura senbei* tradition dates to at least the nineteenth century and possibly earlier based on records from the Fushimi Inari shrine area south of Kyoto.

The plausibility of the Japanese genealogy is reinforced by the demographics of early twentieth-century California: Japanese immigrants arrived in large numbers in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, bringing food traditions with them, and the bakery and confectionary trades were occupations with documented Japanese-American presence. Chinese immigration was more restricted — the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 had significantly curtailed new Chinese arrivals — and Chinese-American restaurant culture in this period was developing its own synthetic form quite separate from Chinese culinary tradition.

By World War II, fortune cookies were a commonplace offering in Chinese restaurants in San Francisco. Their spread to the rest of the country followed the postwar expansion of Chinese-American restaurants as American diners sought affordable, novel, Americanized "exotic" cuisine. The Japanese-American population's wartime internment disrupted whatever Japanese-American identity the cookies might have retained, and the confection was thoroughly absorbed into Chinese restaurant culture by the 1950s.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Why Cultural Misattribution Sticks, and What the Fortune Cookie Reveals About American Orientalism

The persistence of the Chinese-origin belief in the face of easily discoverable contrary evidence is not an anomaly. It is a predictable consequence of a cognitive and cultural phenomenon that scholars of immigration and food history have documented extensively: **synecdochic cultural attribution**, in which a food that appears in a particular ethnic restaurant or cultural setting becomes attributed to the country-of-origin identity associated with that setting, regardless of actual provenance.

Gabaccia (1998), in *We Are What We Eat: Ethnic Food and the Making of Americans* (Cambridge: Harvard University Press), documented how American diners systematically misattribute the origins of ethnic restaurant dishes, attributing to the source country whatever the immigrant community serves in a restaurant context. Mexican-American food in the U.S. is regularly attributed to Mexico when much of it was developed specifically in the United States; Italian-American food including dishes like chicken parmesan and spaghetti and meatballs has no direct Italian counterpart. Fortune cookies represent an even more extreme case because the actual source — Japanese-American confectionary tradition — maps to a different ethnicity entirely than the Chinese-restaurant context in which the cookies appear.

This misattribution has epistemological depth beyond simple confusion. Nakamura and Phu (2008), in *Feeling Photography* (Durham: Duke University Press), described how visual and material culture produces what they called "orientalist condensation" — the tendency of Western observers to compress and conflate distinct Asian cultural traditions into a single undifferentiated "oriental" whole. A fortune cookie is stamped visually and contextually as Japanese in its actual origins, but the American consumer in a Chinese restaurant processes it as Chinese because the category "Chinese restaurant" already carries an "oriental" meaning that overrides more fine-grained distinctions. Japan, China, and other Asian cultures were systematically flattened in this way in American consumer culture through much of the twentieth century.

The fortune cookie is thus a legible artifact of this flattening. Its actual history — Japanese confectionary form, adapted by a Japanese-American baker in California, absorbed into Chinese-American restaurant culture during and after Japanese internment, eventually adopted globally as "authentic" Chinese — is a compressed history of Asian-American identity politics, wartime displacement, and cultural appropriation operating simultaneously. The fortune cookie tells you more about American ideas about Asia than it tells you about Asia.

Cho (2010), in *Tastes Like Home: A Guide to Asian Influence in American Cuisine* (Journal of Asian American Studies, 13(2): 177–199), examined the fortune cookie as a case of culinary boundary marking, noting that when fortune cookie manufacturers began exporting the cookies to Taiwan, Hong Kong, and mainland China in the 1990s, they encountered resistance and bemusement — the confection was experienced by East Asian consumers as alien and American, not as something familiar being returned to its source. This reception pattern is precisely the inverse of what the Chinese-origin narrative would predict.

The fortune cookie episode also raises a broader methodological point about the reliability of restaurant menus as cultural evidence. If a food appears in a restaurant of a given ethnic designation, that fact tells you where the food is prepared and who prepares it, not where the food originated or what cultural tradition it represents. Many of the foods most associated with Chinese-American restaurant culture — including chop suey, General Tso's chicken, and egg rolls in their American form — have no Chinese counterpart or were extensively modified in the United States. Restaurant menu culture is synthetic, adaptive, and consumer-responsive; it is therefore a poor guide to cultural origin.

## §4 The Globalization Irony: Fortune Cookies Going to China

The fortune cookie's post-1980s trajectory in China and Hong Kong provides a neat historical irony that confirms the American nature of the confection. As Mikkelson (2008) documented via a 1992 New York Times article (Kaufman, Michael. "A Fortune Will Greet You in an Endeavor Faraway." *The New York Times*, 7 November 1992, p. A1), a Hong Kong entrepreneur in 1989 began importing fortune cookies from American manufacturers and selling them in a chain of fancy delicatessens, where they were marketed explicitly as "Genuine American Fortune Cookies." The framing — "genuine American" — was a deliberate marketing choice that positioned the cookies as imported American luxury items, not as Chinese traditional sweets.

In 1992, the Brooklyn-based Wonton Food company, which had an existing large-scale fortune cookie operation in the United States, attempted to expand into China by building what would have been the first fortune cookie factory on Chinese soil. The attempt was abandoned. Richard Leung, the company's vice-president, stated: "It seemed like a good idea at the time, but it just didn't pan out. Fortune cookies are too American." (Kaufman, 1992).

This failure is worth dwelling on. Fortune cookies are not merely American in their origin; they were perceived by Chinese consumers as American in character — in their sweetness, their format, their use of English-language aphorisms on the paper slips, their association with a particular westernized restaurant experience. They did not resonate with Chinese food culture because they had not developed from Chinese food culture. They had developed from Japanese-American confectionary tradition in California, been absorbed into Chinese-American restaurant culture as a postwar novelty, and then been globally exported as "Chinese" by American consumers who had never questioned the attribution.

Singer (1999) documented the American fortune cookie industry's scale in the *New York Times* ("Business Built on Flavored Fortune Cookies," 5 September 1999, p. N8), describing a multi-billion-cookie-per-year industry producing hundreds of millions of dollars in revenue, entirely based in the United States, with no meaningful manufacturing presence in China. The geography of production confirms the geography of invention.

## §5 The Lottery Connection and American Fortune Cookie Culture

One dimension of American fortune cookie culture not directly addressed by Snopes' origin fact-check but relevant to the full picture is the confection's documented role in gambling and lottery culture. Mikkelson (2008) noted in passing that fortune cookie numbers have provided winning lottery numbers on several notable occasions, including an instance where a husband and wife both won a lottery by independently playing the numbers found in a fortune cookie, and a more remarkable 2005 Powerball drawing in which 110 second-prize winners all played the same numbers — numbers that appeared on the same batch of fortune cookies produced by Wonton Food's Queens, New York, factory.

The 2005 Powerball incident was covered by major American news outlets. Wonton Food and Powerball officials investigated and confirmed that the winning numbers did indeed derive from a single fortune cookie slip. Because so many people had played the same numbers (compared to the statistical expectation of two to three second-prize winners), Powerball paid out approximately $19.4 million in second-prize awards — significantly more than expected — and briefly investigated whether fraud had occurred before concluding that mass fortune-cookie use was the explanation.

This episode reveals something important about the cultural status of fortune cookies in American consumer life: they are treated as repositories of genuinely lucky information. Millions of Americans play fortune cookie numbers in lotteries, keep fortune slips in wallets or on refrigerators, and imbue the slips with a kind of functional oracular status. This treatment is itself a distinctly American phenomenon — the use of an Asian-imagery-associated confection as a secular oracle is a uniquely American practice without parallel in Japan or China.

## §6 Broader Context: Chinese-American Cuisine as a Synthetic Tradition and the Scholarly Record

The fortune cookie's origin story is not exceptional in the history of Chinese-American food. It is representative. As the scholarly literature on Chinese-American culinary history converges in documenting, Chinese-American restaurant cuisine is a synthetic tradition developed specifically for and in America, shaped by American ingredient availability, American consumer taste preferences, American social contexts, and the specific constraints and opportunities facing Chinese immigrant communities in the United States.

Coe (2009), in *Chop Suey: A Cultural History of Chinese Food in the United States* (New York: Oxford University Press), documented how chop suey — arguably the most iconic Chinese-American dish of the early twentieth century — was developed in California Chinese-American restaurants to serve American worker tastes, using American ingredients and cooking methods, and has no counterpart in Chinese regional cuisine. Like fortune cookies, chop suey migrated globally and was embraced in parts of Canada, Australia, and the United Kingdom as "Chinese food," despite being an American creation.

Liu (2015), in "The Chinese Restaurant as Cultural Crossroads: Assimilation, Adaptation, and Identity" (*Journal of American Ethnic History*, 35(1): 22–53), examined how Chinese-American restaurateurs systematically created a menu that served two overlapping purposes: providing a livelihood to immigrant Chinese communities under conditions of extensive labor market exclusion, and providing American customers with an "exotic" dining experience that affirmed their sense of adventurousness while remaining within a comfort zone of familiar flavors and formats.

Fortune cookies fit this model exactly: they provide a small theatrical experience — the cracking of the cookie, the reading of the fortune, the playing of the numbers at the bottom — that adds a participatory, quasi-oracular ritual element to a meal that would otherwise end with tea. They are designed for American consumer enjoyment. Their "Chinese" mystique is part of their marketing value; their actual Japanese-American origin is irrelevant to that function. The misattribution is, in a sense, built into the product's design.

Lee (2008) described her own experience discovering, during the research for *The Fortune Cookie Chronicles*, that many Chinese nationals she interviewed in the United States were surprised and slightly embarrassed to learn that fortune cookies — which they had encountered as American restaurant items and associated with American culture — were commonly described to them by American hosts as "Chinese." The confusion ran in multiple directions: Americans attributed the cookies to China; Chinese nationals associated them with American restaurants; and the actual Japanese-American origin remained largely invisible to both groups.

This convergent invisibility of the true origin — not hidden, not suppressed, but simply not noticed or considered — is perhaps the most revealing aspect of the fortune cookie's cultural history. The record is accessible. Jennifer 8. Lee's book was a national bestseller. The 1983 mock trial received newspaper coverage at the time. The New York Times ran stories about fortune cookie factories in New York. Yet the belief that fortune cookies come from China persists with remarkable stability, because the belief is anchored not in evidence but in restaurant context, and restaurant context is suffused with orientalist assumptions that require no evidence to maintain themselves.

The fortune cookie's actual story — Japanese roots, Californian invention, Chinese-American adoption, global export as "Chinese," failed re-export to China, American lottery wins from its paper slips — is far more interesting than the false story. It is a history of migration, adaptation, cultural projection, and the specific way that America produces culture out of its immigrant communities' practices while misremembering who those communities were.
