# Can You Access Emergency Services by Pressing an iPhone's Lock Button Five Times?


## 1. The Claim

In November and December 2017, a family of memes and social media posts circulated widely on Facebook and other platforms advising iPhone users of a purported discreet method of contacting emergency services. The claim, presented in various forms, stated that pressing the lock button (the side button) on an iPhone five times rapidly would trigger an Emergency SOS option, allowing users in dangerous situations to call police or emergency services without visibly dialling. The posts typically framed this as a safety tip, often using the language of personal security or domestic violence prevention: the ability to discreetly dial for help when one cannot safely make a conventional call was presented as the feature's primary value proposition.

Representative versions of the claim included formulations like: "PSA: If you're ever in an unsafe situation and need to discreetly call the police, hit your iPhone's lock button (on the side) quickly 5 times and you'll get this screen." Some versions specified the home screen button rather than the side button, introduced confusion about the number of presses required, or presented the feature as working on any iPhone. The memes spread rapidly, reflecting both genuine public interest in personal safety tools and the emotional resonance of the scenario they described.

Snopes.com received multiple reader inquiries about the claim in late 2017, including from users who confirmed they did not have iPhones and could not test the feature themselves, and from users asking whether it worked on specific older iPhone models. Writer Kim LaCapria assessed the claim, publishing a rating of Mixture on 28 November 2017. This entry examines the actual feature, its technical scope, its limitations, and the epistemological lessons the claim offers about the communication of safety-relevant information.

The claim exists within a broader tradition of peer-to-peer safety tip sharing that predates smartphones. The specific anxiety the meme addresses — being in a dangerous situation where conventional phone use is visible to a threatening person — is real and widely shared. This emotional resonance contributed substantially to the claim's velocity, and it is the emotional utility, as much as any specific technical claim, that fact-checkers must engage with carefully when evaluating whether to rate such claims false.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Emergency SOS in iOS 11 is real and functional.** Apple introduced the Emergency SOS feature with iOS 11, released in September 2017, approximately two months before the memes began circulating. The feature was publicly documented in Apple's own support materials and was not a secret or unofficial function. For iPhone 7 and earlier models running iOS 11, the feature is activated by rapidly pressing the side button five times, producing an Emergency SOS slider on screen. Dragging the slider places a call to local emergency services. Additionally, Apple documentation notes that users can continue holding the button past the slider appearance to trigger a countdown followed by an automatic call, without requiring the user to drag the slider.

**Post-call notifications to emergency contacts.** Beyond placing an emergency call, the feature automatically sends a text message to any emergency contacts the user has designating in the iPhone's Medical ID or Health app, including the user's current location. If the user's location changes after the call, the emergency contacts receive updated location information, potentially for up to 24 hours. This automated location sharing capability is a significant public safety feature that the memes sometimes mentioned and sometimes omitted.

**Different activation for different iPhone models.** The five-press gesture is correct for iPhone 7 and earlier models running iOS 11. However, for iPhone X, iPhone 8, and iPhone 8 Plus — all released in or around the same autumn that the memes circulated — the Emergency SOS activation method is different. On those models, the user must press and hold the side button and one of the volume buttons simultaneously until the Emergency SOS slider appears, rather than pressing the side button five times. This distinction is significant and was frequently omitted from the memes, which presented the five-press method as universal. A user of a newly purchased iPhone 8 following the meme's instructions would not reach the Emergency SOS screen using the method described.

**iOS version and hardware requirements.** Emergency SOS requires iOS 11, which in turn requires a hardware minimum of iPhone 5s. Devices older than the iPhone 5s cannot be updated to iOS 11 and therefore cannot access the feature regardless of how many times the lock button is pressed. Additionally, iPhone 5s and later device owners who had not yet updated to iOS 11 would not have access to the feature. At the time the memes circulated (late 2017), not all eligible users had updated — software update adoption is gradual across user populations, and many users at any given moment run the prior major version.

**Geographic variation.** Apple's support documentation noted that the feature's behaviour varies by country. In India specifically, pressing the button three times (rather than five) triggers automatic emergency calling, reflecting different user interface conventions adopted for different national markets. This geographic granularity further complicates any simple universal statement about the feature's operation.

**The meme's core claim was thus partially accurate.** The five-press method works for the iPhone 7 and earlier running iOS 11. It does not work for users of older devices, users who have not updated to iOS 11, or users of the then-latest iPhone X/8/8 Plus models. The meme's framing — "hit your iPhone's lock button quickly 5 times" as a universal instruction — was accurate for a specific subset of iPhone users and potentially misleading for others.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The feature is real.** Unlike some safety tip memes that are entirely fabricated, this one describes a function that genuinely exists. Apple designed and documented Emergency SOS; the meme was not invented from nothing. Users who tested it on compatible devices found it worked as described, which confirmed their confidence in sharing the tip. Real functionality at the core of a claim creates a pattern of selective confirmation: people who can verify it confirm it; people who cannot test it (non-iPhone users, users of incompatible devices) have no mechanism for local disconfirmation and are likely to accept the claim on the basis of reports from those who can verify it.

**Safety-relevant sharing norms differ from ordinary information sharing.** Research on warning and risk communication developed in the emergency management and public health domains (Miletti, D.S., 1999, *Disasters by Design: A Reassessment of Natural Hazards in the United States*, Joseph Henry Press) consistently finds that people apply different transmission standards to safety-relevant information than to other kinds of information. The calculus is asymmetric: the cost of sharing a safety tip that turns out to be false or scope-limited is perceived as low (someone might be briefly misinformed), while the cost of failing to share a safety tip that is true is perceived as high (someone might be injured or killed unnecessarily). This asymmetry leads to lower verification thresholds for sharing safety claims. The iPhone SOS meme benefited directly from this dynamic.

**Specificity creates impressions of accuracy.** The instruction to press the button "five times" has the surface quality of an official technical specification — a specific number sounds like the product of authoritative documentation rather than rumour. Vague safety tips ("your phone has emergency features, explore them") do not spread because they demand follow-up action. The specific numerical instruction requires no further research and is immediately actionable, which is both its social appeal and its epistemic problem: the specificity is compelling but it elides the version and model conditions that determine whether the instruction actually applies.

**The surreptitious contact scenario resonates.** The meme commonly framed the feature in the specific context of being in an unsafe situation — typically implying domestic violence, personal confrontation, or similar — where openly dialling emergency services would be dangerous. This scenario connects the feature to a real and serious problem that resonates emotionally with a large audience. The emotional salience of the scenario creates strong motivation to share the tip "just in case" someone in one's network is in exactly that situation. The mobilising power of this framing is significant: people who would not share a technology tip might well share the same information framed as a potential lifesaver.

**The danger of false security.** The specific risk identified by the Snopes assessment — that the meme might give users a false sense of security about a feature they cannot actually access — is a particularly concerning variant of misinformation harm. If a person in a genuinely dangerous situation has relied on a memory of the meme and attempts to trigger Emergency SOS on an iPhone 4 or an unupdated iPhone 6, the failure to activate the feature during an emergency could be materially harmful. Safety tip misinformation has a higher potential harm profile than misinformation in many other domains, which is part of why the Snopes fact-check noted the importance of testing the feature before relying on it in practice.


## 4. Verdict

**Mixture.** The Emergency SOS feature is real, functional, and a genuinely useful public safety tool for qualifying users. The five-press activation method is correct for iPhone 7 and earlier models running iOS 11. The claim is false or inapplicable for users of iPhone models older than iPhone 5s, users who have not updated to iOS 11, and users of iPhone X, iPhone 8, or iPhone 8 Plus (which use a different gesture). The meme's failure to specify these conditions meant it circulated as an unconditional claim applicable to all iPhones, which is incorrect.

The LaCapria (2017) Snopes rating of Mixture is appropriate. The core claim is true for a substantial category of users; it is inapplicable or false for others; and the meme's framing as a universal safety tip for all iPhone users introduced a scope error that could have created false security in people relying on a feature they could not access.

A more precise and useful version of the claim would specify: "iPhone 7 and earlier models running iOS 11 or later can activate Emergency SOS by pressing the side button five times; iPhone 8, 8 Plus, and X models running iOS 11 use a different gesture (hold side button and either volume button simultaneously). Feature requires iOS 11 minimum; test it before an emergency."


## 5. The Wider Picture

The iPhone SOS meme situates within a long history of peer-to-peer safety tip propagation — transmission of advice about emergency preparedness, self-defense, safety features, and survival techniques — that predates digital networks. Similar patterns of rapid informal distribution with degraded precision characterise the oral and print transmission of safety advice throughout recorded history. The emergence of social media has dramatically accelerated both the reach and the accuracy-decay rate of this tradition.

Emergency managers and public health communicators have long grappled with the tension between message simplicity and accuracy in risk communication. Simplified messages reach more people but sacrifice technical precision; precise messages retain accuracy but may not spread beyond specialist audiences. The field of risk communication has developed frameworks for navigating this tension (Covello, V.T. & Allen, F., 1988, *Seven Cardinal Rules of Risk Communication*, U.S. Environmental Protection Agency), but these frameworks are designed for institutional communicators with professional responsibilities, not for the informal peer networks through which memes like the iPhone SOS tip propagate.

The Emergency SOS feature itself represents a significant and underappreciated integration of communications technology with public safety infrastructure. The ability to automatically share location with emergency services and designated contacts — and to maintain that sharing as location changes after an emergency call — goes substantially beyond what a conventional emergency call provides. This capability is particularly relevant in scenarios involving domestic violence, where callers may be unable to speak freely or may call from locations they cannot describe. The National Domestic Violence Hotline and similar resources have developed guidance for safe use of digital tools in dangerous situations; the meme's circulation, despite its inaccuracies, likely did increase awareness of the feature's existence among populations for whom that awareness was genuinely protective.

Subsequent iOS versions have continued to refine the Emergency SOS feature. iOS 16 introduced a Crash Detection feature on newer iPhone models; iOS 17 added expanded Emergency SOS via Satellite capability in regions without cellular coverage. The feature family has expanded considerably since the 2017 meme's reference point, meaning that any single description of how the feature works will become increasingly dated as the product evolves. This is a general challenge in technology fact-checking: platform features change across versions at a pace that often outstrips any single fact-check's shelf life.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

LaCapria's (2017) Snopes fact-check addresses the claim efficiently and with reference to Apple's own official documentation, which is the primary and authoritative source for any claim about iOS feature behaviour. The approach of quoting directly from Apple Support's "Use Emergency SOS on your iPhone" page, including the model-specific and iOS version-specific instructions, provides clear falsification of the universal framing while affirming the underlying capability for qualified users.

The Mixture rating is the correct framework for a claim where the core function exists but the claim overstates its scope. The fact-check also identifies the related memes about other iPhone emergency features — the Siri "charge my phone to 100 percent" police-calling claim (rated mostly false) and the landline off-hook 911 call myth — situating the iPhone SOS claim within a broader ecosystem of emergency communication myths and partially-accurate safety tips. This contextualisation is editorially valuable because it helps readers develop a habit of scepticism toward safety tip memes in general without dismissing the category entirely.

For technology fact-checking methodology, this case illustrates the importance of hardware and software version specificity. Many technology claims circulate in a version-agnostic form ("iPhone does X") when the reality is version-specific ("iPhone X running iOS Y does X when configured as Z"). The default toward simplicity in popular technology communication creates systematic scope errors of exactly this type, and fact-checkers of technology claims should routinely establish the version and configuration conditions under which any described feature operates.

The practical lesson for readers is clear: if you intend to rely on a technology feature in an emergency, test it yourself in advance in a safe context. The habit of testing safety-relevant features before they are needed — knowing how to use your fire extinguisher, having verified that your car's emergency flashers work, confirming that your iPhone's Emergency SOS activates when you expect it to — is both a more reliable strategy than relying on a remembered meme and an application of the general principle that emergency tools require familiarisation before the emergency. Apple itself recommends this habit in the support documentation that LaCapria's fact-check cites.
