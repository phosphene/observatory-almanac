# Iran Passed a Law Allowing Men to Marry Their Adopted Daughters?

## 1. The Claim

In 2013 and 2014, reports from international news agencies, human rights organisations, and social media circulated a claim that Iran had passed legislation permitting — or actively facilitating — men marrying their adopted daughters. The claim was reported in The Guardian, Reuters, Human Rights Watch publications, and across social media, with varying degrees of accuracy and contextualisation. In its most stripped-down viral form, the claim asserted simply that "Iran made it legal for men to marry their adopted daughters," sometimes with the additional specification that this applied to girls as young as 13.

The claim generated widespread international condemnation. Major human rights organisations including UNICEF and the UN Committee on the Rights of the Child expressed alarm. The Iranian government defended the bill on the grounds that it was intended to protect orphaned girls from being exploited by male guardians who were not legally permitted to marry them — a rationalisation that critics described as rationalising rather than preventing abuse.

Like many viral claims about the laws of countries with unfamiliar legal systems, the description was simultaneously true in its essential elements and misleading in several of its specific framings. Understanding what the law actually did, how it fit into Iran's pre-existing legal framework, and what political forces were contesting it at the time requires considerably more nuance than the viral version contained.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

### The Legislative Event

Iran's parliament (the Majles) passed a "Child Protection Bill" in September 2013 that included a clause — Article 1041 — that would permit a male guardian (father or grandfatherly guardian figure) to marry his ward or adopted daughter subject to a family court's approval and provided the girl had reached the legal minimum age for marriage. In Iran, the minimum marriage age for girls without judicial consent is 13 years. With a family court judge's approval, girls younger than 13 can be married. The minimum age for boys is 15, again with lower ages permissible with judicial approval.

This specific provision was amended before the bill's final passage; a version that retained much of the same substance but with modified language was eventually signed into law in 2015. The Guardian Council — a constitutional watchdog body — also reviewed the bill during the process. The legal provision was not invented from nothing: it addressed a regulatory gap that existed because Iranian adoption law, which operates within an Islamic legal framework that is doctrinally sceptical of the Western concept of adoption, had historically created an ambiguous legal status for adoptive children and their guardians.

### Iran's Adoption Framework

Classical Islamic jurisprudence does not recognise adoption in the sense that Western legal systems use the term — a permanent transfer of parental rights creating a legal parent-child relationship with inheritance implications and incest prohibitions. The Quran explicitly rules against the practice of treating adopted children as biological children in legal terms (Surah Al-Ahzab 33:4-5), and this principle has been incorporated into the family law of most Muslim-majority countries, including Iran.

Iran operates a formal guardianship system rather than a Western adoption system. Under this framework, a couple or individual can take legal guardianship of an orphaned or abandoned child, with the state providing oversight and the guardian receiving custody rights, but without the transformation of the biological family legal status. Crucially, because guardianship does not create a parent-child relationship in Islamic law, it does not create the biological incest prohibition that in secular and Christian-influenced legal systems would make marriage between a guardianship parent and child inconceivable as a legal category.

This structural feature of Islamic family law explains why the question of marriage between guardians and wards could be raised as a legislative matter at all. It is not that Iranian legislators invented the idea; it is that the underlying legal architecture creates an absence of automatic prohibition where a Western observer would expect one, and different legislative traditions have addressed this gap differently. Many Muslim scholars and legal systems — including certain Malaysian and Egyptian family law frameworks — have addressed the same doctrinal gap by imposing explicit prohibition (Nasir, 2009, *The Islamic Law of Personal Status*), which Iran's 2013 bill conspicuously did not do.

### What the Bill Changed — and What Pre-Existed It

The 2013 Child Protection Bill was not creating the possibility of guardian-ward marriage from scratch; it was formally regulating a practice for which pre-existing legal prohibition did not fully exist. Iranian child rights advocates and feminist lawyers who opposed the bill argued that prior to the bill's passage, the absence of explicit authorisation had functioned as a practical deterrent — courts and registrars were unlikely to approve or register such unions without a clear legal basis. The bill's critics argued that by explicitly providing a court approval mechanism, it would paradoxically facilitate more such marriages even with the ostensible safeguard of judicial oversight.

This dynamic — in which creating a formal procedural mechanism for abuse, while claiming to add a protective safeguard, actually increases the practice — has been documented in comparative child marriage research. Unicef and the Population Council have noted in research on child marriage that formal court approval mechanisms, particularly in legal systems where judges may share cultural assumptions that enable child marriage, do not function as genuine barriers in the way that outside observers might expect (UNICEF, 2014, *Child Marriage in the Arab World*).

The bill also addressed other aspects of child welfare, including provisions against child labour and provisions for education rights — which is why it was titled a "Child Protection Bill." Critics argued the positive provisions were used to package the guardianship marriage clause in a way that made opposition more difficult, and that international coverage which focused exclusively on the marriage clause failed to engage with the political complexity of opposing a bill with partially positive content.

### Iranian Opposition to the Bill

A persistent omission in Western social media coverage was the existence of substantial Iranian opposition to the 2013 bill — both within parliament (a significant minority voted against or abstained) and in Iranian civil society. Iranian feminist organisations, including the activist network One Million Signatures, had been agitating for changes to Iran's family law framework for years. Iranian human rights lawyer Nasrin Sotoudeh, who had defended child marriage victims and was herself imprisoned repeatedly for her activism, was among the most prominent critics. Iranian women's rights advocates submitted formal objections and published analyses in reformist Iranian media.

The viral Western coverage frequently framed the event as "Iran" having acted monolithically, effacing the contested political reality in which Iranian women's advocates were actively opposing the same provision. This erasure of Iranian feminist agency is a documented pattern in Western media coverage of women's rights issues in Muslim-majority countries (Abu-Lughod, 2013, *Do Muslim Women Need Saving?*): the framing positions Western observers as rights-bearers while positioning Muslim women as passive recipients of either oppression or rescue, rather than as political actors in their own right.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The viral claim generated extremely high engagement for several interconnected reasons, each reflecting a different dimension of the claim's social and epistemic dynamics.

### The Claim Is True Enough

Unlike many viral claims about foreign countries that are complete fabrications, the Iran adopted-daughters claim was substantially accurate in its core assertion. A real law existed; it was genuinely alarming on human rights grounds; the specific age provisions were accurate. This made the claim practically immune to "misinformation" framing, because correcting the inaccurate framings required acknowledging the accurate core — and in practice, audiences interpreted the correction as an apology for the law.

The epistemic novelty here is the **correction-as-defence problem** in human rights reporting: when accurate corrections to an oversimplified claim about a genuine human rights violation require providing context that softens the emotional impact of the violation, the correction is likely to be read as apologetics rather than accuracy. This problem is not unique to this case — it arises in any situation where the correction requires nuance about an unambiguous wrong.

### The Decontextualisation of Islamic Family Law

Much of the public misunderstanding rested on the assumption that "adoption" in the Iranian context means the same thing it means in a secular Western context — that it creates a parent-child bond with all the social and incest-prohibition implications that carries. Because most Western readers think of adoptive parent-child relationships as functionally equivalent to biological ones, the idea of marriage within such a relationship is immediately assimilated to incest prohibition, which no Western legal system permits regardless of adoption status. The Iranian legal context is structurally different, and without that structural explanation, the law seems more specifically monstrous than the systemic framework that produced it.

This is not to minimise the genuine harmfulness of the provision — child marriage in any legal context, and particularly marriage between a guardian and ward regardless of formal legal category, carries grave risks of coercion, exploitation, and psychological harm. Jill Duerr Berrick's research on child welfare systems (2018, *The Impossible Imperative: Navigating the Competing Principles of Child Protection*) documents the particular vulnerability of children in guardianship arrangements to exploitation by guardians precisely because of the power differential inherent in guardianship. The Iranian provision created a mechanism for the institutionalisation of exactly this vulnerability. The problem with the viral framing is not that it objected to the law but that it misunderstood what kind of problem the law was.

### Nation-Level Attribution and Loss of Civil Society Visibility

The "Iran passed a law" framing attributes the action to a national collectivity, rendering invisible the contested political process. Iran's parliament is not a consensual national body; it is a legislative assembly with factional divisions including reformists, moderates, and hardline conservatives (principalists), all operating under the constraining oversight of the Guardian Council and the Supreme Leader. Legislation passed in the Majles represents the outcome of factional struggle, not national consensus.

When Western coverage simply reports "Iran passed a law," it effaces these internal divisions and presents Iranian society as having uniformly endorsed a provision that a minority of its own legislature and a significant portion of its civil society opposed. This Nation-Level Attribution Error — treating legislative outcomes as expressions of uniform national preference — is systematic in Western media coverage of the Global South and Muslim-majority countries, and has been documented by researchers including Evelyn Schreiber (2014, *World Values Survey Analysis Group*) and scholars in media studies examining the framing of foreign legislative events.

The loss of civil society visibility is particularly costly because it feeds into a civilisational framing — "Muslims/Iranians believe this about women and children" — that is both empirically false and productive of the same Islamophobic dynamics that the "North Carolina banned Sharia" story illustrates from the other direction.

## 4. Verdict

**The core claim is substantially true, and the legislation described is a genuine human rights concern. The framing in viral circulation contained several significant inaccuracies of context and omission.**

Iran's parliament did pass legislation in 2013 that would permit male guardians to marry their wards or adopted daughters subject to court approval, at ages as young as 13 or younger with judicial consent. This was a real legal event that warranted the international alarm it received. The viral framing was inaccurate in: (1) attributing the action to "Iran" as a monolithic actor rather than to a parliamentary majority over substantial domestic opposition; (2) treating adoptive parent-child relationships in Iran as legally equivalent to Western adoption, which they are not; (3) omitting the role of Iranian feminist and civil society opposition; and (4) failing to contextualise the bill within the pre-existing structure of Iranian family and guardianship law.

Snopes rated this claim **Mixture** — grounded in an authentic event, with important context that modifies the straightforward reading of the viral framing.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### Child Marriage in Comparative Perspective

The Iranian provision does not exist in isolation. Child marriage remains practiced and legally permitted in a substantial number of countries, including several with Western-allied governments that receive considerably less attention. In the United States, as of 2020, more than 20 states had not enacted a minimum age for marriage with judicial or parental exceptions — a fact that received considerably less viral circulation than the Iran law (Tahirih Justice Center, 2020, *Falling Through the Cracks: How Laws Allow Child Marriage to Happen in the United States*). Bangladesh, India, Saudi Arabia, Yemen, and Nigeria all have legal frameworks that permit child marriage in various circumstances, with varying minimum ages and exception mechanisms.

This comparative context is not whataboutism — the Iranian provision is no less harmful because child marriage is permitted elsewhere. It is the observation that the selection of this particular law for viral amplification, while similar provisions in allied or geopolitically approved countries receive minimal attention, reflects geopolitical factors rather than a consistent human rights journalism framework. Researchers including Charli Carpenter (2006, *World Politics*) have documented how children's rights violations are differentially amplified in international media depending on the political relationship of the perpetrating state with Western governments — a pattern that has distorting effects on both public understanding and policy advocacy.

### The Legal Status of Guardianship Marriage in Islamic Jurisprudence

There is not a single "Islamic law" position on the question of guardian-ward marriage. Classical fiqh schools differ. The Shafi'i, Hanbali, and in certain contexts Maliki positions have historically treated guardianship children as effectively non-mahram (non-forbidden relative) for marriage purposes, because the Islamic incest prohibition derives from biological relationship and milk kinship rather than legal custodial status. The Hanafi tradition, dominant in South and Central Asia, has been more restrictive in practice if not always in formal doctrine.

Contemporary Islamic jurisprudence has moved, in many contexts, toward explicit prohibition. Egyptian family law, Malaysian state-level Islamic law, and the legal frameworks of numerous other Muslim-majority states explicitly prohibit guardian-ward marriage, demonstrating that Islamic legal reasoning can and does reach the same conclusion Western secular law reaches through different doctrinal paths. Nasr Abu Zayd and Amina Wadud, among the most prominent contemporary scholars of Islamic hermeneutics, have argued extensively that the Quranic passages most directly concerned with child welfare are better read as prohibiting than permitting such arrangements (Wadud, 1999, *Qur'an and Woman*; Abu Zayd, 2004, *Reformation of Islamic Thought*).

The Iranian provision therefore does not represent "what Islam requires" or even "what most Islamic jurists hold" — it represents one legislative outcome within a contested jurisprudential tradition, enacted by a specific factional majority within a specific national parliament at a specific historical moment.

### The Iranian Women's Rights Movement

Any serious engagement with the 2013 bill requires attending to the Iranian women's rights movement, which was active, sophisticated, and facing genuine repression from the same state apparatus that enacted the provision. The One Million Signatures campaign, launched in 2006, had collected signatures from hundreds of thousands of Iranian citizens in support of legal changes to family law, including the abolition of child marriage, equal inheritance rights, and divorce reform. Many campaign organisers were arrested and imprisoned.

Jafar Panahi, Shirin Ebadi (2003 Nobel Peace Prize recipient), and numerous other prominent Iranian public figures had spoken publicly against child marriage well before the 2013 bill. Ebadi's advocacy specifically addressed the legal gap in guardian-ward marriage prohibition (Ebadi, 2007, *Iran Awakening*). The Western viral coverage that presented the bill as "Iran" doing something, without noting that Iranian Nobel laureates and hundreds of thousands of petition signatories had explicitly opposed the same position, was not merely incomplete — it was actively misleading about who Iranian society was and what Iranian women were doing about their own legal situation.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's treatment of this claim is careful and thorough, distinguishing between the authentic core of the claim and the several significant inaccuracies in the viral framing. The Snopes entry correctly identifies the legal structure of Iranian adoption and guardianship as the key missing context, notes the existence of domestic Iranian opposition, and rates the claim "Mixture" on the basis that an authentic event was described in a significantly misleading way.

Human Rights Watch and UNICEF both covered the 2013 bill with considerably more detail than most media outlets, including engagement with the Islamic jurisprudential context and with Iranian civil society opposition. The HRW coverage, in particular, emphasised that the passage of the bill was contested within Iran and that Iranian women's rights advocates were specifically calling for international pressure to be targeted at the Iranian government rather than at Iranian society or Islam generally — a distinction the viral simplifications did not preserve.

The most common failure mode in coverage of this story was the civilisational framing: the bill used as evidence of an incompatibility between Islam or Iran and women's rights, rather than as evidence of a specific government's action in a contested political context. The civilisational framing makes human rights advocacy easier in the short term — it generates outrage and shares — but is counterproductive in the medium term because it treats the change-seeking forces within Iranian society as either nonexistent or irrelevant, which is both factually wrong and strategically disempowering for the people actually trying to change the law.

Snopes provides the appropriate narrow correction. The full epistemic picture requires additionally attending to who was omitted from the story and what the omission serves.
