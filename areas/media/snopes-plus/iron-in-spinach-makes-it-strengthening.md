# Iron in spinach makes it strengthening


## 1. The Claim

The claim that spinach is an extraordinarily iron-rich food, and that this iron content makes it uniquely strengthening, is one of the most recognisable nutrition myths of the twentieth century. Its cultural staying power owes much to a fictional sailor: Popeye the Sailor Man, created by E.C. Segar in 1929 for the Thimble Theatre comic strip and later adapted into a phenomenally popular series of animated cartoons by Fleischer Studios and subsequently Paramount Pictures. In the canonical Popeye narrative, eating a can of spinach produces an instantaneous transformation — the sailor's forearms swell, his posture straightens, and he acquires superhuman strength sufficient to defeat his adversary Bluto (or Brutus, depending on the era). The visual grammar is unmistakable: spinach equals strength.

The cartoon connection gave the claim enormous cultural reach. Popeye cartoons ran from 1933 through the 1950s in theatres and subsequently in perpetual television syndication, embedding the spinach-strength association in the popular imagination across multiple generations. The spinach industry was a direct beneficiary: spinach growers credited Popeye with a significant increase in domestic spinach consumption during the 1930s, and a statue of Popeye was erected in Crystal City, Texas — a major spinach-producing region — in 1937 as a tribute to the cartoon character's promotional effect. The claim was not merely believed; it was commercially consequential.

Behind the cartoon imagery, the factual claim has a specific structure: spinach contains exceptionally high levels of iron; iron is the nutrient most directly associated with physical strength and vitality (through its role in haemoglobin and oxygen transport); therefore, spinach consumption produces strength. Each link in this chain contains either an error or a significant overstatement, but the chain as a whole has proved remarkably resistant to correction.

The origin most frequently cited for the iron exaggeration involves a measurement error attributed to the late nineteenth century. The standard account, popularised by T.J. Hamblin in a 1981 article in the *British Medical Journal*, holds that a German chemist — usually identified as E. von Wolff, publishing in 1870 — recorded the iron content of spinach with a misplaced decimal point, reporting a figure approximately ten times higher than the actual value. This tenfold exaggeration then propagated through nutritional reference tables for decades before being identified and corrected by subsequent researchers. The narrative has become a famous cautionary tale about the persistence of data errors in scientific literature.

However, as we shall see, the decimal-error story is itself more complicated than this clean narrative suggests. The actual history of the spinach iron measurement involves multiple researchers, different analytical methods, confusion between dried and fresh-weight measurements, and a chain of citation transmission in which the nature of the original error — if it was a single discrete error at all — is difficult to pin down with certainty. The myth about spinach has, in a sense, generated its own secondary myth about how the original myth arose.


## 2. What Is Actually True

**Spinach's actual iron content.** According to the USDA FoodData Central database (USDA, 2023), raw spinach contains approximately 2.71 mg of iron per 100 g. Cooked spinach, which is denser due to water loss, contains approximately 3.57 mg per 100 g. These figures place spinach in the moderate range for iron content among vegetables — it is not iron-poor, but it is far from exceptional. For comparison, raw kale contains approximately 1.5 mg per 100 g, raw Swiss chard approximately 1.8 mg per 100 g, and cooked lentils approximately 3.3 mg per 100 g. Beef liver, one of the most iron-dense commonly consumed foods, contains approximately 6.5 mg per 100 g. A 100 g serving of cooked beef steak provides approximately 2.6 mg of iron.

The claim that spinach is uniquely iron-rich is therefore quantitatively false. Its iron content is in the same range as many other commonly consumed plant foods and is substantially below that of organ meats and legumes. The idea that spinach stands out as a special source of iron among foods does not survive comparison with standard nutritional databases.

**Bioavailability: the oxalate problem.** Even if spinach's gross iron content were higher than it is, a second and more consequential problem undermines the claim. Iron in spinach is almost entirely non-heme iron — the form of iron found in plant foods, as distinct from the heme iron found in animal tissues. Non-heme iron is inherently less bioavailable (less efficiently absorbed by the human gastrointestinal tract) than heme iron. Heme iron absorption rates are typically 15–35%, while non-heme iron absorption rates are typically 2–20%, depending on the dietary context and the individual's iron status (Hurrell & Egli, 2010, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*).

But spinach has a specific additional problem beyond the general non-heme disadvantage: it is exceptionally high in oxalic acid (oxalate). Oxalate is a potent inhibitor of non-heme iron absorption because it binds to iron in the gastrointestinal tract, forming insoluble iron-oxalate complexes that cannot be absorbed across the intestinal mucosa. Spinach contains approximately 970 mg of oxalate per 100 g — one of the highest oxalate concentrations among commonly consumed vegetables (Noonan & Savage, 1999, *Asia Pacific Journal of Clinical Nutrition*).

Gillooly, Bothwell, Torrance, MacPhail, Derman, Bezwoda, Mills, and Charlton (1983, *British Journal of Nutrition*) directly measured iron absorption from spinach and other vegetables using radioisotope-labelled iron in human feeding studies. They found that iron absorption from spinach was remarkably low — approximately 1.7% of the iron present was actually absorbed, compared with substantially higher absorption rates from other vegetables with lower oxalate content. This means that of the approximately 2.7 mg of iron in 100 g of raw spinach, only about 0.05 mg is actually absorbed and available for physiological use. By this measure, spinach is one of the worst dietary sources of bioavailable iron among common foods, despite its moderate gross iron content.

Hallberg and Hulthén (2000, *European Journal of Clinical Nutrition*) confirmed this finding in a comprehensive analysis of iron absorption determinants, identifying oxalate as one of the most potent dietary inhibitors of non-heme iron absorption and specifically noting spinach as a food in which the inhibitory effect is particularly pronounced.

**The decimal-error story: what really happened.** The popular narrative of a single decimal-point error, most famously told by Hamblin (1981, *British Medical Journal*), holds that a German researcher — variously identified as E. von Wolff (1871) or sometimes attributed to Gustav von Bunge's laboratory (Bunge, 1892) — published an iron value for spinach that was tenfold too high due to a misplaced decimal point. This clean narrative has been questioned by subsequent historians of science.

Sutton (2010, *Internet Journal of Criminology*) conducted a detailed investigation of the primary sources and concluded that the decimal-error story, while containing a kernel of truth about erroneous early measurements, does not accurately represent the actual chain of events. The confusion appears to have arisen partly from the conflation of dry-weight and fresh-weight measurements. Fresh spinach is approximately 91% water; dried spinach therefore contains roughly 10 times the concentration of nutrients per gram compared with the fresh vegetable. Early nineteenth-century analyses sometimes reported values on a dry-weight basis, and if these figures were subsequently entered into tables or cited as though they represented fresh-weight values, the result would be approximately a tenfold overstatement — not because of a decimal mistake on a page, but because of a unit-of-measurement confusion in secondary transmission.

Kohnke's 1892 data, analysing dried spinach samples in Bunge's laboratory, reported iron values consistent with the expected concentration for dehydrated plant material. If a subsequent compiler entered this dry-weight figure into a fresh-weight table without adjustment, the factor-of-ten error would follow naturally. Whether this actually happened, and if so at which point in the citation chain, remains debated among historians of nutrition science. What is clear is that by the early twentieth century, several widely used nutritional reference works listed iron values for spinach that were substantially higher than the true fresh-weight figure, and that these inflated values persisted in reference materials for decades.

The correction is sometimes attributed to Professor Arnold Bender of Queen Elizabeth College, London, who identified the discrepancy in the 1970s, though similar corrections appear in earlier German-language nutritional literature. By the time Hamblin publicised the story in the BMJ in 1981, the corrected values were already standard in modern nutritional databases, but the myth of exceptional iron content had been independently sustained by the Popeye cultural complex for half a century and did not depend on reference tables for its continued transmission.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The Popeye amplification effect.** The single most influential factor in the persistence of the spinach-iron myth is the Popeye franchise. No other food-nutrient myth has benefited from a comparable cultural amplification vehicle. The Popeye cartoons did not merely state that spinach was strengthening; they dramatised the causal connection in maximally vivid, memorable, and emotionally satisfying narrative form. A character eats spinach; the character becomes visibly powerful; the character triumphs. This narrative template, repeated across hundreds of cartoon episodes over decades, embedded the association between spinach and strength at a level of cultural saturation that no nutritional data table could reach.

The Popeye effect also demonstrates a general principle about myth propagation: fictional depictions of causal relationships can create beliefs about factual causal relationships, particularly when the fictional depiction is consistent with a pre-existing factual claim (spinach contains iron) and the audience does not clearly distinguish between the fictional dramatisation and the factual assertion embedded within it. Children who watched Popeye cartoons were not performing a critical evaluation of nutritional claims; they were absorbing a narrative association that would persist into adulthood as a vague background belief that "spinach is good for you because it has iron."

**The green-equals-healthy heuristic.** Spinach benefits from a broader cultural heuristic that associates deep green colour with nutritional virtue. Dark leafy greens are, in general, nutrient-dense foods — they are genuinely good sources of various vitamins and minerals. The inference from "dark green vegetable" to "must be very healthy" to "must be very high in iron" involves a series of reasonable-sounding but ultimately unsupported inferential leaps. The association between greenness and iron content is not biologically grounded — plant greenness is determined by chlorophyll content, which has no direct relationship to iron concentration — but it is intuitively satisfying enough to resist casual questioning.

**The confusion between presence and bioavailability.** Even when people become aware that spinach's gross iron content is moderate rather than exceptional, they often retain a residual belief that spinach is "good for iron" because they do not distinguish between iron content and bioavailable iron. The concept of bioavailability — the fraction of a nutrient that is actually absorbed and physiologically utilised — is not intuitively obvious and requires understanding of intestinal absorption chemistry that most non-specialists do not possess. The default assumption is that if a food contains a nutrient, eating the food delivers that nutrient to the body. The reality that oxalate in spinach sequesters most of the iron into unabsorbable complexes is a mechanistic detail that contradicts this default assumption but requires specific biochemical knowledge to access.

**Epistemic novelty: the decimal error persistence phenomenon.** The spinach myth provides one of the most instructive case studies in the history of science communication of what might be called the "decimal error persistence" phenomenon — the tendency of a single measurement error to propagate through secondary sources for decades or centuries because citation chains transmit numerical values without re-checking them against primary data. The dynamics of this propagation are worth examining in detail because they illustrate a structural vulnerability in scientific information transmission that extends well beyond nutrition.

When a numerical value is first published in a primary source, it carries with it the analytical context — the method used, the sample preparation, the units of measurement, the potential sources of error. When that value is subsequently cited in a secondary reference work (a textbook, a nutritional table, a review article), the analytical context is typically stripped away; only the number travels. A reader of the secondary source sees the number but not the conditions under which it was generated. If the number is wrong — due to a decimal error, a unit confusion, a non-representative sample, or any other source of measurement error — the secondary source transmits the error without the contextual information that might allow a reader to identify it.

This stripping of analytical context during citation transmission creates a one-way ratchet for certain types of error. Correct values and incorrect values travel with equal ease through citation chains, but incorrect values are less likely to be identified and corrected because the context that would reveal the error is lost in transmission. The corrective mechanism — returning to the primary source and re-evaluating the original measurement — requires an active effort that most secondary-source users do not undertake because they trust the citation chain. When a German researcher's 1870s dry-weight iron measurement for spinach was entered into a fresh-weight nutritional table without unit correction, the tenfold error was transmitted to every subsequent user of that table, and from there to every subsequent table compiled using that table as a source. The error persisted not because no one could have caught it, but because the institutional structure of citation transmission created no routine occasion when anyone would have checked. Each compiler assumed the previous compiler had verified the figure, and none of them had.

This phenomenon — citation-chain propagation of unchecked numerical values — is not unique to the spinach case. Similar dynamics have been documented in pharmaceutical dosing references, engineering material properties tables, and environmental toxicology thresholds. The spinach example is merely the most culturally visible instance of a general structural vulnerability in how quantitative scientific information moves between sources.


## 4. Verdict

**False.** Spinach is not exceptionally high in iron, and its iron content does not make it a superfood for strength. The claim fails on multiple levels: spinach's gross iron content (approximately 2.7 mg per 100 g raw) is moderate and comparable to many other vegetables, legumes, and animal foods; the iron in spinach is non-heme and therefore inherently less bioavailable than the heme iron in animal sources; and spinach's high oxalate content further reduces iron absorption to approximately 1.7% of iron present, making it one of the poorest practical sources of bioavailable iron among common foods.

The cultural persistence of the claim is attributable to the Popeye franchise's decades-long dramatisation of the spinach-strength association, reinforced by an early measurement error (likely involving confusion between dry-weight and fresh-weight iron values) that inflated spinach's apparent iron content in nutritional reference tables for much of the twentieth century. The conjunction of a cultural amplification vehicle (Popeye) and a scientific transmission error (the decimal/unit confusion) created conditions uniquely favourable to the myth's persistence.

Confidence in this verdict is high. The actual iron content of spinach is well-established in modern nutritional databases (USDA FoodData Central). The bioavailability limitation imposed by oxalate has been demonstrated in multiple human feeding studies using radioisotope-labelled iron (Gillooly et al., 1983, *British Journal of Nutrition*; Hallberg & Hulthén, 2000, *European Journal of Clinical Nutrition*). The historical origin of the measurement error has been investigated by multiple scholars (Hamblin, 1981, *BMJ*; Sutton, 2010, *Internet Journal of Criminology*), and while the precise chain of events remains debated, the existence of significantly inflated iron values in early-to-mid twentieth-century reference works is well-documented.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The spinach iron myth is worth examining not merely as a curiosity of nutritional misinformation but as an entry point into the broader phenomenon of "superfood" mythology — the cultural tendency to attribute extraordinary health properties to specific foods, typically driven by a combination of genuine but overstated nutritional attributes, commercial interests, and media amplification.

**What spinach is genuinely good for.** Debunking the iron claim should not be taken as an assertion that spinach lacks nutritional value. Spinach is a genuinely nutrient-dense food — it is an excellent source of vitamin K (approximately 483 μg per 100 g raw, providing several times the adequate daily intake) which is essential for blood coagulation and bone metabolism; a good source of vitamin C (approximately 28 mg per 100 g), folate (approximately 194 μg per 100 g), vitamin A as beta-carotene, and the carotenoid lutein, which has a well-documented role in macular health (Seddon, Ajani, Sperduto, Hiller, Blair, Burton, Farber, Gragoudas, Haller, & Miller, 1994, *JAMA*). Spinach is also a reasonable source of manganese, magnesium, and potassium. Its genuine nutritional merits are substantial — they simply do not include exceptional iron bioavailability.

**The superfood myth economy.** The spinach case is a prototype for a pattern that recurs throughout contemporary nutrition culture: a food acquires a reputation for a specific extraordinary property; that reputation is amplified by media coverage, commercial marketing, and cultural repetition; the reputation becomes self-sustaining regardless of its accuracy because it provides a satisfying narrative (eat this specific food and receive this specific benefit) that aligns with a desire for simple, actionable health advice. Acai berries and antioxidants, turmeric and inflammation, cranberries and urinary tract infections, green tea and metabolism — each case involves real nutritional properties that have been inflated, through a combination of preliminary research findings, media enthusiasm, and commercial incentive, into claims substantially beyond what the evidence supports.

The economic dimensions of superfood mythology are considerable. Foods that acquire superfood status experience significant demand increases, price premiums, and centre placement in retail and restaurant contexts. The spinach industry's acknowledgement of Popeye's effect on demand in the 1930s — culminating in the Crystal City statue — is an early and unusually candid example of the commercial benefits of nutritional mythologising. Modern superfood marketing is more sophisticated but operates through the same mechanism: a credible-sounding health claim drives consumer demand, and the demand itself becomes evidence for the claim's validity in a self-reinforcing cycle.

**Iron deficiency and its real solutions.** The spinach myth has a practical cost beyond mere inaccuracy: people who believe spinach is an excellent iron source may rely on it as a dietary strategy for addressing iron deficiency, which is the most common nutritional deficiency worldwide (WHO, 2023). Iron deficiency anaemia affects an estimated 1.2 billion people globally and is particularly prevalent among women of reproductive age, young children, and populations with limited access to animal-source foods. For these populations, accurate dietary guidance about iron is a genuine public health priority. Directing people toward spinach as a primary iron source is not merely unhelpful; it may displace attention from more effective dietary strategies including consumption of animal-source foods (for heme iron), legumes and fortified cereals (for more bioavailable non-heme iron), and vitamin C-rich foods consumed alongside iron sources to enhance non-heme iron absorption (Hallberg, Brune, & Rossander, 1989, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*).


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The spinach iron myth is one of the most frequently fact-checked nutritional claims, and it has been assessed as false or misleading by Snopes, numerous popular science publications, medical reference sites, and science journalism outlets. The standard treatment identifies three key points: the actual iron content is not exceptional, the Popeye connection amplified the myth, and a historical measurement error contributed to the inflated reputation.

Where fact-checking treatments of this claim tend to fall short is in their discussion of bioavailability. Many popular debunkings focus on the gross iron content comparison — "spinach has 2.7 mg per 100 g, which isn't that much" — without adequately explaining that even this moderate figure vastly overstates the amount of iron that a human body actually absorbs from spinach. The oxalate binding mechanism, which reduces absorption to approximately 1.7%, is the most consequential fact about spinach and iron, but it requires biochemical explanation that many popular-audience fact-checks understandably simplify or omit. The result is that readers may come away understanding that spinach is not the iron champion they thought but still overestimating its practical contribution to iron intake.

A second gap in standard fact-checking treatments involves the decimal-error story itself. The Hamblin (1981) narrative — a single misplaced decimal point — has become the standard origin story for the myth, repeated in hundreds of fact-checks and popular science articles. But as Sutton (2010) and others have shown, the actual history is more complicated, involving possible dry-weight/fresh-weight confusion rather than a literal decimal displacement, multiple researchers and publications rather than a single error event, and a citation transmission chain whose specific links remain partially obscured by the passage of time. Fact-checkers who repeat the clean decimal-error story without noting its contested status are, ironically, propagating a simplification of the origin story that has some of the same self-perpetuating citation-chain properties as the original spinach myth. The story about how the myth started is itself an example of how appealing narratives propagate through secondary retellings without adequate verification — a meta-level irony that makes the spinach case doubly instructive for media literacy education.

For educators and media literacy practitioners, the spinach myth is particularly valuable because it illustrates multiple mechanisms of misinformation operating simultaneously: a measurement error in primary scientific data, citation-chain propagation of that error through reference works, cultural amplification through entertainment media (Popeye), commercial reinforcement by industry interests, and the persistence of all of these factors long after the original error was identified and corrected. Few other myths combine so many distinct misinformation pathways in a single, easily communicated example. Understanding the spinach case in its full complexity provides a template for analysing how misinformation about other topics — pharmaceuticals, environmental risks, dietary supplements — can be generated, amplified, and sustained through similar multi-channel mechanisms.
