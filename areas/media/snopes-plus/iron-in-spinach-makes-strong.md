# Spinach is exceptionally high in iron and makes you strong

## 1. The Claim

For nearly a century, spinach has been culturally coded as the ultimate strength food — a superfood before the term existed, the source of Popeye the Sailor Man's improbable musculature, the vegetable that mothers insisted upon with nutritional authority. The claim embedded in popular culture has two components: first, that spinach contains exceptional amounts of iron; second, that consuming it leads to increased strength. Both components are regularly invoked when parents push greens at the dinner table and when Popeye cans are cited as evidence that vegetables actually matter.

The full mythology is richer and stranger than most people realize. Not only is the claim about spinach's iron content exaggerated, but the most popular explanation for why the myth exists — a supposed decimal point error by a German scientist in 1870 that gave spinach ten times too much iron — is itself a myth. We are dealing, unusually, with a legend that has generated a false counter-legend about its own origins.

This is a case of the "distorted-but-grounded" type: spinach genuinely is nutritious, genuinely does contain iron, and consuming vegetables genuinely is associated with health outcomes. But the specific claims about exceptional iron content and direct strength effects are not accurate, and the cultural transmission mechanism for the myth is more complicated and interesting than the decimal-point story suggests.

## 2. What's Actually True

**Spinach's iron content.** Raw spinach contains approximately 2.7 milligrams of iron per 100 grams, according to USDA nutritional data. Cooked spinach — which reduces dramatically in volume — contains about 3.6mg per 100g (though the serving is much smaller after cooking). This is not an exceptional amount compared to other leafy greens and vegetables. For comparison: lentils contain approximately 3.3mg/100g (cooked), tofu 2.7mg/100g, soybeans 15mg/100g dry, pumpkin seeds 8.8mg/100g, white beans 3.7mg/100g (cooked). Among leafy greens specifically, spinach is not at the top of the iron chart.

**The bioavailability problem.** The more important issue is not how much iron spinach contains in total, but how much of that iron the body can actually absorb. Spinach contains high levels of oxalic acid (oxalates), which bind to iron and calcium, dramatically reducing their absorption. Studies have found that the bioavailability of iron from spinach is approximately 2-5%, compared to 15-20% from heme iron in meat. This means that even if spinach contained modestly more iron than it does, the oxalate-bound fraction would be largely unavailable to the body.

Oxalates form insoluble compounds with iron in the digestive tract that pass through without absorption. Blanching or lightly cooking spinach reduces oxalate content somewhat, but does not eliminate the absorption problem. Consuming spinach with vitamin C sources can improve non-heme iron absorption (by converting iron to a more soluble form), but even with this enhancement, spinach remains a poor iron source compared to legumes, meat, or even many other vegetables.

**Spinach's actual nutritional strengths.** This is where the "distorted-but-grounded" designation earns its keep: spinach really is nutritionally valuable, just not primarily for iron. Spinach is rich in vitamin K (critical for blood clotting and bone metabolism), vitamin A (as beta-carotene), folate (essential for cell division and pregnancy), vitamin C, magnesium, and manganese. It contains lutein and zeaxanthin, carotenoids associated with reduced risk of age-related macular degeneration. It is a source of antioxidants including flavonoids and polyphenols. Research has found associations between spinach consumption and reduced inflammation markers, cardiovascular benefits, and eye health.

Spinach is, by most nutritional assessments, a very good food to eat. The case for eating it is strong. It simply isn't primarily the iron case that popular culture presents.

**The "strength" claim.** Spinach does not cause measurable strength increases in the timeframe implied by Popeye's cartoon physiology (he opens a can and immediately has superstrength). Iron is essential for oxygen transport in red blood cells, and iron deficiency anemia does cause fatigue and reduced physical performance. Treating iron deficiency can restore physical capacity — but this is correction of a deficit, not augmentation of normal function. For people who are not iron deficient, consuming spinach does not cause increased muscle strength. There is no mechanism by which it would.

A 2021 study by German researchers did find that ecdysterone, a plant steroid found in spinach, produced significant strength gains in weightlifters over a ten-week period — gains comparable to banned anabolic substances. This finding, covered in media as "Popeye was right," is interesting but preliminary, and the quantities of spinach required to obtain meaningful ecdysterone doses are far beyond normal dietary consumption.

**The decimal point myth — and its debunking.** The most widely cited explanation for Popeye's spinach obsession is that a German scientist named E. von Wolf made a decimal point error in 1870, calculating spinach's iron content as 35mg/100g instead of the correct 3.5mg/100g. This error, the story goes, propagated through nutritional literature for decades and eventually inspired the Popeye cartoonists.

This story is itself false. Mike Sutton, a British criminologist and mythologist, investigated the provenance of the decimal point claim in 2010 and found that it appeared to originate in a 1981 article by T.J. Hamblin in the *British Medical Journal*. Hamblin cited it as fact but provided no primary source. Sutton traced the nutritional literature and found no evidence that the decimal error ever appeared in von Wolf's original work or in subsequent citations. The 1870 figure of 35mg/100g does not appear in any 19th or early 20th century nutritional table that Sutton could locate. The decimal point story appears to have been invented or confabulated, then repeated as fact because it was a satisfying explanation.

Furthermore, Sutton found no evidence that Popeye's creator, Elzie Crisler Segar, was inspired by inflated iron statistics. The historical record suggests Popeye ate spinach for strength — as a general cultural association between vegetables and health — without specific reference to iron content.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**Popeye as cultural transmission.** The *Thimble Theatre* comic strip debuted in 1929, and Popeye became one of the most widely distributed cartoon characters in the world through animated shorts produced from 1933 onward. The Popeye shorts reached an enormous audience at a time when mass media distribution was limited: the same cultural products reached nearly everyone who had access to a cinema or, later, a television. Popeye's spinach-eating was not a minor background detail — it was the central narrative mechanism of most episodes. The sequence is essentially the same in hundreds of cartoons: Popeye is overpowered, eats spinach, and wins. The association between spinach and strength was hammered home with the repetitive clarity of operant conditioning.

**The appeal of a nutritional anchor.** Spinach had been recognized as a nutritious vegetable in European culinary traditions since at least the Renaissance, when it arrived from Persia through Arab agricultural networks. It was associated with health and vigor through general cultural intuition before any scientific nutritional data existed. When nutritional science began generating iron content data in the 19th and early 20th century, spinach registered as iron-containing, which gave the cultural association a scientific gloss. The combination — traditional wisdom + cartoon enforcement + scientific-sounding number — was robust enough to survive multiple generations.

**Vegetables need a champion.** Getting children to eat vegetables is a perennial parenting challenge. Spinach's status as Popeye's food gave it a cultural sponsorship that made it uniquely useful for parental persuasion. "Eat your spinach — it'll make you strong like Popeye" is a more tractable argument than "eat a variety of vegetables for balanced micronutrient intake." The narrative function of spinach in child nutrition contexts likely reinforced the belief in adults who had used it persuasively and then implicitly committed to its truth.

**The decimal point myth satisfies curiosity.** When someone already doubts the "spinach is iron-rich" claim and encounters the decimal point story, it provides a clean causal explanation: we were all misled by a scientific error. This type of story — scientists made a mistake that fooled everyone — is culturally satisfying because it explains the widespread belief without attributing it to irrationality. The decimal point myth persists partly because it's a good story that appears to vindicate skeptics while explaining believers.

**The kernel of truth about vegetables.** Spinach is genuinely good for you. People who eat spinach regularly as part of varied diets tend to have better health outcomes than people who eat few vegetables. This epidemiological correlation is real. When people eat spinach and feel good, the cultural attribution to iron and strength finds apparent confirmation. The fact that the actual benefits come from vitamins, folate, antioxidants, and fiber — not primarily from iron — is invisible at the level of individual experience.

**Iron's role in strength.** The connection between iron and strength is not entirely fabricated: iron is essential for hemoglobin, the protein that carries oxygen in red blood cells. Iron deficiency anemia produces weakness, fatigue, and reduced physical performance. People who become less anemic (for any reason, including dietary iron) do experience strength restoration. This creates a real associative pathway: iron → strength. The error is applying this association to populations and dietary amounts where iron deficiency is not the limiting factor.

## 4. Verdict

**Distorted-but-grounded.**

Spinach is a nutritious vegetable. It contains iron. Eating vegetables is associated with better health. These are true. But the specific claim that spinach is *exceptionally* high in iron is false: it contains a modest 2.7mg/100g and several common foods contain substantially more. More importantly, the iron in spinach is poorly bioavailable due to oxalates — only 2-5% is absorbed, compared to 15-20% from meat sources. Spinach is genuinely not a reliable iron source, despite containing iron.

The "makes you strong" claim is not supported for people with normal iron status. Popeye's immediate superstrength is cartoon physics. The ecdysterone research from 2021 is an interesting exception that requires quantities far beyond normal dietary intake to produce effects — and awaits replication and regulatory review before it could support dietary advice.

The decimal point myth that supposedly explains the exaggeration is itself a myth — a fictional origin story for a real myth — which is unusual enough to deserve emphasis.

The bottom line: eat spinach because it's rich in vitamin K, folate, vitamin A, antioxidants, and fiber. Eat it because it's a good food. Don't eat it primarily for iron, and don't expect it to function like a performance supplement.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**The history of nutritional mythology.** Spinach's iron myth is a case study in how nutritional beliefs form and persist. The history of dietary science is strewn with confident claims that later turned out to be wrong, overextended, or complicated. Dietary fat causes heart disease; margarine is healthier than butter; carbohydrates make you fat; red wine is cardioprotective — each of these claims dominated public nutritional advice at some point and has since been significantly revised. The confidence with which nutritional claims are communicated in public culture rarely matches the uncertainty inherent in nutritional science.

Spinach's trajectory illustrates a specific pattern: a real property (vegetable contains nutrient) becomes culturally amplified to a false degree (vegetable is the best source of that nutrient and eating it causes measurable effects), then gets institutionalized in mass culture (Popeye), generating further amplification across decades. The scientific fact at the core becomes nearly invisible under the cultural elaboration. Correction, when it arrives, is partial: people stop believing the strongest version ("spinach has 35mg iron") without updating the moderate version ("spinach is an excellent iron source") or the behavioral version ("eat spinach for iron").

**Bioavailability as the hidden variable.** The bioavailability issue in spinach nutrition illustrates a problem that runs through much popular nutritional communication: the difference between a nutrient's total content and the amount actually absorbed and used by the body. Nutritional labels report total nutrient content; bioavailability depends on the food matrix, accompanying compounds, preparation methods, gut microbiome, individual genetics, and interaction with other dietary components. Spinach's oxalate content is the most cited example, but the phenomenon is widespread. Phytates in whole grains reduce iron and zinc absorption. Vitamin D without fat reduces absorption. The gap between "this food contains X" and "eating this food gives you X" is routinely larger than public communication acknowledges.

**The Popeye propaganda thesis.** A persistent variant of the spinach myth's origin story holds that the US spinach industry funded Popeye's creation or paid for product placement in the cartoons, and that the spinach industry saw real commercial benefit from the association. The commercial link between Popeye and the spinach industry is real but more complex than simple paid promotion: spinach industry associations did use Popeye imagery in marketing (with licensing), and industry representatives did cite the cartoon as having increased spinach consumption among children. Whether this constitutes "propaganda" or routine commercial co-optation of cultural phenomena is a frame question. What is documented is that spinach consumption did increase in the United States during the 1930s, and that Popeye was culturally associated with this; causation is difficult to establish.

**Vegetables and strength in the long run.** The Popeye narrative is nutritionally misleading in its mechanism (spinach → immediate superstrength via iron) but not entirely wrong in its direction (eat vegetables → be healthier and more robust over time). The epidemiological evidence for plant-heavy diets and long-term health outcomes is substantial. The mistake is the specific mechanism, the specific vegetable, and the specific timescale — not the general principle that diet affects physical capacity. This is what makes the spinach case "distorted-but-grounded" rather than simply false: a real principle (vegetables matter) is attached to a specific claim (spinach → strength via iron) that doesn't survive scrutiny.

**The role of meta-myths.** The decimal point origin story is a meta-myth — a myth about the origin of a myth. Meta-myths are particularly interesting because they spread via the skeptic community rather than the believer community. People who disbelieve the spinach iron claim are the ones who spread the decimal point story; it circulates in "did you know" formats that signal epistemic sophistication. The fact that the meta-myth is also false adds a layer: being skeptical of the spinach myth is correct; the specific explanation for why the myth exists (the decimal point error) is not correct. This means that correcting someone on the spinach claim often requires two corrections, and the second one — "also, the decimal point story is made up" — is likely to be received with greater skepticism because it sounds like defending the original myth.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed the "spinach makes you strong" claim in the spinach-iron framing, though it has covered adjacent food myths. The claim is complicated enough that it splits into at least three separate fact-checking questions:

1. **"Spinach is exceptionally high in iron"** — False or Misleading. At 2.7mg/100g, spinach is not exceptional compared to legumes, seeds, or many other foods; bioavailability compounds the issue.

2. **"Eating spinach makes you strong"** — False as stated. Iron does not directly produce muscular strength in people with adequate iron status; Popeye's physiology is cartoon physics. The ecdysterone finding is preliminary and requires extraordinary quantities.

3. **"The Popeye spinach thing came from a decimal point error"** — False. The decimal point story has no traceable primary source; Sutton (2010) found no evidence for it.

The fact-checking challenge here is managing the "debunking the debunking" dynamic. When fact-checkers correctly identify that the decimal point story is false, they risk appearing to defend the original spinach-iron claim. The correct framing is:

- The spinach-iron claim is exaggerated; spinach is nutritious but not primarily for iron, and its iron is poorly bioavailable.
- The Popeye/decimal-point origin story for this myth is itself a myth without traceable primary sourcing.
- Both corrections can be true simultaneously; the second doesn't rescue the first.

Good fact-checking on this topic resists the temptation to simply replace one satisfying story with another. The honest conclusion is that we don't know the precise origin of spinach's iron reputation; we know it circulated in nutritional literature before Popeye, and Popeye massively amplified it; the decimal point mechanism is not supported; and the underlying claim is exaggerated in both the absolute and bioavailability-adjusted senses.

The cultural lesson: not all food myths have tidy origin stories, and the desire for a tidy story can generate its own mythology. Sutton's investigation of the decimal point claim is itself a case study in rigorous provenance checking — the same intellectual virtue that good nutritional science requires when evaluating food claims. The spinach story is not just about spinach; it's about how food myths form, propagate, and resist correction even when their origin explanations are themselves fictional.

Eat the spinach. Just eat it for the right reasons.
