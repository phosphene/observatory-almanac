# Spinach's Reputation for Iron Content Comes from a Misplaced Decimal Point

## The Claim

In 1870, a German chemist named Emil von Wolff published an analysis of spinach's nutritional content, including its iron. In that analysis, he made a typographical error: a misplaced decimal point caused him to record spinach's iron content as ten times higher than it actually was. This error propagated through the nutritional literature for decades without correction. By the time scientists noticed the mistake, the inflated figure had become established canon, and spinach's iron-rich reputation — the basis for Popeye's spinach-fueled strength — was already fixed in popular culture.

This story has become a secondary classic of scientific folklore: not just a myth about spinach being healthy, but a cautionary tale about how a single careless error can propagate through scientific literature and shape public behavior. It appears in popular science books, magazines, and educational materials as an example of how scientific misinformation spreads.

---

## What's Actually True

The decimal point story is itself a myth — what researchers sometimes call a "meta-myth" or myth-about-a-myth. It was comprehensively investigated and debunked by British historian of science Mike Sutton in a 2010 paper published in the *Internet Journal of Criminology*.

**Sutton's investigation.** Sutton traced the decimal point claim back through the literature and found that it could not be verified. He examined the original sources attributed to von Wolff and found no evidence of a decimal error of the kind described. The iron figures in nineteenth-century German nutritional analyses do not show the pattern that a tenfold misplacement error would produce. More significantly, Sutton found no contemporary or near-contemporary correction to such an error — which is exactly what you would expect to find if a major numerical error had been identified and corrected at some point in the scientific literature. No such correction record exists because no such error existed.

Sutton identified the decimal point story as an example of how scientific folklore works: a plausible-sounding cautionary tale, well suited to the rhetorical purposes of anyone making points about the dangers of unchecked scientific error, circulates widely enough that it becomes "known" without ever being verified against primary sources. The story is memorable, morally satisfying, and structured as a lesson — exactly the properties that make scientific folklore propagate effectively.

**Spinach's actual iron situation.** While the decimal point story is false, there is a genuine scientific issue underlying spinach's iron reputation, and it is actually more nuanced and interesting than a simple error correction.

Spinach does contain iron. Its iron content is real — approximately 2.7 milligrams of iron per 100 grams of raw spinach, or around 3.6 mg per 100g when cooked (cooking reduces water content, concentrating the iron by weight). By raw weight, this is a respectable iron figure, and it partly explains why spinach acquired a reputation as an iron-rich food in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when nutritional science was less sophisticated about the distinction between total nutrient content and bioavailable nutrient content.

The problem with spinach as an iron source is not its iron content but its iron bioavailability. Iron in food comes in two forms: heme iron (bound to the hemoglobin and myoglobin in animal tissues) and non-heme iron (found in plant foods). Heme iron is efficiently absorbed by the human gastrointestinal system, with bioavailability typically estimated at 15–35% depending on the individual's iron status and other dietary factors. Non-heme iron, the form found in spinach and other plant foods, is absorbed far less efficiently, with bioavailability typically in the range of 2–20% and strongly dependent on the presence of absorption enhancers or inhibitors in the same meal.

Spinach contains a particularly potent iron absorption inhibitor: oxalic acid, which forms oxalate compounds that bind tightly to iron in the gut, rendering much of it unavailable for absorption. Oxalate-bound iron passes through the digestive system largely unabsorbed. This means that even the genuine iron in spinach delivers considerably less to the body than an equivalent amount of iron in animal foods would.

The upshot is that spinach is not an exceptional iron source when assessed on a bioavailable-iron-per-serving basis. Its reputation as an iron superfood — whatever its origins — overestimates spinach's practical contribution to iron nutrition. This underlying truth is valid, even though the decimal point story invented to explain it is false.

**Popeye's actual rationale.** The claim that Popeye's creators chose spinach because of an inflated iron figure — whether due to a decimal error or otherwise — is also not well supported by primary evidence. The creators of Popeye (Elzie Crisler Segar, who introduced the character in 1929) appear to have promoted spinach primarily for its vitamin A content, which was genuine and significant. Spinach is genuinely rich in vitamin A precursors (beta-carotene and other carotenoids), and early twentieth-century nutritional campaigns around spinach emphasized its vitamin content more broadly. The iron connection was amplified by popular retellings and cultural association rather than being the explicit logic of the original character design.

## Why People Believe This

The decimal point myth has properties that make it extraordinarily well-suited to propagation, and it has circulated as a cautionary tale in scientific and educational contexts for decades.

**The story serves a clear rhetorical purpose.** Scientific communicators and educators often want to illustrate that scientific knowledge can be built on errors, that such errors can propagate, and that even widely accepted figures should be verified against primary sources. The decimal point story is a perfect parable for this lesson. It is concrete, specific (a named chemist, a specific year, a specific error type), and consequential (the error shaped public behavior and popular culture). These properties make it ideal for use in lectures, books, and journalism, which is why it was used in these contexts long before anyone checked whether it was actually true.

**The underlying issue with spinach is real.** People who have heard the decimal point story often believe something correct: that spinach's iron reputation oversells its practical utility as an iron source. The bioavailability problem and the oxalate problem are real. The decimal point story is false, but it is a false explanation for a valid conclusion. This is a structurally unusual situation — the story is wrong, but the thing it is warning you about (don't rely on spinach for iron) is right. This ambiguity makes the myth harder to straightforwardly correct: saying "the decimal point story is wrong" risks being heard as "spinach is fine for iron," which is also wrong.

**Scientific folklore has weak verification norms.** Stories that circulate as cautionary tales, historical anecdotes, or illustrations of general principles are rarely verified against primary sources. The incentive structure of academic and popular writing rewards colorful, illustrative examples, not extensive primary source verification of every example. A writer making a point about the dangers of unchecked numerical errors in scientific literature has little reason to verify the specific example they are citing — particularly when it has appeared in many prior published works. The citation chain provides apparent verification without actual verification.

**The Popeye connection adds a pop-cultural anchor.** The spinach story is linked to an iconic cartoon character, which gives it cultural salience that a purely technical nutritional story would lack. Stories embedded in popular cultural reference points are stickier and more shareable than purely technical ones. The Popeye connection ensured that the spinach-iron story would spread far beyond nutritional science.

**The decimal error is a plausible mechanism.** Decimal point errors in numerical data are genuinely common. Anyone who has worked with numerical data knows that misplaced decimals happen, that they can persist undetected, and that they can have significant downstream consequences. The decimal error mechanism is therefore entirely plausible as a story about how misinformation propagates in science. Its plausibility makes verification feel unnecessary.

## Verdict

**Distorted but grounded.** The decimal point story is a myth-about-a-myth: the claim that spinach's iron reputation stems from an 1870 decimal error by Emil von Wolff is not supported by primary sources and was effectively debunked by Mike Sutton in 2010. However, the underlying conclusion — that spinach is not an exceptional iron source — is valid on different and more interesting grounds: non-heme iron bioavailability is poor, oxalates further inhibit absorption, and Popeye's actual promotional logic centered on vitamin A rather than iron. The spinach story is a case study not just in nutritional misconceptions but in how myths-about-myths propagate.

## The Wider Picture

The spinach story is remarkable because it operates at two levels simultaneously. At the first level is a misconception about nutrition: spinach is not as exceptional an iron source as its reputation suggests. At the second level is a misconception about the history of science: the decimal point story, which claims to explain the nutritional misconception, is itself factually wrong.

This structure — a myth explaining a myth — is unusual and reveals something interesting about how scientific folklore works. The decimal point story gained traction not because people wanted to believe that spinach was overhyped (the first-level truth) but because they wanted an instructive mechanism that would make the case against uncritical acceptance of scientific figures. The decimal point story provided that mechanism. Its function was not primarily informational but rhetorical: it served as a parable about scientific error.

Mike Sutton's investigation is itself an example of the kind of rigorous primary source verification that the decimal point story was urging as a lesson. The story's conclusion — verify scientific claims against primary sources — was confirmed by the investigation that debunked the story. This is either deeply ironic or perfectly fitting, depending on your perspective.

The bioavailability issue that actually undermines spinach's iron reputation is part of a larger story about how early nutritional science measured total nutrient content without adequate attention to absorption and bioavailability. Much of early twentieth-century nutritional science was conducted in terms of total measured content — how much of a nutrient is present in a food — without the biochemical tools to assess how much of that nutrient would actually be absorbed by the human body. Bioavailability turned out to be a crucial dimension that total-content measurements systematically missed.

This gap had practical consequences beyond spinach. The iron bioavailability of plant-based diets generally is lower than that of omnivorous diets, which has significant implications for populations relying heavily on plant foods. Strategies for improving iron bioavailability from plant sources — such as consuming vitamin C alongside iron-rich plant foods (vitamin C enhances non-heme iron absorption), or fermenting or soaking legumes to reduce phytate content (phytates also inhibit iron absorption) — represent practical applications of bioavailability science that were not available to earlier generations of nutritional scientists.

Oxalic acid specifically is interesting as a nutritional factor because it is not uniformly distributed across spinach leaves. Baby spinach tends to have lower oxalate content than mature spinach. Cooking spinach does reduce oxalate content somewhat, though not dramatically. The relationship between oxalates, iron, and human absorption continues to be studied, and the practical advice for iron nutrition has become considerably more nuanced than "eat foods high in iron" as a result.

The Popeye connection adds a dimension that intersects with the history of early twentieth-century nutritional campaigns. The promotion of specific foods as health-enhancing during the 1920s and 1930s drew on the emerging science of vitamins and minerals, and Popeye's spinach promotion was part of a broader context in which American children were being encouraged to eat vegetables through various public health and marketing campaigns. The selection of spinach, and the framing of its nutritional virtues, reflects the nutritional science priorities of that era as much as any specific claim about iron.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The spinach decimal point myth occupies an unusual position in the fact-checking landscape because it is a myth that circulates primarily in contexts that are already critically minded — scientific journalism, educational materials, popular science writing.

**Mike Sutton's 2010 paper** in the *Internet Journal of Criminology* is the primary scholarly debunking, and it is cited in many subsequent treatments. Sutton's approach — tracing the claim back through its citation chain to primary sources — is exactly the methodology the decimal point story was urging people to apply to nutritional claims, making it a particularly pointed form of factual correction.

**Snopes** has not published a dedicated entry on this specific myth, though the fact-checking community has generally absorbed Sutton's finding.

**Science communication challenges.** The main difficulty in communicating the correction is structural: the correction has to accomplish three distinct things simultaneously. It has to (1) debunk the decimal point story; (2) explain why spinach's iron reputation is overstated anyway, on different grounds; and (3) avoid giving the false impression that spinach has no nutritional value. Spinach is genuinely nutritious — it is high in vitamin K, vitamin A precursors, folate, vitamin C, and manganese, as well as containing real if poorly bioavailable iron. The corrective message is: "the decimal point story is false, spinach's iron bioavailability is genuinely poor for different reasons, but spinach is still a nutritious food." This is more complex than a simple "myth busted" verdict allows.

The spinach story is also useful as a teaching case in research methodology, precisely because Sutton's investigation demonstrates the importance of going back to primary sources rather than relying on citation chains. Every instance of the decimal point story in secondary literature cites a prior secondary source that cites another secondary source. The chain has no primary source terminus. Recognizing this pattern and taking the methodological step of verifying the claim against original documents is what produced the correction — and is exactly the kind of epistemic practice that any good scientific education should cultivate.
