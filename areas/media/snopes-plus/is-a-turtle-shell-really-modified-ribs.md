## The Claim

In mid-June 2021, a graphic shared by Ceri Weber — a post-doctoral researcher at UC San Diego specialising in turtles — went viral on Twitter, accumulating nearly 10,000 retweets within days. The graphic illustrated the claim that a turtle's shell is not an external structure added over or beside the animal's body, like a snail's shell or a crab's carapace, but is instead formed from the modified and broadened ribs of the turtle itself, fused together with the vertebral column and the shoulder girdle bones in a structure unique among living vertebrates. In short: the turtle's shell is on the inside of the body plan, not the outside, because it is the body plan.

Snopes journalist Madison Dapcevich, writing on June 20, 2021, confirmed the claim as "True." She cited a 2013 study published in *Current Biology* and referenced the fossil discoveries of *Odontochelys semitestacea* in China (2008) and *Eunotosaurus africanus* in South Africa, which together illuminate the evolutionary sequence by which turtle ribs expanded and fused over deep geological time to produce the shell we recognise today.

## What's Actually True

The turtle's shell — technically consisting of two structural components, the dorsal carapace and the ventral plastron — is not an external bony structure added onto the outside of a normal tetrapod body. It is formed from the fusion of modified skeletal elements that, in most other vertebrates, remain internal. The dorsal carapace develops from the lateral fusion and dorsal broadening of the ribs, fused with an expanded series of spinal vertebrae and dermal bone elements. The ventral plastron arises from the ventral ribcage, clavicles, and interclavicle, also fused with dermal ossifications.

The consequence of this developmental architecture is anatomically striking and initially counterintuitive: the turtle's shoulder girdle — the set of bones that, in other tetrapods, sits on the *outside* of the rib cage — sits on the *inside* of the shell in turtles. This is the reverse of the configuration in every other tetrapod lineage. A turtle cannot be removed from its shell any more than a human could be removed from their ribcage; the shell is the ribcage (Rieppel, 2013, *Current Biology*).

The developmental biology underpinning this arrangement has been characterised in detail by a series of studies across the 2000s and 2010s. Chuong et al. and subsequent workers identified the role of a specific developmental signalling pathway, involving the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) cascade, in producing the unusual lateral expansion of turtle ribs. In normal tetrapods, ribs grow downward and then curve anteriorly to meet the sternum. In turtles, the ribs grow outward and posteriorly, then flatten and expand into the broad bony plates that form the carapace. This change in rib growth trajectory is established during embryological development and represents a major rerouting of a conserved developmental programme (Nagashima et al., 2009, *Science*).

The fossil record provides a temporal dimension to this transition. Two key specimens bracket the evolutionary sequence. *Eunotosaurus africanus*, from the Permian of South Africa, is estimated at approximately 260 million years old and has distinctively broadened ribs without a fully fused dorsal carapace — it represents an early-stage version of the body plan in which rib expansion has begun but shell fusion has not completed. Lyson et al. (2013, *Current Biology*) established *Eunotosaurus* as an early representative of the turtle lineage, using a combination of morphological and phylogenetic analysis, and proposed it as a "bridging taxon" that fills the approximately 30–55 million year gap in the turtle fossil record that had previously troubled evolutionary accounts of shell origins.

*Odontochelys semitestacea*, from the Triassic of China, discovered in 2008 and dated to approximately 220 million years ago (Li et al., 2008, *Nature*), provides the other anchor point. This animal had a fully-formed plastron (the ventral shell plate) but only a partial carapace — the dorsal broadened ribs and vertebrae are present but not yet fused into the completed dorsal shell. This intermediate condition suggests that the plastron evolved first, and the dorsal carapace completed later — a sequence that runs counter to earlier hypotheses that had predicted the reverse order of appearance.

## Why People Believe This

The viral success of Ceri Weber's tweet taps into a specific experiential context that makes the "turtle shell = ribs" claim emotionally satisfying as a revelation: almost everyone has spent time holding a turtle or looking at turtle-shaped objects, and almost everyone has an intuition — informal, usually unexamined — that the shell is somewhat like a snail's shell or a hermit crab's borrowed shell: a home the animal lives inside, potentially separable from the animal. The idea that the turtle literally cannot leave its shell because the shell is its skeleton produces a genuine "wait, what?" response.

This response is a sub-category of what cognitive scientists call the "counterintuitive-true" surprise: the fact is not just interesting but actively contradicts a prior assumption the listener was unaware they were making. Weber's graphic, and the subsequent Snopes confirmation, work as correctives not to a false belief held with conviction, but to a default assumption held without examination. That kind of correction is particularly satisfying because it requires no relinquishment of prior identity or allegiance — the reader never argued for anything, they just never thought it through — and produces clean resolution.

The timing of the virality — mid-June 2021 — was driven primarily by Weber's own communication strategy (targeting Twitter's science community) rather than by any exogenous news event. This makes it a case of bottom-up scientific communication: a researcher sharing a compelling piece of evolutionary biology in an accessible format, directly to a public platform, without a press release or institutional announcement, and achieving substantial reach through the organic mechanisms of retweet and follows-within-network. The episode is studied in science communication research as an example of scientist-led public engagement with genuine reach, as opposed to press-release-mediated institutional communication.

## The Epistemic Gap

The Snopes entry is compact and primarily functions to confirm the biological claim and provide the fossil record context. The gap_category here is "developmental-evo-devo-depth" because the most intellectually generative material — the developmental biology of how the turtle body plan is actually constructed, and what its origins tell us about vertebrate evo-devo — is beyond the scope of what the fact-check format covers.

The turtle shell is one of the most studied problems in vertebrate evolutionary developmental biology (evo-devo) because it represents a major morphological innovation with a clear stratigraphic record and accessible modern developmental tractability. You can watch turtle embryos develop in culture. You can apply pharmacological agents to perturb BMP signalling pathways and observe what happens to rib growth trajectories. You can compare gene expression patterns between turtle embryos and other reptile embryos at comparable developmental stages and identify where the developmental programmes diverge. This experimental accessibility makes turtle shell evolution a model system for studying how major morphological novelties originate — not just in turtles, but in vertebrates generally.

The key developmental finding — that turtle ribs grow outward and posteriorly rather than downward and ventrally, due to modifications in the body wall musculature and signalling environment during embryogenesis — establishes that the shell did not arise by "adding a shell" to an existing tetrapod body plan, but by reprogramming an existing developmental pathway (rib growth) to produce a dramatically different output. Burke (1989, *Development*) established the developmental evidence for this reprogramming using the "carapacial ridge" — a thickening of the lateral body wall in turtle embryos that acts as an organiser for the outward rib growth — as the key developmental innovation separating turtle from non-turtle body plans.

This developmental architecture has implications for how we understand the relationship between genes, development, and evolutionary change. The turtle shell suggests that major morphological innovations can arise through relatively simple developmental reprogramming events — a small change in the spatial expression of a signalling molecule shifts the growth trajectory of ribs from "downward" to "outward," and a radical new morphological structure follows as a downstream consequence. This resonates with the broader evo-devo literature on the role of developmental bias and developmental constraint in directing evolutionary change (Hallgrímsson & Hall, 2011, *Epigenetics: Linking Genotype and Phenotype in Development and Evolution*, University of California Press).

## Broader Scientific Context

Turtles (Order Testudines) are the only living members of Archelosauria, a clade that also includes archosaurs (crocodiles and birds). Their distinctive shell — carapace above, plastron below — is the defining apomorphy of the order and has persisted structurally stable for over 200 million years, making turtles one of the most morphologically conservative of all vertebrate lineages. The apparent stasis of turtle shell morphology over this timescale is itself an evolutionary puzzle: despite dramatic climate changes, continental rearrangements, mass extinction events, and the diversification of major predator lineages, the turtle body plan has not been substantially modified.

Numerous living turtle species have been described — estimates range from approximately 350 to 360 described species — and they span aquatic, semi-aquatic, and fully terrestrial ecologies. Despite this ecological diversity, the fundamental shell architecture of carapace and plastron connected by lateral bridges remains uniform. The shell functions as defence, thermoregulation support, and body plan organiser simultaneously. Morris (2011, *Developmental Biology*) notes that the co-option of ribs and vertebrae as shell components produces downstream developmental constraints: the thoracic musculature of turtles cannot expand the chest cavity as it does in other amniotes, which is why turtles cannot breathe by expanding their ribs (as mammals do with the thoracic diaphragm), and instead use a specialised muscular system to move the organs against the rigid shell wall, creating pressure differentials that drive gas exchange.

This breathing constraint — itself a secondary consequence of building a shell from ribs — makes turtles anatomically unique among amniotes in their respiratory mechanics. It is a classic case in evo-devo of a major innovation producing evolutionary trade-offs: the shell buys extraordinary defensive advantage and structural stability, but at the cost of the conventional respiratory mechanism available to all other amniote lineages.

## Verdict and Reflection

The Snopes verdict of "True" is fully warranted. Turtle shells are formed from modified and fused ribs, vertebrae, and shoulder girdle bones. The developmental and palaeontological evidence for this conclusion is robust, multi-stranded, and spans more than a century of scientific investigation.

The claim's viral appeal is a product of its genuinely counterintuitive quality — it corrects an assumption most people didn't know they were making. Ceri Weber's tweet performed an important science communication function: getting a technical result about vertebrate evo-devo into a form that a non-specialist audience could absorb and transmit, without sacrificing the core biological content.

The fuller context — evo-devo mechanisms, developmental constraints, the unusual position of the shoulder girdle, the evolutionary puzzle of morphological stasis — is where the profound work lives. That context is not refutation of the claim; it is its elaboration. The shell-is-ribs fact is a gateway into one of the genuinely fascinating chapters in the story of how vertebrate bodies are built.
