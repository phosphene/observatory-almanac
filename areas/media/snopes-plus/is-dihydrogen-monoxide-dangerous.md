# Is Dihydrogen Monoxide Dangerous?

## 1. The Claim

A document circulating since at least the early 1990s — appearing first in printed student publications, then propagating through email chains and early internet forums before taking residence on social media — presents a public health warning about a chemical called "dihydrogen monoxide," abbreviated DHMO.

The warning is structured to resemble legitimate regulatory or scientific risk communication. It typically includes claims of the following kind: Dihydrogen monoxide has been found in concentrations above safe thresholds in water supplies across the country. It is a component of acid rain and is implicated in thousands of deaths annually. In its gaseous form it causes severe burns. In its solid form, prolonged contact causes tissue necrosis. It is present in cancerous tumours. It has been found in the excised tissue of every terminal cancer patient ever tested. Industrial plants use it in enormous quantities. It is not regulated by any federal agency. Multiple attempts to restrict its use have failed due to industry lobbying. The document sometimes concludes with a petition or survey question: "Do you believe dihydrogen monoxide should be regulated or banned?"

Dihydrogen monoxide is water. H₂O. Two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom. The chemical name is constructed from valid Greek and Latin roots — "di" (two), "hydrogen" (the element), "monoxide" (a compound with one oxygen atom) — and is technically defensible, if unusual. The more standard systematic IUPAC name for H₂O is "oxidane," but "dihydrogen monoxide" is not incorrect.

Every factual claim in the standard DHMO document is, in a specific and narrow sense, accurate: water is present in acid rain; steam burns; ice causes frostbite; drowning kills thousands annually; water is found in tumour tissue; industrial facilities use it at enormous scale. The hoax does not require fabricated facts to achieve its effect. It requires only a chemical name its audience does not recognise and the selective deployment of contextless hazard information.

The question "Is dihydrogen monoxide dangerous?" therefore has a genuine and interesting answer that depends entirely on how "dangerous" is being evaluated and to whom — and the exploration of that answer reveals important things about how risk, language, and cognition interact.

**Versions and variants:** The DHMO framing has proliferated in multiple forms. It appears as sincere-seeming petition text, as a deliberate classroom exercise, as a rhetorical device in debates about chemical regulation, and occasionally as the basis for news items testing public scientific literacy. It has been presented to high school students, college students, members of the public at county fairs, and online survey respondents. In most documented deployments, a substantial fraction of respondents indicated support for banning DHMO before learning what it was.

## 2. What's Actually True

To answer the surface question: yes, water is dangerous under specific conditions. No, this does not mean it should be banned or treated as a novel chemical hazard.

**The chemical identity.** Dihydrogen monoxide is water. The molecular formula H₂O expresses two hydrogen atoms (H₂) covalently bonded to one oxygen atom (O). The name "dihydrogen monoxide" applies the same naming convention used for nitrogen monoxide (NO) and carbon dioxide (CO₂) to water. It is technically valid. The usual IUPAC name is "oxidane" (per 2013 recommendations of the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry), but "dihydrogen monoxide" has historical currency in chemistry education and parody alike. The American Chemistry Society and cognate bodies have addressed the DHMO phenomenon primarily in the context of science literacy education rather than as a genuine hazard concern.

**The individual hazard claims are accurate.** Each claim in a standard DHMO document can be traced to real properties of water:

*Drowning and inhalation risk.* Aspiration of liquid water — the entry of water into the lungs — blocks oxygen uptake and causes hypoxic death. This is drowning. The World Health Organization estimates approximately 236,000 drowning deaths annually worldwide, making unintentional drowning a leading cause of death globally, particularly among children under five (WHO Global Report on Drowning, 2014). The statistic is real, terrifying in aggregate, and usually omitted from the DHMO document's deliberate withholding of the word "drowning."

*Thermal burns from steam.* Water in its gaseous state — steam — carries substantial thermal energy and causes severe burns on contact with skin. Steam burns in industrial and culinary contexts are well-documented occupational and domestic hazards. Li, Liu, & Tan (2018, Burns, 44(5), 1044–1053) reviewed scalding injuries across clinical registries and confirmed steam as a significant contributor to burn injury, particularly in industrial settings.

*Frostbite from ice.* Sustained contact with ice below the tissue-freezing threshold causes ice crystal formation in extracellular fluid, cell membrane disruption, and ischemic damage — frostbite. The mechanism is described in Imray, Grieve, & Dhillon (2009, Lancet, 373(9673), 1748–1759) in a clinical review of cold injury. This too is factually accurate.

*Acid rain.* Water is the primary solvent carrier in acid rain. Acid rain — precipitation with pH below 5.6, resulting from the dissolution of sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides in atmospheric moisture — does cause documented environmental harm, including acidification of lakes and rivers, damage to forest ecosystems, and corrosion of built structures. The chemistry is described in Likens, Wright, Galloway, & Butler (1979, Scientific American, 241(4), 43–51), the landmark study that brought acid rain to public and regulatory attention.

*Presence in tumour tissue.* Water is present in tumour tissue as it is in all tissue; the human body is approximately 60% water by weight. The DHMO claim that it has been found in the tissue of cancer patients is trivially true in the way that noting "dihydrogen monoxide has been found in the blood of all murder victims" would be trivially true.

*Industrial use.* Water is used at industrial scale in cooling systems, hydroelectric generation, chemical manufacturing, food processing, and countless other applications. Failures in industrial water systems — steam pipe ruptures, cooling tower leaks, dam failures — can and do cause deaths and environmental harm.

All of this is accurate. None of it, presented as the facts are in a DHMO warning, would allow a reader unfamiliar with the chemical name to correctly identify the subject.

**What is false.** The false element is not any individual claim but the implicit conclusion structure: that this catalogue of hazards constitutes grounds for regulatory concern about a substance distinct from the things audiences already know to be managed (steam systems, flood risk, industrial cooling). The implied meta-claim — that DHMO is a novel, unregulated chemical deserving public alarm — is not only false but inverted. Water is the most carefully monitored substance in public infrastructure, subject to extensive national and international regulation of everything from drinking water purity standards to industrial effluent composition, dam safety engineering, and flood management systems.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The DHMO hoax succeeds not because its audiences are unintelligent or undereducated but because it exploits cognitive mechanisms that operate reliably across populations. Identifying these mechanisms is more valuable than diagnosing gullibility.

**Nomenclature as epistemological gate.** The first and most important mechanism is the unfamiliar chemical name. When a reader encounters "dihydrogen monoxide," they have no prior mental model to apply. The absence of prior knowledge triggers a precautionary inference: "I do not know what this is; someone is warning me about it; perhaps I should be worried." This inference is heuristically reasonable —  precautionary responses to unfamiliar hazards generally serve survival — but it is exploitable precisely because it functions independently of the underlying reality of the named substance.

Psychologists Slovic, Fischhoff, & Lichtenstein (1982, in Judgment Under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases, Cambridge University Press, pp. 463–492) established that unfamiliarity is a primary contributor to perceived risk, independent of objective hazard measures. Their psychometric risk research found that laypeople reliably assign higher risk to novel, poorly understood hazards than to familiar ones with equivalent or higher objective harm rates. Chemical names, particularly systematic ones that resemble industrial compound nomenclature (methyl isocyanate, polychlorinated biphenyls, formaldehyde), carry a strong unfamiliarity signal for non-specialist audiences.

The DHMO hoax weaponises this cognitive architecture with surgical precision. "Dihydrogen monoxide" sounds like the kind of compound that would appear on a hazardous materials list. The audience's risk-perception system is activated by the form of the name before the content of the hazard claims is even evaluated. By the time the list of accurate hazards is encountered, the reader is already primed to treat them as alarming rather than as the familiar properties of a substance they consume daily.

**The structure of authority performs credibility.** The DHMO document mimics the structure of legitimate safety and regulatory communication: chemical name, hazard summary, exposure pathways, usage data, conclusion and call to action. This format, long established in Chemical Safety Data Sheets (SDSs) and environmental impact reports, carries implicit institutional authority. Research by Cialdini (2001, Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion, Allyn & Bacon) on authority as a persuasion principle shows that the formal characteristics of authoritative documents — systematic organisation, technical language, data references — activate compliance responses in audiences who have learned to treat such formats as credible. Petty & Cacioppo (1986, Communication and Persuasion: Central and Peripheral Routes to Attitude Change, Springer) demonstrated in their Elaboration Likelihood Model that peripheral cues to credibility (source authority, presentation format) exert strong influence on attitude formation when motivation or ability to process content deeply is limited. Many readers of forwarded DHMO warnings are not deeply processing the content; they are pattern-matching the format to "official warning" and responding accordingly.

**Selective hazard presentation as epistemic manipulation.** The deeper epistemic mechanism is not simply the unfamiliar name but the structured omission of context. Every claim in the DHMO document is technically accurate, but each is stripped of the contextual information that would allow risk calibration: the quantities at which water is harmful; the specific exposure routes required; the comparison to naturally occurring background levels; the fact that these same hazards apply to familiar, regulated substances the audience does not fear. Kahneman (2011, Thinking, Fast and Slow, Farrar Straus & Giroux, pp. 199–207) describes this as "what you see is all there is" (WYSIATI): the cognitive system that produces fast evaluative judgments works with whatever information is present and does not generate adjustments for absent information. The DHMO document exploits WYSIATI by presenting accurate hazard information and providing no context for its calibration.

This is what makes the DHMO case genuinely important as an epistemic phenomenon: it demonstrates that **the assembly of true statements can constitute a lie**. The individual facts are factual. The conclusion they are designed to produce — that DHMO is a chemical hazard distinct from known safe substances — is not. This gap between local factual accuracy and global impression management is not unique to parody; it is the architecture of a substantial fraction of real-world health and environmental misinformation.

**The "Zohnerism" in its natural habitat.** In 1997, fourteen-year-old Nathan Zohner of Eagle Rock Junior High conducted a science fair project by distributing the standard DHMO petition to fifty of his classmates. Forty-three supported a ban. Zohner titled his project "How Gullible Are We?" and won first place in the environmental science category of his regional science fair. The episode was widely reported, and the science communicator David Emery proposed the term "Zohnerism" to describe the practice of using accurate facts to support misleading conclusions. Whether the term has stuck is less important than what the study demonstrates: that even among a sample of adolescents with access to normal educational resources, unfamiliar nomenclature and credible-format hazard presentation is sufficient to produce false risk assessments at high rates. Adult baseline performance in similar tasks is not reliably better. Lichtenstein & Slovic (1973, Journal of Experimental Psychology, 101(1), 76–83) demonstrated that frequency judgments made under uncertainty are systematically distorted by the availability and vividness of presented information, independent of statistical accuracy.

**Iterative context-stripping across forwarding chains.** The DHMO document acquired a second deployment context on the internet. What may have originated as a clearly framed satirical exercise lost its satirical framing through forwarding. By the time a given copy arrives in a new reader's email or social media feed, the original framing — written as a joke or classroom exercise — has often been stripped away, replaced by a message that looks like an earnest concern. Research on what Ferrara, Varol, Davis, Menczer, & Flammini (2016, Communications of the ACM, 59(7), 96–104) describe as the amplification dynamics of social media shows that decontextualisation is not an accident of transmission — it is selected for, because shared-without-context items produce higher engagement responses (alarm, concern, curiosity) than items clearly marked as parody.

## 4. Verdict

**FALSE** — as a genuine hazard claim.

The claim that dihydrogen monoxide is a dangerous chemical warranting regulatory attention or banning is false, in the specific sense that the implied meta-claim — that there exists some industrial or environmental chemical called DHMO that is not adequately regulated and constitutes a novel public health risk — does not correspond to any real situation. The substance is water, which is the most carefully regulated substance in the public water system and which is hazardous only in specific exposure conditions that are universally understood and managed through existing infrastructure.

The accompanying factual claims are individually accurate. Water does kill people when aspirated. Steam does burn. Ice does cause frostbite. These facts are not in dispute. What is false is the framing structure that positions these facts as arguments for novel regulatory action. The verdict classification is: **fictional hazard identification grounded in factual properties of a familiar substance**.

This belongs in the "distorted-but-grounded" epistemic gap category because the component knowledge is real and the rhetorical architecture of the document deliberately exploits the gap between factual accuracy and correct global inference.

One nuance deserves note: "Is dihydrogen monoxide dangerous?" admits a technically accurate "yes" answer: water is dangerous under specific conditions, as demonstrated by drowning statistics. A fully accurate answer is more modulated — "water (like all substances) has conditions under which it causes harm; there is no novel chemical hazard warranting regulatory action beyond the well-developed infrastructure already governing water safety." The hoax's value as a pedagogical instrument lies precisely in how it forces this distinction between local claim accuracy and the correctness of the overall evaluative frame.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The DHMO hoax occupies an unusual position in the media literacy canon: it is simultaneously a joke, a test, a pedagogical device, and an inadvertent demonstration of authentic misinformation architecture. Understanding its mechanics has direct implications for how we evaluate a wide class of real-world health and environmental claims.

**The structural technique is widely deployed in real misinformation.** The combination of accurate individual facts, alarming technical nomenclature, and omitted context that makes DHMO effective is not an invention of the hoax. It is a structural feature of a large category of genuine misinformation:

*Vaccine ingredient lists* present formaldehyde, thimerosal, polysorbate 80, and aluminium salts under their full chemical names without quantities, without comparison to naturally occurring exposure levels, and without context for their regulatory evaluation. The individual chemical names are real; the implied conclusion — that vaccines contain a witch's brew of uniquely dangerous industrial chemicals — is not. Formaldehyde, for instance, is produced endogenously by human metabolic processes; the quantity in vaccine preparations is negligible compared to naturally circulating levels. Offit & Jew (2003, Pediatrics, 112(6), 1394–1397) reviewed this class of vaccine misinformation and documented how the technique functions.

*Anti-GMO hazard communication* employs similar framing, presenting the presence of specific measured compounds in GMO crops as evidence of danger without comparison to organic equivalents or established safe thresholds. Miller & Conko (2001, Regulation, 24(2), 40–46) reviewed regulatory frameworks and found that the selective chemistry of anti-GMO communications consistently relied on DHMO-type structural omissions.

*Environmental contamination journalism* sometimes reproduces the structure inadvertently when naming trace contaminants found in drinking water or consumer products by their full chemical designations without context for typical background levels or established harm thresholds. This does not make the journalism dishonest, but it does create conditions for DHMO-type misframing in audience inference.

**The nomenclature gap as structural vulnerability.** What DHMO exposes is a deep structural vulnerability in public scientific literacy: the inability of most individuals to assess the meaning of systematic chemical or molecular nomenclature. This is not a failure of intelligence or curiosity — systematic chemical nomenclature was not designed for public communication, and there is no reasonable expectation that non-specialists should decode it. But it creates a category of epistemically predatory communication in which the very precision of scientific language can be weaponised to produce false impressions. The solution is not widespread chemistry education (though that helps at the margins) but the development of robust contextualising habits: "Who is making this claim? Is a comparative dose-response context provided? What regulatory framework governs this substance? Is the named chemical identifiable through a simple search?"

**The value of the hoax as a teaching instrument.** Science educators including Norris, Osborne, & Ratcliffe (2003, Research in Science Education, 33(1), 1–25) have documented that engaging students with the DHMO exercise produces measurable improvements in critical evaluation of health and science claims, specifically in the recognition of selective framing and the importance of contextual calibration. The "aha moment" — when the student realises that dihydrogen monoxide is water — operates as a visceral demonstration that the student's evaluative process failed, and that identification of the failure is more valuable than simple exposure to correct information. This aligns with research by Lewandowsky, Ecker, Seifert, Schwarz, & Cook (2012, Psychological Science in the Public Interest, 13(3), 106–131) showing that misinformation correction is most durable when it provides a coherent causal account of why the original belief was produced and how the evaluative process was compromised.

**The limits of fact-based correction.** One counter-intuitive implication of the DHMO case is that presenting accurate information about DHMO after someone has already accepted the hoax framing is not sufficient for correction. If a person has already categorised DHMO as a dangerous chemical, telling them it is water does not automatically reorganise all the associated beliefs. Research by Thorson (2016, Political Communication, 33(3), 460–480) on the "continued influence effect of misinformation" shows that even after explicit correction, initially accepted false information continues to affect subsequent reasoning. For DHMO specifically, the correction is powerful enough that it usually overrides the prior belief — but the mechanism of correction (the simplicity and memorability of "it's water") is specific to this case and does not generalise to more complex misinformation scenarios.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The DHMO hoax presents fact-checkers with an unusual challenge: the target claim is not conventionally false in the way that most fact-check targets are. There is no specific factual error to identify and refute. The deception operates at the level of framing, nomenclature, and inference rather than at the level of individual claim accuracy. Most fact-checking methodologies are designed to evaluate the accuracy of specific factual claims, not the correctness of the evaluative frames constructed from accurate claims.

**The Snopes history.** Snopes addressed the DHMO claim in its earlier years, and the claim is identified across their archives in entries addressing the compound's history, the satirical intent, and the identity of the substance. The site consistently presents the hoax as a deliberate piece of satire and media literacy demonstration rather than a serious deception. This framing is accurate but may not fully capture the extent to which the document circulates unsatirically in contexts stripped of its original pedagogical intent. As of the time of this writing, Snopes does not maintain a comprehensive, stand-alone DHMO fact-check entry (snopes_url: not-addressed), but the claim recurs in discussions of water regulation hoaxes and science literacy exercises.

**The limits of verdict categories.** The standard fact-checker verdicts — True, False, Misleading, Mixture, Satire — map imperfectly onto the DHMO structure. Calling the document "False" elides the fact that every individual claim is accurate. Calling it "True" would be absurd as a communication of its deceptive potential. "Satire" is partially accurate for the original iterations but misrepresents the many copies circulating without satirical intent or framing. "Misleading" is most accurate from a structural standpoint but underspecifies the mechanism. The DHMO case illustrates how the claim-type taxonomies of professional fact-checking, which were developed for a world of straightforwardly false or true political statements, are not well-calibrated for parody-as-misinformation or structurally-accurate misleading content.

**The teaching opportunity.** Several fact-checking organisations — including First Draft, the MediaWise program, and the News Literacy Project — have incorporated DHMO into their media literacy curricula as a canonical example of the selective-framing technique. The approach treats the hoax not as an information retrieval problem (did you know DHMO is water?) but as a process demonstration — working through the specific mechanisms by which the framing produces its effect, and identifying the evaluative questions that would have short-circuited that effect.

Those questions are worth stating explicitly:
1. Can this chemical name be looked up in a public molecular database? (Yes: PubChem entry for water lists "dihydrogen monoxide" as a synonym.)
2. Does the hazard document provide dose-response context? (No: doses, concentrations, and comparison baselines are systematically absent.)
3. What regulatory status does this substance have? (Extensive: water quality is regulated by the EPA Safe Drinking Water Act, EU Water Framework Directive, and cognate frameworks in every developed jurisdiction.)
4. Does the list of hazards apply identically to a substance the reader considers safe? (Yes: every property described applies equally to water in its familiar form.)

These questions do not require chemistry knowledge to ask or answer. They require only the habit of contextual calibration — the disposition to seek comparative and regulatory context before treating a hazard list as grounds for alarm. That habit is the practical takeaway from the DHMO exercise, and it transfers across the full range of technically-accurate-but-misleading health and environmental claims that constitute a significant fraction of real-world health misinformation.
