# Frying Food Sounds Are Used as Rain Sound Effects in Movies


## 1. The Claim

Few pieces of film trivia spread as reliably as the assertion that the sound of rain in movies actually comes from frying bacon — or frying food more broadly. The claim circulates in popular media, trivia books, behind-the-scenes documentaries, and the kind of dinner-party conversation that traffics in pleasantly counterintuitive facts. It evokes a satisfying image of old Hollywood ingenuity: before digital audio libraries, a sound engineer wielding a cast-iron skillet achieved in a studio kitchen what microphones in the field could not reliably capture.

The versions of the claim vary in their specifics. Some name bacon as the specific food; others say frying oil or frying anything in a pan. Some claim it is a historical technique from radio drama or early sound cinema; others present it as an ongoing and current practice in professional film production. A small number of accounts attribute the technique specifically to certain legendary sound designers — names like Walter Murch, Ben Burtt, or unnamed Golden Age studio engineers appear depending on the teller. In most popular forms, the claim is offered as a statement of current practice: this is how rain sounds are made in movies, full stop.

The acoustic logic of the claim is intuitive once stated: frying food produces a broad-spectrum stochastic noise — a continuous, statistically random distribution of many small pops, crackles, and hisses — that shares fundamental spectral and temporal characteristics with the sound of rain striking various surfaces. Both consist of many small, randomly-timed impact events whose combined effect produces a continuous noise floor with particular spectral coloration. The ear, habituated to recognising rain by these acoustic signatures rather than by any individual raindrop sound, is susceptible to being convinced.

But is it true? And if it is, in what sense and under what conditions?


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim has a genuine and well-documented historical basis in both radio drama and early cinema sound design, and it continues to represent a legitimate foley and sound design technique that professional practitioners actively defend. Multiple foley artists have confirmed using frying bacon or frying oil as a rain effect in interviews and sound design literature. The technique is real.

The complication is that the claim is most often stated in a form that implies universality or exclusivity — "this is how rain sounds are made" — whereas the more accurate formulation is that this is one documented method among several, whose use depends on the specific type of rain being simulated, the production context, whether digital libraries are available, and the aesthetic choices of the individual sound designer.

Ben Burtt, the Academy Award-winning sound designer known primarily for his work on the Star Wars franchise, has been credited in numerous secondary sources with confirming frying sounds as a rain technique. Foley artist Gary Hecker — one of the most frequently cited practitioners in press coverage of the craft — has described using frying oil sounds in talks at film industry events. Documentary coverage of Foley recording for theatrical productions has periodically shown practitioners demonstrating the technique.

However, the modern sound design landscape has changed significantly since the technique was most commonly employed. Digital sample libraries — commercial collections like Sound Ideas, Soundscapes Pro, and proprietary studio libraries maintained by major post-production houses — include thousands of recorded rain samples in every variety: light drizzle, heavy downpour, rain on glass, rain on foliage, rain on tin roofs, urban rain, and so on. The availability of these libraries means that frying food is now a foley artist's first-principles solution to a problem that professional sound designers can often address with a library click.

This does not make the claim false — it makes it contextual. In independent production, in foley demonstrations, in situations where a specific textural quality is needed and library sounds feel too literal or too polished, frying remains a viable and used technique. The claim is True in its historical dimension and partially true in its contemporary dimension: it is employed, but it is not universal, and it competes with alternatives that did not exist when the technique was developed.


## 3. The Acoustic Science of Simulated Rain

The epistemic gap in most fact-checks and popular accounts of this claim is that they discuss the technique as a piece of film lore without explaining why it works — and the acoustic explanation is itself the most interesting and generative part of the story. Understanding the physics makes several things immediately clear: why the technique functions at all, why it works better for some types of rain than others, why it can fail, and why the variety of frying sounds used matters as much as the basic technique.

Rain is, acoustically speaking, a stochastic point process: a large number of individual impact events — raindrops striking surfaces — that occur at randomly distributed times, producing a composite noise that our perceptual system integrates into a continuous texture. The acoustic character of rain depends on several variables: drop size, which determines impact energy; falling velocity; the acoustic properties of the struck surface (foliage, glass, soil, water); and the atmospheric propagation path (the sound of rain in the open air includes not just impact sounds but also sound produced by drops splashing in puddles, wind-entrained water droplets, and the acoustic modulation produced by falling precipitation itself).

The acoustic spectrum of rain is broadly characterised by a concentrations of energy in the range of approximately 1 kHz to 20 kHz, with spectral peaks that vary by surface type and drop size. Rain on leaf canopy produces a somewhat different spectral signature than rain on glass or rain on a tarmac surface, because each surface has different mechanical impedance and resonant properties that shape how the impact energy is radiated acoustically (Shaw, E.A.G., 1986, "Noise environments outdoors and the effects of community noise exposure," *Noise Control Engineering Journal*, 26(3), 100-112; for rain specifically, see also Laville, F., et al., 2004, "Vibrational response of a plate to a rain-like excitation," *Journal of Sound and Vibration*, 275, 1-21).

Frying food produces noise through an entirely different physical mechanism — the rapid nucleation and collapse of vapour bubbles at the boundaries of water droplets in contact with hot oil or fat — but the resulting acoustic signal has striking similarities to rain in its statistical structure. The frying process consists of many small, randomly-timed events (individual bubble nucleation and collapse events) that sum to a continuous noise floor. The spectral content of frying — concentrated in roughly the 2 kHz to 15 kHz range depending on oil type, water content of the food being fried, and temperature — overlaps substantially with the spectral regions that are perceptually salient for rain recognition.

The key acoustic concept here is that the human auditory system does not decode rain as rain by identifying individual droplet impacts. Instead, it performs a statistical analysis of the aggregate signal, responding to the combination of spectral content (the frequency distribution of energy), temporal texture (the statistical character of the envelope fluctuations), and spatial cues. Because frying food shares the relevant statistical and spectral properties with rain, the auditory system's classification mechanism treats them as equivalent under the conditions in which the substitution is typically employed (Ballas, J.A., 1993, "Common factors in the identification of an assortment of brief everyday sounds," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance*, 19(2), 250-267).

There are systematic limits to this perceptual equivalence. The frying technique works best for generic, moderate-intensity rain at listening distance — what might be called "established rain ambience" where the specific texture of impacts is less salient than the general character of continuous precipitation. It works less well for very light rain — where individual drop impacts are perceptually resolved rather than integrated into a continuous texture — because the bubble-nucleation process in frying produces a denser event rate than light rain. It also works less well for heavy rain accompanied by wind, which has distinctive low-frequency and spatial components that frying oil does not reproduce.

The technique has been modified by practitioners to address some of these limitations. Frying with very thin layers of oil produces sparser, lower-energy events that more closely simulate light rain. Using different water content in the food changes the bubble dynamics and therefore the spectral character of the output — frying wet ingredients like fish or poultry produces more dramatic sizzle events, while frying dryer items produces gentler, more even textures. Adjusting microphone distance and room acoustics allows the sound designer to shape the reverberant tail of the frying noise, providing a degree of environmental context that changes the perceived distance and intimacy of the rain being simulated.

The broader acoustic principle this illustrates is source-independent perceptual classification: the auditory system classifies sounds not by their physical source but by their acoustic gestalt — the combination of spectral, temporal, and spatial properties that characteristically define a sound category. This is why foley art in general works: a human fist striking a watermelon produces an acoustic signal that shares enough properties with a human punch that the auditory classification system assigns it to the "punch" category rather than the "watermelon impact" category, especially when the visual context provides strong prior expectation. Rain from frying is a particularly clean example of this principle because the category membership criteria for "rain" are relatively straightforward statistical properties that are shared by a number of non-rain physical processes.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Mixture — historically true, currently contextual
**Confidence:** High
**Epistemic Basis:** Practitioner testimony + acoustic corroboration
**Phenomenological Status:** Real-phenomenon
**Mechanistic Status:** Confirmed
**Folk Wisdom Value:** High — the technique is the epistemic key to understanding auditory perception

The claim is true in its historical dimension and true-with-important-context in its contemporary dimension. Frying food sounds have been used to simulate rain in film, radio, and television sound production for nearly a century, the technique has been confirmed by multiple professional practitioners, and the acoustic mechanism that makes it work is well-understood in psychoacoustics. This is not film lore or myth; it is confirmed practice.

The qualification necessary for epistemic accuracy is that the claim-as-typically-stated implies universality, currency, and primacy that the technique no longer enjoys in most large-scale professional productions. Digital audio libraries have provided alternatives that are in many contexts more convenient and more varied, and most major productions now use library rain sounds as a first resort, calling on foley techniques — including but not limited to frying — when the library material does not meet specific aesthetic needs.

The claim is thus neither straightforwardly true nor straightforwardly false, but a True-With-Nuance: the technique exists, works, and is used, but does not represent the primary method for rain sound production in contemporary mainstream film. The typical popular framing — "the rain you hear in movies is the sound of frying bacon" — overstates the technique's universality while accurately describing a real and interesting practice.

For public understanding of film sound and perceptual psychology, the more interesting truth is the acoustic mechanism: the auditory system's susceptibility to source-independent spectral and temporal mimicry. This is the knowledge that makes the frying-rain connection genuinely illuminating rather than merely surprising.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The frying-for-rain technique belongs to a much larger tradition of acoustic substitution in sound design that reveals fundamental properties of human auditory perception. Understanding where the technique came from, how it has evolved, and what it shares with other foley methods provides context that substantially enriches the narrow claim.

**The Origins of Foley and Sound Design**: The term "foley" commemorates Jack Foley, a sound effects artist at Universal Pictures who developed systematic methods for adding recorded sound effects to films in synchronisation with the visual image during the early sound era of the late 1920s and 1930s. The transition from silent film to sound was not a simple technical upgrade; it required developing entirely new workflows for recording, editing, and mixing sound elements that could replicate the acoustic texture of visual events convincingly.

Early sound recording technology was profoundly limiting. On-set microphones were directional, sensitive to ambient noise, and often positioned to capture dialogue at the expense of ambient and incidental sounds. The acoustic environments in which foley was developed — studio buildings, urban streets, practical locations — frequently produced capture problems that made direct-recording of many sounds impractical. Foley artists therefore developed libraries of substitute sounds that would pass perceptual examination when applied to a visual context.

The development of these substitutes drew on empirical intuition rather than acoustic science: practitioners tried things that "seemed like they might work" and kept the ones that did. The frying-for-rain substitution almost certainly emerged through this kind of trial-and-error experimentation, possibly with theoretical backing from practitioners who understood something about the statistical character of rain as an acoustic source. The fact that it worked — convincingly enough to survive in professional practice for decades — effectively confirms the acoustic equivalence from the other direction: if the auditory system were not performing source-independent statistical classification, the technique would have been abandoned as unconvincing.

**Radio Drama and the Refinement of Acoustic Substitution**: The BBC Sound Archive and equivalent collections at NBC and CBS in the United States represent some of the most systematic historical documentation of sound substitution techniques, developed for radio drama where there was no visual context to support perceptual categorisation (Millerson, G., 1990, *The Technique of Radio Production*, Focal Press). Radio sound designers faced a harder problem than their film counterparts: without any visual cue to prime the listener's expectation, acoustic substitutes had to be more acoustically faithful to their referents, not less.

The BBC Radiophonic Workshop, established in 1958, brought systematic acoustic experimentation to sound design in ways that documented and extended techniques including rain simulation. Workshop technicians applied spectral analysis and perceptual testing — informal by modern standards but empirically rigorous by the standards of the era — to identify what acoustic properties were and were not necessary for convincing category membership. Their findings informed the standard foley vocabulary that was subsequently adopted across the film and television industry.

Documentation from BBC archives and practitioners including Desmond Briscoe and Roger Limb confirms that frying and similar stochastic noise sources were used for rain in radio drama from at least the 1940s, with systematic refinements made through the postwar decades as recording technology improved and allowed more careful spectral monitoring.

**The Foley Vocabulary and Other Notable Substitutions**: The frying-for-rain technique is one of many acoustic substitutions in the foley vocabulary, several of which are equally counterintuitive. Cellophane, crumpled slowly, has been used for fire (sharing the temporal and spectral stochastic character of flames without the heat, risk, or variable acoustic output of actual fire in a controlled recording environment). Corn starch squeezed in a leather glove has been used for footsteps in snow. Sheets of flexible metal ("thunder sheets") have been used for thunder. The crunching of dried pasta in a cloth bag has been used for bone breaking.

What these substitutes share is acoustic category membership — they possess the spectral, temporal, or spatial signatures that the auditory system uses to assign sounds to categories — without sharing physical source mechanism. This is the principle at the heart of foley art, and it applies as reliably to the frying-rain equivalence as to any other substitution.

The limits of substitution are also instructive: some sounds cannot be convincingly substituted because the acoustic properties that define their category are too complex or multi-dimensional to be reproduced by a studio physical process. Water sounds in general are difficult to substitute convincingly at close microphone distances because the broadband spectral content, temporal texture, and spatial distribution of water sounds encode enough source-specific information that close-field substitutes introduce audible artefacts. Rain at intimate distance — the sound of rain on a roof directly above — tends to require actual rain recording because the spatial and reverberant properties of real rain in a real building are difficult to synthesise from a studio source.

**Digital Audio Libraries and the Transformation of Sound Design Practice**: The progressive development of digital audio libraries from the mid-1980s onward has transformed the economics and practice of sound design in ways that directly affect the contemporary relevance of foley techniques including the frying substitution. Early digital sample libraries were distributed on CD-ROMs; contemporary libraries contain tens of thousands of samples in lossless formats, searchable by metadata, licensable for a single annual subscription, and deployable in real-time within non-linear editing software.

The existence of professionally recorded rain samples in hundreds of varieties — recorded with high-quality microphones in acoustically appropriate environments, normalised for consistent level, tagged with metadata about intensity, surface, microphone position, and spatial character — means that achieving a convincing rain sound is no longer primarily a foley problem for most productions. The library has industrialised the acoustic equivalent.

However, this has not eliminated foley entirely or the specific virtues of foley techniques for rain. Library sounds have a generic quality that experienced sound designers find limiting in specific contexts: a library rain sound has a fixed texture that cannot be shaped in real time to match the visual rhythm of a specific scene, whereas a foley artist performing rain live with frying or other techniques has the flexibility to modulate intensity, character, and timing with the visual edit. High-budget productions often combine library sources for establishing and continuity purposes with foley for close-cut moments where acoustic precision matters.

**Psychoacoustics: Why Perceptual Substitution Works at All**: The frying-rain story is instructive for what it reveals about auditory object recognition more broadly. Auditory scene analysis — the perceptual process by which the auditory system separates and identifies individual sound sources in a complex acoustic environment — relies on heuristics that assign source identity based on acoustic gestalt rather than physical source mechanism. These heuristics are effective in natural environments because, under ordinary conditions, the most reliable predictor of future auditory experience is the past acoustic character of identified categories.

The heuristics fail, predictably and elegantly, when confronted with artificial acoustic substitutes that meet the statistical criteria for a category without being members of that category. The frying-for-rain case is a particularly clean demonstration because the acoustic mechanism of frying (bubble nucleation and collapse) and the acoustic mechanism of rain (droplet impact and splash) are physically unrelated, yet the resulting acoustic signals share sufficient spectral and temporal properties to produce identical categorical classification outcomes (Gaver, W.W., 1993, "What in the world do we hear? An ecological approach to auditory event perception," *Ecological Psychology*, 5(1), 1-29; Gaver explores ecological acoustic theory relevant to why everyday sound perception is susceptible to exactly this kind of substitution).

This means the frying-rain claim is not merely a curiosity about film production history; it is evidence bearing on fundamental questions in perceptual science: how does the auditory system identify sound sources? What information is strictly necessary for category assignment versus merely correlated with it? What are the systematic conditions under which the system can be reliably deceived? Foley art, understood this way, is an applied science of perceptual category hacking — and the frying-rain substitution is one of its oldest and most robustly confirmed demonstrations.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim as a Mixture, reflecting the genuine complexity: the technique is real and historically documented, but it is not universal, and the popular framing often implies that all film rain comes from frying, which is inaccurate. This is a reasonable verdict given that the claim is typically stated in an absolutist form that does not survive contact with the full range of sound design practice.

The Snopes treatment confirms the historical practice with reference to foley artist testimony and production documentation, notes the availability of digital alternatives in contemporary practice, and qualifies the claim appropriately without dismissing it. The analysis is accurate but relatively brief.

What is missing from the Snopes treatment — and from most mass-media fact-checks of this claim — is the acoustic explanation. Establishing that the technique works is necessary but insufficient for a full understanding of the claim; establishing why it works is the more productive epistemic contribution. The acoustic theory of stochastic noise equivalence and source-independent perceptual classification is straightforward and accessible, and its absence from most treatments leaves readers with an interesting fact about film production but no framework for understanding what makes the substitution possible.

The broader issue for fact-checking methodology is that claims about technique (this is how X is done) require contextually sensitive verification that accounts for the gap between what is sometimes done, what is usually done, and what is done exclusively. The frying-rain claim is entirely true as a sometimes claim, partially true as a usually claim, and false as an exclusively claim. Binary True/False verdicts — and even the more sophisticated Mixture verdict — do not fully capture this gradient. A fully adequate fact-check of this claim would describe the technique's history, confirm its current (if non-universal) use, explain the acoustic mechanism that makes it work, and characterise the conditions under which it is and is not employed.
