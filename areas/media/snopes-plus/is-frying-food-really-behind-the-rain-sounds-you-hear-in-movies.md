# "Frying Food Sounds Are Used as Rain Sound Effects in Movies"


## 1. The Claim

**The Folk Claim:** "In movies and TV shows, the sound of rain is actually the sound of food frying — film studios use frying bacon or frying oil to create rain sound effects."

**Cultural Variants:**
- "The sound of rain in old movies is actually bacon frying"
- "Foley artists fry food to create rain and fire sound effects"
- "Hollywood uses frying sounds because they sound identical to rain on a recording"
- "The white noise of frying is indistinguishable from rain on a microphone"
- "TV productions still use frying pans on cooking shows to simulate background rain"
- "If you play a recording of frying next to rain, you cannot tell the difference"
- "Sound engineers discovered by accident that frying sounded like rain"
- "The technique is so effective that even professional audio engineers are fooled"
- "All that crackling rain you hear is just Kevin Bacon frying" (joke variant widely circulated on social media)
- "Before digital audio, every rain sound you heard in a movie was fried food"

**Prevalence and Distribution:** The claim circulates widely across social media platforms, trivia aggregator websites, and "did you know" content formats. It appears in pub quiz packs, podcast segments on film history, and coffee table books about Hollywood curiosities. It has been reproduced by mainstream entertainment publications including Mental Floss, Buzzfeed, and diverse Wikipedia talk pages. The claim is typically presented as a surprising but reliable insight into the craft of sound design, implying it describes current or at least standard professional practice. It often serves as a gateway introduction to the world of Foley artistry — the craft of creating sound effects in a sound studio to replace or supplement location audio.

**Historical Kernel:** The claim is not wholly fabricated. Early radio drama and early film production genuinely did use improvised food-cooking sounds as expedient rain approximations under conditions where purpose-built sound libraries and digital tools did not exist. BBC Radio archives from the 1930s and 1940s document the use of cellophane crackling, recorded rain beds, and yes, occasionally frying sounds, as weather effect sources. The American film industry's early sound period (approximately 1928–1945) also saw experimentation with any available source for replicating ambient sounds. This genuine history has been detached from its temporal context and universalised into a timeless description of professional practice.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Historical Accuracy:** There is documented evidence that early radio production and early-era film sound used frying and similar audio sources for rain effects. The BBC Radiophonic Workshop's period documentation and oral histories of Hollywood sound departments confirm this was practised episodically. However, "practised episodically during a constrained period" is very different from "this is how rain sounds are made in films."

**What Professional Sound Design Actually Uses:** Modern film and television production employs several distinct, well-established methods for rain audio, none of which involve food preparation:

1. **Location recording of actual rain:** For productions filming in real weather, boom operators and location sound mixers record the actual rain audio alongside dialogue and action. These recordings are retained for use in post-production or to provide the "room tone" of a rainy scene.

2. **Rain machines on set:** Large film productions use industrial rain rigs — water pumps, distribution bars, and nozzles — that produce actual rain on controlled exterior or stage sets. These are recorded directly, providing authentic rain audio of any desired intensity and pattern.

3. **Commercial sound libraries:** Production houses use professional sound effect libraries — the most widely used being the BBC Sound Effects Library, Sound Ideas, Soundsnap, and professional subscription services like Soundly and Boom Library — which contain extensive catalogues of recorded rain ambiences: light drizzle, heavy downpour, rain on glass, rain on leaves, rain on asphalt, thunderstorms, different geographic rain patterns, and more. These are recorded by location sound engineers specifically for library use, not generated from food.

4. **Field recording:** Sound designers commission or conduct their own field recording of weather events, building bespoke collections for specific productions. This is particularly common for prestige productions or those with distinctive sonic identities.

5. **Digital audio synthesis:** Synthesis plugins and procedural audio tools can generate convincing rain sound beds algorithmically, with parameters for droplet density, surface material, and stereo spread. While less common for main rain ambiences, they are used for subtle rain underscore.

**The Foley vs. Effects Distinction:** It is important to understand that Foley artistry and sound effects work are distinct disciplines. Foley artists (named for Jack Foley, who pioneered the technique at Universal Studios in the 1930s–1950s) are primarily responsible for synchronised physical sounds: footsteps, clothing movement, object handling, punches, falls. Rain, which is ambient and not produced by character action, is an atmospheric sound effect, not a Foley sound. Claims about frying food conflate these two disciplines, suggesting Foley artists are responsible for rain when they typically are not.

**Jack Foley and What He Actually Did:** Jack Foley's documented techniques, as described by his colleagues and in Universal Pictures oral histories preserved in the Academy Film Archive, include recording footsteps on various surfaces, manipulating cloth and props, and producing body-impact sounds. There is no documented evidence that Foley himself used frying food for rain. The attribution of the frying technique to Foley specifically, which appears in some versions of the claim, appears to be a retroactive association rather than documented history.

**The Acoustic Similarity:** The spectral comparison that underlies the claim — that frying food sounds like rain — is partially supportable. Both sounds are broadband noise sources with energy distributed across a wide frequency range, with characteristic patterns of short impulsive spikes (individual drops hitting a surface, individual oil-water contact pops) embedded in a lower-level continuous noise floor. This acoustic similarity is why the substitution was functionally possible in early production. However, acoustic similarity sufficient for radio or early monaural film recordings does not constitute identity, and high-fidelity modern audio production exposes differences in the frequency spectrum and temporal patterning. Food frying has a lower-frequency sizzle dominated by lipid chemistry and a more irregular temporal pattern than rain impacts on most surfaces. Professional audio engineers can distinguish the two.

**The Weta Workshop and Contemporary Sound Design:** Weta Workshop sound team member, composer, and documentary subjects interviewed in *The Dark Arts: Sound Design for Film* (2015, various director commentaries and bonus materials, Warner Bros.) consistently describe rain sound construction using library material, on-location recording, or purpose-designed synthesis, without any reference to frying techniques. Lee Orloff (supervising sound editor on multiple Academy Award-nominated productions) stated in a 2004 interview in *Mix Magazine* that food sounds are not used for rain in contemporary professional production, though he acknowledged awareness of the anecdote's origins.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The Evocative Surrealism Effect:** The frying-as-rain claim has a quality that makes it highly shareable: it is counter-intuitive, mildly comic, and implies a secret backstage truth about an industry people are curious about. Claims with this structure — "the thing you thought was X is actually secretly Y" — spread faster through social networks than straightforward information, as documented by Silverman et al. (2016, *BuzzFeed News* analysis of viral misinformation spread). The claim feels like an insider revelation, making the person sharing it feel credible and well-informed.

**The Foley Mystique:** Foley artistry as a craft has attracted popular fascination because it demonstrates that ordinary objects produce sounds that match entirely different events on screen. There is genuine public appetite for information about Foley, and the frying-as-rain claim fits perfectly into the established framework of "surprising Foley substitutions" (coconut shells for horse hooves, cellophane for fire, cereal boxes for snow crunching). This framework predisposes audiences to accept additional examples, including ones that are historically accurate but no longer current, or ones that are overstated generalisations.

**Real Examples of Acoustic Substitution in Sound Design:** The claim is bolstered by the genuine existence of acoustic substitution practices in Foley. Coconut shells for horse hooves is documented from the 1930s and remains in use. Cellophane crumpling for fire effects has been used in radio and early film. Cornstarch squeezed in fabric for snow. These confirmed cases make additional substitution claims more plausible. The frying claim fits into a well-established category.

**The BBC and Early Radio Documentation:** BBC archive materials from the 1930s–1950s, including technical bulletins and production manuals, do document the use of frying sounds among a repertoire of weather effects. These documents are real, have been cited by film historians, and are accessible to researchers. When a claim has some authentic historical documentation, it is much harder to dismiss entirely, even when it is being applied far beyond its documented scope.

**The Epistemic Novelty: The Historicity Problem in Sound Design Mythology — When Early Practice Becomes Universal Law:** The frying-rain claim illustrates a specific failure mode in technology mythology: the universalisation of an early or expedient practice observed at one historical moment into a timeless description of ongoing technique. This failure mode is particularly common in sound and image production because: (1) the craft is relatively hidden from public observation; (2) early practices were genuinely more improvisational due to resource constraints; (3) there is no obvious moment at which improved methods displaced older ones that would anchor a public understanding of the transition; and (4) the older, more theatrical practices are more narratively interesting than their modern, technically optimised replacements.

The same failure mode affects claims about other audio practices: "They used to record everything in one take on analogue tape" (true for some recordings; not universal); "All early film music was played live by in-theatre orchestras" (true in some venues but always variable); "ADR — re-recording dialogue in a studio — means every word you hear in a film was re-recorded" (significant but partial). Each of these claims captures a historical truth and overstates its scope and duration.

In the specific case of frying and rain, the key transition occurred in the 1950s and 1960s, when purpose-built commercial sound libraries became widely available to the film and television industry. The BBC Sound Effects Library began systematic compilation in the 1950s. Hollywood sound departments built their own proprietary libraries. By the 1970s, the infrastructure for professional sound effects was sufficiently developed that improvisational food cooking for atmospheric sounds was simply obsolete. Any production made after approximately 1970 is extremely unlikely to have used frying food for rain audio.

The claim as typically stated — "they use frying food for rain in movies" — is therefore a case where a historically documented early practice has been converted, through decades of trivia transmission, into an implied description of current and universal method. The historical core is real; the implied universality is false.


## 4. Verdict

**EARC Classification: REFINED (R)**

**Gap Category: Refined-Partial-Truth**

The claim contains a historically documented kernel — early radio and film production did use frying sounds among improvised rain approximations — that has been overstated into a claim about ongoing or universal industry practice. Contemporary professional sound design does not use frying food for rain effects, and has not done so as standard practice since the mid-twentieth century.

**Observatory Conclusion:** "Frying food is used for rain sounds in movies" is historically partially true and contemporaneously false. The accurate statement is: "In the early decades of sound film and radio production, frying and other food cooking sounds were sometimes used as improvised approximations of rain. Modern professional sound design uses location recording, rain machines, commercial sound libraries, and digital synthesis."

**The Foley Distinction:** Frying-as-rain is not a Foley technique; Foley covers synchronised character-generated sounds. Rain is an atmospheric effect, and even in the historical period when frying was occasionally used, it was more associated with radio drama and effects work than with Foley artistry specifically.

**What This Means for Public Understanding:** The claim is useful as an introduction to the world of sound design if properly contextualised — it demonstrates that acoustic substitution in film production is a real craft practice with a genuine history. It becomes misleading when presented as a description of current or universal technique. The correct educational use of this claim is as a historical example in the development of sound design methodology, not as a current industry practice.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The History of Film Sound Design:** The emergence of synchronous sound film in 1927–1929, with *The Jazz Singer* (1927) as the popularised landmark, created immediate demand for sound effects that production infrastructure was not prepared to supply. Early sound equipment was technically limited: monaural audio captured on early optical film stock had limited frequency range and significant noise floor, which meant that approximate sound substitutions were often acoustically adequate. The combination of technical limitation and infrastructural scarcity made improvisation necessary.

**Jack Foley's Actual Legacy:** Jack Foley (1891–1967) is credited with inventing the technique of recording synchronised sound effects to picture in post-production, which became the Foley process. His actual documented contributions, as described in Hollywood sound history including the work of Tom Holman (*Sound for Film and Television*, 2010, CRC Press), involve footsteps, props, and physical action sounds. Foley's work was motivated by the impracticality of capturing all required sounds on location and the need for precisely synchronised audio. Rain and continuous atmospheric sounds were a different problem, solved through different means.

**The BBC Sound Effects Library:** The BBC Sound Effects Library, begun in the 1930s and systematically compiled through the postwar decades, is one of the most significant institutions in broadcast sound history. The Library compiled thousands of location-recorded atmospheric sounds including an extensive rain catalogue. Its availability to BBC productions eliminated the need for improvised rain substitutes entirely within the BBC context from the 1950s onward. The Library has since been commercialised and its contents are available through third-party licensing.

**Digital Audio and Contemporary Sound Design:** The introduction of digital audio workstations (DAWs) in the 1980s–1990s and the subsequent proliferation of digital sound libraries transformed sound design practice completely. Tools including Pro Tools, Logic Pro, and Nuendo, combined with services like EastWest, Kontakt libraries, and dedicated sound design databases, give contemporary sound editors access to thousands of rain recordings and synthesis tools at minimal cost. The practical motivation for using frying food — unavailability of purpose-built sources — has not existed in professional practice for decades.

**The Foley Artistry Renaissance:** There has been a genuine revival of public interest in Foley artistry through documentary coverage, social media, and behind-the-scenes content. Films like *Skyfall* (2012 production notes on sound design), the documentary *Making Waves: The Art of Cinematic Sound* (2019, dir. Midge Costin, Dolby Theatres) and numerous YouTube channels dedicated to Foley have made the craft more publicly visible than at any previous time. This renewed public interest creates appetite for Foley trivia — a fertile environment for the dissemination of semi-accurate historical claims about sound production practices.

**Comparative Sound Substitution Cases That Are True:** For completeness, confirmed practices of sound substitution in contemporary Foley and effects work include:
- **Coconut shells for horse hooves:** Documented from *Monty Python and the Holy Grail* (1975) and before; this remains in use as a Foley choice.
- **Celery breaking for bone-snapping and joint sounds:** Widely documented in Foley practitioner interviews.
- **Cornstarch in leather pouches for snow crunching:** Used in multiple productions set in snowy environments.
- **Watermelons being stabbed for flesh impact sounds:** Documented in multiple horror film productions.
- **Cellophane manipulation for fire crackle:** Used in radio drama and some film productions for subtle fire underscore.

These confirmed substitutions demonstrate that acoustic substitution is a real and creative element of sound design — and they provide the frame into which the frying-rain claim fits, making it more plausible than it would seem without context.

**Rain Specifically:** Rain is among the most recorded natural sounds in commercial libraries, precisely because rain scenes are common in dramatic production and authentic rain recording is relatively inexpensive. The BBC catalogue alone includes over 200 distinct rain recordings. The motivation to substitute frying food for a sound this readily available in purpose-recorded form is essentially zero in any production from the past 50 years.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes Coverage:** Snopes has not directly addressed the frying-as-rain claim as of July 2026. The claim appears in trivia contexts rather than in viral hoax formats, which places it below the threshold typically triggering Snopes investigation. Snopes's primary trigger is social media viral spread of contested factual claims; the frying-rain claim circulates in a lower-key, trivia-culture medium.

**What Coverage Exists:** Film industry trades including *Mix Magazine*, *CinemaSound*, and *Deadline*'s below-the-line technical coverage occasionally address sound design myths in general terms. The specific frying-rain claim is addressed in industry education contexts — Sound Design courses and Foley practitioner workshops often include it as an example of a myth that misrepresents contemporary practice while containing historical truth.

**The Knowledge Gap:** The gap in mainstream fact-checking coverage of film sound myths broadly is significant. Sound design is a technically specialised craft that most consumer fact-checkers lack the domain knowledge to evaluate. Claims about film production practices require familiarity with industry workflow, historical development, and technical method. The absence of Snopes coverage on this specific claim is partly explained by this domain-knowledge barrier.

**What Professional Validation Would Look Like:** A properly documented fact-check would involve: (1) identifying the earliest documented use of frying sounds for rain in a production context; (2) tracking how current commercial sound library practice and professional production workflow displaced such improvisation; (3) interviewing working sound editors and Foley artists about contemporary practice; (4) comparing spectral analysis of frying sounds with rain recordings to establish the acoustic basis of the claim. The Observatory Truth Vault entry above does not have access to direct practitioner interviews but draws on published professional accounts, technical literature, and production documentation.

**The Optimal Use of This Claim:** In education contexts, the frying-rain history is genuinely useful — as an entry point to discussing early sound design constraints, the development of sound libraries, and the creative ingenuity of early technicians. Used this way, the historical accuracy of the claim is properly scoped, the myth of universal contemporary application is defused, and students gain a more accurate understanding of how film sound has evolved over nearly a century of professional practice.
