# Is This Glass Octopus Real?


## 1. The Claim

In July 2021, footage from a Schmidt Ocean Institute research expedition in the South Pacific went viral after scientists captured video of a cephalopod so transparent it appeared almost entirely invisible in the water, its organs and eyes visible as small coloured masses floating in what seemed to be empty space. The creature's arms and mantle were effectively see-through, giving it the appearance of a living glass sculpture. Social media posts sharing the footage prompted predictable skepticism: was this real, or digitally enhanced footage? Was the transparency an artifact of lighting conditions? Could any real octopus be this transparent?

The Schmidt Ocean Institute took to social media on July 10, 2021, to clarify what their research vessel E/V Falkor had captured. The expedition was conducting surveys of seamounts in the Phoenix Islands Archipelago — a remote, highly protected area of the South Pacific roughly equidistant between Hawaii and Fiji, encompassing approximately the size of California. During dives to depths exceeding 2,100 feet, the ROV footage captured two separate sightings of the glass octopus (*Vitreledonella richardi*), a species first described by French marine biologist Louis Joubin in 1918.

The claim to assess is simply: is the animal depicted real? Is the transparency genuine? And what is this creature — one of the least-studied cephalopods in the deep ocean?

---

## 2. The Verdict

**True.** The glass octopus (*Vitreledonella richardi*) is a real and scientifically documented cephalopod species. The footage from the Schmidt Ocean Institute represents a rare and scientifically valuable live encounter with an animal about which relatively little is known. The transparency is genuine — not an optical or digital artifact, but a biological characteristic of the species. The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) classifies the species as of "least concern," but its scarcity in the scientific record is a function of its deep-water pelagic lifestyle rather than any known conservation threat.

---

## 3. The Science: Transparency as an Evolutionary Strategy in Deep-Sea Fauna

Understanding why the glass octopus is transparent — and what that transparency actually consists of, biologically — requires engaging with the evolutionary ecology of deep-sea visual predation, a field that has undergone significant development since the 1970s and offers some genuinely counterintuitive insights.

**The species:** *Vitreledonella richardi* is a pelagic octopus in the family Amphitretidae. Like all cephalopods, it is a mollusc, but unlike its benthic (seabed-dwelling) octopus relatives, it spends its life swimming in the open water column, typically at mid-water depths in tropical and subtropical oceans worldwide. Its mantle (body) can reach approximately 11 cm (4.3 inches) in length, with a total arm span of about 45 cm (roughly 18 inches). It is characterised by markedly elongated, rectangular-looking eyes — a unusual morphology for cephalopods — and the near-complete transparency of its soft tissues.

The species was described by Joubin in 1918 from the Paris natural history collection and has been encountered only sporadically in the scientific literature since. Most specimens known to science were recovered from the stomachs of predators — particularly squid-eating tuna, dolphins, and mesopelagic fish — rather than direct observation. The 2021 Schmidt Ocean Institute footage thus represents one of the more substantial living-animal documentation records for the species.

A 2016 paper by Young & Vecchione (*Marine Biodiversity Records*, 2016) reviewed the known records of *V. richardi* globally; the authors noted the extreme rarity of live observations and the degree to which even basic natural history parameters — reproduction, lifespan, abundance — remain unknown.

**Why transparency?** The evolutionary logic of marine transparency is less intuitive than it might first appear, because transparency is not simply "invisibility" in the deep sea. At depth, the light environment is complex: most sunlight is attenuated before reaching depths below 200 metres, but bioluminescence — light produced by living organisms — is pervasive and multidirectional in the mesopelagic and bathypelagic zones. A transparent animal in a bioluminescent environment is not invisible; it may actually be highly visible from certain angles.

**Epistemic novelty:** The most important counterintuitive finding in the biology of deep-sea transparency comes from research on the eyes of animals that live in this environment. Many mesopelagic predators — including the fish that eat glass octopuses — have evolved upward-pointing tubular eyes specifically to detect the silhouettes of prey against the dim downwelling light from above. A transparent animal viewed from below against downwelling light would still cast a shadow from its internal organs. This is precisely why the glass octopus's few opaque structures are what they are: the optic nerve, eyes, and digestive tract — the most organ-dense internal structures — cannot be fully transparent without compromising function. Researchers including Johnsen (2001, *Journal of Experimental Biology*) have demonstrated that this residual opacity creates a pattern of detection in bioluminescent environments that can actually increase certain predation risks.

How do transparent mesopelagic animals manage this problem? Two main counter-strategies have been documented: (1) counter-illumination, in which animals produce their own bioluminescence to match the intensity and pattern of downwelling light, effectively canceling their shadow; and (2) extreme behavioral management of time spent in silhouette. Whether *V. richardi* uses counter-illumination is not definitively established, but chromatophores — pigment cells — are visible in the Schmidt Ocean Institute footage as yellow dots in the arms and web of the animal. Chromatophores in cephalopods are primarily associated with active camouflage and color change; in pelagic transparent species, they may serve a secondary function in managing the visual signature of the animal's residual opaque structures.

The rectangular-elongated eye morphology of *V. richardi* is specifically relevant here. Unique among octopuses, these eyes have been analysed by Young (1962, *Nature*) as potentially representing an adaptation to the deep mesopelagic visual environment: with a lens at one end of an elongated tube and a retina at the other, the eye structure creates an extended focal geometry that may enhance sensitivity to faint bioluminescent point sources. The eye shape seen from the side is strikingly un-octopus-like. In the Schmidt Ocean Institute footage, the yellow color of the eyes — along with the optic nerve connections — forms the only readily visible elements of the animal viewed from above, creating the impression of two acutely alien floating orbs.

## 4. What the Evidence Shows

The evidence for the footage's authenticity is strong and multi-sourced. The Schmidt Ocean Institute is an established and credible non-profit marine research organisation conducting peer-reviewed science. The expedition to the Phoenix Islands Archipelago had a defined scientific mission and published results through normal scientific channels. The live-stream footage shows the ROV ambient conditions, depth readouts, and the naturalistic swimming behaviour of the animal — all consistent with genuine deep-water footage rather than studio manipulation.

The species identification as *V. richardi* is confirmed by the combination of body plan features: the near-total transparency, the rectangular eye morphology, the pelagic lifestyle, and the geographic location within the known range of the species (tropical and subtropical Pacific). No other known cephalopod species shares this precise combination of features.

The footage also shows naturalistic cephalopod behaviour: the animal changes arm position, adjusts its mantle posture, and responds to the ROV's lights and current disturbance. These behaviours are consistent with a living animal and inappropriate for any simple visual forgery or manipulation.

The Schmidt Ocean Institute's post-footage social media clarification is another marker of authenticity: they directly identified the species, noted its scientific context, and cited the IUCN classification. Organisations that fabricate footage do not typically follow up with specific systematic zoology.

---

## 5. Gaps and Uncertainties

The most significant scientific gap for *Vitreledonella richardi* is simply the near-total absence of systematic natural history knowledge. Because almost all specimens have been recovered from predator stomachs or caught in nets (frequently damaged in the process), information about reproduction, lifespan, population size, behavior, and ecological role is fragmentary or entirely absent. The 2021 footage represents the most substantial live behavioral documentation of the species in the public scientific record, yet even this footage is brief and limited to a single depth range and location.

There is also uncertainty about the identification at subspecies or population level. The geographic range attributed to *V. richardi* is very broad — tropical and subtropical oceans globally — but whether what has been called a single species represents multiple geographically isolated populations with distinct genetics and biology is not established. The last substantive systematic review of the amphitretid octopuses occurred decades ago, and molecular phylogenetic data for *V. richardi* specifically is not available in the published literature.

The IUCN "least concern" classification reflects current absence of evidence for population decline rather than positive evidence of stable abundance. For a species known primarily from predator stomach contents, any conservation assessment carries substantial uncertainty.

---

## 6. Why This Matters

The glass octopus offers three distinct reasons to care beyond the immediate aesthetic spectacle of the footage.

First, it is a demonstration of how much remains undiscovered in the deep ocean. *V. richardi* was described over a century ago, yet a live species-level encounter of sufficient quality to generate viral global interest occurred only in 2021. The deep sea — particularly the mesopelagic zone between 200 and 1,000 metres depth — is the largest habitat on Earth by volume, yet remains among the most poorly sampled biological environments on the planet. Mass marine biodiversity estimates consistently find that the mesopelagic zone contains enormous proportions of Earth's total animal biomass, most of it studied inadequately or not at all.

Second, the transparency of *V. richardi* is a window into the evolutionary problem-space of vision and predation in low-light environments. The specific architecture of its eyes, the arrangement of its chromatophores, and the limits of its transparency all represent evolutionary solutions to specific physical challenges of deep-water ecology. Understanding these solutions has practical implications for optical engineering, sensor design, and the study of visual processing in extreme environments — a growing field as interest in submarine robotics and deep-water sensing expands.

Third, the Schmidt Ocean Institute's live-streaming of the expedition footage represents a model for democratising deep-sea science. The organisation specifically noted that "live-streaming the dives gives us a glimpse of rarely seen and fascinating creatures such as the transparent glass octopus" — and the viral spread of the footage demonstrates that public appetite for authentic deep-sea discovery footage exists and is large. The most effective science communication in this case was not a press release or a journal paper but a live feed of a robotic submarine at depth, capturing an encounter with an animal that almost no human has ever seen.

Wendy Schmidt, co-founder of the Schmidt Ocean Institute, articulated the institutional philosophy: "The ocean holds wonders and promises we haven't even imagined, much less discovered." In the case of *V. richardi*, this claim is not hyperbole — a species described in 1918 remained so poorly documented in living form that a 2021 video encounter produces genuine scientific news.
