## §1 — Claim and Verdict Summary

In January 2024, a viral social media post claimed to authentically show actor Jason Momoa—widely recognized for his long dark hair and prominent beard—without his characteristic facial hair. The image, circulated on Reddit and elsewhere with captions such as "Momoa without his beard. To all the women, I'm sorry in advance," depicted Momoa with a noticeably altered appearance: a double chin, softened jawline, and beard erased. The implication was that Momoa's physical attractiveness was significantly dependent on his beard.

Snopes fact-checker Nur Ibrahim investigated the claim and published a verdict of **Fake** on February 13, 2024. The verdict rests on multiple independent lines of visual and photographic evidence:

1. The edited image retains artifacts of the original beard—specifically, shadow on the neck created by the beard's presence—indicating that the editing removed beard hair but failed to account for how light interacts with the underlying facial structure.
2. Comparison with verified unedited photographs of Momoa, including confirmed Getty Images photographs from the same event and outfit (the August 2022 premiere of *See* on Apple TV+), shows that his actual appearance differs materially from the edited image.
3. A 2019 video in which Momoa actually shaved his beard—posted to his verified YouTube channel—provides direct visual evidence of his actual beardless appearance, which shows a noticeably different jaw and chin profile from the fake image.
4. More recent 2024 photographs show Momoa continuing to wear a beard, consistent with his established personal styling.

The earliest traceable version of the edited image appeared on Instagram in November 2022, predating its viral January 2024 spread by over a year.

## §2 — Visual Forensics: Identifying Digital Manipulation

The evidentiary core of the Snopes investigation is a careful reading of the visual artifacts left by the editing process. Two specific forensic indicators identified by Ibrahim are particularly significant:

**Shadow persistence:** In the edited image, Momoa's neck area retains the shadow that would be produced by a thick beard blocking the light source—yet the beard itself is absent. This is a classic artifact of editing tools that remove visible hair without modeling the resulting changes to shadow, highlight, and subsurface scattering. Human face recognition is highly sensitive to shadow consistency with light source direction; the preserved beard shadow creates a visual incoherence detectable on close inspection.

**Jaw contour alteration:** The editing software modified Momoa's visible jawline and chin structure in ways inconsistent with his actual anatomy as documented in authenticated photographs. The presence of a double chin in the edited image was noted by many viewers as a major surprise. However, comparison with Momoa's verified July 2022 Instagram posts—taken when he wore a shorter beard and had a more visible neck—reveals a noticeably sharper profile than the edited image suggests. The double chin in the fake is an artifact of the image manipulation, not a genuine feature of his unbearded appearance.

This type of forgery—targeting celebrity appearance and implicitly making claims about physical attractiveness—occupies a distinct space in the taxonomy of synthetic media. Chesney and Citron (2019, *California Law Review*, 107[6], 1753–1820) categorized synthetic media threats by their primary social harm. Appearance-manipulation fakes of celebrities do not necessarily fit the "non-consensual intimate imagery" category, but they do constitute unauthorized alteration of a person's documented likeness for social purposes—in this case, participation in a humor trope (the "attractive celebrity without their attractive feature" joke) at the subject's expense.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis: The Authentic Comparison Method and Its Limits

The Snopes investigation deploys what might be termed the *authenticated comparator method*: using verified photographs from the same occasion (matching outfit, background, and Getty Images confirmation) and from documented prior beard-shaving events (Momoa's 2019 YouTube video) to establish a ground-truth baseline against which the suspect image can be tested. This method is epistemically sound and represents best practice for image verification in the shortform context of rapid fact-checking.

The epistemically novel challenge this case illustrates is the *plausibility gradient problem* in celebrity image manipulation. Momoa's appearance has genuinely varied across time—his weight, beard length, and apparent facial structure change based on diet, fitness regimen, camera angle, and lighting. The fact-checker Ibrahim acknowledges this explicitly: "everyone changes as time goes by, and Momoa is no exception. His weight and appearance have changed frequently and sometimes he appears to have a double chin, while other times it looks sharper based on camera angles." This is a significant acknowledgment: the fakery exploited *genuine variance* in the subject's appearance to make the edited image implausible-but-deniable.

This represents an underrecognized feature of AI-assisted celebrity image manipulation: by targeting aspects of appearance that genuinely fluctuate, manipulators can construct images that fall within the range of possible authentic appearances, making definitive "impossible" verdicts harder to issue. Wang, Tan, Yang, and Liu (2020, *IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security*, 15, 2974–2988) found that GAN-generated face manipulations designed to stay within the authentic variance envelope of a specific individual's known photo corpus were significantly more resistant to automated detection. Human pattern recognition faces analogous challenges: when an edited image depicts something that *could* be real (the subject could have that appearance under some conditions), the falseness depends on comparison with authenticated reference images rather than on internal impossibility.

The ultimate verdict here relies on two sources that are direct and irrefutable: the shadow artifact (a technical impossibility in an authentic photograph under the lighting conditions shown) and the 2019 YouTube shaving video (direct documented evidence of actual beardless appearance, inconsistent with the fake). The broader range of "this seems implausible" evidence is suitably hedged, which reflects sound epistemic practice.

## §4 — The Cultural Context: Beard-Removal Humor as Social Media Genre

The Momoa fake-beardless image belongs to a broader social media genre: the "what does [famous attractive person] look like without their signature feature?" post. This genre combines elements of parasocial engagement (public fascination with celebrities' appearance), humor (the comedic premise of a widely desired person being revealed as "merely normal"), and crowd psychology (the invite to share a reaction). The caption "to all the women, I'm sorry in advance" is structurally a punchline setup, positioning Momoa's beard as the supposed source of his attractiveness.

This genre predates AI image editing. Before generative AI tools, such posts circulated as rough Photoshop edits (often quite obviously artificial) or as archival photographs meeting the "before they were famous" or "without their signature look" curiosity. The availability of better generative tools raised production quality significantly: an image that "passes" even brief inspection—as this one did, spreading to 710,000+ Instagram likes before significant debunking—represents a new capability threshold.

The January 2024 viral iteration of this image was originally posted to a Facebook group called "Cursed AI"—a group explicitly dedicated to AI-generated content. This is notable: the original posting context was *explicitly labeled as AI*, yet decontextualized sharing propagated the image widely without that context. Ibrahim notes that the Facebook post to this context-labeled group received 20,000 reactions, 12,000 comments, and 42,000 shares before the image's spread to adjacent networks where it circulated as documented reality. This trajectory is diagnostic of the viral lifecycle of AI-generated content: produced in labeled AI-appreciation spaces, circulated without labels into general social networks, received as real by audiences without the labeling context.

The earliest appearance of the specific edited image tracked by Ibrahim dates to November 2022 on Instagram (account @meecal.co), predating the January 2024 viral spread by more than a year. This suggests the image was created and circulated at small scale initially, then rediscovered and amplified over a year later—a pattern consistent with how viral content achieves delayed traction when discovered by a higher-follower account.

## §5 — Physical Appearance, Authentic Records, and the Right to Accurate Representation

The Momoa beardless fake intersects with a growing body of concern about AI-assisted image manipulation of celebrities for non-consensual purposes. While this particular case falls at the relatively low-harm end of the spectrum—it is not sexually explicit, does not constitute defamation of professional conduct, and is framed as humor—it nonetheless raises legitimate questions about consent, accuracy, and the social infrastructure for verifying authentic depictions of real persons.

Momoa is a public figure who has documented his own appearance extensively through social media, professional imagery, and public character promotion built around his specific physical identity (his role as Aquaman, for instance, prominently features his physique and grooming). Manipulated images that misrepresent his appearance—even humorously—without consent constitute a form of unauthorized image use that courts and legislators in multiple jurisdictions have begun to address through right-of-publicity frameworks (Hartmann, 2022, *Harvard Journal of Law and Technology*, 35[2], 595–630).

Beyond individual harm, the structural problem is one of *visual epistemics*: social media audiences now encounter a continuous stream of photographic and videographic content in which authenticity cannot be assumed. Traditional heuristics ("photographs don't lie," "seeing is believing") are technically obsolete but psychologically persistent. Paul and Royakkers (2011, *Journal of Information, Communication and Ethics in Society*, 9[2], 114–130) documented how pre-AI photo manipulation already eroded public trust in documentary photography. Generative AI has accelerated this erosion while lowering the technical barrier to production, creating a structural mismatch: manipulation capability far exceeds broad public visual literacy for detecting manipulation.

The Snopes investigation's forensic methodology—authentic comparators, shadow analysis, verified reference images—represents the corrective infrastructure available at current detection levels. Its limitation is scale: individual fact-checking cannot keep pace with the volume of AI-generated imagery in circulation.

## §6 — Synthesis and Conclusion

The viral image purporting to show Jason Momoa without his signature beard is **fake**—a digitally manipulated image bearing the artifacts of post-production editing, inconsistent with authenticated photographic records of Momoa's appearance. The investigation by Nur Ibrahim deployed sound visual forensics: shadow artifact analysis, authenticated comparator photographs (Getty Images, verified Instagram), and direct documentary evidence from Momoa's own 2019 beard-shaving YouTube video. The earliest known version of the fake circulated in November 2022 on Instagram; a January 2024 rediscovery by a linked Facebook-Instagram account with millions of followers drove its subsequent viral spread.

This case exemplifies a broader pattern in AI-assisted celebrity appearance manipulation: images produced in explicitly labeled AI-content spaces circulate without labels into general networks, achieving false-reality reception at second and third remove from their production context. The "plausibility gradient" challenge—where edited images stay within the range of possible authentic appearance variance—makes internal-impossibility arguments insufficient; external comparison against verified references is required.

The epistemic gap noted in this entry is the lack of information about what specific tool or algorithm generated the manipulation. Identifying AI model fingerprints in generated images is an active area of forensic research (Rossler, Cocchi, Davide et al., 2019, *Proceedings of the IEEE/CVF International Conference on Computer Vision*, 1–11), and closer analysis might narrow attribution—but Snopes' corrective purpose is served without it. The key verdict stands on the forensic evidence.

**Snopes Verdict: Fake.** The image is digitally manipulated; Momoa's actual beardless appearance differs markedly from the edited depiction.
