# Is use of lye-based hair relaxers linked to a higher risk of breast cancer?

## 1. The Claim

The claim circulating in health news coverage and social media since at least 2021 holds that women who regularly use lye-based chemical hair relaxers face a meaningfully elevated risk of developing breast cancer—and that this risk is disproportionately borne by Black women, who use these products at significantly higher rates than other demographic groups.

The specific form the claim takes varies depending on the source. In its most conservative version, it appears in peer-reviewed public health journalism: studies have found a statistically significant association between heavy, long-term use of lye-based relaxers and estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer in Black women. In its more amplified social media form, it becomes: hair relaxers cause cancer and the beauty industry has been poisoning Black women for decades while regulators looked the other way. Both versions contain real information, but they carry very different levels of precision and implication.

The October 2022 Snopes coverage followed a wave of mainstream media reporting on a study published in the *International Journal of Cancer*, which found elevated breast cancer risk associated with chemical hair straightener use in the NIH Sister Study cohort. That study generated substantial public attention, not least because a separate NIH study published that same month had shown that chemical hair straighteners were associated with a roughly doubled risk of uterine cancer—lending urgency to the question of what these products might be doing to hormonal health more broadly.

The questions the claim raises are clinically live, socially charged, and epidemiologically complex. Hair relaxers have been used in the Black community for more than a century—straightening natural hair has long been both a practical grooming choice and, for some women, a negotiated response to racially discriminatory beauty standards that penalized natural Black hair in professional and social settings. The possibility that these products carry cancer risk therefore lands in a context of compounded injustice: not only might the beauty industry have sold harmful products, but the women most likely to have used them were already navigating a discriminatory cultural environment that made such products feel necessary.

This complexity—epidemiological, regulatory, and racial-political—makes the claim worth examining with precision.

## 2. What's Actually True

The epidemiological evidence for an association between heavy lye-based hair relaxer use and breast cancer, particularly ER-positive breast cancer in Black women, is real and has been replicated across multiple independent large-scale studies. Understanding what this means—and what it doesn't—requires attention to the nature of the evidence.

**The Black Women's Health Study findings.** A key paper published in the *International Journal of Cancer* in 2021 drew on data from the Black Women's Health Study (BWHS), a prospective cohort study that has followed more than 59,000 Black women across the United States since 1995. Researchers found that Black women who used lye-based hair products at least seven times a year for 15 or more years had approximately a 30% increased risk of ER-positive breast cancer compared with women who used such products less frequently. The association was specific to lye-based relaxers and to ER-positive disease—the subset of breast cancers that are driven by estrogen signaling and that represent the most common category of breast cancer diagnosis.

**The NIH Sister Study.** The National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences' Sister Study, which enrolled more than 50,000 women across the U.S. and Puerto Rico between 2003 and 2009 (all of whom had a sister with breast cancer but were cancer-free themselves at enrollment), has produced multiple analyses on hair product use and cancer outcomes. A 2020 study in the *International Journal of Cancer* using Sister Study data found that women who used chemical hair straighteners more than four times per year had a 31% higher breast cancer incidence compared with non-users. Importantly, a 2022 analysis by Dr. Adana Llanos and colleagues found that women who had used relaxers before age 12 and for more than ten years were more likely to develop larger and more aggressive breast tumors—a finding that went beyond incidence rates to suggest impact on tumor biology.

**The uterine cancer parallel.** A separate NIH study published in the *Journal of the National Cancer Institute* in October 2022 found that women who used chemical hair straightening products were more than twice as likely to develop uterine cancer as those who did not use these products. This finding, because it was highly specific and based on a large sample, drew significant media coverage and lent credibility to the question of whether the broader body of hair relaxer/cancer research was pointing toward a genuine exposure-disease relationship.

**The chemical plausibility argument.** Hair relaxers—both lye-based (containing sodium hydroxide) and no-lye (typically calcium hydroxide or guanidine carbonate)—contain or produce a range of chemicals associated with endocrine disruption. These include formaldehyde and formaldehyde-releasing agents (used in some straightening formulations), parabens (used as preservatives), phthalates (frequently found in fragrance components), and cyclosiloxanes. Formaldehyde is classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) as a Group 1 human carcinogen. Phthalates and parabens are estrogenic compounds—they can mimic or disrupt estrogen signaling in ways that are biologically plausible contributors to hormone-sensitive cancers like ER-positive breast cancer. The scalp is a particularly permeable surface for chemical absorption, and the heat applied during relaxer treatments can enhance penetration.

**What the evidence doesn't establish.** The studies described above are epidemiological—they observe associations in populations but cannot isolate individual causal pathways. Observational data, even from large prospective cohort studies, cannot fully account for all confounding variables. Women who use hair relaxers more frequently may differ from non-users in other ways—diet, access to healthcare, stress exposure, other chemical exposures—that could independently affect cancer risk. While researchers attempt to control for known confounders, residual confounding is a persistent limitation of observational studies.

The magnitude of the association is also important context. A 30–31% increased risk sounds alarming, but must be understood in absolute terms. Breast cancer affects approximately 13% of American women over their lifetimes. A 30% relative risk increase on a baseline of 13% would suggest approximately a 17% lifetime risk for heavy users—meaningfully elevated, but not the dramatic transformation implied by some media coverage. The elevated risk is real and clinically significant; it is not a tripling or quadrupling of absolute risk.

Finally, the question of lye versus no-lye formulations is genuinely unsettled. Many of the largest studies, including the Sister Study, did not consistently differentiate between lye-based and no-lye products, because product ingredient information is often incomplete or unreliable in self-reported survey data. The BWHS findings, which did find specificity for lye-based relaxers in relation to ER-positive breast cancer, are a notable exception, but the field has not yet converged on whether lye products are specifically more harmful than no-lye alternatives or whether the relevant exposures (phthalates, parabens, formaldehyde) are comparably present across formulation types.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The reasons the hair relaxer/breast cancer research has received widespread attention—and sometimes been understood in oversimplified or inflated terms—involve a complicated intersection of legitimate scientific findings, racial health justice advocacy, media amplification, and social media dynamics.

**Legitimate epidemiological concern.** People believe this, first and most importantly, because there is something real to believe. Unlike many health scares that rest on a single poorly designed study or a biological mechanism that doesn't hold up to scrutiny, the hair relaxer findings have been replicated across multiple independent large datasets, are consistent in pointing to heavy long-term use as a key risk modifier, and are supported by plausible endocrine-disruptive chemistry. Women reading about these findings are not being misled when they take them seriously.

**The racial health justice dimension.** Black women in the United States face disproportionate breast cancer mortality—while their incidence rates have historically been reported as slightly lower than those of white women, their death rates are significantly higher. The burden of ER-positive breast cancer and more aggressive triple-negative breast cancer at younger ages disproportionately affects Black women. In this context, the possibility that a product used predominantly in the Black community—sometimes beginning in early childhood—may be contributing to this disparity carries an emotional and political charge that goes well beyond any individual risk estimate. Advocates for Black women's health have raised hair relaxers as a reproductive justice issue, arguing that the beauty industry's failure to adequately disclose chemical hazards represents a continuation of broader patterns of environmental racism.

**Media compression and risk communication failures.** When epidemiological studies are translated into news headlines, the nuances of relative versus absolute risk, the distinction between association and causation, and the caveats around confounding and study design typically disappear. A headline reading "Hair Relaxers Linked to 31% Higher Breast Cancer Risk" accurately summarizes a finding but invites a reading—that regular relaxer use makes you one-third more likely to get cancer overall—that is not precisely what the research shows. This compression is a structural feature of science journalism, not a deliberate distortion, but it systematically encourages people to understand risk findings in more alarming terms than the underlying science warrants.

**Historical product safety failures in personal care.** The beauty industry has a documented history of selling products containing ingredients that later proved harmful—from formaldehyde in Brazilian blowout treatments to asbestos in talc-based powders, to the long fight over whether certain hair dyes and nail polish chemicals warranted stronger safety testing. This history creates a justified skepticism about product safety claims made by manufacturers and a corresponding readiness to believe that a newly identified risk has been known longer than the industry has acknowledged. That skepticism is not unfounded; the precautionary principle has frequently proven warranted in personal care product safety.

**Social media amplification.** Once research findings enter social media ecosystems, they circulate selectively according to emotional resonance and sharability. A post claiming that hair relaxers are linked to cancer is more shareable than a thread carefully explaining relative risk, study design limitations, and the difference between association and causation. The result is that the dramatic version of the finding spreads further and faster, reaches audiences with less context, and solidifies into a firmer belief than the underlying evidence strictly supports. This does not make the core finding false—it makes the public representation of the finding less accurate than it ought to be.

## 4. Verdict

**Mixture — with a genuine evidentiary core.** The claim that lye-based hair relaxers are linked to a higher risk of breast cancer reflects real epidemiological evidence from multiple large, well-designed prospective cohort studies. The association is most strongly established for ER-positive breast cancer among Black women who are heavy, long-term users. It is supported by biologically plausible mechanisms involving endocrine-disrupting chemicals in relaxer formulations.

The claim is not a conspiracy theory, a fabrication, or a simple misreading of one isolated study. It is epidemiology with real-world clinical and public health implications that warrant serious attention from researchers, regulators, and consumers.

At the same time, precision matters. The association is not proven causation. The magnitude of the absolute risk increase is meaningful but not catastrophic. The lye/no-lye distinction has not been fully established across all studies. The relative contributions of specific chemicals have not been isolated in human trials. And the overall picture remains more complex and more uncertain than either alarmed social media coverage or dismissive industry response suggests.

What the evidence supports is a reasonable concern—strong enough to inform personal choices, to justify continued and accelerated research, to motivate regulatory review of chemical ingredients in personal care products, and to support policy measures like the FDA's Closer to Zero initiative and proposed legislation mandating stricter heavy metal and chemical limits in consumer products. It does not support the narrative that the science is settled, that causation is established, or that women who have used relaxers are certain to develop cancer.

The claim is grounded in real science. Its strongest amplified versions overstate the certainty and magnitude of what the current evidence shows.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The hair relaxer cancer research sits within a much larger and long-overdue investigation into the safety of personal care products, the adequacy of chemical regulation in the United States, and the racial disparities in whose bodies bear the burden of environmental and product-related chemical exposures.

**The regulatory gap in personal care products.** The United States regulates cosmetics far less stringently than food, pharmaceuticals, or environmental contaminants. The Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, which governs cosmetics regulation, was last comprehensively updated in 1938. For most of the intervening eight decades, the FDA did not have the authority to mandate pre-market safety testing of cosmetic ingredients, require adverse event reporting from manufacturers, or impose recalls without court orders. The 2022 Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act (MoCRA) gave the FDA significantly expanded authority—requiring safety substantiation, adverse event reporting, and facility registration—but the regulatory infrastructure for rigorous pre-market toxicological review of cosmetic ingredients remains far weaker in the U.S. than in the European Union, which bans or restricts more than 1,300 chemicals from cosmetic use compared to roughly 11 banned or restricted by the U.S.

**Formaldehyde and the straightening product class.** Formaldehyde is a known human carcinogen—IARC Group 1, meaning the evidence for its carcinogenicity in humans is sufficient and conclusive. Brazilian blowout treatments and some keratin straightening products were found to contain undisclosed formaldehyde at high concentrations, prompting FDA warning letters and eventually policy action in several states. The European Union banned formaldehyde-releasing chemicals from hair straightening products in 2023. The FDA proposed a rule in 2023 prohibiting formaldehyde and formaldehyde-releasing chemicals in hair straightening products sold in the United States—but as of the time of writing, that rulemaking process has not been finalized.

**Product testing and ingredient disclosure.** One of the structural problems identified by researchers is that the ingredient labels of many hair relaxer products—particularly older formulations—did not accurately or completely list chemical components, particularly fragrance compounds, which can contain phthalates without disclosure under the existing "fragrance" exemption in cosmetics labeling law. This means that consumers—and even researchers studying exposure—have often been operating with incomplete information about what chemicals are present in the products they are evaluating. Efforts to require full ingredient disclosure and to eliminate fragrance exemptions have been partially advanced by MoCRA but have not been fully implemented.

**The Black community as a disproportionate research subject and disproportionate risk bearer.** The concentration of hair relaxer use in the Black community has meant that the health effects of these products, if real, fall disproportionately on Black women. Research has also raised concerns about early exposure: many Black girls begin using relaxers before puberty, and the Llanos 2022 study's findings about early initiation and more aggressive tumors raise questions about the particular vulnerability of the pre-pubertal hormonal environment to endocrine-disrupting chemical exposures. The breast development window during puberty is a period of heightened susceptibility to hormonal perturbation, which is why exposure timing—not just cumulative dose—may be a critical variable in understanding risk.

**The litigation landscape.** In the years following the major hair relaxer/cancer study publications, a substantial wave of product liability litigation has been initiated against hair relaxer manufacturers, including L'Oréal, Revlon, Dark & Lovely, and others. Plaintiffs allege that manufacturers knew or should have known about the chemical hazards in their products and failed to warn consumers. These cases present complex causation and damages questions, and their outcomes will likely depend on how courts evaluate the epidemiological evidence for specific products and specific disease endpoints. The litigation itself has generated additional investigative reporting and scientific review that has further elevated the issue.

**The distinction between lye and no-lye products.** Lye-based (sodium hydroxide) relaxers are generally considered more chemically aggressive than no-lye alternatives, which use milder alkaline agents. The lye-specific association found in the BWHS is biologically interesting—it may reflect the greater scalp irritation and permeability enhancement that sodium hydroxide-based products produce, which would facilitate greater dermal absorption of co-present endocrine-disrupting chemicals. However, both lye and no-lye products have been found to contain concerning chemicals (parabens, phthalates) and the regulatory and advocacy response has generally not made a sharp lye/no-lye distinction in calling for reform.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes covered this topic in both 2021 and 2022 in a mode that reflects the particular challenge of fact-checking health claims based on real but probabilistic epidemiological evidence. Unlike claims that are straightforwardly true or false, the hair relaxer/breast cancer association requires a treatment that conveys the genuine scientific basis of concern while accurately representing the limitations of observational evidence.

The 2022 Snopes coverage—timed to coincide with the wave of NIH study publications and associated media reporting—functioned less as a debunk than as a science explanation and context article. Snopes' approach in these cases is to present the relevant research accurately, convey what it does and does not establish, and help readers understand the distinction between "this has been studied and associations have been found" and "this has been proven to cause cancer." This is a genre of fact-checking distinct from debunking outright falsehoods: it is evidence synthesis and risk communication.

The fact-checking challenge here is that both over-inflation and dismissiveness are misleading in different directions. An article that says "there is no proven link between hair relaxers and breast cancer" would be technically accurate in the strict causal sense but would seriously misrepresent the epidemiological literature, which does show significant associations that public health researchers take seriously. An article that says "hair relaxers cause breast cancer" would accurately convey the emotional content of the scientific concern but would overstate the certainty of a literature that is still accumulating evidence.

Responsible fact-checking in this territory requires what might be called calibrated affirmation: affirming that the concern is real and evidence-based, specifying what kind of evidence exists and what its limitations are, and resisting both the narrative convenience of a simple true/false verdict and the false balance of treating the evidence as more uncertain than it is.

The racial health disparities dimension also poses a challenge for fact-checking platforms that reach diverse audiences with different relationships to this issue. For many Black women and their advocates, an article that leads with caveats about observational limitations before affirming the evidence base reads as institutional minimization of a serious health threat that has been underfunded and underresearched for decades. For readers with a more sceptical orientation, an article that leads with the risk finding before getting to caveats reads as alarmism. Navigating this without flattening the political context or compromising the scientific communication is genuinely difficult; the best approaches foreground both the evidence and the systemic context from the outset rather than treating them as separate domains.

The FDA's proposed rulemaking on formaldehyde in hair straightening products, the expanding body of Sister Study data, and the ongoing litigation landscape all ensure that this topic will continue to generate new fact-checking obligations as the research and regulatory picture develops. The ideal approach is not a single article but a living record that tracks the evidence as it accumulates—which is precisely the kind of longitudinal editorial commitment that distinguishes serious fact-checking organizations from one-time claim assessors.
