# Israel used ChatGPT to generate targeting lists for military operations


## 1. The Claim

The claim that Israel used ChatGPT to generate targeting lists for its military operations in Gaza became prominent in online discussion in 2024, amplified primarily in technology-focused media, AI ethics commentary, and social media networks concerned with both the Israeli-Palestinian conflict and the ethics of artificial intelligence deployment in warfare. The claim takes several forms, ranging from the most specific — that OpenAI's ChatGPT product was used as an artificial intelligence system to identify and list targets for IDF strikes — to broader formulations that treat ChatGPT as a metonym for large-language-model AI generally: that Israel's military used AI of the type represented by ChatGPT to assist in targeting decisions, and that this AI was contracted from or involved American technology companies.

The most specific version of the claim appears to have emerged from two partially distinct news events that were compressed together in online sharing: first, the April 2024 reporting by the Israeli investigative outlet +972 Magazine and Local Call about the "Lavender" AI targeting system and the related "Gospel" system, which documented in detail how the IDF had deployed machine learning tools to generate large lists of potential targets during the Gaza campaign; and second, reporting from later in 2024 about a contract between the Israeli Ministry of Defense and Microsoft that included access to Azure cloud services, including Azure OpenAI services. The second story — involving Microsoft, Azure, and commercial AI services — was interpreted by many readers and commentators as confirming or elaborating on the first, and "ChatGPT" rapidly became the shorthand for the entire complex of AI-assisted targeting, even though the two stories involve distinct technologies, institutional relationships, and operational functions.

The resulting claim — "Israel used ChatGPT to generate targeting lists" — circulated in activist and advocacy contexts as a concrete, attributable accusation against OpenAI and against ChatGPT specifically, with implications both for how we understand the Gaza campaign and for how we should evaluate the ethics and governance of large language model companies and their commercial contracting.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Lavender and Gospel systems: the AI targeting story that is real.** The +972 Magazine and Local Call reporting published in April 2024 ("Lavender: The AI machine directing Israel's bombing spree in Gaza," +972 Magazine, April 3, 2024) constitutes the most substantively important journalistic contribution to understanding AI use in the Gaza campaign. Based on interviews with current and former Israeli intelligence personnel who asked to remain anonymous, the reporting documented the existence and operational use of two machine learning systems:

*Gospel* (in Hebrew, HaBashora) is an AI-assisted targeting generation system that the IDF has acknowledged using. Its function, per the reporting and subsequent IDF statements, is to identify targets such as buildings, infrastructure, and weapon storage facilities by analysing large volumes of surveillance data, communications intercepts, and prior targeting records. The system generates what the IDF describes as targets for air strikes. Gospel was deployed in earlier operations in Gaza and was reportedly used heavily in the 2023–24 campaign.

*Lavender* is a more controversial system, described in the +972/Local Call reporting as one that generates lists of people — individual human beings — assessed by the AI as warranting lethal targeting on the basis of their alleged associations with Hamas or Palestinian Islamic Jihad. Per the reporting, Lavender generated lists of tens of thousands of potential human targets during the first weeks of the Gaza campaign. The reporting further alleged, on the basis of source testimony, that IDF targeting protocols during parts of this period gave relatively compressed timelines for human review of each Lavender-generated recommendation — in some cases as few as twenty seconds of review per target — effectively delegating substantial targeting authority to the AI system.

The IDF disputed aspects of the +972 reporting, calling it "inaccurate and devoid of operational and technical understanding" in an official statement, while declining to specify which aspects were inaccurate. The reporting nevertheless received significant attention from international law scholars, AI ethics researchers, and military technology observers, and a subsequent report from Amnesty International (2024) examined the IDF's use of AI-assisted targeting and raised questions about compliance with international humanitarian law obligations requiring meaningful human review of targeting decisions.

**Neither Lavender nor Gospel is ChatGPT.** This is the central correction required by the claim under examination. ChatGPT is a conversational large language model product developed by OpenAI and made commercially available beginning in 2022. It is designed for text generation, dialogue, summarisation, coding assistance, and related natural language tasks. It is not a machine learning system designed or trained for military targeting. It does not have access to military intelligence data, satellite imagery, signals intelligence, or the data streams that military targeting systems consume. It is not, and was not, functionally involved in generating the lists reported in the +972 coverage of Lavender.

Lavender, as described in the reporting, appears to be a classification system trained on military intelligence data to identify individuals associated with militant organisations — a purpose-built system with a specific input domain (signals intelligence, network analysis, device tracking) and a specific output (risk scores or targeting flags for individuals). This is a fundamentally different architecture, training domain, and operational function from ChatGPT. Conflating the two because both are "AI" is equivalent to conflating a credit card fraud detection algorithm with a word processor because both run on computers.

**The Microsoft Azure contract: what it actually covered.** In September 2024, reporting by Time Magazine and others confirmed the existence of a contract under the Israeli government's "Project Nimbus" initiative between the Israeli Ministry of Defense and Microsoft providing access to Azure cloud infrastructure, including Azure OpenAI services. This contract — and a parallel one with Google under the same initiative — had been reported on earlier in 2024, provoking employee protests at both Microsoft and Google from workers objecting to providing cloud services to the Israeli military during an active military campaign.

Azure OpenAI services are Microsoft's commercial offerings providing API access to OpenAI models (including GPT-4 and related models) for enterprise customers. These services can be used for a wide range of applications: document processing, internal communications, code generation, logistics support, administrative efficiency, and many other purposes. The contract's existence does not establish that Azure OpenAI services were used for targeting. Microsoft, in response to press inquiries, stated that the contract did not cover offensive military capabilities and that its services were used for "cloud computing capabilities" including data storage, communications infrastructure, and productivity tools.

Whether Microsoft's characterisation is accurate and complete is a matter that the available public record does not decisively resolve. It is possible that Azure OpenAI tools deployed under the contract were used for intelligence processing functions that sit at the edge of targeting-adjacent activity without constituting direct targeting list generation. The absence of definitive public disclosure makes this question genuinely uncertain.

**What the claim gets right.** The kernel of truth in the wider claim is that AI systems — including machine learning models that bear a family resemblance, in their architecture, to the models that underpin ChatGPT — were meaningfully integrated into Israeli military targeting processes during the Gaza campaign, and that these processes raised serious questions about the adequacy of human oversight and compliance with the proportionality and distinction requirements of international humanitarian law. This part of the claim, properly stated, is supported by credible investigative reporting, Israeli military acknowledgements, and the accumulated body of analysis from international law scholars and AI ethics researchers.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**ChatGPT as the universal AI signifier.** ChatGPT's public launch in November 2022 and its rapid emergence as a culturally dominant term for large language model AI created a linguistic environment in which "ChatGPT" functions as a synecdoche for AI broadly — in the same way that "Google" became a verb meaning "to search the internet" irrespective of which search engine is used, or "Xerox" became a verb for photocopying regardless of which machine makes the copy. When people encounter reporting about artificial intelligence being used in military targeting, the mental model most readily accessible is ChatGPT, because ChatGPT is the most publicly discussed and culturally salient AI product. This substitution is cognitively natural but factually confused.

This mechanism — the dominant-example heuristic, in which a category becomes represented in popular cognition by its most visible member, and subsequent instances of the category are assimilated to the representative rather than distinguished from it — is a general feature of how non-expert populations process technology news. It is exploited in disinformation contexts but also operates entirely innocently in the minds of people who encounter accurate reporting about Lavender and accurately understand that AI was used, but whose mental model of AI is ChatGPT, and who therefore, without any deceptive intent, transmit the story in the form "Israel used ChatGPT."

**Epistemic novelty — the governance gap created by dual-use AI infrastructure.** The more important epistemic challenge raised by the Israel case — and largely obscured by the ChatGPT conflation — is the question of what obligations commercial AI providers incur when their services are contracted to military customers for general-purpose infrastructure use. Microsoft's response that Azure did not cover "offensive military capabilities" is formally meaningful but operationally ambiguous: general-purpose cloud infrastructure — storage, computation, communications — can support military operations at every level, including targeting support, without any individual contract clause specifying that it will do so.

This ambiguity creates a governance gap that existing international humanitarian law frameworks and technology company acceptable use policies are inadequately equipped to address (Roff, H.M., 2014, *The Strategic Robot Problem: Lethal Autonomous Weapons in War*, Journal of Military Ethics, 13(3), 211–227). The law of armed conflict's requirement of meaningful human control over targeting decisions was developed in the context of direct human action or clearly bounded weapons systems — not in the context of algorithmic systems that make probabilistic assessments that feed into human decisions in ways difficult to characterise as either purely human or purely automated (Schmitt, M.N. & Thurnher, J.S., 2013, *"Out of the Loop": Autonomous Weapon Systems and the Law of Armed Conflict*, Harvard National Security Journal, 4, 231–281).

The employee protests at Google and Microsoft — organised by workers who objected to Project Nimbus and similar contracts on ethical grounds — represent one mechanism by which private actors are attempting to insert governance into a space where formal law has not yet caught up with technological capability (Paul, K. & Heller, J., reporting in The Guardian, 2024). These protests are epistemically significant not as legal enforcement mechanisms but as evidence that the governance gap is felt as a moral problem by people with direct knowledge of the systems involved.

The displacement of the substantive governance question (what oversight obligations apply to commercial AI providers contracting with military customers during active conflicts?) by the factual confusion (was it ChatGPT?) is itself a media and epistemics phenomenon worth noting. The specific error — attributing targeting functions to a named commercial consumer product — generates a concrete and attributable accusation that is easier to distribute and argue about than the underlying governance question, which is abstract, legally contingent, and genuinely uncertain.


## 4. Verdict

**Distorted but grounded — the specific claim is wrong; the underlying concern it encodes is real.** Israel did not use ChatGPT, the OpenAI conversational product, to generate targeting lists for military operations. This specific claim is a category error arising from the conflation of a named commercial AI product with purpose-built military AI systems that share a broad architectural family resemblance but are entirely distinct in design, training, data access, and operational function.

The underlying concerns that the claim imperfectly encodes are, however, real and significant: AI systems were used in Israeli military targeting processes during the Gaza campaign in ways documented by credible investigative journalism and involving genuine questions about human oversight, algorithmic accountability, and compliance with international humanitarian law. A commercial cloud computing contract between the Israeli Ministry of Defense and Microsoft, which includes access to OpenAI-based services, has been confirmed and has raised genuine questions about the scope and governance of dual-use AI infrastructure in active conflict zones.

The claim, as it typically circulates, substitutes the wrong noun — ChatGPT — for the right concept — AI-assisted targeting — and in doing so both overstates what is attributable to any named commercial product and understates what is actually known about the operational AI systems that were deployed.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The use of machine learning in military targeting is not unique to Israel. The U.S. military's Project Maven — a Department of Defense initiative to apply computer vision machine learning to drone surveillance footage analysis for targeting support purposes — became publicly visible in 2018 when Google engineers protested the company's involvement under a DoD contract, ultimately leading Google to decline contract renewal (Metz, C. & Shane, S., 2018, "Google Will Not Renew Pentagon A.I. Contract", The New York Times). Maven established that large technology companies' willingness to support military AI applications was a contestable policy question, not a settled corporate commitment.

The Israeli military's integration of AI into targeting marks a significant operational evolution in the use of machine learning in warfare, moving beyond surveillance image analysis toward something closer to AI-assisted target generation at scale. This evolution makes the governance questions raised by the Lavender reporting qualitatively different from the questions raised by Maven: not whether AI assists in surveillance, but whether AI effectively generates lists of human beings to kill, with human review reduced to a formality.

International efforts to govern autonomous weapons systems through the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) process at the United Nations have stalled over definitional disputes — particularly over what constitutes "meaningful human control" — without producing binding legal instruments (ICRC, 2021, *Autonomous Weapon Systems: Implications of Increasing Autonomy in the Critical Functions of Weapons*, International Committee of the Red Cross). The gap between the pace of military AI deployment and the pace of governance development is likely to widen in the near term, making cases like the Israeli targeting AI systems significant as precedents for how AI is used in warfare, and therefore for what norms eventually crystallise around such use.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The claim that Israel used ChatGPT for targeting is not formally adjudicated by the major fact-checking platforms, which is why this entry carries the "not-addressed" designation. The underlying story of AI-assisted targeting in Gaza — the Lavender and Gospel systems — was primarily investigated through independent journalism (+972 Magazine, The Guardian, Al Jazeera) and policy and legal analysis rather than through the fact-checking infrastructure. The Microsoft Azure/Project Nimbus contract story was covered by Time, The Guardian, and other news organisations but similarly has not received a formal Snopes or PolitiFact treatment focused on the specific ChatGPT conflation.

The absence of formal fact-check coverage here is partly attributable to the operational complexity of the claim. Fact-checking binary propositions ("did X happen?") is structurally more tractable for major platforms than fact-checking structural category errors ("is the product named in this claim actually the product being discussed?"). The ChatGPT claim is not simply true or false in the usual sense; it is a misidentification that distributes responsibility to the wrong product while the underlying conduct it points toward is documented and significant. Handling that nuance requires more space and analytical context than standard rating infrastructure provides.
