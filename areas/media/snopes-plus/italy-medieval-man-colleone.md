# A medieval Italian condottiero's coat of arms featured testicles


## 1. The Claim

Among the stranger-seeming facts that circulate in historical and art-historical discussion of the Italian Renaissance, one of the more reliably attention-catching concerns a coat of arms. The claim, variously framed, is that a famous Italian mercenary military commander of the fifteenth century had a coat of arms that depicted testicles. In more specific versions, the commander is named as Bartolomeo Colleoni — the condottiere who served Venice for decades and whose equestrian statue by Verrocchio still stands in Venice's Campo Santi Giovanni e Paolo — and the number of pairs is specified as three. In looser versions, the story circulates simply as "a medieval Italian soldier had balls on his coat of arms," sometimes with the added detail that the design represented a play on his name.

The claim appears in art history teaching and survey texts, in popular history writing about the Italian Renaissance, in tourism writing about Bergamo and Venice, in podcasts about Renaissance history and sexual culture, and in the kind of cultural footnote that circulates via social media when users encounter Verrocchio's statue or images of Colleoni monuments. The heraldic claim is often paired with or used to introduce the etymological claim about Colleoni's surname — the two facts reinforce each other and tend to travel together.

This entry treats the heraldic claim as its primary subject. The etymological claim about the surname is addressed in a companion Truth Vault entry on the name's meaning.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The arms are real and well documented.** Bartolomeo Colleoni's coat of arms — the Colleoni family arms, used by him and displayed throughout his monuments and commissions — featured three pairs of testicles arranged in heraldic pattern. The specific blazon describes the arms as bearing six *coglioni* (testicles) arranged in pairs, typically depicted in a field of alternating colours — the exact blazon and colouring varies somewhat across depictions, as was common with heraldic representations before strict standardisation. The arms are visible on multiple surviving monuments and artworks from the fifteenth century.

The most important surviving display of the arms is on the Cappella Colleoni in Bergamo. This funerary chapel, which Bartolomeo commissioned from the sculptor and architect Giovanni Antonio Amadeo and which was begun in 1470, is one of the jewels of northern Italian Quattrocento architecture — a polychrome marble exterior of extraordinary richness, comparing in ambition to the greatest Venetian ducal monuments. The Colleoni arms appear prominently and repeatedly on the chapel's exterior and interior decoration. Visitors to Bergamo who consult the chapel can see the heraldic testicles rendered in marble at one of the most significant architectural commissions of the period. The arms are not hidden or displayed in minor positions but integrated into the main decorative programme.

The arms also appear on the Venetian cenotaph and memorial associated with Colleoni's legacy, on manuscript illuminations from his period, and in heraldic surveys compiled in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries that record Italian noble and mercenary family arms. They have been described and reproduced in art historical literature since the nineteenth century.

**The heraldic tradition: canting arms.** The design of the Colleoni arms follows a well-established tradition of medieval and Renaissance heraldry called canting arms, or in French *armes parlantes* (speaking arms). In this tradition, the visual elements of a coat of arms are chosen to pun on, pictorially represent, or otherwise speak to the bearer's name. The tradition was extremely widespread in medieval and Renaissance European heraldry and operated across the heraldic traditions of England, France, Germany, Italy, Spain, and other European cultures.

English examples are numerous and well known. The Arundel family bore swallows — the word arundel or hirondelle being the Norman-French word for swallow. The Lucy family bore three pike fish — luces being a medieval English term for pike. The Shelley family bore shells; the Bassett family bore bassets (a breed of dog); the Castles family bore castle towers; the Mallet family bore mallets. The Hunt family bore hunting horns; the Bowes family bore bows. Moving to Italy, the Orsini family bore bears (orsi); the Visconti bore serpents (a different tradition, but visually programmatic). The practice was so common that heraldic scholars estimate a substantial fraction of medieval European coats of arms incorporate some element of canting or visual wordplay.

For Colleoni, the canting arms followed the logic of the tradition without deviation: his surname derived from the word for testicles, so the arms bore testicles. The anatomical specificity of the referent made the result memorably explicit, but the heraldic logic was entirely conventional. Colleoni was not doing something eccentric or unprecedented; he was doing what families acquiring arms regularly did in his era, applied to a surname whose etymological meaning happened to be anatomical.

**Who chose the arms?** The design of heraldic arms for a person of Colleoni's status and era was not an accident or an imposition by others. Arms were either inherited (in which case Colleoni would have received them as part of his family identity) or were granted and/or chosen when a person or family acquired armorial bearings. The Colleoni family arms appear to have been in use by the family in some form before Bartolomeo's rise to prominence. Whether Bartolomeo inherited arms with the testicle motif already established, modified existing arms, or commissioned them, he made extensive and prominent use of them throughout his lifetime. A man capable of commissioning the Cappella Colleoni — one of the most expensive architectural projects in late fifteenth-century northern Italy — and of negotiating the terms of his legacy bequest to Venice was unquestionably capable of changing his heraldic symbolism if he had wished to. The continued and prominent use of the testicle arms throughout his career and in his funeral monuments represents an active heraldic choice.

**Contemporaries and the arms.** Renaissance Italy was a culture in which heraldic imagery was widely read and its symbolism understood by educated observers. The canting tradition was familiar enough that the visual pun in the Colleoni arms would have been transparent to anyone who saw them and knew the bearer's name. Contemporary observers and correspondents would have read the arms correctly. That Colleoni continued to use them, had them carved on his funerary chapel, and incorporated them into his artistic commissions — including some of the most prestigious commissions of the period — demonstrates a confident, even aggressive, embrace of the design and its implications.

**The Verrocchio statue and the arms.** Andrea del Verrocchio's equestrian bronze statue of Colleoni in Venice, widely considered one of the greatest works of Renaissance sculpture, depicts the condottiere astride a powerful horse in an attitude of barely contained aggression. The statue does not display the arms directly — it is a portrait monument rather than a heraldic display — but it exists in the context of a man whose visual identity included the testicle heraldry. The statue's famous expression of fierce, almost threatening confidence has led art historians to discuss the monument in terms of the assertion of personal power and virility — themes that the heraldic arms reinforced from a different visual direction.


## 3. Why People Believe This

As with the companion entry on the Colleoni surname, the interesting epistemological question is not why people believe this claim (it is true and they are right to believe it) but rather why it circulates with such persistence and what functions it serves in popular historical discourse.

The primary driver of circulation is that it is genuinely striking. Heraldic arms are usually associated with martial symbols — eagles, lions, swords, chevrons, castles — or with natural imagery — flowers, trees, animals — whose symbolic meaning is either transparent or has been culturally elaborated into a conventional system. The sudden appearance of anatomical genitalia in a heraldic context violates the expectation in a way that is comic and memorable. The violation is what makes it stick.

The secondary driver is that the claim is verifiably true and can be confirmed by anyone who looks at images of the Cappella Colleoni or heraldic surveys of Italian Renaissance arms. It has the quality of a fact that rewards investigation — you can go and see it — which gives it stronger circulation legs than claims that require taking someone's word for it. When a teacher mentions it in an art history class, students can find images immediately on their phones. This confirmability reinforces rather than undermines the claim's spread.

A third driver is the function this kind of material serves in public education about the Renaissance. Renaissance history can acquire a quality of stiff cultural reverence — the period of Botticelli, Michelangelo, Leonardo, Pico della Mirandola — that makes it feel remote and inaccessible to audiences who are not already invested in it. Material that evokes a more visceral, bodily, comic Renaissance helps to humanise the period and make it feel less like a museum. The testicle coat of arms is a reliable access point for audiences who might otherwise find the Quattrocento forbidding.

A fourth driver is the consistent popular appetite for content about the gap between formal historical dignity and underlying human realities. The claim is structurally similar to other popular historical facts that subvert expectations: the sexual slang in Shakespeare's plays, the ribald content of medieval marginalia, the explicit material in Roman frescoes at Pompeii. Each of these instances says, in effect: the past had the same human preoccupations you do, and some of what was considered normal would surprise you. The Colleoni arms fit this category precisely.


## 4. Verdict

**True.** The coat of arms of Bartolomeo Colleoni, the fifteenth-century Venetian condottiere, featured three pairs of testicles in a heraldic design that constitutes a canonical example of canting arms or *armes parlantes* — visual wordplay in which the charges of the arms correspond to the bearer's name. The arms are prominently displayed on the Cappella Colleoni in Bergamo and in other surviving contexts. The design was not accidental, eccentric, or unusual in heraldic terms: it followed a well-established European tradition of name-based visual punning that operated across all the major medieval heraldic traditions.

The EARC classification is **A** (Accurate): the evidence is clear, the sources are strong, and there is no meaningful scholarly controversy about the heraldic facts. The claim requires contextualisation in the canting arms tradition rather than qualification of its core accuracy.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The European canting arms tradition in depth.** The prevalence of canting arms in medieval European heraldry is a subject that heraldic scholars have studied extensively. The best scholarly treatments establish that somewhere between a quarter and a third of medieval English noble arms incorporate at least a partial canting element, and comparable proportions appear in French, German, and Italian heraldry. The tradition reflects the heraldic system's origin in a period of widespread Latin literacy among the administrative class but often oral culture among the general population: visual puns on names encoded the bearer's identity in a form that could be read by those who could not read a written name.

The tradition also reflects the serious importance that medieval and Renaissance culture attached to names and their meanings. Name-day celebrations, the practice of giving children the names of saints under whose protection they would be placed, humanist analysis of the meanings of classical names, and the widespread practice of sobriquets and nicknames — all these reflect a culture that took names as meaningful rather than merely arbitrary labels. Encoding one's name in heraldic imagery was an extension of this cultural attitude toward the significance of names.

The specifically anatomical content of the Colleoni arms sits alongside other heraldic examples that modern audiences might find unexpected. The English family de Neville bore arms featuring billets described by some sources as potentially derived from nail (clou) imagery; the Balliol family bears connection to "ball" cognates; various families bore charges that in their original contexts had quite specific bodily references. The line between conventional heraldic symbolism and bodily reference is not always clear, and the Italian tradition (perhaps influenced by the relative frankness of humanist literary culture about bodily matters) was not uniquely exceptional in allowing anatomical referents into armorial design.

**The Cappella Colleoni as a monument to self-fashioning.** The Cappella Colleoni in Bergamo deserves extended attention beyond its architectural merit. The chapel is a document of an extraordinary act of Renaissance self-presentation. Bartolomeo Colleoni commissioned it as his funerary monument — his mausoleum, effectively — and spared nothing in its elaboration. Giovanni Antonio Amadeo's polychrome marble façade, completed after Colleoni's 1475 death under the supervision of his heirs according to his commission, incorporates sculptural programmes of extraordinary richness: reliefs of ancient Roman triumphs, narrative scenes from scripture, equestrian figures, decorative vocabulary drawn from both the antique and the contemporary Lombard sculptural tradition.

Among all this splendour, the Colleoni arms appear repeatedly. A man who chose to be commemorated through one of the most ambitious architectural commissions of the fifteenth century also chose to have his heraldic testicles carved in marble and installed at the heart of that monument. These two facts together — the grandeur of the chapel and the specificity of the arms — make the Cappella Colleoni a singular document of Renaissance self-fashioning. Colleoni was not embarrassed by the anatomical content of his name and arms. He used his great wealth to ensure it would be legible in marble for centuries.

The chapel also contains an equestrian statue of Colleoni over the tomb itself, by Amadeo — a monument to the warrior's martial identity that parallels the Verrocchio bronze in Venice. Together, the Venice and Bergamo monuments frame Colleoni's legacy in both the city he served and the city of his birth, each asserting his identity with the confident heraldry that the testicle arms encode.

**Renaissance attitudes to the body and to heraldic content.** Popular accounts of the Colleoni arms sometimes imply that Renaissance culture had uniquely relaxed attitudes to sexual and bodily content that explain the design's existence. This is partially true but requires nuance. The Renaissance did inherit from classical antiquity a body of literature, art, and discourse that was frank about anatomical matters in ways that post-Reformation and particularly post-Victorian European culture suppressed or marginalised. Latin poetry was full of explicit content; Italian humanist literature including Boccaccio and Aretino circulated in educated circles with relatively little censorship anxiety in the early and mid Quattrocento; carnival culture and vernacular comedy had robust traditions of bawdy content.

But the heraldic tradition that Colleoni's arms inhabit is older than the Renaissance humanist rehabilitation of explicit content. Canting arms with anatomical referents appear in the medieval period, before the Renaissance's conscious reinvention of classical attitudes toward the body. The Colleoni arms are part of a medieval heraldic tradition, expressed through a Renaissance artistic vocabulary, by a man who happened to have a name with anatomical content. The Renaissance context is not irrelevant, but the explanation for the arms does not depend primarily on Renaissance sexual liberalism — it depends on the simple mechanics of canting heraldry applied to a specific name.

**The arms in art historical scholarship.** The scholarly treatment of the Colleoni arms in Italian art history illustrates how a topic that might seem marginal to serious scholarship has been integrated into substantive historical and art historical discussion. The arms are not merely an amusing footnote: they are evidence relevant to understanding how Colleoni understood and presented his identity, which is in turn relevant to understanding the patronage decisions that produced some of the period's most important works. Scholars including Joanna Woods-Marsden (who has written extensively on Renaissance self-portrait and self-presentation) and art historians working on Verrocchio and Amadeo have addressed the heraldic programme as part of a broader analysis of Colleoni's self-fashioning.

The integration of the arms into serious scholarship models the correct approach to this kind of material: not treating it as a titillating footnote to be whispered, nor treating it as so remarkable that it overwhelms everything else about the subject, but placing it in its proper heraldic, cultural, and biographical context as one element of a rich documentary record.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim has not been formally addressed by English-language fact-checking organisations, which is appropriate given that it is straightforwardly true. It does not appear in the Snopes database, nor has it been addressed by PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, or similar organisations, for the simple reason that there is nothing to check in the sense of determining whether a claim is true or false. The claim is true, the evidence is visually and textually abundant, and no significant counter-narrative exists that would call it into question.

The fact-checking that this claim most needs is of a different and subtler kind: contextualisation that prevents accurate information from being misunderstood. Two misunderstandings deserve specific attention.

The first potential misunderstanding is that the testicle arms were somehow shocking or scandalous in their original context. They were not. The canting arms tradition was normative, the anatomical content of the Colleoni name was transparent to contemporaries, and the heraldic choice was entirely legible within the visual conventions of the period. What reads as surprising or funny to modern audiences did not read as transgressive in the same way to fifteenth-century Italian viewers, for whom the heraldic logic was familiar. Contextualising the arms within the canting tradition, and providing examples of parallel cases from across European heraldry, helps audiences understand that Colleoni was doing something conventional rather than audacious.

The second potential misunderstanding is that the arms are some sort of secret or hidden joke, known only to specialists and invisible to the general public. This misunderstanding is encouraged by the "fun fact" framing in which the claim most commonly circulates — the framing implies that you have been let in on something. But there is no secret: the arms are carved in marble on one of the most visited architectural monuments in Bergamo, they appear in heraldic surveys compiled in the Renaissance itself, and their meaning has been noted in art historical literature since the nineteenth century. What is actually happening when someone encounters the claim as a "fun fact" is not the discovery of a hidden truth but the restoration of a context (the canting arms tradition and the etymology of the surname) that makes the visible fact intelligible. The arms were never hidden; what was missing was the interpretive frame.

A properly contextualized presentation of the claim — which this entry attempts — would affirm the basic truth, explain the canting arms tradition and the surname etymology, situate the arms in the context of Colleoni's broader programme of self-fashioning through patronage, and use the case as an entry point into both the history of Renaissance heraldry and the cultural history of how famous men in the period constructed public identities through visual means. This turns a fun fact into a genuinely illuminating historical lesson rather than simply a source of amusement at a dead man's expense.
