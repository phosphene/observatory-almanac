## The Claim

The claim is precise, memorable, and flatly false: Nintendo's iconic plumber Mario does not say "It's-a me, Mario" — he says "Itsumi Mario," which is Japanese for "Super Mario," indicating that his catchphrase has been heard incorrectly by English-speaking audiences for decades. Some versions of the claim elaborate that "itsumi" means "great" or "superb" in Japanese, which would make the phrase a self-description: "Great Mario," or equivalently, "Super Mario" — the name of the flagship Nintendo game series.

The claim circulated online in English, French, German, and Spanish, attracting millions of views across X, Facebook, TikTok, Reddit, and YouTube. A Spanish-language post amassed over 362,000 views; an X post attracted more than 4.1 million views as of the time of Snopes' reporting (Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com). The broad international uptake suggests the claim succeeded not merely as an anglophone curiosity but as a piece of gaming trivia with cross-cultural appeal. For audiences who had grown up with Mario, his catchphrase was one of the most recognisable sounds in entertainment media — and the idea that it carried a hidden Japanese meaning that pointed self-referentially to the game series title had an elegant intellectual quality that made it satisfying to believe.

Historical examples of the claim on 4chan archives date to June 2020. The most likely populariser of the modern viral form appears to be a September 2021 X post by linguist Jo Fruehwald (@JoFrhwld), who subsequently told Snopes that the post was never intended seriously — it was "just a good post, and an obvious language based joke" (Fruehwald, quoted in Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com). Whether or not this origin story is fully accurate, the claim has since been reproduced millions of times as if it were genuine trivia.

## What's Actually True

Nintendo's catchphrase for Mario is definitively "It's-a me, Mario." The game company has used this phrase consistently across decades of official materials, products, and communications. Nintendo's US website listed a Mario figurine called the "It's-A Me, Mario™! Figure," whose product description explicitly references the phrase: "Includes the voice of Mario with iconic phrases like 'woo-hoo', 'it's-a-me' and many others!" (Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com). Nintendo's websites in multiple countries — the US, UK, France, Canada, and others — reference the "It's-a-me" catchphrase in various contexts.

The voice actor who played Mario for decades, Charles Martinet, embeds the phrase in his own identity. His Instagram username is "charlesmartinetitsame," his bio references "It's a me," and he appeared in a Guinness World Records YouTube video titled "Charles Martinet: It's-A Me, Mario! - Guinness World Records" (December 2018) (Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com). The character's most recent major film appearance, in "The Super Mario Bros. Movie" (2023), also has the catchphrase as "It's-a-me, a-Mario," as confirmed across multiple movie transcript websites including the film's official fan wiki pages (Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com).

The core linguistic claim — that "itsumi" is Japanese for "super" or "superb" — is false on straightforward grounds. Using Google Translate and Japanese dictionary resources: "super" translates to "Subarashī (素晴らしい)" in Japanese; "superb" translates to "Subarashīdesu (素晴らしいです)." Neither of these is "itsumi" (Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com). "Itsumi" in Japanese is attested as both a Japanese surname (written 逸見) and a Japanese female given name (written 逸美), with English translations of "Hemi" and "Itsumi" respectively. Neither meaning has any relationship to "super" or "superb" (Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com, citing Wikipedia on "Itsumi"). "Super Mario" in Japanese translates to "Sūpāmario (スーパーマリオ)" — a straightforward phonetic borrowing into katakana with no lexical relationship to "itsumi."

There are no examples of Nintendo ever using "Itsumi Mario" to refer to the catchphrase on any of its regional websites; a specific Google search of Nintendo's official domains for the phrase returned no results (Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com).

## Why People Believe This

The "Itsumi Mario" claim is an illustrative case of viral misinformation that is false on every specific empirical point but succeeds because it exploits several well-understood mechanisms of belief formation and social sharing.

**The satisfying hidden meaning structure.** The claim belongs to a highly effective category of false trivia: the revelation that a familiar cultural object contains a hidden layer of meaning that retroactively enriches it. "You've been hearing this catchphrase your whole life, but now I can tell you it actually means something more than you thought." This narrative structure is intrinsically appealing. It offers a reward for the believer — an upgrading of their understanding — that does not require any independently verifiable evidence beyond the claim itself. The emotional reward of feeling that you now understand something more deeply than you did is significant enough to substantially reduce the motivation to verify.

**The plausibility of Japanese gaming etymology.** Nintendo is a Japanese company. Mario was created by Japanese designer Shigeru Miyamoto. The possibility that the character's catchphrase contains deliberate Japanese wordplay is not a priori absurd — it is the kind of thing that does happen in gaming culture, where Easter eggs, embedded references, and multilingual puns are part of the craft tradition. The claim exploits genuine cultural knowledge (Nintendo is Japanese; Japanese games often contain cultural layers not immediately accessible to Western audiences) to give a false claim a plausible mechanism. A claim built on a plausible mechanism is substantially harder to disconfirm than one built on no mechanism at all.

**The Italian accent as phonemic ambiguity.** Mario's voice in the games is rendered in a caricatured Italian accent, which exaggerates certain phonemic features and compresses others. "It's-a me, Mario" is itself already slightly unusual phonologically — the "-a" attached to "it's" is a vernacular Italian-accent marker, not standard English pronunciation. For listeners who had never closely attended to the phrase's exact phonology, "Itsumi Mario" as an alternative parse is within the range of plausible auditory misconstrual. The claim does not require a complete auditory hallucination; it only requires that a listener find the reparse ("it's-a-me" → "itsumi") phonemically plausible on first presentation, which many apparently do.

**Epistemic novelty: the origin attribution effect and claim laundering.** One of the least-examined dynamics in viral false trivia is what might be called the claim laundering effect — the way that a claim's origin becomes progressively invisible as it spreads. Jo Fruehwald, by his own account, posted the "Itsumi Mario" content in September 2021 as an obvious joke intended to amuse linguistics-aware followers (Fruehwald, quoted in Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com). Within the context of his academic linguistics account, with a small specialist audience familiar with linguistic jokes, the claim was presumably readable as tongue-in-cheek. As the post spread to general audiences — first through X, then through TikTok, Facebook, Reddit, and YouTube — the contextual markers that signalled its ironic register disappeared. The claim arrived in these new contexts stripped of tone-of-voice cues, in-group linguistic jokes context, and any sense that it was meant humorously. What was posted as a knowing joke by a linguist became, through decontextualisation and amplification, a widely believed piece of video game trivia. This laundering of origin and intent is a structural feature of how social media handles content, not a special pathology of this particular claim.

The earlier 4chan instance from June 2020 — identified by a Snopes reader who contacted the site in May 2026 (Hardinges, 2024/2026, Snopes.com, updated) — predates Fruehwald's post and suggests the joke format may have circulated in specific online subcommunities before achieving mainstream viral spread through the 2021 X post. It is not clear whether Fruehwald independently generated the same joke or was influenced by prior versions.

## Verdict

The claim is false. "Itsumi Mario" is not Mario's actual catchphrase, has never been used by Nintendo or any official source associated with the character, and does not represent a valid Japanese translation of any phrase relating to "super" or "superb." The phrase "itsumi" is a Japanese personal name with no meaning relationship to "super," "great," or "superb" — all of which translate to different Japanese words. "Super Mario" in Japanese is "Sūpāmario," a standard transliteration of the English words into katakana (Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com).

Nintendo consistently uses "It's-a me, Mario" across its products, marketing, and official communications in all languages. The voice actor, Charles Martinet, used the phrase as a self-identifier in his personal social media presence. The 2023 Mario Bros. film scripted the catchphrase as "It's-a-me, a-Mario."

Snopes rated the claim False (Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com). The claim appears to have originated as a deliberate joke in online spaces, possibly on 4chan in June 2020 and then popularised by a September 2021 linguistics scholar's social media post never intended to be taken seriously.

## The Wider Picture

The "Itsumi Mario" claim is more than a simple false trivia item — it is a useful case study in how false origin myths around beloved cultural objects form and persist, in the particular vulnerability of phonetically distinctive accented speech to misparsing, and in how institutional attributions of origin affect the credibility of false claims.

**The false etymology genre in popular culture.** False etymologies and false origin stories for familiar words, phrases, and cultural objects constitute a substantial and well-documented genre of misinformation. Many people believe that "golf" stands for "Gentlemen Only Ladies Forbidden" (it does not; the word's etymology is obscure but not acronymic). Many people believe that "the whole nine yards" derives from the length of a machine gun ammunition belt in World War II aircraft (contested and probably false). Many people believe "rule of thumb" derives from a law permitting a man to beat his wife with a stick no wider than his thumb (false; the phrase's origin is unrelated). In each case, a memorable story about the hidden origin of a familiar phrase provides a satisfying narrative that is intrinsically more compelling than the accurate but typically less dramatic explanation. Research on the psychology of narrative comprehension by Heath, Bell, and Sternberg (2001, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology) found that stories with emotionally evocative or counterintuitive elements are more easily recalled and transmitted than neutral factual content — exactly the property that makes false etymologies and origin stories viral-ready.

**The Italian caricature as phonemic noise.** Mario's Italian accent is a stylised caricature developed by voice actor Charles Martinet in 1990 when he was commissioned to provide voice work for the character. Martinet's rendering — an exuberant, high-pitched Italian-inflected voice — was warmly received and became canonical. The accent involves characteristic features including vowel insertion (the "-a" added to "it's"), a relatively consistent pitch pattern, and a tendency toward exaggerated energy. These features, while immediately recognisable as belonging to the "Mario voice" signature, also introduce phonemic ambiguity: the compressed Italian accent renders "It's-a me" in a form that, under certain listening conditions or with certain prior expectations, is susceptible to mishearing as "itsumi." Mishearing is not randomly distributed: it is shaped by what the listener expects or has been primed to hear. The viral spread of the "itsumi" parsing in 2021-2025 may have induced genuine mishearing in new listeners who encountered the catchphrase after having absorbed the false claim — a case of belief shaping perception rather than perception informing belief.

**Nintendo's linguistic care.** Nintendo as a company is known for meticulous attention to character lore, continuity, and official attribution. The fact that the company has maintained "It's-a me, Mario" as the canonical catchphrase formulation across decades, in products across multiple linguistic markets, and through internal and external documentation is significant. Nintendo's official materials in French Canada, the UK, continental Europe, and the US all use the same phrase. The institutional continuity of the formulation — not just in content but in specific typography, including the trademark symbols in "It's-A Me, Mario™!" — is entirely inconsistent with the "Itsumi Mario" claim. A company that had accidentally been transcribing their character's iconic phrase incorrectly would not have maintained the error uniformly across all markets for thirty-plus years.

**The role of the alleged originator.** The Fruehwald case is worth examining carefully because it illustrates the limits of origin attribution for viral misinformation. Fruehwald told Snopes he may have been the originator of the viral form of the claim, acknowledged it was meant as a joke, and expressed that he did not intend to plant the seeds of an urban legend (Fruehwald, quoted in Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com). The 4chan instance from June 2020, identified in a 2026 follow-up to the Snopes article, predates Fruehwald's 2021 post, suggesting either independent parallel origin or Fruehwald's exposure to the joke in a form he then reproduced unknowingly. Neither scenario is unusual in the ecology of internet memes, where the same joke idea emerges independently multiple times in similar subcultural environments.

What is more broadly notable is the irrelevance of the joke frame to the real-world impact. A claim posted as an ironic joke by someone with a small specialist audience achieves tens of millions of views across multiple languages and is seriously reported as trivia fact in a range of multilingual social media contexts. The intentionality of the originator has no bearing on the downstream effects. This has implications for how we think about responsibility for false information: statements made in jest, in ironic registers, or in contexts where the joke is obvious can become serious misinformation as they travel across contextual boundaries.

**Games, mythology, and fan meaning-making.** The Nintendo gaming community is notable for the density and sophistication of its fan mythology: detailed tracking of lore continuity, debate over canonical interpretations, excavation of original Japanese game text for differences from localised versions, and intensive attention to character history and cultural origin. This community is generally well-equipped to verify gaming-related factual claims — but the "Itsumi Mario" claim spread widely within gaming-adjacent communities precisely because it mimicked the format of legitimate fan-archaeology claims. Legitimate gaming scholarship regularly discovers that Japanese original texts diverge from Western localisations, that characters' names carry specific cultural meanings, and that games contain regional cultural references not accessible without Japanese language knowledge. "Itsumi Mario" mimicked this legitimate pattern so closely that the fraud could not be easily distinguished from the real thing by readers who were not Japanese-language literate.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by Nick Hardinges (Hardinges, 2024, Snopes.com; updated November 2024 and May 2026) demonstrates methodical false-claim verification with strong primary source discipline.

**Official documentation as primary evidence.** The core evidence against the claim is Nintendo's own institutional documentation. Rather than relying on secondary sources or expert opinion, Hardinges checked Nintendo's official websites in multiple countries, found consistent use of "It's-a-me," and documented the absence of any "Itsumi Mario" usage on any Nintendo official domain. This is the correct first step for a claim about what a company's official practices are: check the company's official materials. The specificity of the search — including a documented Google search of Nintendo's domains for the phrase "Itsumi Mario" that returned no results — makes the evidence replicable and transparent.

**Japanese translation verification.** The explicit verification of the linguistic claim — that "itsumi" does not mean "super" or "superb" in Japanese — is necessary and was correctly handled through translation tools and dictionary resources. The documentation of what "itsumi" actually means in Japanese (surname 逸見, female given name 逸美) adds a layer of analysis beyond simple negation: not only does "itsumi" not mean what the claim says, it means something entirely different that has no conceivable relationship to the Mario franchise context.

**Tracking the claim's origin.** The investigation of the claim's origin — identifying Fruehwald's 2021 post through an X archive search, contacting Fruehwald directly for comment, and later updating the article with the earlier 2020 4chan instance — is exemplary investigative practice. Documenting who made a claim and when is important for understanding its genealogy, but it is especially important in cases like this where the claim has a plausible-but-false linguistic-expertise character. Knowing that the originating post was made by a linguistics academic who confirmed it was a joke helps explain the claim's particular credibility profile: it circulated carrying an implicit aura of specialist authority (linguist says Mario's accent encodes a Japanese phrase) that was entirely false.

**Updates and revision.** The two updates to the fact-check — in November 2024 adding Fruehwald's account, and in May 2026 adding the earlier 4chan example — demonstrate responsible living documentation of a fact-check. The Snopes team acknowledged being contacted by a reader with new information, verified the earlier 4chan archive source, and updated the article to reflect the more complete origin picture while noting the residual uncertainty about whether the 4chan instance is the earliest iteration. This is how fact-checking should evolve when new evidence emerges: transparently, with clear attribution of what was added when.
