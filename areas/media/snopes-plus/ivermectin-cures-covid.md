# "Ivermectin Cures COVID-19"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: Ivermectin, an antiparasitic drug used in human and veterinary medicine, is an effective treatment or cure for COVID-19 — the disease caused by the SARS-CoV-2 coronavirus. Under this claim, ivermectin prevents viral replication, reduces disease severity, prevents hospitalisation, and may prevent initial infection if taken prophylactically. Some versions of the claim assert that ivermectin is a cure that public health authorities and pharmaceutical companies have suppressed to protect vaccine market revenues.

**Who made this claim**: The ivermectin-for-COVID claim was not solely a fringe internet phenomenon. It was advocated by some practicing physicians, amplified by several peer-reviewed journal publications (subsequently retracted), endorsed by a small number of governments (notably El Salvador, Bolivia, and Peru at various points during the pandemic), and promoted by physician advocacy organisations including the Frontline COVID-19 Critical Care Alliance (FLCCC). In the United States, tens of thousands of prescriptions for ivermectin were written outside approved indications during peak periods of the pandemic. In some countries, the drug was distributed through official channels for COVID-19 ahead of or in lieu of vaccines.

**The livestock pharmacy phenomenon**: One of the more visible markers of the claim's impact was the surge in purchases of ivermectin formulated for horses and livestock from farm supply retailers. Because prescription ivermectin for humans became difficult to obtain (pharmacies were declining to fill off-label prescriptions), many people purchased the veterinary paste formulation, which is physically identical in active compound but manufactured at much higher concentrations and with different binders. Poison control centres in the United States recorded significant spikes in ivermectin exposure calls during 2021, associated with self-medication with veterinary formulations (Morabito et al., 2021, *Morbidity and Mortality Weekly Report*).

**Scope of the claim**: The most aggressive version of the ivermectin claim was not restricted to therapeutic treatment of infected patients but extended to mass prophylaxis — populations taking low doses on regular schedules to prevent infection at the population level. This version was implemented or unofficially encouraged in parts of Brazil, India, Bangladesh, Zimbabwe, and several Latin American countries. The claim that regions using ivermectin prophylaxis showed lower death rates was a key piece of evidence cited by advocates; this analysis, as examined below, has significant methodological problems.

**The suppression narrative**: The ivermectin claim was embedded within a broader suppression narrative asserting that ivermectin, as an inexpensive off-patent drug, was being systematically suppressed by regulatory agencies, pharmaceutical companies, and captured scientific journals because its efficacy would reduce demand for COVID-19 vaccines (which were profitable) (Kory & Meduri, 2021, *Journal of Intensive Care Medicine* — see below for retraction and critique). This narrative significantly increased the claim's resistance to correction — evidence against ivermectin's efficacy could be reframed as evidence of successful suppression, making contrary evidence unfalsifiable within the narrative's own logic.

**The significance of this claim's arc**: The ivermectin story is not merely about one wrong treatment. It is a case study in the epistemology of medical evidence in crisis conditions — how preliminary findings get laundered into confident claims, how social networks accelerate belief adoption ahead of evidence, how motivated reasoning can persist in the face of well-designed refutations, and how the sociology of medical authority both enables and constrains the correction of dangerous misinformation.


## 2. What's Actually True

**What ivermectin actually is**: Ivermectin is a semisynthetic compound derived from avermectin, discovered by Satoshi Ōmura and William Campbell (shared Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 2015). It was developed in the 1970s and became one of the most impactful antiparasitic drugs in human history. It is highly effective against a wide range of parasitic organisms including the nematodes responsible for river blindness (onchocerciasis) and lymphatic filariasis, as well as scabies and head lice. Its mechanism against parasites involves binding to glutamate-gated chloride channels unique to invertebrate nervous systems, causing paralysis and death in parasites with negligible direct human toxicity at therapeutic doses.

**The in vitro finding that started it all**: In April 2020, Caly et al. published a study in *Antiviral Research* demonstrating that ivermectin inhibited SARS-CoV-2 replication in a laboratory cell culture setting. This was a real finding — in the specific conditions of the experiment (Vero-hSLAM cells, specific viral strain, specific drug concentrations), ivermectin did reduce viral replication. The problem, which was noted in the scientific community almost immediately, was that the concentrations required to produce the antiviral effect in cell culture were approximately 35 to 100 times higher than the concentrations achievable in human lung tissue at safe therapeutic doses (Caly et al., 2020, *Antiviral Research*; Schmith et al., 2020, *Clinical Pharmacology & Therapeutics*). The pharmacokinetic gap between in vitro efficacy and achievable in vivo tissue concentrations is a standard problem in early drug discovery — most compounds that look good in cell culture fail for exactly this reason — but it received inadequate attention in popular reporting on the finding.

**The wave of early observational studies**: Following the Caly et al. laboratory finding, a surge of clinical studies examined ivermectin in COVID-19 patients. Many appeared to show benefit. The problem was that these studies were overwhelmingly small, observational (not randomised), and located in settings where confounding variables were difficult to control. A substantial number were plagued by data irregularities. Elgazzar et al. (Egypt, 2020, preprint) — one of the most-cited positive studies — was retracted following a scientific integrity investigation that found duplicated data, patient records inconsistent with the study's timeline, and statistical distributions inconsistent with real data collection (Davey, 2021, *The Guardian* — reporting on the Elgazzar retraction; subsequent retraction confirmed by the journal). Other positive studies showed similar problems.

**The Surgisphere scandal**: Among early ivermectin advocacy's most publicised validation failures was the involvement of Surgisphere Corporation, a data analytics firm that supplied data for multiple high-profile COVID-19 preprints, several of which were subsequently retracted when Surgisphere's database was found to contain implausible anomalies. While Surgisphere's direct role in ivermectin studies was limited, the broader episode — in which unverified data sources drove global treatment decisions during a pandemic — highlighted the fragility of the observational evidence base.

**Systematic reviews of contaminated evidence**: When meta-analyses were performed on the early ivermectin trial literature, they initially appeared to show benefit — because the included studies themselves were infected with the same biases and data problems as the individual trials. Hill et al. (2021, *Open Forum Infectious Diseases*) published a meta-analysis showing benefit that was subsequently undermined by the removal of studies found to contain fabricated data. When fraudulent or severely biased studies were removed, the positive signal largely disappeared. This was a documented case of garbage-in, garbage-out in meta-analytic synthesis.

**The definitive randomised controlled trials**: Beginning in late 2021 and continuing through 2022, large, well-designed, placebo-controlled randomised controlled trials (RCTs) began reporting results. These study designs, with adequate sample sizes and pre-registered protocols, are the gold standard for establishing clinical efficacy:

- **TOGETHER trial** (Brazil, 2022): A multi-armed adaptive platform trial including ivermectin vs. placebo in 1,358 patients with early COVID-19. Result: ivermectin was not superior to placebo for preventing hospitalisation or prolonged emergency care (Reis et al., 2022, *New England Journal of Medicine*).
- **ACTIV-6 trial** (United States, 2022): A decentralised placebo-controlled RCT of ivermectin in outpatients with mild COVID-19. Result: no significant reduction in duration of symptoms compared with placebo (Naggie et al., 2022, *New England Journal of Medicine*).
- **PANORAMIC trial** (United Kingdom, 2023): A large platform trial of multiple COVID-19 treatments including ivermectin in primary care patients. Result: no significant benefit on primary outcomes (Butler et al., 2023, *PLOS Medicine*).

These three trials alone enrolled more than 5,000 participants under rigorous conditions. Their results were consistent with each other and with subsequent meta-analyses restricted to high-quality trials.

**The Cochrane review conclusion**: The Cochrane Collaboration, whose systematic reviews are widely regarded as the highest standard of evidence synthesis in medicine, published a systematic review of ivermectin for COVID-19 (Popp et al., 2022, *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*). The conclusion: "Based on the current very low- to low-certainty evidence, we are uncertain about the efficacy and safety of ivermectin used to treat or prevent COVID-19. The completed studies are small and few are considered high quality. Several studies are still ongoing that may help resolve the remaining uncertainty here." As high-quality trials have since accumulated, subsequent analyses have confirmed no demonstrated clinical benefit.

**The parasitic worm confound hypothesis**: One residual hypothesis offered to explain why some early observational studies in tropical countries showed apparent benefit is the strongyloides confound. In regions with high endemic burden of intestinal parasites — particularly Strongyloides stercoralis — COVID-19 patients may benefit incidentally from the antiparasitic action of ivermectin, not through any antiviral effect but because deworming concurrent with COVID-19 infection reduces a confounding source of immunosuppression (Choudhary et al., 2022, *Open Forum Infectious Diseases*). This hypothesis would explain some apparent benefits in tropical settings without requiring any antiviral activity of ivermectin. It remains plausible but is not the cure claim.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty — the evidence time-lag crisis and motivated inference**:

The ivermectin-COVID story is one of the most important case studies in the epistemology of pandemic medicine because it exposes in unusually visible form a structural vulnerability of evidence-based medicine under crisis conditions: the evidence-generation pipeline operates on a ten-to-twenty-four-month timescale; pandemics demand therapeutic decisions on a two-to-four-week timescale. This mismatch is not a failure of any individual actor — it is a systemic property of how high-quality clinical evidence is produced.

When Caly et al. published their in vitro findings in April 2020, there was no diagnostic test yet available for most of the world, no approved treatment, no vaccine, and a terrifying new pathogen killing tens of thousands per week. In that environment, the rational threshold for taking action on a promising in vitro signal was dramatically lowered for everyone — physicians, patients, policymakers, and advocates. This is not irrational: in a genuine emergency where no alternative exists, preliminary evidence rationally carries more decision weight than it would in normal times. The error was not that preliminary evidence was taken more seriously during the acute emergency — it was that this evidential promotion was never adequately reversed as higher-quality evidence became available.

The second novel dimension is how the suppression narrative functioned epistemically. The conspiracy claim — that ivermectin's efficacy was being suppressed to protect vaccine revenues — is not merely false; it is epistemically structured to be unfalsifiable. Under this narrative, evidence of efficacy confirms the claim; evidence against efficacy is reinterpreted as evidence of successful suppression. This renders the claim immune to ordinary empirical refutation within its own framework. People who adopted the suppression narrative were not simply making an empirical error that new evidence could correct; they had adopted an epistemic framework that preemptively discredited the mechanism by which their belief could be corrected. Reaching these people required addressing the epistemic framework, not simply adducing more clinical data.

The third novelty is the role of data fraud. At least one highly influential positive ivermectin study (Elgazzar et al.) appears to have been based on fabricated data. This is significant not merely as an instance of research misconduct but because fabricated research, when it appears to confirm a popular prior belief in a high-stakes context, has an asymmetric impact on belief revision. The initial publication of false positive data influences beliefs across a wide population before any retraction. Retractions are published in technical journals read by specialists; the original claim spreads through social media, word of mouth, and popular media to audiences who never encounter the retraction. By the time the Elgazzar study was retracted, it had been cited thousands of times and had entered the popular understanding of the evidence base. Retractions do not propagate at the same speed or breadth as original claims.

The fourth dimension involves the sociology of the communities that advocated for ivermectin. The FLCCC and similar groups included credentialed physicians with genuine clinical experience in intensive care. This credential-lending was important: it allowed the claim to circulate with the authority of medical expertise rather than purely as lay internet rumour. When physicians with real credentials and real trauma care experience asserted confidently that ivermectin was lifesaving, patients and non-specialist physicians had limited tools for evaluating whether those specific claims were supported by the evidence those credentialed individuals cited. Authority signalling and credential presentation substitute for evidence quality in environments where evidence quality is not easily evaluable by the audience.

**The Mexico City and Uttar Pradesh apparent successes**: During the pandemic, several jurisdictions that distributed ivermectin at scale appeared to show subsequent reductions in COVID-19 deaths. These natural experiments were cited widely by ivermectin advocates. Subsequent analyses found that the timing confounds these claims: case reductions coincided with vaccination rollouts, prior immunity from earlier wave exposure, changes in variant virulence characteristics, and other public health interventions. The apparent correlations between ivermectin distribution and outcome were real but attributable to confounders rather than drug effect — a textbook instance of the ecological fallacy, where population-level correlation between two time series is mistaken for individual-level causal effect.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE**: Ivermectin does not cure COVID-19. Large, well-designed, randomised controlled trials conducted across multiple countries and clinical contexts have consistently found no clinically meaningful benefit from ivermectin treatment for COVID-19and outcomes including viral clearance, symptom duration, hospitalisation, or death.

**What the evidence shows**: Ivermectin has in vitro antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2 at concentrations approximately 35–100× above achievable therapeutic levels. At human therapeutic doses, no consistent benefit across any validated clinical endpoint has been demonstrated in high-quality trials. The Cochrane review and the results of TOGETHER, ACTIV-6, and PANORAMIC provide the strongest and most reliable evidence summary.

**What is not in dispute**: Ivermectin is a real and genuinely important medicine. It has saved millions of lives from parasitic disease, and its discovery merited a Nobel Prize. The question of its COVID-19 efficacy is entirely separate from its established antiparasitic indications, and the conclusory evidence against its COVID-19 efficacy does not diminish its value for the diseases it demonstrably treats.

**The suppression narrative is false**: There is no credible evidence that health authorities or pharmaceutical companies suppressed evidence of ivermectin's COVID-19 efficacy. Large publicly-funded trials specifically designed to test ivermectin were funded by governments and academic institutions that had no financial interest in vaccines (notably the UK PANORAMIC trial and the US ACTIV-6 trial). Their negative results were published in premier medical journals without suppression. If there had been a genuine ivermectin-vaccination conflict of interest, the NIH — which co-funded ACTIV-6 — would have been suppressing its own trial results.

**The residual clinical questions**: The strongyloides confound hypothesis — that apparent benefits in some tropical settings reflect antiparasitic rather than antiviral action — has not been definitively tested in a dedicated trial. And the broader question of whether there exist patient sub-populations or viral strain interactions where some small benefit might exist remains technically open, though existing trials have found no signal in the populations they enrolled.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The history of repurposed antiparasitic drugs in virology**: Ivermectin is not the first antiparasitic drug to generate speculation about antiviral activity. Chloroquine and hydroxychloroquine — antiparasitic and antimalarial drugs — were also advocated for COVID-19 early in the pandemic on similarly weak preliminary evidence and were similarly refuted by large RCTs (RECOVERY trial, UK, 2020; WHO Solidarity trial, 2020 — Horby et al., *New England Journal of Medicine*, 2020). The parallel is instructive: repurposed drugs with known biological activity and acceptable safety profiles create a predictable pattern of premature advocacy driven by the same combination of in vitro plausibility, observational signal, and motivated reasoning.

**The infodemic concept and WHO response**: The WHO introduced the term "infodemic" to describe the phenomenon of excess information — including false information — about COVID-19 circulating alongside the actual pandemic. Ivermectin advocacy was among the most significant components of the COVID infodemic in terms of direct behavioural consequence (measurable surge in harmful self-medication with veterinary preparations) and policy consequence (adoption as formal treatment policy in multiple countries). The WHO issued explicit guidance against ivermectin for COVID-19 outside of clinical trials, as did the FDA and EMA, but this guidance did not terminate popular advocacy. The regulatory posture of "don't use outside trials" was widely reinterpreted by advocates as confirmation of institutional suppression.

**The self-medication harm**: While human ivermectin at prescription doses has an excellent safety profile, the shift to veterinary formulations produced real harm. The American Association of Poison Control Centers reported a 24-fold increase in ivermectin exposure calls in August 2021 compared with August 2019 (National Poison Data System, as reported by CDC, 2021). Veterinary paste ivermectin contains much higher concentrations than human therapeutic doses, and improper self-dosing resulted in ivermectin toxicity including gastrointestinal symptoms, confusion, sedation, and in rare cases hospitalisation. These were direct iatrogenic harms flowing from a false medical claim.

**The media responsibility debate**: The ivermectin controversy reignited significant debate about how science journalists should cover preliminary medical findings during public health emergencies. Many early reports on the Caly in vitro study used language implying clinical applicability ("ivermectin may be COVID treatment") that the scientific caveats in the paper did not support. The subsequent widespread adoption of ivermectin advocacy, driven partly by these early media framings, illustrates how the translation layer between scientific literature and popular media can selectively amplify promising signals while failing to communicate the distance between in vitro findings and clinical utility.

**The scientific community's self-correction**: Despite the failures in early evidence quality and media framing, the scientific community did eventually produce the high-quality trials needed to resolve the question. TOGETHER, ACTIV-6, PANORAMIC, and the Cochrane review represent the evidence-generation apparatus working as intended — if more slowly than the crisis demanded. The lesson for future pandemic preparedness is not that the system failed entirely, but that mechanisms for rapidly generating high-quality evidence (adaptive platform trials, pre-established trial networks, fast Cochrane review pathways) need to be operating at the start of a pandemic rather than being assembled during it.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Snopes rating**: Snopes rates the core ivermectin-cures-COVID claim as false, with detailed historical analysis of the evidence lifecycle from the 2020 Caly in vitro finding through the 2022–2023 large RCT results. The Snopes analysis addresses both the direct efficacy claim and the suppression narrative, rating the suppression claim as lacking any credible supporting evidence. Snopes notes that the claim's longevity reflects social and political factors rather than any ambiguity in the mature scientific evidence.

**Full Fact and Reuters fact checks**: Full Fact (UK) and Reuters Fact Check both addressed ivermectin-COVID multiple times during the pandemic, initially at a phase when evidence was genuinely uncertain and more recently when large trials had settled the question. The evolution of their assessments — from "no evidence of benefit and risk of harm from self-medication" to "large trials show no benefit" — illustrates responsible updating of fact-checker positions as evidence matures.

**PolitiFact coverage**: PolitiFact rated ivermectin claims by public figures as False or Pants On Fire depending on the specificity and certainty of the assertion, consistently applying the standards of evidence-based medicine's RCT hierarchy rather than deferring to the volume of advocacy or the credentials of advocates.

**The ongoing challenge of correcting motivated belief**: Perhaps the most challenging fact-checking dimension of the ivermectin story is that the definitive refutation by large trials has not substantially changed belief among populations who adopted the claim within a suppression narrative framework. Survey data from the United States suggest that a meaningful fraction of adults retain belief in ivermectin's COVID-19 efficacy despite the convergent evidence against it — a pattern consistent with motivated reasoning research showing that corrections are more effective for people with low prior commitment to the corrected position than for those with strong prior commitment (Nyhan & Reifler, 2010, *Political Behavior*; Swire-Thompson et al., 2020, *Cognition*). The ivermectin case suggests that for deeply motivated health beliefs embedded in conspiratorial frameworks, fact-checking alone is an insufficient response, and the more productive intervention target is the epistemic infrastructure — media literacy, science communication norms, support for adaptive platform trial infrastructure — rather than the specific claim.
