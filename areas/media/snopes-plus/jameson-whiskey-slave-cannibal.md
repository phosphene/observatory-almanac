# Jameson Whiskey Heir and the Cannibal Sketches: Colonial Horror and the Limits of Fact-Checking

## 1. The Claim

The claim, as it has circulated in social media and various online retellings, is stark and stomach-turning: James Sligo Jameson, heir to the Jameson Irish whiskey fortune, once purchased a slave girl in Africa specifically so that he could watch her be murdered and cannibalized. The story, which resurfaced prominently on social media in 2021, carries the weight of a family name still visible on liquor shelves worldwide. It is the kind of accusation that seems almost too grotesque to be true — and yet its documented provenance in nineteenth-century colonial records makes it impossible to dismiss as fabrication. The Snopes fact-check, published in June 2021 by Dan MacGuill and updated in January 2023, rates the claim as a "Mixture," acknowledging that elements of the story are demonstrably true while others remain contested or unverifiable. This rating, while technically defensible, warrants closer examination than a simple binary of "true" or "false" can provide.

The claim as popularly stated contains several embedded assertions: that Jameson was a member of the Jameson whiskey dynasty (true — he was the grandson of John Jameson, the founder of the brand); that he was in Africa (true — he participated in the Emin Pasha Relief Expedition of 1886–1888); that he witnessed the murder and cannibalization of a young girl (true, by his own account); that he paid for the act to occur (contested — the causal link between his payment of handkerchiefs and the murder is the central dispute); and that he did so out of a morbid desire to witness and sketch the act (the most incendiary element, supported by the testimony of a translator named Assad Farran but denied by Jameson himself). Each of these layered assertions carries different evidentiary weight, and unpacking them requires navigating a documentary record that is fragmentary, self-interested, and profoundly shaped by the colonial context in which it was produced.

The popular framing of the claim — that Jameson "bought a slave girl to watch her get cannibalized" — compresses a complex sequence of events into a headline that is gripping but imprecise. The compression matters. It matters because the imprecision is where the most important historical questions reside. Did Jameson's payment of six handkerchiefs constitute a "purchase" of a human being for the purpose of witnessing her death? Or was it, as Jameson claimed, a casual gift to a man who then perpetrated an act Jameson did not anticipate? The distance between these two characterizations is vast — the difference between a man who commissioned a murder and a man who, through callous indifference and colonial entitlement, enabled one. Snopes recognizes this distinction but ultimately lands on a "Mixture" rating that, while not wrong, perhaps understates the moral gravity of even the most charitable reading of events.

## 2. What's Actually True

What is not in dispute — what cannot be in dispute, because Jameson himself admitted it in his own journal — is that James Sligo Jameson witnessed the brutal murder and cannibalization of a young girl in what is now the Democratic Republic of the Congo in 1888. This fact alone is horrifying enough to warrant attention, regardless of the precise degree of Jameson's agency in bringing it about. Jameson's own words, preserved in his journal and later published by his widow, describe the scene with a clinical detachment that is itself disturbing, even as he insists on his own horror.

According to Jameson's account, he had been expressing skepticism about the prevalence of cannibalism in the region — dismissing it, he said, as stories told to extract goods from European travelers. A man identified in Jameson's account as an "Arab" (likely a Swahili-speaking trader or intermediary in the Congo Arab zone of influence) reportedly responded to Jameson's skepticism with the phrase: "Give me a bit of cloth and see." Jameson, by his own admission, then provided six handkerchiefs — a form of trade cloth that functioned as currency in the Congo basin during this period. What followed was, in Jameson's own words, "the most horribly sickening sight I am ever likely to see in my life." A girl was stabbed to death and dismembered, and the body was cannibalized by those present.

Jameson's defense rests on the claim that he did not believe the man's proposition was serious. He described it as a "ruse to get money out of me" — implying that he thought he was being scammed for cloth, not that he was commissioning an act of murder. He insisted he had no sketching materials with him at the time and that events unfolded too quickly for him to have drawn anything even if he had wished to. This is a critical point of divergence between Jameson's account and that of Assad Farran, the Syrian translator who accused Jameson of premeditated voyeurism. Farran claimed Jameson made six sketches during the killing and later painted them with watercolors — a detail that, if true, would dramatically undercut Jameson's profession of horror and spontaneous helplessness.

Jameson was in the Congo as a member of the Emin Pasha Relief Expedition, a venture that was itself a product of late-nineteenth-century European colonial ambition. The expedition, led by Henry Morton Stanley, was nominally organized to relieve Emin Pasha, the governor of Equatoria (then a southern province of Egyptian Sudan), who had been cut off by the Mahdist uprising. But the expedition also served as a vehicle for European exploration, territorial assertion, and resource assessment in the Congo basin — activities that were inseparable from the brutal colonial extractive machinery then being constructed by King Leopold II of Belgium. Jameson's presence in the region was not accidental; he was there as a man of wealth and standing participating in a project of imperial reach. He was 31 years old when he died of "haematuric fever" (likely typhoid) in Bangala in August 1888, before the cannibalism allegations against him became public.

The documentary record, then, establishes beyond reasonable doubt the following facts: Jameson was in the Congo as part of a colonial expedition. He witnessed the murder and cannibalization of a girl. He provided six handkerchiefs to a man shortly before the act occurred. He documented the event in his journal. He died before the allegations of premeditation became public. These facts are not in serious dispute. What is in dispute — and what Snopes correctly identifies as the contested core of the claim — is whether Jameson's payment of handkerchiefs constituted a knowing commission of murder, whether he watched with morbid intent, and whether he sketched the act as it occurred.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes' fact-check of this claim is, in its broad strokes, responsible and well-researched. Dan MacGuill's article correctly identifies the key primary sources — Jameson's own journal, published by his widow Ethel in 1891; Assad Farran's affidavit of March 1890, published in The Times on November 14, 1890; Farran's earlier retraction of September 1888; and Jameson's final telegram to his wife denying the allegations. The article accurately represents the "Mixture" rating by acknowledging that Jameson witnessed the event and provided the handkerchiefs while noting that his account of his own intentions is contested. Snopes is also appropriately cautious about the reliability of each source, noting that Farran's retraction and subsequent affidavit create a deeply contradictory evidentiary record.

Where Snopes excels is in its refusal to flatten the narrative. The article does not simply declare Jameson guilty or innocent; it presents the competing accounts and allows the reader to grapple with their contradictions. This is the appropriate posture for a fact-check of an event that occurred nearly 140 years ago in a colonial context where the documentary record is inherently compromised by power asymmetries, self-interest, and the impossibility of independent verification. The article also correctly notes the significance of Jameson's own admission — that he provided the handkerchiefs and witnessed the act — while distinguishing this from the stronger claim that he acted with premeditated intent to cause the murder.

However, the Snopes article falls short in several important respects that are worth examining in detail.

First, the article does not sufficiently interrogate the colonial context that shaped the production and preservation of the documentary record. Every primary source in this case was produced by or filtered through European colonial actors. Jameson's journal is a colonial document, written by a man with every reason to minimize his own culpability. Farran's testimony, while coming from a Syrian translator rather than a European, was nonetheless produced within a colonial legal and journalistic framework — his affidavit was signed in London, published in The Times, and shaped by the expectations of British metropolitan audiences. The voices of the Congolese people who actually committed and witnessed the act — the man who said "Give me a bit of cloth and see," the girl who was murdered, the community in which these events unfolded — are entirely absent from the record. Snopes treats the European documentary sources as if they exist in an evidentiary vacuum, without acknowledging that the colonial context systematically privileged European accounts over African ones.

Second, the article's framing of Farran's retraction is arguably too even-handed. Snopes notes that Farran first told the story, then retracted it, then signed an affidavit contradicting his retraction — and observes that this sequence "poses more questions than it answers." This is true, but the article does not adequately explore the pressures that may have led to Farran's retraction. Farran was a Syrian translator working within a British colonial expedition — a man of precarious standing whose livelihood depended on the goodwill of European patrons. William Bourdett-Coutts, the MP who publicized the retraction, claimed Farran admitted fabricating the story because he was angry at having been fired by Edmund Barttelot, another expedition leader. But this explanation itself raises questions: if Farran fabricated the story out of spite, why would he later sign a detailed affidavit restating the fabrication? And if the retraction was coerced or purchased — as seems at least plausible given the enormous reputational stakes for the Jameson family and the broader colonial project — then the retraction should be weighted less heavily than Snopes treats it. The article notes the contradiction but does not sufficiently analyze the power dynamics that may have produced it.

Third, and most critically, Snopes does not engage with the question of what Jameson's own account actually reveals about his moral culpability, even on its most charitable reading. Jameson admitted that he provided six handkerchiefs to a man who said "Give me a bit of cloth and see" — a phrase that, on its face, clearly proposed an exchange of cloth for a demonstration of cannibalism. Jameson's defense is that he did not believe the proposal was serious, that he thought it was "a ruse to get money out of me." But this defense is extraordinarily thin. Jameson was a man of considerable education and colonial experience; he had been in Africa long enough to know that cannibalism was practiced in certain contexts in the region. His skepticism about its prevalence does not equate to a reasonable belief that a man proposing to demonstrate cannibalism in exchange for cloth was merely joking or scamming. The very fact that Jameson provided the cloth — by his own admission — means that he, at minimum, was willing to entertain the possibility that something would happen. His claim that he did not believe it "until the last moment" is not exculpatory in the way Snopes implicitly treats it. It is, rather, a confession that he provided the means for an act he considered plausible enough to pay for.

This is the critical gap in Snopes' analysis: it treats the question of "intent" as a binary — either Jameson intended to cause the murder, or he did not — without considering the spectrum of moral culpability that exists between deliberate commission and callous indifference. Jameson may not have set out that morning with the intention of purchasing a human being for slaughter. But he paid a man who proposed to kill and cannibalize a person, and then he stood by and watched as the act was carried out. His own journal suggests he did not intervene, did not protest, and did not attempt to prevent the murder even when it became clear what was about to happen. Whether or not he sketched the act — and the dispute over this point remains unresolved — his passive complicity is not in doubt. Snopes' "Mixture" rating, while technically accurate, does not adequately convey this moral reality.

Fourth, the Snopes article does not address the broader historiographical context of the Emin Pasha Relief Expedition and its role in the colonial project in the Congo. The expedition itself was deeply implicated in violence against local populations — Stanley's expeditions were notorious for their brutality, and the Congo basin was being actively transformed into a site of extractive colonial violence that would, within a decade, become the personal fiefdom of King Leopold II and the scene of one of the great atrocities of the modern era. Jameson's actions cannot be fully understood in isolation from this context. He was not a detached observer who happened to witness an atrocity; he was a participant in a colonial project that systematically devalued African lives and treated African bodies as instruments of European curiosity, commerce, and ambition. The handkerchiefs he paid were not merely a personal transaction — they were a microcosm of the broader colonial economy of extraction in which human lives were traded for trifles.

Finally, the article's engagement with the question of the sketches is cursory. The question of whether Jameson sketched the act is treated as a factual dispute — Farran says he did, Jameson says he did not — without examining what the existence or absence of sketches would mean for the broader assessment of Jameson's culpability. Sketches would indicate premeditation and voyeuristic intent; their absence would support Jameson's claim of spontaneous horror. But the sketches themselves, if they existed, have never been produced. Farran claimed they were painted with watercolors and later shown to members of the expedition. Jameson's widow, who published his journal and had every reason to produce or deny the existence of sketches, did not mention them in her 1891 publication. The absence of the sketches from the documentary record is itself significant — but Snopes does not explore this absence as evidence.

## 4. Why This Matters

The Jameson cannibalism story matters on several levels, each of which illuminates different facets of the relationship between colonial history, corporate legacy, and the limits of fact-checking as an epistemological practice.

At the most immediate level, the story matters because it concerns a real human being — a girl, approximately ten years old according to Farran's affidavit — who was murdered, dismembered, and cannibalized. Her name is not recorded. Her voice does not appear in any of the primary sources. She exists in the historical record only as an object of European observation and dispute — a fact that is itself a form of violence, a second erasure that compounds the first. Every retelling of this story, including this one, risks perpetuating that erasure by centering the experience of the perpetrator rather than the victim. The Snopes fact-check, by focusing on the question of Jameson's intent, inadvertently reproduces this dynamic: the girl becomes a MacGuffin in a story about a white man's culpability, rather than a person whose death demands recognition and mourning. Any responsible engagement with this history must begin by acknowledging this structural asymmetry and attempting, however imperfectly, to recentre the humanity of the victim.

The story also matters because the Jameson brand remains a global commercial presence. Walk into any liquor store in the world and you will find Jameson Irish Whiskey on the shelf. The brand, now owned by Pernod Ricard, trades on its heritage — the John Jameson name, the Bow Street distillery, the family legacy. The continued commercial success of a brand bearing the name of a family that produced a man who, at minimum, paid for and watched the murder and cannibalization of a child is a fact that deserves contemplation. This is not to suggest that the brand bears direct responsibility for the actions of a long-dead family member — corporate moral liability does not extend infinitely backward. But the selective memory of heritage branding is itself a form of historical manipulation. Brands that invoke their founders' names for commercial benefit cannot credibly claim that the darker aspects of those families' histories are irrelevant. The Jameson story is a test case for how corporate heritage intersects with historical atrocity, and the fact that it remains largely unknown to the average consumer of Jameson whiskey is itself a form of selective forgetting.

More broadly, the story matters because it exposes the structural limitations of fact-checking as applied to historical events. Snopes' "Mixture" rating is, in a narrow sense, correct: some elements of the claim are true and others are contested. But this rating obscures more than it reveals. The "Mixture" framing implies that the claim is a composite of true and false elements, as if the truth and falsehood could be neatly separated and weighed against each other. In reality, the claim exists within a zone of radical evidentiary uncertainty — a zone shaped by colonial power dynamics, self-interested testimony, documentary gaps, and the impossibility of independent verification. No rating system, however carefully designed, can fully capture this complexity. The Snopes rating is not wrong, but it is insufficient — and recognizing its insufficiency is itself an important step toward a more honest engagement with the unresolved moral questions this history poses.

The story also matters because it is a microcosm of the broader colonial project in the Congo. The transaction Jameson described — the exchange of six handkerchiefs for a human life — is a distillation of the colonial economy in its purest form. The Congo basin was being systematically depopulated, brutalized, and extracted during the period Jameson visited, and within a few years of his death would become the site of the Congo Free State, Leopold II's personal domain, where an estimated ten million people died as a result of forced labor, violence, and disease. Jameson's handkerchiefs are a tiny, almost trivial instance of the broader economy of dispossession and death that defined the colonial Congo. But they are a particularly revealing instance because they capture, in miniature, the casualness with which European actors treated African lives as commodities. Whether Jameson intended to cause the girl's death or merely enabled it through callous indifference, the underlying dynamic — a European man with trade goods treating African lives as expendable — was the foundational logic of the entire colonial enterprise.

Finally, the story matters because of what it reveals about the dynamics of colonial testimony and the politics of who is believed. Jameson's account was privileged because he was a wealthy, educated European man whose family had the resources to publish his journal and advocate for his reputation after his death. Farran's account was treated with suspicion because he was a Syrian translator — a man of lower social standing within the colonial hierarchy, whose testimony could be dismissed as the product of resentment or dishonesty. The African witnesses to the event — if there were any whose testimony could have been recorded — are entirely absent from the record. This hierarchy of credibility is not merely a historical curiosity; it is a structural feature of colonial epistemology that continues to shape how historical events are understood and adjudicated. Fact-checking that does not interrogate this hierarchy risks reproducing it.

## 5. Research Notes

The primary source base for the Jameson cannibalism story is, despite its age, relatively rich — but every source is compromised by the circumstances of its production.

Jameson's journal, published by his widow Ethel Jameson in 1891 under the title "The Story of the Rear Column of the Emin Pasha Relief Expedition," is the most important primary source for Jameson's own account. It is a self-interested document — Jameson wrote it knowing that his actions might be scrutinized, and his widow edited and published it with the explicit goal of defending his reputation. The journal's account of the cannibalism incident is careful, qualified, and structured to minimize Jameson's culpability. It emphasizes his skepticism, his disbelief, the speed of events, and his lack of sketching materials. Each of these details serves a defensive function, and each should be treated with appropriate skepticism. At the same time, the journal is candid enough to admit facts that are deeply damaging to Jameson's reputation — most importantly, that he provided the handkerchiefs and witnessed the act. This mixture of candor and self-protection is characteristic of colonial-era ego-documents and should be read with an awareness of the genre's conventions.

Assad Farran's testimony exists in three contradictory forms: his original statement to the Press Association in September 1888, his retraction later that same month, and his detailed affidavit signed in March 1890 and published in The Times on November 14, 1890. The affidavit is the most detailed account of the incident and includes specific details — the girl's age (approximately ten), the number of sketches (six), the location (Riba Riba, now Lokandu), the words Jameson allegedly spoke — that give it an air of specificity and reliability. But Farran's prior retraction undermines his credibility, and his motivations for each version of the story are opaque. He may have told the truth initially, been pressured to retract, and then reaffirmed the truth in his affidavit. Or he may have fabricated the story, retracted it under pressure or out of guilt, and then reaffirmed the fabrication for reasons of his own — perhaps resentment, perhaps a desire for attention, perhaps a belief that the truth had been suppressed. The documentary record does not allow us to distinguish between these possibilities with confidence.

The role of William Bourdett-Coutts, the MP who publicized Farran's retraction, deserves scrutiny. Bourdett-Coutts was married to Angela Burdett-Coutts, a prominent philanthropist with connections to the colonial establishment. His intervention on Jameson's behalf — claiming that Farran had admitted fabrication — should be read in the context of a colonial establishment with a strong interest in minimizing scandal and protecting the reputations of its agents. The Emin Pasha Relief Expedition was a high-profile venture with political and financial implications; allegations that one of its members had commissioned an act of cannibalism would have been deeply embarrassing to all involved. The retraction should be treated as a document produced under institutional pressure, not as a neutral statement of fact.

Ethel Jameson's publication of her husband's journal and her advocacy on his behalf represent another form of institutional pressure. As a widow defending her husband's reputation — and by extension her own social standing and the reputation of the Jameson family — she had every reason to present the most favorable possible account. Her decision to publish Farran's retraction alongside the journal suggests a strategic effort to undermine Farran's credibility while bolstering Jameson's. The fact that she did not address the sketches — which Farran claimed were made and painted — is a conspicuous absence that may indicate she could not deny their existence or chose not to draw attention to them.

The broader context of the Emin Pasha Relief Expedition is essential for understanding the environment in which these events occurred. The expedition, led by Henry Morton Stanley, was characterized by violence, discipline, and a disregard for African lives that was extreme even by colonial standards. Several members of the expedition died under mysterious circumstances, and the "Rear Column" — the detachment Jameson was part of, led by Edmund Barttelot — descended into chaos, violence, and alleged atrocities against local populations. The environment was one of breakdown, fear, and moral collapse. Jameson's actions cannot be assessed in isolation from this context of institutional dysfunction.

The location of the incident — Riba Riba, now Lokandu in the Maniema province of the DRC — was within the zone of Arab-Swahili influence in the Congo, where slave trading and resource extraction were conducted by networks of traders operating from the east coast. The man who proposed the exchange of cloth for a demonstration of cannibalism was likely connected to these networks. The girl who was killed was likely enslaved within these networks — her murder was not an act of "tribal ritual" as colonial narratives would have it, but a commodity transaction within an economy of human trafficking that was itself a product of regional and global commercial dynamics. Understanding the incident requires situating it within this economy, rather than treating it as an isolated act of "native savagery" as colonial sources tend to do.

Historiographical engagement with this incident has been limited. The story has been retold in various popular histories and online articles, usually in sensationalized form. Serious scholarly treatment is scarce, in part because the primary sources are contradictory and in part because the incident is a minor footnote in the larger history of colonial violence in the Congo. Adam Hochschild's "King Leopold's Ghost" (1998), the landmark popular history of the Congo Free State, does not dwell on the Jameson incident but provides essential context for understanding the environment of colonial violence in which it occurred. Further research in the archives of the Emin Pasha Relief Expedition, particularly the papers of Henry Morton Stanley and other expedition members, might yield additional context — though the likelihood of finding definitive evidence either confirming or exonerating Jameson is remote. The most productive avenues for future research would lie not in attempting to resolve the factual dispute — which is likely unresolvable — but in situating the incident within the broader pattern of colonial violence and in attempting to recover, however partially, the perspective of the Congolese communities in which it occurred. Oral history traditions in the Maniema region may preserve memory of the incident, though the challenges of conducting such research across more than a century of subsequent violence, displacement, and political upheaval are formidable.

One additional avenue worth noting is the question of the sketches themselves. Farran claimed that Jameson made six sketches during the killing and later painted them with watercolors. If these sketches exist — in a private collection, an archive, or among the papers of expedition members — their discovery would be the single most consequential piece of evidence in the case. Sketches made during the act would demonstrate premeditation and voyeuristic intent beyond any reasonable dispute. Their absence from the published record, after more than 130 years, suggests either that they were destroyed, that they never existed, or that they remain in private hands. The Jameson family's continued prominence and the brand's commercial significance create an obvious incentive for the suppression of any such evidence, if it ever existed.

## 6. Verdict

Snopes rates the claim that "James Sligo Jameson once bought a slave girl in Africa so that he could live out his desire to watch her being murdered and cannibalized" as a "Mixture." This rating is, in the narrowest technical sense, accurate: some elements of the claim are established by the documentary record, while others remain contested. Jameson did witness the murder and cannibalization of a girl. He did provide six handkerchiefs to a man who proposed an exchange of cloth for a demonstration of cannibalism. These facts are admitted by Jameson himself. What remains contested is whether his provision of the handkerchiefs constituted a knowing purchase of a human being for the purpose of witnessing her death — that is, whether he acted with premeditated intent — and whether he sketched the act as it occurred.

However, the "Mixture" rating, while technically defensible, is epistemologically and morally insufficient. It implies a neat partitioning of the claim into true and false components, when the reality is a single, continuous act whose moral character cannot be cleanly divided. Jameson paid a man who proposed to kill and cannibalize a person. He then watched the act occur. Whether he paid with the specific intent of causing the act, or paid out of skepticism-induced curiosity and then watched in horror, the chain of causation runs through him. The handkerchiefs were the catalyst. Without his payment, the man's proposal would have remained words. With his payment, it became murder.

This is not to say that Jameson's own account should be dismissed. His claim that he did not believe the proposal was serious is plausible — not because it is inherently credible, but because colonial actors routinely dismissed African agency and treated African statements as either deception or entertainment. Jameson's skepticism was not a form of innocence; it was a form of colonial contempt. He did not believe the man because he did not believe that an African man's words could be taken seriously. His skepticism was the mechanism through which the act became possible. He paid not because he wanted to see murder, but because he did not believe the man's proposition merited refusal. This is not murder by procurement. It is something more banal and more damning: murder by indifference.

The Snopes rating also fails to adequately account for the evidentiary asymmetry that structures the entire case. Jameson's account was preserved, published, and treated as authoritative because he was a wealthy European man whose family had the resources and the standing to defend his reputation. Farran's account was treated as unreliable because he was a Syrian translator of lower social standing within the colonial hierarchy. The African voices that might have provided independent testimony are entirely absent. This asymmetry means that the documentary record is not a neutral set of facts to be weighed — it is a record produced within and shaped by colonial power. Any fact-check that treats this record as if it were a neutral evidentiary baseline is reproducing the very power structures that produced the record in the first place.

Our verdict, such as it is, is this: the claim as popularly stated is a simplification of a more complex and more disturbing reality. Jameson did not, so far as the evidence can establish, set out to purchase a slave for the express purpose of watching her be cannibalized. What he did was provide the means for a murder to occur, watch it happen, and then write about it in his journal with a mixture of horror and clinical detail that is itself a form of participation. The distinction between "he bought a girl to watch her be cannibalized" and "he paid a man who proposed to cannibalize someone, then watched it happen" is a real distinction — but it is a distinction that separates two forms of moral culpability, not culpability from innocence.

The girl whose death is at the center of this story remains nameless, voiceless, and unrecorded in any source that has survived. She was, in all likelihood, a child — Farran's affidavit gives her age as approximately ten years old. Her murder was enabled by a European man's casual payment of trade cloth. Her death was witnessed by a colonial expeditionary force that was itself an instrument of the broader project of European extraction in the Congo. Her cannibalization was the subject of a British newspaper controversy that debated the reputation of a wealthy white man while ignoring her existence. And her story, as it has come down to us, is filtered through layers of self-interested testimony, institutional pressure, and colonial epistemology that make it impossible to know with certainty what happened on that day in 1888.

The Snopes "Mixture" rating is not wrong. But it is not enough. It does not grapple with the colonial context that made the act possible. It does not interrogate the power dynamics that shaped the documentary record. It does not acknowledge the moral weight of Jameson's admitted actions, even on the most charitable reading of his account. And it does not center — or even name — the victim whose murder is the actual subject of the claim. A fact-check that asks "Did Jameson do this?" without asking "Who was the girl, and what did her life and death mean?" is a fact-check that has accepted the colonial framing of the question. The claim is a mixture of true and false elements. The history is a matter of life and death. The gap between those two statements is where the real work begins.
