# Jamie Foxx and Will Smith are making a Tombstone remake


## 1. The Claim

Beginning in late 2023, a fictitious movie poster circulated widely on Facebook and subsequently spread across other social media platforms and entertainment commentary spaces, depicting Jamie Foxx and Will Smith in Western period costumes alongside the title "TOMBSTONE" in design elements closely resembling official studio marketing materials. The poster indicated that Foxx would portray Wyatt Earp and Smith would portray Doc Holliday — the two central characters of the beloved 1993 Western film *Tombstone*, which starred Kurt Russell and Val Kilmer — with a supposed theatrical release date in the summer of 2024.

The claim propagated in several distinct forms depending on the platform and the framing of those sharing it. In its most direct form, posts stated simply that a remake had been announced, treating the poster as evidence of a genuine studio announcement. In variant forms, posts asked whether followers had "heard about" the remake or whether they were "excited" about the casting, formats that implied factual grounding while maintaining the plausible-deniability of opinion-seeking. In a third category, the content was framed as news — sometimes with fabricated quotes attributed to the actors or to unnamed studio executives — and shared by accounts whose other content similarly mixed satirical invention with presentation styles mimicking entertainment journalism.

The claim gained enough traction to merit coverage in multiple entertainment and general news outlets. The *Economic Times of India* published a dedicated article examining whether the rumour was true (*Economic Times*, 2023). Snopes assigned reporter David Emery to investigate and published a full fact-check on December 26, 2023 (Emery, David, 2023, Snopes.com). The claim's reach demonstrated both the speed with which fabricated entertainment news can circulate and the specific power of the *Tombstone* property as an object of nostalgic audience investment.

The original 1993 *Tombstone* holds a culturally distinctive position: it is not primarily remembered as a critical success but rather as a cult phenomenon built around Val Kilmer's performance as Doc Holliday. Kilmer's portrayal of the consumptive gunslinger, with his sardonic wit, his "I'm your huckleberry" line, and his air of elegiac menace, became one of the most quoted and referenced film performances of its era. The film's cult status made any suggestion of a remake both culturally charged and automatically attention-grabbing — generating the kind of engagement that makes entertainment misinformation particularly effective.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The source of the fake poster.** Snopes traced the circulating movie poster and its associated content to a Facebook account known for publishing satirical and fabricated movie news (Emery, David, 2023, Snopes.com). The account's broader content portfolio included other fake film announcements in comparable formats, establishing a clear pattern of deliberate invention rather than innocent error. The Tombstone poster was designed with sufficient visual fidelity to pass casual inspection — using fonts and visual design conventions drawn from genuine action-film marketing — but had no relationship to any actual studio project.

**Neither actor's confirmed projects.** At the time the claim circulated most widely (late 2023 and into 2024), both Jamie Foxx and Will Smith had documented, confirmed projects in their respective near-term pipelines that made their simultaneous availability for a major Western production implausible. Foxx had publicly discussed recovery from a serious medical emergency in 2023 and had confirmed upcoming projects including *Back in Action* (2024, Netflix). Will Smith, following the widely covered Academy Awards incident in March 2022, had a publicly discussed reduced public profile and confirmed projects in various stages of development. Neither had made any public statement about a Tombstone remake, and no representative for either actor confirmed the claim to any outlet.

**No studio announcement.** The claim can also be tested against the structural facts of how major studio film announcements are made. A theatrical remake of *Tombstone* — a recognized cult property — produced by a major studio with two of the most globally famous actors in the world as its leads would not be announced through an unauthorised fan-made poster on Facebook. Such a project would be announced through official studio channels, with simultaneous reporting in the major entertainment trades including *Variety*, *The Hollywood Reporter*, and *Deadline Hollywood*. It would involve official statements from the actors' representatives, rights holders for the original 1993 film, and the producing studio. None of these verification markers existed. The absence of any official announcement or trade coverage is itself dispositive.

**Snopes rating.** Snopes reviewed the claim and rated it False (Emery, David, 2023, Snopes.com). The investigation confirmed the poster's origin in a satirical/fake-news Facebook account and found no evidence of any genuine announcement, studio activity, or actor involvement.

**Rights and IP status.** The 1993 *Tombstone* was produced by Hollywood Pictures, a Walt Disney Company subsidiary, in association with Cinergi Pictures. The intellectual property landscape for a theatrical remake would require navigation of rights arrangements involving successor entities to these production companies. No such rights acquisition was reported by any credible entertainment industry source.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty: the celebrity casting signal.** Entertainment fabrications involving real, well-known celebrities as supposed cast members have a specific and documentable advantage over fabrications involving fictional studio names or invented projects: they inherit the credibility accumulated by the celebrities' real careers. When an audience member encounters a fake movie poster featuring Jamie Foxx and Will Smith in a genuine film context, their prior knowledge of both actors' careers — their established persona in film, their star power, their previous genre work — performs a kind of automatic plausibility verification that the false claim exploits without earning.

This mechanism can be named **celebrity credibility laundering**: a fabricated claim becomes more plausible not through any intrinsic evidentiary support but by associating itself with people and properties whose factual existence is beyond question. Jamie Foxx is real. Will Smith is real. *Tombstone* is real. The fake movie is not real, but the claim that it exists rides on the undeniable reality of everything surrounding it. The critical question — is this claim itself verified? — tends to be displaced by the more easily answerable question — are the people in this poster real? — producing an affirmative answer that the audience mistakenly treats as equivalent to verification.

The specific casting choices in the fabrication reinforced this dynamic. The assignment of Foxx to Wyatt Earp and Smith to Doc Holliday was not random. Both actors had demonstrated genre range that made Western casting plausible: Foxx had delivered an acclaimed performance in Quentin Tarantino's *Django Unchained* (2012), a revisionist Western in which he played a formerly enslaved man who becomes a bounty hunter. The *Django* connection meant that audiences encountering the Foxx-as–Wyatt Earp claim had a specific positive reference point. Similarly, Will Smith's sustained action-film brand, including films that involved period aesthetics and physical performance, made the claim congruent with audience mental models of Smith's typical roles. The fabrication was designed — consciously or instinctively — to be maximally plausible given who both actors actually are.

This is a novel observation: the fabricator's casting choices constituted an epistemological trap. The better the fake casting fit with real career evidence, the harder it was to reject the claim on intuitive grounds. A poster claiming the same film starred actors with no genre history would be immediately implausible. The *Tombstone* fake poster chose its cast with enough sophistication to defeat the simplest heuristic tests.

**Social media design and engagement mechanics.** The claim's propagation was also structurally assisted by platform design. Facebook's engagement algorithms reward content that generates high numbers of reactions, comments, and shares — and content about beloved cultural properties that surprises or excites its audience generates precisely those metrics. The fake Tombstone poster was designed as a highly shareable object: it named a beloved property, made a surprising casting announcement, and invited the emotional reactions (excitement, nostalgia, skepticism) that generate the comment-and-share behaviour that algorithms amplify. The platform's structural incentives were aligned with the claim's propagation regardless of its truth value.

**Val Kilmer, cult status, and nostalgic investment.** The 1993 *Tombstone* occupies an unusual position in popular culture as a film whose reputation improved substantially after its initial release. In 1993 it was received as a solidly entertaining if conventional Western; over the following three decades, through cable television, DVD, streaming, and online clip culture, it became a genuine cult phenomenon driven almost entirely by Val Kilmer's performance. The film's cultural standing in 2023 — thirty years after release — was arguably higher than at any previous point, driven partly by renewed audience engagement during Kilmer's public battle with throat cancer and the *Val* (2021) documentary about his life.

This cultural context made any suggestion of a remake inherently attention-grabbing. The audience for the fake poster was not a neutral one: it was an audience predisposed by genuine affection for the source material to react emotionally before thinking analytically. Nostalgic engagement with beloved properties is not in itself a cognitive failing, but it does create an emotional activation state that is not conducive to dispassionate assessment of whether a claim is verified. The click happens before the evaluation.


## 4. Verdict

**False — fabricated entertainment content, no credible sourcing.** The claim that Jamie Foxx and Will Smith are making a *Tombstone* remake is a fabrication. The poster that generated the claim originated on a Facebook account known for producing fake movie content. No studio has announced such a film. No official statement from either actor or their representatives has confirmed the claim. The major entertainment trades have not reported the project. Snopes investigated and rated the claim False in December 2023 (Emery, David, 2023, Snopes.com).

The claim exhibits all the hallmarks of a deliberately crafted entertainment fabrication: a beloved property with high nostalgic value, credible and era-appropriate casting that exploits real career evidence, visual design mimicking official marketing, and a social media format optimised for engagement over accuracy. It is not a case of ambiguous or distorted information but of straightforwardly invented content that spread because it was designed to be convincing.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The fake *Tombstone* poster is an instance of a broader and growing phenomenon that might be called **entertainment fabrication culture** — the production and distribution of fake movie announcements, posters, trailers, and casting news designed to generate engagement, drive traffic to content farms, or in some cases deliberately mislead audiences about the activities of specific public figures.

This phenomenon has been significantly enabled by the widespread availability of graphic design tools that allow convincing poster creation without professional expertise, combined with social media platforms whose architecture rewards engagement over verification. A competently designed fake movie poster requires perhaps an afternoon of work using consumer-available design software and stock photography licenses. The person who creates it may have no sophisticated intent beyond generating engagement on their account; the downstream effects of their creation, however, can reach millions of people before any correction is issued.

Entertainment fabrication culture is notable for its relatively low profile in media literacy education compared to political disinformation. Major fact-checking organisations and media literacy curricula have invested heavily in helping audiences identify fabricated political news — fake quotes, doctored photographs of political figures, invented policy announcements. Fake entertainment news has received less systematic attention, partly because its stakes appear lower (no election is being influenced by a fake movie poster) and partly because the harm is less immediately legible.

But entertainment fabrication does carry real costs. It consumes audience attention that could be directed toward factual coverage of the entertainment industry. It contributes to an ambient loss of epistemic confidence about what is real and what is not — a form of low-grade uncertainty that aggregates across exposures even when individual instances are easily dismissed. It sometimes imposes reputational costs on the named individuals: Will Smith and Jamie Foxx were, in 2023, both navigating significant public scrutiny, and their association in a fabricated story with a high-visibility project created an additional layer of public attention they had not sought or approved.

The *Tombstone* case is also worth examining in relation to the genuine remake landscape for classic Westerns. The post-*Django Unchained* and *Brokeback Mountain* era has seen significant creative and commercial interest in examining Western genre conventions through new racial and gender perspectives. An actually-produced film casting two Black A-list stars in roles previously played by two white actors in one of the most beloved Westerns of the past thirty years would be a culturally significant event. The fake exists in the space created by that genuine cultural appetite — it is not a randomly chosen subject but one chosen because its premise was culturally resonant with real conversations happening in popular media. The most effective misinformation typically operates at the intersection of what is false and what people either hope or fear might be true.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addressed this claim directly and rated it False in December 2023. Their methodology followed the standard pattern for entertainment fabrication fact-checks: identify the original source of the claim, verify whether the source has a track record of satirical or fabricated content, check official studio channels and entertainment trade journalism for any corresponding announcement, and contact representatives for the named individuals if the claim's reach warrants direct response.

The *Economic Times of India* also investigated and published coverage that similarly concluded the claim was false (*Economic Times*, 2023). The relatively swift response from legitimate fact-checking infrastructure in this case reflects the claim's wide reach and the straightforward nature of the debunk: no counter-evidence needed to be weighed, and no nuanced assessment of ambiguous sourcing was required. The claim was simply fabricated.

For media literacy purposes, the *Tombstone* fake poster case illustrates the value of what might be called **announcement structure verification**: before accepting an entertainment industry claim, ask where an announcement of this significance would appear if it were real. A studio remake starring two of the world's biggest film stars would generate immediate coverage in *Variety*, *The Hollywood Reporter*, and *Deadline* — the three primary entertainment industry trades — as well as in general entertainment media and in the entertainment sections of major newspapers. If a claim of that magnitude exists only in social media posts and cannot be located in trade media, the claim is almost certainly unverified, and quite possibly fabricated.

This heuristic is easy to apply and highly reliable for entertainment industry claims because the major trades' financial model depends on breaking genuine industry news quickly. They have strong institutional incentive to be first on real stories and equally strong institutional incentive to avoid publishing fabricated ones. The absence of their coverage is therefore a meaningful absence. Audiences who have been trained to apply this test — who ask not just "does this exist on social media?" but "does this appear in outlets that cover industry announcements professionally?" — are substantially more robust to entertainment fabrication than those who have not.

The fake poster's origins in a social media satire account also raises questions about format and platform labelling. Facebook and similar platforms have policies requiring satirical content to be clearly labelled as such, precisely to prevent this kind of misidentification. Whether those policies were or could have been applied in this specific case is a question about platform governance rather than audience credulity, but it is worth noting as a systemic rather than purely individual failure: the spread of the fake *Tombstone* poster was not only a story about people who failed to check their sources but also about platform architectures that did not prevent a clearly fabricated object from circulating as news.
