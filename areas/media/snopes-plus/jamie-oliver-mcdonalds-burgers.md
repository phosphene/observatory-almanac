## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In January 2018, a wave of viral social media posts claimed that celebrity chef Jamie Oliver had "proven" that McDonald's hamburger meat was "unfit for human consumption" due to the company's use of beef trimmings treated with ammonium hydroxide — a substance used as an antimicrobial agent in certain meat processing operations. The posts linked to "natural health" websites, including one called The Essence of Life, and presented Oliver's campaign as a current, ongoing revelation. The implicit message was that McDonald's continued to use this process and that consumers should be alarmed.

Snopes investigator David Emery (2018) assigned this claim the rating **Outdated**. The core underlying facts were substantially true: Oliver did wage a high-profile media campaign against McDonald's use of ammonia-treated beef, specifically in an April 2011 episode of his television show *Jamie Oliver's Food Revolution*, and as a consequence of the ensuing controversy, McDonald's USA stopped using ammoniated lean beef trimmings (sometimes called "pink slime") in its burgers in 2012. But the 2018 viral posts presented this seven-year-old dispute as current, never disclosed the dates, and — critically — omitted the pivotal outcome that McDonald's had already changed its practice. By 2018, the entire controversy had been resolved in Oliver's favor, making the warning both temporally deceptive and factually incomplete.

This entry examines the specific technology and regulatory history of ammoniated beef, Oliver's 2011 television campaign, the science of ammonium hydroxide as an antimicrobial agent, and the broader regulatory and industry context that both explains why the practice was used and why both the FDA and McDonald's ultimately reached the positions they did.

## §2 What Is "Pink Slime"? The Science and History of Ammoniated Lean Beef Trimmings

"Pink slime" is a colloquial term for a product known in the food industry as Lean Finely Textured Beef (LFTB) or, in the variant relevant to McDonald's, Select Lean Beef Trimmings (SLBT). The product was developed in the late 1980s and early 1990s by Beef Products Inc. (BPI) of Dakota Dunes, South Dakota, as a method for recovering additional meat yield from beef trimmings that would otherwise be discarded or diverted to low-value uses.

The process works by separating fat from the connective tissue and thin meat portions of beef trimmings at low temperature, then treating the resulting meat slurry with ammonium hydroxide (ammonia dissolved in water) to reduce pathogen load. The pH-adjustment step was designed to kill or inhibit bacteria including *E. coli* O157:H7 and *Salmonella*, both of which are significant food safety concerns in ground beef. The U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA) and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) both classified ammonium hydroxide used in this context as "Generally Recognized As Safe" (GRAS), and the USDA further found that ammonium hydroxide-treated LFTB was acceptable for incorporation into ground beef without a separate label disclosure, provided the beef was otherwise USDA-inspected — a regulatory determination that became deeply controversial when it was publicly scrutinized.

Michael Moss (2009) in the *New York Times* first brought widespread public attention to the safety record of ammoniated LFTB in a landmark investigative article published December 30, 2009. Moss reported that internal USDA correspondence indicated concerns about whether the ammonia treatment was achieving the pathogen-reduction efficacy BPI had claimed during the approval process, and that *E. coli* and *Salmonella* contamination had been detected in samples at plants producing the ammoniated product. The USDA promised increased oversight as a result of the reporting, and the article catalyzed an extended period of public and regulatory scrutiny.

Oliver's April 2011 *Food Revolution* episode intervened at a moment when this scrutiny had partially subsided in public consciousness but had not resolved. Oliver's dramatization — literally dousing a pile of beef scraps with liquid ammonia and running it through a grinder — was designed to maximally disgust his audience and was effective in reinvigorating the social media controversy. His statement that they were "taking a product that would be sold in the cheapest way for dogs, and after this process, is being given to human beings," was powerful rhetoric and not without factual basis: LFTB and similar beef trimmings products had historically been directed toward pet food and rendering applications before the ammonia treatment process made them viable for human food use.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Outdated Safety Controversy and the Problem of Settled Science Communication

The "outdated controversy" structure of this case — in which a legitimate past concern was resolved but viral posts represented it as ongoing — raises important questions about how settled scientific and regulatory controversies are communicated to public audiences over time.

The McDonald's pink slime situation was, by 2018, a resolved episode in two distinct senses: the practice had been discontinued, and the public controversy had subsided. Yet both elements of the resolution were invisible to audiences encountering the 2018 viral posts, because the posts were constructed with no timestamps and presented only the "bad" portion of the story (the use of ammoniated beef) without the "resolved" portion (the discontinuation). This selective narration is a form of misinformation that does not require any individual sentence to be false; it operates entirely through omission.

Research on how temporal framing affects risk perception is directly relevant here. Tversky and Kahneman (1981) in their foundational *Science* paper on decision framing established that the way information is presented — particularly what is included versus omitted — determines how risk is evaluated, independently of the underlying objective risk level. Applied to food safety, Fischhoff (1995) in *Risk Analysis* documented that consumers' risk assessments of food safety concerns are highly dependent on whether information about risk-reduction measures is presented alongside information about the hazard; presenting the hazard in isolation generates disproportionately elevated risk estimates relative to the objective probability after controls are in place.

The 2018 viral posts about Jamie Oliver and McDonald's are therefore not merely temporally deceptive but are structurally risk-inflating: by presenting the hazard without the mitigation, they generated consumer alarm about a risk that had been addressed seven years earlier. Readers who shared the posts and avoided McDonald's based on the implied warning were responding to a misinformation environment that denied them access to the resolution.

The deeper structural problem this illustrates is what might be called "the settled controversy recall gap": public controversies in food safety, medicine, and environmental science often reach resolutions that are less newsworthy than the original exposés, and thus receive less media amplification. A controversy that generates 1,000 news articles and a celebrity chef campaign generates perhaps 50 follow-up articles when the problem is addressed. The original 1,000 articles remain indexed and searchable; the 50 resolution articles are easily missed. This asymmetric archiving creates a persistent legacy of apparent active controversy around resolved issues.

## §4 The Regulatory Context: USDA, FDA, and the GRAS Determination

The regulatory history of ammoniated beef trimmings is important to understand both the original controversy and the current status. The FDA's GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) determination for ammonium hydroxide in meat processing predates the pink slime controversy and is part of a broader regulatory framework governing the use of pH-adjusting agents in food processing.

Ammonium hydroxide (NH₄OH) is a compound that forms when ammonia gas is dissolved in water. At food-processing concentrations, it is used as an antimicrobial agent because pathogenic bacteria including *E. coli* O157:H7 and *Salmonella* are sensitive to pH shifts. The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) conducted risk assessments on the effectiveness of ammonia-based antimicrobial treatments and found the process to be effective when applied at appropriate concentrations and contact times. The FDA's GRAS notice for ammonium hydroxide (FDA, GRN 000074) classified it as acceptable for food use, and the USDA's determination that LFTB produced using the process was eligible for the same inspection classification as conventional ground beef followed from these findings.

The controversy generated by Moss (2009) and Oliver (2011), however, introduced evidence that the effectiveness of the treatment in practice was variable — that *E. coli* and *Salmonella* had in fact been detected in ammoniated LFTB product from some facilities, contrary to the implied efficacy of the treatment process. The USDA's response of promising "closer oversight" rather than immediate regulatory action was widely criticized as inadequate given the documented contamination events.

Bottemiller (2012) in *Food Safety News* reported that McDonald's, Taco Bell, and Burger King all discontinued use of ammoniated LFTB in their products in early 2012, following the renewed public controversy sparked in part by Oliver's campaign. McDonald's was careful in its public statements to note that the product had been "safe" — consistent with its regulatory classification — while also acknowledging that it was no longer part of their supply. This careful formulation — valid product, discontinued use — reflects the company's dual obligation to acknowledge the regulatory reality while responding to the consumer perception.

McDonald's 2013 corporate statement, quoted in full by Emery (2018), is unambiguous: "McDonald's does not use lean beef trimmings treated with ammonia, what some individuals call 'pink slime,' in our burgers, and hasn't since 2011. Any recent reports that we do are false."

## §5 Jamie Oliver's Campaign: Accuracy, Rhetoric, and Impact

Assessing the Oliver episode requires distinguishing between his underlying factual contentions and the rhetorical framing he used to communicate them. Emery (2018) noted that despite Oliver's characterization of the beef as "unfit for human consumption," Oliver did not actually make a scientific or nutritional case for that conclusion — his claim was rhetorical rather than empirically argued. The FDA and USDA maintained throughout that properly treated ammoniated beef was safe for human consumption, and no peer-reviewed clinical evidence exists demonstrating consumer harm from ingesting it in the concentrations found in commercial products. Oliver's framing exploited the disgust response — the ammonia procedure, visualized dramatically, triggered powerful visceral reactions — rather than presenting a toxicological argument.

This distinction matters epistemically. A food safety argument can be valid, partially valid, or invalid on two independent axes: (1) the factual accuracy of the underlying claims about what a product contains, and (2) the accuracy of the health risk characterization derived from those facts. Oliver was substantially accurate on (1) — McDonald's did use ammoniated beef trimmings — but overstated on (2) — the claim that the product was "unfit for human consumption" was not supported by available toxicological or epidemiological evidence. The FDA specifically addressed similar claims in Congressional testimony, maintaining the GRAS classification throughout.

Oliver's rhetorical strategy, however, was highly effective as a public health intervention in a broader sense: it catalyzed the removal of ammoniated LFTB from not only McDonald's but Taco Bell, Burger King, and a significant portion of the school lunch supply. Whether this outcome constitutes a net public health benefit is a separate empirical question — the product had been serving a legitimate pathogen-reduction function, and its removal created incentives to use conventional ground beef that, without the ammonia treatment, may carry marginally higher bacterial loads. Schultz (2012) in *Ad Age* reported on the industry and marketing aftermath, noting that BPI filed a defamation lawsuit against ABC News over their network coverage of pink slime, eventually settling for an undisclosed amount in 2017.

## §6 Conclusion: Verdict Affirmation and the Limitations of Celebrity Food Advocacy

Emery's verdict of Outdated is precisely correct: Oliver's campaign was real, its factual basis regarding McDonald's use of ammoniated beef was largely accurate, and it had genuine impact — but by 2018 all of it was history. McDonald's had discontinued the practice in 2012. The 2018 viral posts presented a resolved controversy as current, omitted the resolution, and incorrectly characterized the product as actively posing a consumer health hazard.

The episode also illuminates important limitations of celebrity-driven food advocacy as a public health communication mechanism. Oliver's framing — visceral, emotionally loaded, and rhetorically overstated — was extremely effective at generating consumer response and corporate change, but it accomplished this through persuasion techniques that are structurally similar to misinformation even when applied to a legitimate concern. The rhetorical tools that make celebrity advocacy compelling (disgust, horror, urgency) are the same tools that make misinformation compelling, and they leave the same cognitive residue: a strong negative association with a specific brand that persists even after the underlying concern is addressed.

Seven years after McDonald's removed ammoniated beef from its supply, viral posts continued to exploit the brand association Oliver had so effectively established, recirculating the most alarming elements of his 2011 campaign without any acknowledgment that the company had remediated the issue. This is the long-tail problem of effective advocacy: the emotional scar tissue outlasts the wound.

For consumers and media literacy practitioners, the practical lesson is the same as in the Target Easter egg case: when a safety-related claim about a corporate product is encountered online, the most important question after "is this true?" is "is this still true?" — a temporal verification step that catches outdated controversies being recirculated as current warnings.
