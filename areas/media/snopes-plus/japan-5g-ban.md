# Did Japan Ban 5G Technology Over Health Concerns?


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: A story published on June 9, 2019 by a website called "Putin Tomorrow" claimed that the Japanese government would be banning 5G cellular technology "for the health of its citizens." The article reported that Japan's "Ministry of High Technologies" had posted an official announcement prohibiting the development of, and investment in, fifth-generation wireless networks. The piece quoted an individual named "Nobunari Kabato," described as the "Minister of High Technologies," as the authority for the decision.

The alleged quote attributed to Kabato read: "We need to think about the health of the Japanese. Our country has already accelerated economically so that today we have nowhere to hurry. It is necessary to stabilize this state. 5G is fraught with great danger. So we think not only we, yesterday this information was confirmed by the US ambassador to Japan. If America, China or the EU countries are ready to risk their people for the sake of progress, then such a strategy is unacceptable for our welfare state."

**Context of the claim**: The claim emerged at a moment of high public anxiety about 5G technology. By mid-2019, fifth-generation wireless standards were transitioning from laboratory demonstration to initial commercial rollout in several countries. The technology was simultaneously being adopted by telecommunications companies and industries and being targeted by a growing ecosystem of health conspiracy claims alleging that the higher-frequency millimeter-wave signals associated with some 5G implementations posed novel health risks to humans and animals. Fear-based 5G content was circulating widely on social media platforms; a story claiming that Japan — one of the world's most technologically sophisticated nations — had determined 5G was too dangerous to deploy would have carried significant apparent credibility for audiences already primed with health concerns about the technology.

**Snopes investigation by Alex Kasprak, published October 16, 2019**: Snopes investigative journalist and science writer Alex Kasprak examined the Putin Tomorrow claim and identified three independently sufficient reasons to rate it false: the named ministry does not exist, the named minister cannot be verified as a real person, and Japan's actual policy position was the diametric opposite of a ban. Each of these failures is severe enough on its own to falsify the claim; together they establish with high confidence that the Putin Tomorrow article was a fabrication rather than a mischaracterization of real events.

**Broader significance**: Fabricated stories about national governments banning or restricting 5G technology have been a recurring feature of the anti-5G disinformation ecosystem. The Japan claim is representative of a genre that uses real countries, plausible-sounding government entities, and quotations from invented officials to create the impression of geopolitical consensus against the technology. These fabrications are difficult to fact-check for audiences without access to Japanese-language government records, giving them longer shelf lives than claims about countries with more accessible public records.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The "Ministry of High Technologies" does not exist in Japan**: Japan's governmental structure is a parliamentary system with a cabinet of ministers comprising well-defined ministries established by law. A complete list of Japanese ministries is publicly accessible through the National Government Organization Act (行政組織法). No ministry named "Ministry of High Technologies" exists in this structure. The government body with closest nominal proximity to such a name would be the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (文部科学省, MEXT), which was established in 2001 through the merger of the Ministry of Education and the Science and Technology Agency. At the time of the claim's publication in June 2019, MEXT was headed by Kōichi Hagiuda, not anyone named "Nobunari Kabato."

The Ministry for Internal Affairs and Communications (総務省, MIC) oversees telecommunications regulation in Japan and would be the relevant body for any decision about 5G deployment policy. That ministry's head at the relevant period was Sanae Takaichi, again not a person named "Nobunari Kabato." There is no position in the Japanese government — ministerial, subordinate ministerial, or advisory — that could be accurately described as "Minister of High Technologies."

**"Nobunari Kabato" does not exist**: A search for the name "Nobunari Kabato" in any language returns only one result: the Putin Tomorrow article and subsequent articles that quoted it. The name does not appear in any Japanese government roster, any telecommunications industry record, any Japanese news archive, or any other retrievable source. The construction of the name is consistent with a Japanese given name (Nobunari is a plausible Japanese masculine given name; Kabato could be a Japanese-style surname) but the specific combination does not correspond to any identifiable person in Japanese public life. The invented name is the most straightforward indicator that the Putin Tomorrow article was constructed from whole cloth rather than being based on real events that were misinterpreted.

**Japan was actively investing in 5G infrastructure**: The most comprehensive empirical refutation of the Japan ban claim is Japan's actual 5G policy during this period. Far from banning the technology, the Japanese government approved in April 2019 — two months before the Putin Tomorrow story was published — plans by four mobile network operators (NTT Docomo, SoftBank, KDDI, and Rakuten Mobile) to deploy nationwide 5G networks. The total investment commitment was approximately ¥1.6 trillion (approximately $14.4 billion USD at 2019 exchange rates) over five years. This plan was reported by the Nikkei Asian Review (Hotta, Takafumi, "Japan's Carriers Plan $14bn Spending Blitz for Nationwide 5G," Nikkei Asian Review, October 3, 2019) and described as a "spending blitz."

Japan ultimately held its 5G spectrum auctions in April 2019 and conducted its formal commercial 5G launch in March 2020, making Japan among the earlier major economies to offer commercial 5G service. The country subsequently established a national 5G strategy as part of its Society 5.0 initiative — a comprehensive government framework for using advanced digital technology to address social challenges — explicitly treating 5G as critical infrastructure for economic development. None of this is compatible with a January 2019 ban on 5G development.

**The origin website "Putin Tomorrow"**: The Putin Tomorrow website, at which the Japan 5G ban claim originated, is not identified in Snopes' analysis as a well-established or credible news organization. The website's name alone — referencing the Russian president in the context of alleged Japanese health-based technology policy — suggests an editorial orientation unrelated to mainstream Japanese government affairs reporting. The combination of an obscure, ideologically named website, a claim about a government entity that does not exist, and a quotation from a person who cannot be identified makes this an unusually clear-cut fabrication.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty: the asymmetric credibility cost of bureaucratic specificity**

The Japan 5G ban claim exploits a structural asymmetry in how audiences evaluate the credibility of news narratives: the inclusion of specific bureaucratic detail — a named ministry, a named official, a verbatim quotation — signals authenticity in ways that are difficult to evaluate without access to primary-language government records. For most English-speaking readers, there is no practical way to verify in real time that Japan has no "Ministry of High Technologies." The claim looks like a piece of real international news: it names a ministry, names a minister, and includes what appears to be a direct quotation.

This asymmetry — between the ease of constructing plausible-sounding bureaucratic detail and the difficulty of verifying it in real time — is a fundamental feature of the disinformation environment for international claims. Fabricated stories about foreign governments are systematically harder to correct at the speed of social media diffusion than fabricated stories about domestic institutions, because the verification overhead (accessing foreign-language government records, understanding foreign governmental structures, identifying named officials) is substantially higher. The Putin Tomorrow article was designed to operate in this verification gap.

The broader psychological mechanism driving belief in the Japan claim is what Sunstein and Vermeule (2009) describe as "conspiracy thinking" in *Journal of Political Philosophy* (vol. 17, pp. 202–227): the belief that significant events have intentional covert causes, and that official explanations systematically conceal those causes. For audiences already embedded in anti-5G conspiracy narratives, the claim that Japan's government had quietly banned the technology aligned with a prior belief that the real dangers of 5G were being suppressed by telecommunications industry-allied regulators in most Western countries. Japan's ban, in this cognitive frame, was evidence of what "they" didn't want people to know. Confirming evidence — even fabricated confirming evidence — is processed quickly and shared widely; corrective information must overcome both the original impression and the psychological resistance to revising beliefs that confirm prior commitments.

**5G as a target for health concern**: The specific domain of 5G health fears provides fertile soil for fabricated government ban stories because the technology is genuinely new (in the sense that commercial 5G rollout began only in 2018–2019), technically complex (the distinction between different frequency bands and their interaction with biological tissue requires electromagnetic physics to evaluate properly), and associated with a prior tradition of concern about electromagnetic radiation from mobile phones (concerns that themselves were extensively investigated and largely resolved in the research literature). Fears about relatively unfamiliar electromagnetic technologies have a long history: concerns about high-voltage power lines began in the 1970s, concerns about mobile phone radiation intensified in the 1990s and 2000s, and concerns about WiFi followed in the 2000s. Each round of concern generated extensive research (reviewed in Repacholi, 1998, *Environment International*, vol. 24, pp. 535–547; Ahlbom et al., 2004, *Annals of Oncology*, vol. 15, pp. 1143–1149), and the resulting literature does not support a conclusion of significant health risk at regulatory exposure levels — but the concerns themselves have proven resilient.


## 4. The Broader Pattern

**The anatomy of anti-5G fabrications**: The Japan 5G ban story is structurally representative of a well-defined subgenre of technology-panic disinformation. These stories typically feature: (1) a credible-sounding government action (ban, moratorium, investigation) by a technologically advanced nation; (2) a named official who appears to have appropriate authority; (3) a quotation from that official that frames the action in terms of health protection and implicitly criticizes other countries that have not taken the same action; (4) a causal mechanism that appeals to prior health concerns without providing technical detail; and (5) an implicit claim that mainstream media is not reporting the story because of industry pressure or regulatory capture.

This formula appeared repeatedly in 2019–2020 in claims about alleged 5G bans or restrictions in Belgium, Switzerland, the Netherlands, and various other countries. Many of these stories contained substantial misrepresentation of real events: Belgium's local moratorium on 5G deployment related narrowly to specific environmental data rights issues and was not a national health-based ban; Swiss concerns about radiation limits were technical and regulatory rather than a finding that 5G was unsafe. The Japan story is unusual in that it appears to be entirely fabricated — no real event, however mischaracterized — rather than a distortion of real regulatory activity.

**The COVID-19 amplification**: The anti-5G ecosystem in which the Japan claim circulated in 2019 became dramatically larger and more intense during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, when conspiracy theories directly linking 5G infrastructure to coronavirus transmission circulated widely and prompted real-world arson attacks on telecommunications towers in the United Kingdom and other countries (Ahmed et al., 2020, *Journal of Medical Internet Research*, vol. 22, pp. e19458). The Japan ban story, published in 2019, was part of the epistemic infrastructure that primed audiences for this escalation: by establishing through fabrication that advanced industrial nations had recognized 5G as dangerous, the story contributed to an environment in which more extreme causal claims about the technology could find receptive audiences.


## 5. Evidentiary Assessment

**Strength of the refutation**: Very high. The falsification of the Japan 5G ban claim operates at three logically independent levels. First, the institutional claim is false: the "Ministry of High Technologies" does not exist and cannot have issued an announcement. Second, the personal claim is false: "Nobunari Kabato" cannot be verified as a real person, and no Japanese government official with authority over telecommunications policy bore that name. Third, the policy claim is the inverse of reality: Japan's government not only did not ban 5G but approved and funded the largest telecommunications infrastructure buildout in the country's recent history in the same period. Any single one of these falsifications would suffice to rate the claim false; all three together make it among the more thoroughly debunked claims in the anti-5G literature.

**The underlying health claim**: Snopes' fact-check, authored by Alex Kasprak, focuses on the specific fabricated-Japan-ban claim rather than providing a comprehensive review of 5G health research, but the broader empirical record on 5G and health is relevant context. The World Health Organization (WHO), the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection (ICNIRP), the U.S. Food and Drug Administration, and national health authorities in the UK, Australia, Germany, France, and Japan have all reviewed the available evidence and concluded that 5G technology operating within regulatory exposure limits does not pose demonstrated health risks (ICNIRP Guidelines, Health Physics, 2020, vol. 118, pp. 483–524). The regulatory exposure limits established by ICNIRP apply across all 5G frequency bands, including millimeter wave, and are set to provide a substantial safety margin above demonstrated biological effect thresholds.

**Known limitations**: The health effects of very prolonged, very high-intensity exposure to 5G millimeter-wave frequencies in specific industrial or occupational settings were not fully characterized as of 2019 research reviews, though the consumer exposure levels produced by commercial 5G infrastructure are orders of magnitude below occupational thresholds. The absence of evidence of harm is not identical to evidence of the absence of harm for all possible exposure scenarios, and ongoing monitoring of health data in 5G-deployed populations is scientifically appropriate. This caveat, however, in no way supports the fabricated Japan ban claim.


## 6. Verdict and Implications

Snopes rates the Japan 5G ban claim **False**. The rating is amply supported: the institutional, personal, and policy claims in the Putin Tomorrow article are each independently false, and Japan's actual policy was the direct opposite of a ban. The article represents a straightforward fabrication rather than a misidentification or misinterpretation of real events.

The implications for media literacy are instructive. This claim illustrates the "bureaucratic specificity trap" — the cognitive tendency to treat named institutions and named officials as signals of authenticity, without applying the equally important heuristic of checking whether named institutions and officials actually exist. Audience members encountering international news claims of this type would benefit from a consistent question: Is this government entity real? Does this government official exist? These are simple questions that can typically be resolved within seconds through official government websites or encyclopedia entries, and they would have sufficed to identify this claim as fabricated before any sharing.

The claim also illustrates the persistence asymmetry characteristic of disinformation: the Japan ban story made a strong, simple, emotionally resonant claim (an advanced nation rejected dangerous technology); the correction that Japan has no Ministry of High Technologies, no Minister Kabato, and a $14 billion 5G buildout is more complex and less emotionally resonant. Corrections consistently face structural disadvantages against original false claims, a pattern documented empirically by Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018) in *Science* (vol. 359, pp. 1146–1151), who found that false news spreads faster and farther on social media than true news across all news categories tested. This asymmetry underscores the importance of rapid, authoritative, widely distributed fact-checking for claims of this type before they complete their primary virality cycle.
