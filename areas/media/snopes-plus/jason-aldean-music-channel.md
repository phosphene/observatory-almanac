# Jason Aldean Was Banned from a Music Channel or Awards Show?

## 1. The Claim

Beginning in July 2023 and recurring in conservative media and entertainment commentary ever since, a claim has circulated that Jason Aldean was "banned" from Country Music Television (CMT) — or, in some variants, from the CMA Awards, or from various other industry platforms — following the controversy over his song "Try That in a Small Town." The claim appears in social media posts, online petitions, and opinion columns, generally framed as evidence of cultural censorship, double standards in the entertainment industry, or ideological policing of country music.

The word "banned" does the key work in most versions. When the story says Aldean was "banned," it implies a formal, comprehensive, and personal exclusion: CMT will not play his music, will not have him on their channel, will not allow him to participate in their programming or award ceremonies. This personalised-exclusion framing is distinct from a narrower claim that a specific piece of content was removed from a specific platform's rotation — which is what actually happened.

The distinction matters because the wide claim (personal ban from a channel) carries substantially different implications than the narrow reality (one music video pulled from regular rotation for approximately one year, followed by the artist's return to normal industry participation). This entry examines what actually happened in July 2023, how the narrative of a "ban" was constructed from a real but more limited action, and why the amplified version proved so durable.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

**The video removal.** On approximately July 18, 2023 — about four days after the music video for "Try That in a Small Town" was published to YouTube on July 14, 2023 — CMT removed the video from its rotation. CMT's statement at the time did not specify a reason; the network simply confirmed it had pulled the video and offered no further comment. The action was a content-programming decision affecting one music video, not a formal statement about Aldean as a person or performer.

**The song and its controversy.** "Try That in a Small Town" generated intense public controversy in mid-July 2023 for two overlapping reasons:

*Lyrics and thematic content.* The song's lyrics — which describe violent reprisals against people who assault police officers, loot stores, spit on flags, or threaten others — were interpreted by critics as encouraging vigilante violence against social justice protesters, many of whom were Black. Lines including "see how far ya make it down the road / Around here, we take care of our own" were read against the backdrop of Black Lives Matter protests that followed the murder of George Floyd in 2020.

*Filming location.* The music video was filmed in front of the Maury County Courthouse in Columbia, Tennessee. That courthouse is historically significant as the site of the 1927 lynching of an 18-year-old Black man named Henry Choate, and as the backdrop to the 1946 Columbia Race Riot — a violent confrontation between white residents and Black veterans that left two Black men dead. Critics argued that filming a video about community-level violence against protesters at a site with that specific racial history was either deliberately provocative or irresponsible.

**The video's content.** The original music video interspersed Aldean's performance footage with news clips showing protests, riots, and confrontations with police — some of which were identified as footage from Black Lives Matter demonstrations in 2020. The official YouTube version was later quietly edited to remove some of those clips. Aldean's record label, BBR Music Group, attributed the edits to "third-party copyright clearance issues" rather than to any political considerations.

**Aldean's defence.** Aldean rejected the characterisation of the song as racist or as a pro-vigilante anthem. In public statements in July 2023, he described the song as an expression of small-town community solidarity and said it had "nothing to do with race." He called critics' interpretation a "calculated, sensationalist misinformation campaign" and characterised the news footage in the video as "real news footage" documenting events his audience would recognise.

**Industry response.** Sheryl Crow, Jason Isbell, Maren Morris, and other musicians publicly criticised the song or its framing. Then-former President Donald Trump publicly supported Aldean. The controversy substantially boosted the song's commercial performance: "Try That in a Small Town" reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100, becoming Aldean's first national chart-topper. The dynamics here are consistent with what communications researchers call the "Streisand effect" — the phenomenon in which attempts to suppress or criticise a piece of content inadvertently amplify it (Jansen, B. J., Zhang, M., Sobel, K., & Chowdury, A., 2009, "Twitter power: Tweets as electronic word of mouth," *Journal of the American Society for Information Science and Technology*, 60(11), 2169–2188; the Streisand mechanism itself was first named and documented in internet culture in 2003).

**The 2024 CMT Music Awards.** Jason Aldean performed at the 2024 CMT Music Awards ceremony, approximately nine months after the video removal. This is directly inconsistent with the claim that he had been "banned from CMT." CMT's relationship with Aldean normalised within the year following the controversy.

**About the CBS New Year's Eve show.** A separate claim circulated that Aldean was "banned" from performing "Try That in a Small Town" on a CBS New Year's Eve special. Reporting by Whiskey Riff in January 2026, which reexamined the incident, concluded that the omission of the song from the broadcast was a programming decision by the show's producers rather than a formal network ban on either the artist or the song. This is consistent with the pattern: broadcast producers routinely make choices about what content to include or exclude without issuing formal bans. Calling such decisions "bans" represents a significant upgrade in severity.

## 3. Why People Believe This — And Why the Amplification Happened

The transformation of "CMT pulled one video from rotation" into "Aldean was banned from CMT" is a case study in how real but limited content-moderation decisions get amplified into narratives of industry-wide blacklisting. Several interlocking mechanisms are responsible.

**The semantic work of "ban."** The word "ban" carries heavy freight. In regulatory and legal language, a ban is a formal, comprehensive, enforceable exclusion. In the entertainment industry, "ban" is used loosely to describe anything from an indefinite permanent exclusion to a single programming pass. When news headlines in July 2023 used "ban" language — some reputable outlets used "pulls," while more partisan outlets immediately adopted "bans" — they encoded events using a term whose severity exceeded the action. Audiences reading "CMT bans Jason Aldean" form an expectation of total exclusion that "CMT removes one Aldean video from rotation" does not justify.

Media linguistics researchers have studied how headline word choice shapes audience interpretation of underlying events even when the article body provides more accurate detail, a phenomenon sometimes called "lead contamination" (Ecker, U. K. H., Lewandowsky, S., Chang, E. P., & Pillai, R., 2014, "The effects of subtle misinformation in news headlines," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied*, 20(4), 323–335). In this case, the upgrade from "video removal" to "ban" was the operative distortion.

**The censorship-culture-wars narrative frame.** The political context of summer 2023 made the narrative of Aldean as a censored artist immediately legible and useful to a specific audience. By July 2023, "cancel culture" and entertainment industry censorship of conservative artists were established narrative templates in right-of-centre media. The CMT video removal slotted directly into this template without requiring additional interpretation: a conservative-leaning country artist makes a song about community values and standing up to lawlessness; the entertainment establishment retaliates by suppressing it; ordinary fans must counter-boycott. This story has a clean protagonist, a clear antagonist, and a satisfying moral resolution. The fact that the story required upgrading "video removal" to "ban" and that the song subsequently topped the national charts (suggesting that if censorship occurred, it was extraordinarily ineffective) did not diminish the narrative's usefulness to its audience.

Research on motivated reasoning suggests that factual corrections to ideologically salient beliefs are frequently dismissed or rejected when they conflict with strongly held prior commitments — a mechanism documented by Brendan Nyhan and Jason Reifler in their studies on the "backfire effect" in politically charged fact corrections (Nyhan, B., & Reifler, J., 2010, "When corrections fail: The persistence of political misperceptions," *Political Behavior*, 32(2), 303–330). Subsequent research has refined and partially challenged the backfire effect's universality, but the basic phenomenon — that corrections to ideologically useful beliefs face higher resistance than corrections to neutral claims — remains supported (Wood, T., & Porter, E., 2019, "The elusive backfire effect: Mass attitudes' steadfast factual adherence," *Political Behavior*, 41(1), 135–163).

**The commercially confusing outcome.** A genuine complexity in this case is that the removal of the video from CMT's rotation appears to have substantially boosted the song's commercial performance. The Streisand Effect dynamics meant that the controversy made "Try That in a Small Town" vastly more visible than it would otherwise have been, accelerating its climb to the top of the national charts and generating sales it would not have achieved through ordinary promotional channels alone. If CMT intended to suppress the song's reach, it accomplished the reverse. This outcome is genuinely paradoxical, and the paradox has made the narrative more sticky: supporters of Aldean can simultaneously claim that the entertainment establishment tried to silence him and that popular response proved the attempt failed. Both claims are partially accurate, which is precisely the productive ambiguity that makes the overall narrative hard to correct cleanly.

**The awards-show dimension.** The extension of the claim from "CMT pulled the video" to "Aldean was banned from awards shows" represents a further amplification without factual basis. Aldean was not excluded from the CMA Awards, was not banned from the American Music Awards, and appeared at the 2024 CMT Music Awards. The awards-show dimension of the claim appears to have developed through social media inference: if he was "banned from CMT," users reasoned, presumably the awards would follow. This is the contagion of a false framing: once "banned" was established as the operative word, audiences applied it to any industry event Aldean did not visibly dominate in 2023, treating absence from a given televised moment as confirming evidence of comprehensive exclusion.

## 4. The Stakes

The specific case of Jason Aldean and CMT in 2023 is a relatively constrained piece of cultural industry history. Its importance for media literacy purposes is what it illustrates about four distinct phenomena:

**Content decisions are not the same as personal bans.** When a network, streaming service, or venue makes a programming decision about a piece of content — declining to air a video, removing a show from rotation, passing on booking a performance — it is making an editorial or curatorial decision about content. Treating this as a personal exclusion of the associated artist imposes a category error. Netflix declining to renew a show is not banning the showrunner. A radio station choosing not to add a song to its playlist is not banning the artist. These decisions may reflect judgments about the content's suitability, commercial potential, political risk, or any number of other factors — but they are content-level decisions, not person-level bans.

**The entertainment industry's power to suppress is frequently overstated.** The Aldean case illustrates a common misunderstanding of how cultural industries exercise power over artists. The music industry has genuine mechanisms of suppression — label contract restrictions, radio payola networks, sync licensing gatekeeping. But informal social ostracism or a single platform's content decision rarely produces the comprehensive suppression that "ban" implies. Artists who are genuinely popular with large, loyal audiences tend to reach those audiences despite single-platform decisions and sometimes with acceleration from the controversy such decisions generate.

**Conservative counter-boycotts as political signalling.** Calls to boycott CMT following the video removal — and more broadly the cultural framing of Aldean's situation as martyrdom — functioned as political signalling largely independent of actual commercial impact on CMT. Research on consumer boycotts in politically charged contexts suggests that boycott calls frequently fail to produce measurable economic harm to their targets while successfully communicating political identity to co-partisans (Friedman, M., 1999, *Consumer Boycotts: Effecting Change Through the Marketplace and the Media*, Routledge). The CMT boycott call fits this pattern: CMT's ratings and advertisers were not measurably devastated, but the call itself was clearly legible as a signal of audience solidarity with Aldean and opposition to perceived liberal media bias.

**Why accurate framing matters.** People who believe Aldean was "banned from CMT" are holding a belief that is factually false — and the false version of the story is more useful as political currency than the accurate version. The accurate version (CMT pulled one video; Aldean appeared at CMT Awards nine months later; the song topped the charts) tells a story of temporary, limited, commercially ineffective content moderation followed by normalisation. The false version tells a story of aggressive, comprehensive industry suppression. These stories invite different interpretive conclusions about the entertainment industry, censorship, and the treatment of conservative artists. This is why precision here matters: the available frame shapes available conclusions.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**The 2020 protest footage.** The music video's use of footage from 2020 Black Lives Matter protests — and its subsequent quiet editing — deserves more examination than it typically receives. The editorial decision to include that footage, whatever the intent, placed explicitly racialised social protest imagery in a video whose lyrics describe violent community retaliation against social troublemakers. Whether one believes Aldean's account that the connection was incidental or critics' account that it was deliberate, the combination produced the controversy. The later removal of that footage, attributed to copyright issues, was at minimum a response to the reputational problem the footage had created. Copyright clearance is a standard backstop reason for content changes; whether it was the primary reason or a convenient one is not publicly confirmable.

**The filming location question.** The historical significance of the Maury County Courthouse — as a site of the 1927 lynching of Henry Choate and the 1946 Columbia Race Riot — was reported in numerous outlets including the Associated Press and The Atlantic. Aldean and his team have maintained they were not aware of the courthouse's history when choosing the filming location, and that it was selected for its visual aesthetics; they said the song was written about a general spirit of small-town community. Whether this account is accurate, it draws attention to a broader pattern in which historically resonant sites of racial violence are not systematically catalogued in ways accessible to location scouts and production teams. The failure was one of historical literacy, not necessarily of deliberate intent — but the documentary record of the site's history is straightforward, and several journalists located it within days of the video's release.

**Aldean's subsequent position in country music.** As of 2024 and 2025, Jason Aldean remained an active and commercially successful country music performer with no formal exclusion from major industry platforms. He performed at major venues, appeared on major networks, and released music through standard distribution channels. Whatever the July 2023 controversy indicated about his relationship with certain segments of the entertainment industry, it did not produce the lasting marginalisation that "ban" language implied. This outcome is itself unremarkable — single controversies rarely produce lasting industry ostracism for commercially successful performers — but it is directly falsified by the claim that circulates.

## 6. Verdict

**Mixture — the core event is real; the "ban" framing is significantly overstated.**

CMT genuinely pulled Jason Aldean's "Try That in a Small Town" music video from its rotation in July 2023 — this is documented, confirmed, and real. The reasons for the decision, while not formally stated by CMT, involved the video's controversial combination of vigilante-adjacent lyrics, protest footage, and a historically charged filming location.

What is not accurate: Aldean was not personally "banned" from CMT or from any awards show. He performed at the 2024 CMT Music Awards. He was not banned from the CMA Awards or the American Music Awards. The claim that he was "banned from a music channel" conflates a specific content decision about one video with a comprehensive personal exclusion that did not occur. The song, far from being suppressed, reached number one on the Billboard Hot 100 — Aldean's first national chart-topper.

The narrative of a "ban" is a politically productive amplification of a real, narrower event, shaped by the semantic affordances of the word "ban," the readiness of a culture-wars narrative frame, and the motivated reasoning of audiences for whom the story of Aldean as censored artist is both emotionally satisfying and politically useful.

Rating: **Mixture** — a real content decision amplified into a personal ban that did not occur, with the amplification serving clear political signalling purposes.
