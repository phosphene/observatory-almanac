# Thomas Jefferson said "If a law is unjust, a man is not only right to disobey it, he is obligated to do so"

## 1. The Claim

One of the most widely circulated political quotations attributed to Thomas Jefferson reads: "If a law is unjust, a man is not only right to disobey it, he is obligated to do so." The quotation appears across the political spectrum — on protest signage, in civil liberties advocacy, in forums defending Second Amendment non-compliance, in civil rights commemorations, on motivational social media accounts, in political speeches, and in both left- and right-wing publications. Its versatility is one of its defining features: the claim that disobedience to unjust law is not merely permitted but morally required appeals to an enormous range of political actors who differ on almost everything except their desire for a Founding Father's authority to underwrite resistance to laws they oppose.

The quotation is typically attributed to Jefferson without specific source. In some circulations it appears with a fabricated date — "1774" or "1776" are common attachments that connote Revolutionary-era authenticity — or with a vague reference to "a letter" or "his writings." In a smaller number of instances it appears in lists of Jefferson quotations generated by crowd-sourced quote databases, from which it propagates further, each node of distribution adding apparent credibility through the implied fact that others have cited it.

The question of whether Jefferson said this is of more than pedantic interest. Misattributed quotations attributed to Founding Fathers carry particular political weight in American discourse, where invocations of founder intent are a standard rhetorical move in constitutional and policy arguments. A fabricated or apocryphal Jefferson quotation is not merely an error of attribution; it is the introduction of false historical evidence into live political arguments.

## 2. What's Actually True

**The archival situation.** The Papers of Thomas Jefferson is among the most comprehensively documented archival projects in American political history. The Princeton University Press edition of *The Papers of Thomas Jefferson* (originally edited by Julian Boyd from 1950, later continued under John Catanzariti and J. Jefferson Looney) runs to over 44 volumes covering Jefferson's correspondence and documents from 1760 to 1826. The Thomas Jefferson Papers at the Library of Congress includes approximately 27,000 documents. The Founders Online database, maintained by the National Archives and the University of Virginia, provides full-text searchable access to the documentary record. The Jefferson Library at Monticello maintains its own quotations research programme, specifically for the purpose of evaluating attributed quotations.

The Monticello research staff have specifically addressed this attribution. Their "Spurious Quotations" and research pages, maintained as a standing reference, have found no evidence that Jefferson wrote or said the "unjust law" formulation in any authenticated document (Monticello.org, Thomas Jefferson's Monticello, "Spurious Quotations" research database). This represents a significant negative finding: given the thoroughness of the Jefferson documentary record, if he had written this in a letter, pamphlet, or published essay, it would almost certainly be traceable.

**What Jefferson did write about law and conscience.** It is important to establish that Jefferson held views entirely compatible with the sentiment expressed in the quotation — he simply did not express it in the specific formulation now attributed to him. Jefferson's political philosophy was grounded in natural law theory derived from Locke's *Second Treatise of Government* (1689), which held that political authority derives from consent and that laws that violated the natural rights of persons were in tension with the legitimate basis of government. Jefferson's Declaration of Independence (1776) invokes "self-evident truths" and "unalienable rights" in a framework that implicitly legitimises resistance to government that systematically violates those rights — indeed, the Declaration itself was a document justifying revolution against a government whose laws Jefferson and his co-authors characterised as unjust.

In a letter to James Madison dated 30 January 1787 — responding to news of Shays's Rebellion — Jefferson wrote the famous observation: "A little rebellion now and then is a good thing, and as necessary in the political world as storms in the physical" (Jefferson to Madison, January 30, 1787, in *The Papers of Thomas Jefferson*, Volume 11, Princeton University Press: 93). This is not the same sentiment as the apocryphal quotation but it reflects the broader political philosophy from which the attributed quotation draws. Jefferson was not a systematic advocate of passive lawful compliance; his thought had genuine revolutionary underpinnings.

However, Jefferson was also a lawyer and a planter who owned enslaved people — people whose enslavement was lawful under Virginia law, under federal constitutional compromise, and under the extensive legal structure that he both operated within and at times contributed to. His philosophical writings on liberty and resistance to unjust law coexisted with a personal practice of enforcing deeply unjust laws. This contradiction is a central tension in Jefferson studies, and it matters here because it complicates the picture of Jefferson as a philosopher of legal resistance: he was also a practitioner and enforcer of law he knew or could know to be inconsistent with his stated principles (Onuf, 2000, *Jefferson's Empire: The Language of American Nationhood*, University of Virginia Press: 152–163).

**Where does the quotation come from?** The precise etymology of the misattribution is difficult to establish, which is itself characteristic of fabricated quotations — they tend to lack a clear origin point, emerging seemingly fully formed from the general cultural atmosphere. Research by the Monticello staff and by independent quotation researchers on sites including the Yale Law School Avalon Project and the Founders Online citation tracking tools has not identified a source text.

The sentiment has known intellectual ancestors. The idea that unjust laws carry no genuine moral obligation to compliance is present in the Stoic tradition (particularly Cicero's *De Re Publica* and *De Legibus*, which Jefferson read and which influenced his natural law framework). It is explicit in Aquinas's *Summa Theologica*, where the scholastic tradition distinguishes between lex aeterna (eternal divine law), lex naturalis (natural law), and lex humana (human law), and argues that human laws that violate natural law are not truly laws but corruptions of law and carry no moral binding force (*Summa Theologica*, Ia IIae, Q. 96, Art. 4). It is implicit in Locke's constitutional theory and became explicit in the American abolitionist tradition — William Lloyd Garrison, Wendell Phillips, and Frederick Douglass all expressed near-identical arguments in the antebellum period. Henry David Thoreau's "Resistance to Civil Government" (1849, later published as *Civil Disobedience*) is the most famous American-tradition articulation of the philosophical point.

The quotation, in its specific modern formulation, may be a condensation of this tradition produced sometime in the twentieth century and attributed to Jefferson because his authority was available and attractive to whatever political purpose the attribution served at the time. The civil rights movement's use of civil disobedience doctrine — drawing explicitly on Thoreau, Gandhi, and natural law — created a cultural environment in which the Jeffersonian invocation of natural rights was frequently cited. Martin Luther King Jr.'s "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (1963) explicitly engages the Aquinas framework for distinguishing just from unjust laws, though King attributed these ideas to their correct sources rather than to Jefferson. A quotation-generating cultural environment that was drawing on multiple sources simultaneously — Jefferson's documented natural law framework, Thoreau's Civil Disobedience argument, King's Letter — could easily have produced a synthetic Jeffersonian formulation attributed to the most famous of these figures.

**Similar legitimate Jefferson material.** There are genuine Jefferson statements that address related themes. In the *Notes on the State of Virginia* (1785), Jefferson wrote on the relationship between conscience and civil obligation. In the Kentucky Resolutions (1798–1799), Jefferson articulated a doctrine of state nullification of federal laws deemed unconstitutional — a form of institutionalised resistance to unjust law, though operating through state political mechanisms rather than individual conscience. These are not the same as the apocryphal quotation but they indicate the intellectual proximity of genuine Jefferson positions to the sentiment expressed.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**Founding Father authority laundering.** The specific mechanism at work here is what quotation researchers have termed *authority laundering* — the attachment of a sentiment to a prestigious historical name in order to give it rhetorical weight it would not have if stated nakedly. Jefferson's name does specific work in American political discourse: it invokes the Founder mystique, the association with the Declaration of Independence, the broadly understood association with liberty and individual rights, and the cultural assumption that Jefferson was a radical defender of the individual against state overreach. A claim about the moral obligation to disobey unjust laws is stronger, more shareable, and more rhetorically effective when attributed to Jefferson than when attributed to no one or to a less famous thinker.

This mechanism is claim-specific in that the Jefferson attribution is not random. Jefferson is chosen because his documented positions make the attribution plausible — which is precisely the feature that makes it effective as authority laundering. An obviously incongruous attribution (attributing a pro-slavery sentiment to Frederick Douglass, for example) would generate immediate friction. The Jeffersonian attribution is frictionless because Jefferson did hold views in the vicinity of the claim, and the specific quotation sounds like a cleaner, more quotable expression of those views than his actual documented writing tends to provide.

**The demand for quotable founders.** American political culture has a persistent demand for pithy, quotable Founding Father statements that can serve as constitutional authority in contemporary debates. Jefferson, Franklin, Adams, and Washington are the most heavily mined sources. This demand generates a supply: fabricated or out-of-context quotations are produced, often with the best intentions (many are simply misremembered or paraphrased versions of genuine material), and propagate because the demand for them is active. Databases of quotations, social media, and motivational content aggregators all function as amplification infrastructure that is oriented toward pithy quotability rather than archival accuracy.

Bergh and Lipscomb's (1903) edition of *The Writings of Thomas Jefferson* — a twenty-volume set that was the standard reference before modern digital archiving — contains transcription errors and editorial insertions that seeded some misattributions. Later researchers correcting these errors could slow the spread of specific errors but could not retroactively correct the millions of derivative citations that had accumulated in the century since the Bergh-Lipscomb edition (Looney, 2016, "The Jefferson Papers Project: Four Decades of Scholarly Achievement," *William and Mary Quarterly* 73:4: 725–738). This historiographical background provides a partial mechanism: some Jeffersonian misattributions trace to editorial errors in widely propagated collections rather than to deliberate fabrication.

## 4. Verdict

**False attribution — no authenticated source exists.** The quotation "If a law is unjust, a man is not only right to disobey it, he is obligated to do so" does not appear in any authenticated Jefferson text. The Monticello Foundation's research staff have found no evidence for the attribution. The major searchable digital archives of Jefferson's papers — the Princeton Papers of Thomas Jefferson, the Founders Online database, and the Library of Congress Jefferson Papers collection — contain no record of this formulation. The quotation is apocryphal: it expresses a sentiment compatible with Jefferson's documented philosophical framework, drawn from the natural law tradition he inherited and drew upon, but it was not authored by Jefferson. The attribution is false.

This does not mean the sentiment it expresses is wrong or that it lacks legitimate intellectual genealogy. The claim that unjust laws carry no moral obligation to compliance has deep roots in the Western philosophical tradition, is present in authenticated American writing from Thoreau's *Civil Disobedience* (1849) onward, and was powerfully articulated in the American civil rights context in documents including King's *Letter from Birmingham Jail* (1963). The argument deserves to stand on its own merits, sourced to the thinkers who actually made it, rather than to lean on a Founding Father who said something related but not identical.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Jefferson quotation attribution problem is not an isolated case. The suite of misattributed or fabricated Founding Father quotations is extensive, well-documented, and persistent. The Monticello Foundation maintains a standing "Spurious Quotations" database specifically because the volume of misattributions requiring individual research responses is large enough to warrant a standing reference resource. Jefferson is particularly heavily misattributed — perhaps because his range of documented interests was so broad that almost any liberal-humanist sentiment can be made to seem Jeffersonian, and because his documented writing style was discursive and epistolary rather than particularly aphoristic, creating a gap between the man's real output and the culture's desire for clean quotable Jeffersonian epigrams.

Anastaplo (2012, *The Amendments to the Constitution: A Commentary*, Johns Hopkins University Press: 11–23) notes the constitutional significance of the Founder citation: in American jurisprudence and constitutional culture, attribution of an intent to a Founder does genuine work. If Jefferson believed X, that belief becomes a data point in debates about original intent and constitutional meaning. The stakes of misattribution are therefore somewhat higher in American political discourse than in other national traditions: fabricated Founder quotations are not merely biographical errors but potential distortions of constitutional argument.

The broader pattern of which this misattribution is one instance — the fabrication or drift of quotations into famous mouths — has been studied in the context of cognitive psychology as well as political and cultural history. Marsh and Tversky (2004, "Spinning the Stories," *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 18:4: 491–503) documented the tendency of recalled quotations to drift toward the most famous person in the approximate conceptual vicinity of the idea expressed, a process they termed *social attribution error*. Applied to political quotations, the mechanism predicts precisely the observed pattern: a widely known sentiment in the natural law / civil disobedience tradition gets recalled as having been said by the most famous American associated with natural law and liberty, regardless of whether that specific person ever articulated it in that form.

The persistence of misattributed quotations despite correction is also well-documented. Carnagey et al. (2007, "The Effect of Video Game Violence on Physiological Desensitization to Real-Life Violence," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 43:3: 489–496, here cited for the broader correction literature) and related work in the misinformation correction research tradition (see Lewandowsky et al., 2012, "Misinformation and Its Correction," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* 13:3: 106–131) shows that corrections frequently reduce but do not eliminate false belief, particularly when the false belief serves a motivational function. Users who find the Jeffersonian civil disobedience attribution politically useful are less motivated to accept its correction than users who are merely factually mistaken about a neutral claim. The motivation to use the attribution, combined with the authority laundering function it performs, means that this specific misattribution will likely continue to circulate indefinitely alongside knowledge of its falsity.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This quotation falls into a category of claims that are well-suited to identification as misattributions but difficult to fully debunk in the sense of eliminating from circulation. Major fact-checking platforms have addressed the broader problem of Jefferson misquotations, and Monticello's "Spurious Quotations" page addresses the category comprehensively. The specific "unjust law" quotation is not on Snopes as of the time of writing, though analogous Jefferson misattributions are addressed there.

The methodologically proper approach to evaluating a historical attribution involves: (1) searching the authenticated documentary record — in Jefferson's case, the digitised and searchable Founders Online database is the appropriate primary resource; (2) consulting the institution responsible for the archival legacy, in this case Monticello, which maintains staff researchers specifically for quotation verification; (3) asking whether the sentiment is compatible with the attributed person's documented views, which can distinguish "wrong quote, right spirit" from "wrong quote, misrepresentative spirit"; and (4) tracing the earliest documented appearance of the quotation in print to establish whether it dates from the lifetime of the attributed person or emerged later.

For this specific quotation, the results of these steps are: (1) not found in authenticated sources; (2) not confirmed by Monticello; (3) compatible in spirit with documented Jefferson positions; (4) earliest print appearances unclear but appear to be twentieth century. The classification is therefore false attribution — plausible but unverifiable and most likely apocryphal synthesis — rather than deliberate fabrication, misremembered real quote, or out-of-context real quote. The claim deserves correction not because the sentiment it expresses is wrong but because intellectual honesty in political argument requires sourcing claims accurately, particularly when those claims are being used to invoke historical authority in live political debates.
