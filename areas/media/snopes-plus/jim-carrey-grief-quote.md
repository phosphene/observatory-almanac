# Jim Carrey Said "I Think Everybody Should Get Rich and Famous So They Can See That It's Not the Answer"


## 1. The Claim

A quote is widely attributed to Jim Carrey, the Canadian-American comedian and actor:

> *"I think everybody should get rich and famous and do everything they ever dreamed of so they can see that it's not the answer."*

The quote appears across motivational content websites, self-help newsletters, MBA lecture slides, graduation speech collections, mental health awareness accounts, and entrepreneurship-adjacent social media. It has been cited in newspaper columns, in books on psychological wellbeing, in YouTube video essays about materialism and meaning, and in Reddit threads about the inadequacy of success as a life goal. It has accumulated millions of shares across platforms in various forms over the past two decades.

The specific claim — that Jim Carrey said this — is true. The quote is verified. But the claim as it functions in the information environment is rarely just the attribution; it is the attribution plus a set of implied meanings and uses that the quote accrues as it travels, and those implied meanings are worth examining carefully.

In most of the contexts where the quote circulates, it functions as a testimonial: a celebrity who achieved extreme levels of exactly the things the culture says we should want — wealth, fame, the realisation of dreams — confirming from personal experience that those things did not deliver what they promised. The quote is used as authority for a conclusion: that the pursuit of conventional success is misguided, that inner meaning must come from elsewhere, that the ambition engine is ultimately self-defeating. Carrey is positioned as the expert witness whose credentials (extreme wealth and fame, personal experience with both their acquisition and insufficiency) uniquely qualify him to testify.

The quote circulates in two main configurations. The first is motivational-ironic: it is used to punctuate discussions of work-life balance, burnout, or the anti-hustle backlash, positioned as sophisticated wisdom that undercuts the dominant narrative of success. The second is more directly therapeutic: it appears in content about depression, anxiety, and the experience of achieving goals only to find the expected satisfaction absent — what psychologists call the "arrival fallacy" (Ben-Shahar, T., 2009, *Happier: Can You Learn to Be Happy?*, McGraw-Hill). In this configuration, Carrey's quote becomes clinical support from an unexpected direction.

There is also a flourishing subtler misattribution phenomenon: the quote is sometimes attributed as "Jim Carrey once said" without any contextual anchoring, implying it was a spontaneous, perhaps private, remark of deep personal revelation rather than a prepared observation in a promotional press interview. This framing matters.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The quote is authentic.** Research by Quote Investigator — the reference project maintained by Garson O'Toole that traces quotation origins through primary source documentation — confirms that the earliest traceable instance of the remark is from a December 2005 interview that journalist Jay Stone conducted with Carrey while Carrey was promoting the comedy film *Fun with Dick and Jane*. The interview was published in The Ottawa Citizen in Canada on or around December 16, 2005, and was simultaneously distributed by CanWest News Service, appearing in multiple Canadian newspapers including The Kingston Whig-Standard and The Gazette of Montreal. In the interview, the passage reads:

> *He says that earlier in his career, he believed that making just one more film, getting one more hit, would be enough, but he got tired of being emotionally disappointed. "You just go like, 'Yeah, it was a fantastic hit, but what now?'" Carrey's advice: "I think everybody should get rich and famous and everything they ever dreamed of so they can see that that's not the answer."*

The phrasing is slightly different across syndicated versions — some insert "have" before "everything they ever dreamed of," improving the grammar; one early reprint changes the final phrase to "that's not the answer" versus the more commonly cited "it's not the answer." These are transcription variants, not substantive alterations; the sense is identical. The attribution is unambiguous: the statement is contemporaneously attributed by a named journalist in a publication of record, with no subsequent retraction or clarification by Carrey.

By 2007 the quote was being reprinted in American newspapers. The Knoxville News-Sentinel in Tennessee carried a version of it in 2007. By 2010 it was appearing in online forums and motivational sites; by 2015 to 2020 it had achieved mass viral distribution on social media platforms.

**The context that normally disappears.** What the verified interview record reveals is that the quote was made in a specific discursive context: a press junket interview for a broad comedy in which Carrey was reflecting on the emotional disappointment of repeated professional success. The observation is embedded in a pragmatic narrative — earlier in his career, he believed more success would eventually produce satisfaction; repeated experience showed him it did not. The "advice" framing in Stone's report signals that Carrey was offering a form of warning or counsel rather than a pure philosophical pronouncement.

This context does not change the words, but it does contextualise their intended function. Carrey was not delivering a considered philosophical lecture on the nature of meaning and worldly goods. He was answering a journalist's question during a promotional tour, drawing on personal experience to articulate a recognisable emotional pattern. The observation is genuine, and its emotional content is real — but it is not the polished aphorism it appears to be once stripped of its interview scaffolding.

**What Carrey has said on related themes since.** In subsequent years, Carrey developed the themes of this observation in more extended forms. His 2014 commencement address at Maharishi University of Management is the most cited example: a speech in which he elaborated on the insufficiency of fear-driven ambition and the difference between purpose-driven and fear-driven choices. The speech is available in full, documented video form, and it extends the 2005 quote's implicit logic significantly. Carrey's public engagement with questions of consciousness, painting, and Eckhart Tolle–influenced spirituality across the 2010s provides additional verified context for how the 2005 remark should be understood within his evolving public philosophy.


## 3. Why the True Quote Still Gets the Story Wrong

The fact that this quote is authentic does not mean that the claim — understood as the quote plus its implied meanings in circulation — is fully accurate. The accurate attribution conceals a distortion mechanism that is in some ways more insidious than simple misattribution: *context-laundering*.

**The authority claim is internally circular.** When the quote circulates as evidence that success is "not the answer," it is using Carrey's experience of extreme success as its credential. But Carrey's experience is not generalisable on its own terms without additional argument. The implicit reasoning runs: Carrey got rich and famous, and found it insufficient; therefore rich-and-famous is insufficient. But this inference only holds if we accept that Carrey's subjective experience of insufficient satisfaction is accurate, representative, and universal — none of which follows from the attribution alone. What makes the quote feel authoritative is precisely the authority it claims to undermine: extreme fame and wealth.

Psychologist Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton's landmark income-wellbeing research (Kahneman, D., & Deaton, A., 2010, "High income improves evaluation of life but not emotional well-being," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 107(38), 16489–16493) found that emotional wellbeing increased with income up to approximately $75,000 annual income (in 2010 USD), suggesting that the relationship between wealth and subjective wellbeing is not uniformly negative but plateaus. More recent work by Matthew Killingsworth (Killingsworth, M. A., 2021, "Experienced well-being rises with income, even above $75,000 per year," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 118(4), e2016976118) finds continued wellbeing increases at higher income levels using experience-sampling methodology. The point is not that Carrey was wrong about his own experience but that his experience cannot stand alone as universal evidence — which is exactly how the circulated quote deploys it.

**The quote strips the hedonic treadmill of its technical scaffold.** The observation that achieving goals fails to produce expected lasting satisfaction has a precise psychological name and an extensive research literature. The "hedonic treadmill" or "hedonic adaptation" describes the tendency of humans to return to a relatively stable baseline of happiness following both positive and negative events (Brickman, P., Coates, D., & Janoff-Bulman, R., 1978, "Lottery winners and accident victims: Is happiness relative?" *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 36(8), 917–927). Tal Ben-Shahar's concept of the "arrival fallacy" describes the specific phenomenon of achieving a goal and experiencing the expected satisfaction fail to materialise or persist. These are documented, studied phenomena with predictable parameters.

When Carrey's quote is circulated as isolated wisdom, it presents as novel insight — a celebrity's personal discovery — what is in fact a well-understood psychological mechanism. This is not a problem with the quote itself but with how its circulation forecloses deeper engagement. Readers satisfied with the celebrity testimony have less incentive to engage with the research literature that could actually give them predictive tools for managing hedonic adaptation in their own lives. The quote illuminates a truth but traps the reader in the authority of the illustrative anecdote rather than pointing them toward the explanatory science.

**The self-help register inverts the quote's rhetorical purpose.** Carrey's framing in the original interview is notably ironic and slightly sardonic. "Carrey's advice: get rich and famous so you can see it's not the answer" is a joke with a serious interior — it is the advice of someone who got what everyone wants and found it did not deliver what it promised; the "advice" is consciously absurd because it prescribes a experience that is both extremely difficult to actually have and sufficient to produce a lesson everyone could receive through less costly means. The ironic distance in the original is real.

In the self-help context where the quote most circulates, the irony evaporates. The quote becomes sincere instruction or sincere testimony about the nature of meaning. This is not technically a falsification of Carrey's words, but it inverts their register from wry observation to solemn wisdom — a transformation that changes how listeners process it and what they do with it.


## 4. The Stakes

Understanding that this quote is real but typically decontextualised matters for several reasons.

First, it demonstrates that accurate attribution is not the same as accurate claim. Most quote fact-checking stops at the level of attribution: did X say Y? When the answer is yes, the claim is marked true and the analysis ends. But the claim as it actually circulates in the information environment is attribution plus implied meaning plus implied generalisability plus implied use. These additional layers can be wrong even when the attribution is right.

Second, it illustrates the specific epistemic problem of *parasocial testimony* — using a celebrity's personal report of their inner life as evidence about the world rather than about the celebrity. Carrey reporting his subjective experience of insufficient satisfaction following professional success is evidence about Carrey's psychology, not about the universal relationship between worldly success and wellbeing. These are categorically different claims, and their conflation is common, comfortable, and resistant to correction precisely because the celebrity's authority feels sufficient.

Third, the pattern of context-laundering the quote undergoes — from promotional press interview to standalone wisdom to clinical support for psychological concepts — demonstrates how information loses its provenance as it travels. The original journalists-and-dates context of the 2005 interview disappears almost entirely from most circulations of the quote. This is a general pathology of viral content: the evidentiary context that would allow audiences to assess the material appropriately is exactly what gets stripped in the compression and sharing process.

Research by Hunt Allcott and Matthew Gentzkow (2017, "Social Media and Fake News in the 2016 Election," *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 31(2), 211–236) documents how the sharing format of social media systematically decouples claims from their documentary contexts, contributing to environments in which surface plausibility substitutes for evidentiary evaluation. The Carrey quote is a minor but clean illustration of the mechanism: a verified statement, shorn of its press-junket scaffolding, recontextualised as profound philosophical testimony.


## 5. The Original Context in Full

For reference, the verified reporting context deserves to be stated completely. Jay Stone's interview with Jim Carrey, published in The Ottawa Citizen in December 2005 during the press tour for *Fun with Dick and Jane* (director: Dean Parisot, 2005), captured Carrey reflecting on the emotional exhaustion of a career built around expecting each professional success to finally deliver permanent satisfaction. His observation that it did not — that the cycle of achievement and renewed disappointment was its own trap — was made in the mode of a veteran's wry counsel to an imagined audience still earlier in that cycle. The quote is best understood as a warning dressed as ironic advice, from a working comedian speaking to a reporter, not as philosophical testimony recorded for posterity.

The "not the answer" framing is itself significant. Carrey did not say wealth and fame produce misery, or that they should be avoided. He said they are not the answer. This is a more precise and more interesting claim: the things one pursues as answers to existential questions of meaning and purpose turn out, on acquisition, not to have answered those questions. This is the hedonic treadmill experienced from the inside and described by someone who ran it very fast and very far. The precision of "not the answer" versus "makes you unhappy" is worth preserving — and it is routinely flattened in the quote's online travels.


## 6. Verdict

**True attribution; decontextualised use.** Jim Carrey did say the words attributed to him. The quote originates from a verifiable December 2005 press interview recorded by journalist Jay Stone and published in The Ottawa Citizen, confirmed independently by Quote Investigator's source tracing. The attribution is sound.

The epistemic problem is not the attribution but the context-laundering the quote undergoes in circulation: the press interview scaffolding disappears, the ironic register is flattened into sincere testimony, and the limited individual report of subjective experience is deployed as universal evidence about the relationship between success and meaning. The quote is true as an attribution. Whether the claim it is used to support — that worldly success is universally insufficient as a source of meaning — is established by it is a distinct question, and the answer is: not alone, and not without the psychological literature that explains the mechanism Carrey experienced.

The research on hedonic adaptation (Brickman et al., 1978, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*) and the arrival fallacy (Ben-Shahar, 2009) provides the conceptual framework that makes Carrey's observation interpretable and generalisable. Most circulations of the quote do not mention them.
