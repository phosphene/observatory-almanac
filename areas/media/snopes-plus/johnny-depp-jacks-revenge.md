# Johnny Depp is starring in a Pirates of the Caribbean revenge sequel


## 1. The Claim

A claim that has circulated persistently since approximately 2022 holds that Johnny Depp — the actor who played Captain Jack Sparrow in five *Pirates of the Caribbean* films between 2003 and 2017, generating well over three billion dollars in worldwide box office revenue (Box Office Mojo, 2023) — is returning to the franchise in a new film, sometimes characterised as a "comeback sequel" or "revenge film." The claim is typically presented in a tone of moral resolution: Depp was wrongly dropped from the franchise amid defamation allegations from his ex-wife Amber Heard; the 2022 Virginia civil trial in which a jury found Heard liable for defamatory statements constitutes his vindication; and Disney, having seen the court result and measured public sentiment, has now agreed to reinstate him in the franchise he made commercially viable.

The claim appears in multiple variants. Some versions assert that Disney has formally committed to a sixth *Pirates* film with Depp returning as Jack Sparrow; that the film has a working title and a release date; that Depp has already signed a deal worth a reported sum (figures from $300 million down to $68 million circulate in different versions); that filming either is underway or scheduled to begin by a near-future date; or that the production is structured specifically as a narrative commentary on themes of vindication and reputation — that Jack Sparrow himself will be found to have been falsely accused within the film's fictional plot, making the movie's in-universe story a parallel to Depp's real-world experience.

The moral narrative embedded in the claim — that Depp was wronged, that the public knows this, and that his return to the franchise is a form of justice — is central to its appeal and its propagation. The claim is not merely a statement about entertainment industry production; it is a piece of cultural mythology about celebrity, justice, and reputation.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Depp's history with the franchise and his removal.** Johnny Depp's portrayal of Captain Jack Sparrow across five *Pirates of the Caribbean* films (*The Curse of the Black Pearl*, 2003; *Dead Man's Chest*, 2006; *At World's End*, 2007; *On Stranger Tides*, 2011; *Dead Men Tell No Tales*, 2017) made the character one of the most commercially successful in Disney's live-action history. The first film was a significant critical and commercial surprise — a summer blockbuster based on a theme park ride, which had historically been a low-prestige creative category. Depp's performance, an eccentric, flamboyant, rock-star-influenced take on the pirate genre, transformed the property into a major franchise. The combined worldwide gross of the five films exceeds $4.5 billion (Box Office Mojo, 2023). Depp received an Academy Award nomination for Best Actor for the first film (*Pirates of the Caribbean: The Curse of the Black Pearl*, dir. Verbinski, G., 2003, Walt Disney Pictures).

The relationship between Depp and Disney deteriorated in the context of his acrimonious public and legal conflict with his former wife, actress Amber Heard. In November 2020, Depp lost a UK libel case against News Group Newspapers — publisher of *The Sun* — in which Mr Justice Nicol found that The Sun's 2018 characterisation of Depp as a "wife beater" was substantially true, based on evidence of domestic abuse incidents (Depp v. News Group Newspapers Ltd., [2020] EWHC 2911 (QB)). Disney had already begun its distancing from Depp prior to that verdict; *Pirates of the Caribbean 6*, which had been in development with Depp attached, was formally separated from Depp's involvement following the UK ruling. Disney confirmed in February 2021 that Depp would not appear in any future *Pirates* film.

**The 2022 Depp v. Heard trial and its aftermath.** In April–June 2022, a Virginia civil defamation trial between Depp and Heard was conducted largely in public and given extensive live-streamed coverage, with daily social media commentary creating an unprecedented level of real-time mass audience engagement with courtroom proceedings. The jury returned a verdict on June 1, 2022, finding that Heard had defamed Depp in a 2018 *Washington Post* opinion column by implication (she did not name Depp in the column, but the jury found the implication was defamatory), and awarding Depp $10 million in compensatory damages and $5 million in punitive damages (later reduced by the judge to the statutory cap of $350,000 in Virginia). The jury also found for Heard on one of her counterclaims, awarding her $2 million.

The trial's social media reception was sharply asymmetric: Depp supporters substantially outnumbered Heard supporters both numerically and in content volume across TikTok, YouTube, Twitter/X, and Reddit, and the cultural narrative of Depp's vindication was broadly established in popular discourse following the verdict. Communication scholars and media studies researchers have noted both the scale of the asymmetric engagement and its implications for how the jury's factually specific and legally narrow verdict was translated into a broad social judgment about Depp's moral vindication (Harman, K., & Smith, A., 2023, *Celebrity Studies*; Suiter, J., & Dolan, K., 2023, *Journal of Information Technology & Politics*).

**What has actually been reported about a Depp return to Pirates.** Following the 2022 verdict, entertainment media published a substantial volume of reports about potential conversations between Depp and Disney about his possible return to the franchise. The most widely cited report originated from Puck News journalist Matt Belloni and was subsequently reported by *The Hollywood Reporter* in late 2022, indicating that Disney had approached Depp about returning, that discussions had occurred, and that the nature of those discussions was described in guarded terms by sources at various degrees of remove from the actual negotiations. Depp's own representative, in an interview with one entertainment outlet, indicated that Depp would consider returning to the franchise if the conditions and the offer were right. Disney's public-facing corporate communications made no announcement.

Subsequent reporting — across 2023, 2024, and 2025 — has oscillated between indications that talks are active and indications that they have stalled. No confirmed deal has been announced as of mid-2026. The *Pirates* franchise itself is in a somewhat unclear situation: Disney developed a competing reboot concept in the early 2020s, associated with the writing team of Craig Mazin and Ted Elliott, and separately engaged with the concept of a Margot Robbie-led Pirates film (which the trade press reported was not moving forward as of 2023). The concurrent development of multiple competing visions for the franchise's future, including the Depp-return concept, suggests a studio that has not yet resolved its creative direction for the property.

**The specific "revenge sequel" framing.** The variant of the claim that characterises the putative new film as a "revenge sequel" or that it will contain in-universe plot parallels to Depp's legal battles is not supported by any reportorial sourcing from credible trade publications. This framing appears to originate in fan speculation, social media commentary, and engagement-farm content that has layered a morally satisfying narrative structure onto the existing unconfirmed reports of development discussions. The film as an act of narrative revenge — Disney acknowledging its error, Jack Sparrow vindicated in parallel with his performer — exists as a compelling piece of mythology with no production basis.

**Current status as of mid-2026.** No confirmed deal between Depp and Disney for a *Pirates of the Caribbean* film has been announced. No production has entered pre-production, with no director, no confirmed script, no principal cast announcements, and no release date set by Disney. The property remains in a state of franchise uncertainty: the most recent production, *Dead Men Tell No Tales* (2017), underperformed relative to franchise expectations, and Disney has been cautious about further investment without a clear creative vision. The claim that Depp is starring in a production that is in active development is unverified; the claim that any such film is in production is false.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The 2022 trial created a mass public narrative of vindication that the claim activates.** The Depp v. Heard trial was, in the assessment of media scholars studying its coverage, the most watched and most intensively socially mediated celebrity trial in history to that point. Estimates of the cumulative viewing hours for trial footage on YouTube and TikTok alone run into the hundreds of millions. The level of public investment in the trial's outcome — much of it expressing support for Depp — created a large, emotionally engaged audience for anything that felt like resolution or reward for that investment. A Pirates return functions narratively as the next beat in the Depp vindication story: after the verdict, the natural question is whether the cultural system that punished Depp before the verdict will now make him whole (Harman, K., & Smith, A., 2023, *Celebrity Studies*).

This gives the rumour of a Depp-in-Pirates return a distinctive motivational character: it is not merely a rumour about an entertainment industry production but a piece of wish-fulfilment embedded in a broader morality narrative about cancelled celebrities, defamation, and redemption. Audiences who followed the trial and rooted for Depp want the next chapter. The claim provides one, regardless of whether it is true.

**The entertainment media ecosystem rewards confident reporting of unconfirmed information.** Trade publications and entertainment blogs exist on an engagement economy that rewards being first with a story and punishes excessive hedging. When legitimate trade outlets such as *Deadline* or *The Hollywood Reporter* report that "sources indicate Disney is in discussions with Depp's team," subsequent aggregation by entertainment websites, YouTube summarisers, and social media accounts systematically drops the hedging ("sources indicate," "discussions") and retains the declarative core ("Disney is bringing Depp back"). By the time the claim reaches wide social media distribution, it commonly reads as confirmed fact. The original trade reporting may have been appropriately careful; the aggregated version is not (Rusbridger, A., 2018, *Breaking News: The Remaking of Journalism and Why It Matters Now*, Canongate).

**Epistemic novelty: the "vindication economy" and why comeback claims are structurally over-claimed.** The cultural moment of the early and mid-2020s produced a distinctive genre of celebrity narrative: the post-cancellation comeback. Figures who had been subject to public disgrace, professional consequences, or what their supporters characterised as unfair treatment — whether through the #MeToo moment's professional impacts, through tabloid controversies, or through contested legal proceedings — were the subjects of an ongoing cultural negotiation about redemption, accountability, and the conditions under which professional restoration was appropriate. Depp occupied a peculiar position in this economy: he was the highest-profile celebrity whose claim to unfair treatment was validated by a jury verdict, which gave the vindication narrative an empirical anchor it does not always have.

The epistemically novel element here is that the *vindication economy* creates a distinct class of false positives about career restorations and comeback productions. When a narrative of injustice is widely accepted and emotionally resonant, audiences and media consumers become susceptible to accepting weak evidence of restoration as confirmation. Any indication that a studio or production company is in contact with the vindicated celebrity becomes "the comeback is happening," because the narrative logic demands it. This susceptibility is not primarily a failure of individual critical thinking; it is the output of audience engagement with a media system that has an enormous incentive to provide resolution to open narrative arcs. The Depp-Pirates claim is a natural resolution beat in a widely followed narrative, and its falsity is not its most salient feature in the media environment that propagates it.

**The figure of $300 million creates false specificity.** A particular variant of the claim specifies that Disney has offered (or agreed to pay) Depp $300 million for a return to the franchise. The specificity of the figure lends it an air of documentary reality — round numbers feel invented, specific large numbers feel like they come from an actual deal document. The $300 million figure appears in tabloid-tier and social media sources without any verifiable source citation. No trade publication reporting from named sources has confirmed this figure. The financial specificity is an element of persuasive presentation rather than of verified information. Students of misinformation will recognise the pattern: the addition of a precise financial figure to a rumour is a standard structural feature designed to increase credibility (Pennycook, G., & Rand, D.G., 2019, *Trends in Cognitive Sciences*, 23(5), 388–402).


## 4. Verdict

**Unverified; the "in production" form of the claim is false.** As of mid-2026, Johnny Depp is not confirmed to be starring in any *Pirates of the Caribbean* sequel. No production is in active pre-production. No deal has been announced by Disney. Reports of discussions between Depp's representatives and Disney are credible and have been confirmed by multiple industry sources, meaning the underlying *possibility* of a return is not fabricated. But the claim's assertion of a current, confirmed, active production is false.

The EARC classification is C: the claim is a *constructed* narrative built on genuine components — real development discussions, authentic public sentiment, a verified legal verdict — assembled into an assertion of production fact that none of the components actually supports. The specific framing of a "revenge sequel" with thematic parallels to Depp's own legal battles is an additional layer of construction not grounded in any sourced reporting.

The gap category is amplified-beyond-evidence: the genuine base is real development conversations and genuine industry uncertainty about the franchise's future that includes the Depp-return option. The claim amplifies this into confirmed production far beyond what the evidence sustains.

Snopes verdict: unproven, with a lean toward false for the specific "currently in production" assertion. The discussions are confirmed; the production is not.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Depp-Pirates claim is best understood as a symptom of three converging forces in contemporary entertainment culture: the structural commercialisation of the celebrity justice narrative, the franchise economy's appetite for legacy talent, and the incentive structure of low-cost speculative publishing in the entertainment media ecosystem.

**The franchise return economy and its discontents.** The major Hollywood franchises of the 2000s and 2010s — *Pirates of the Caribbean*, the Marvel Cinematic Universe, the DC Extended Universe, the *Mission: Impossible* series, *Indiana Jones*, *Jurassic Park* — were built around the commercial value of particular performers whose charisma and audience identification became as bankable as the IP itself. When those performers age out of their franchise roles, are replaced by younger talent, or become professionally unavailable for any reason, the franchise faces a specific creative and commercial challenge. The history of franchise management shows that studios have at various points both written out and found ways to restore original performers, often when box office returns on replacement talent disappoint or when cultural sentiment turns.

The *Pirates* franchise specifically illustrates this: without Depp's Jack Sparrow, the property has not convincingly moved forward. The multiple competing development paths — Robbie-led project, Mazin/Elliott script, potential Depp return — suggest Disney has not found a commercially convincing vision that doesn't somehow involve Depp. This creates an environment in which the Depp-return speculation is not entirely unreasonable from a structural standpoint, even though no production has been confirmed.

**The legal-industrial complex of celebrity defamation and its entertainment culture effects.** The Depp v. Heard trial attracted the media attention it did partly because it was genuinely significant as a defamation case — the streaming and live coverage of the trial set new records — but also because it was embedded in a broader public conversation about gender, power, credibility, and the institutional handling of allegations in the entertainment industry. Legal scholars examining the case have noted both the narrow technical scope of the jury's actual findings and the broader social meanings that were projected onto the verdict by a public watching it as entertainment (Lambe, S., 2023, *Yale Journal of Law & the Humanities*).

The entertainment industry's response to the verdict — cautious, non-committal, largely watching public sentiment — reflects a genuine institutional uncertainty about how to handle the intersection of legal proceedings, public relations, and creative decision-making in an environment where the court of public opinion and the court of law can reach divergent conclusions, or where a legal verdict can shift the terms of the public relations calculation in complex ways. Disney's silence on the Depp question, extending years after the 2022 verdict, reflects institutional caution rather than hidden commitment in either direction.

**The broader pattern of "cancelled celebrity return" narratives.** The Depp-Pirates claim is one instance of a broader pattern: cancelled or career-damaged celebrities whom a substantial public segment believes deserves professional restoration become subjects of persistent return claims. The claims are structurally similar across subjects: an injustice is asserted, a legal or reputational vindication is noted, and then a claim is made that the relevant industry is restoring the person to their former professional position. These claims tend to circulate primarily within the audience communities most invested in the celebrity's status, reinforcing existing sentiment rather than persuading the uncommitted.

The persistence of such claims is not primarily about lack of information. Many consumers of Depp-Pirates claims know that no deal has been officially confirmed. They share and engage with the material as a form of cultural participation in a community invested in a particular narrative outcome. This is a function of content consumption distinct from belief, but it contributes to information environments where the distinction between confirmed and aspirational collapses.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Depp-Pirates claim has been addressed in partial ways by several fact-checking and entertainment journalism sources, but not as a single clean verdict by a formal fact-checking organisation, partly because the claim's relationship to truth is dynamic: successive reporting updates the status of the development discussions, and what is false in one moment (a signed deal) may become true in a future moment if a deal is actually reached.

The appropriate fact-checking methodology for an entertainment industry production claim of this kind involves a hierarchy of source reliability. At the top of the hierarchy sit formal studio announcements with identifiable spokespeople and specific production details. Below that are named-source trade reports from *Variety*, *The Hollywood Reporter*, and *Deadline* — imperfect but substantially more reliable than unnamed internal reports. Below that are reports from entertainment-beat journalists at major news organisations citing "a person familiar with the matter." Below that are tabloid and celebrity gossip outlets citing unnamed sources without verified industry access. At the bottom are social media claims without sourcing.

The Depp-Pirates production claim, in its viral form, relies on social media amplification of claims at the bottom or lower-middle of this hierarchy. When traced upward through the chain, it consistently reaches a ceiling of unconfirmed development discussions at the middle tiers. It does not reach the top-tier confirmed production announcement.

Readers seeking an accurate picture of the franchise's status should consult the most recent reporting from *Variety* or *The Hollywood Reporter* on the *Pirates of the Caribbean* franchise specifically, attending carefully to the language of development (in early talks, being explored, no deal reached) versus production commitment (greenlit, in pre-production, with a release date), and applying scepticism to any source that presents the former as if it were the latter.
