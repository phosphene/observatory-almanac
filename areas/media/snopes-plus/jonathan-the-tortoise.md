# Yes, Jonathan the Tortoise Is the Oldest Known Land Animal


## 1. The Claim

In early January 2022, the website MajesticAnimals.net published photographs showing a giant tortoise named Jonathan living on the island of Saint Helena. The post included two photographs: a modern colour image of Jonathan and a black-and-white photograph taken in the 19th century, initially misdated as 1902 but subsequently identified as being from 1886. The older photograph showed a fully grown Jonathan standing on the grounds of Plantation House, the official residence of the Governor of Saint Helena.

The photographs prompted widespread social media sharing and news coverage, with the claim circulating that Jonathan is not merely very old, but the oldest known living land animal on Earth. Some versions of the claim stated Jonathan was approximately 190 years old, which would place his birth around 1832 — well before the abolition of slavery in the British Empire, before Queen Victoria's coronation, before Charles Darwin published *On the Origin of Species*. The claim also noted Jonathan lives at the governor's residence on Saint Helena, an island famous as Napoleon Bonaparte's final place of exile.

The claim is substantially true, and Snopes correctly assigned it a "True" verdict. Jonathan does hold the Guinness World Record for oldest known living land animal. The 1886 photograph is authentic. The geographical facts are correct. However, the full picture of what "oldest known land animal" means — and the considerable epistemic complexity hidden within each word of that phrase — warrants systematic elaboration.


## 2. What's Actually True

The essential facts are well established and publicly documented. Jonathan is a Seychelles giant tortoise (*Aldabrachelys gigantea hololissa*, formerly designated as a subspecies of *Aldabrachelys gigantea*) who lives on the island of Saint Helena, a remote British Overseas Territory in the South Atlantic Ocean. Jonathan resides on the grounds of Plantation House, the official residence of the Governor of Saint Helena, Ascension and Tristan da Cunha. He was brought to Saint Helena from the Seychelles around 1882, when he was already estimated to be approximately 50 years old based on his physique. He is documented in a photograph from 1886 as a fully grown adult.

Guinness World Records has officially certified Jonathan as the oldest known living land animal. As of his birthday celebrations in January 2022, organized by the government of Saint Helena, Jonathan was estimated to be approximately 190 years old, implying a birth date of around 1832. Guinness's certification has been updated periodically as Jonathan has continued to survive.

The 1886 photograph — taken at Plantation House and credited to Teeny Lucy, director of the NGO Creative Saint Helena Island — is an important piece of evidence because it provides a terminus ante quem for Jonathan's age: he must be at least as old as the photograph, and a fully grown male Seychelles giant tortoise reaches sexual maturity at around 20–25 years and maximum size somewhat later, suggesting Jonathan was substantially older than the photograph by 1886. The BBC documentary archive contains the photograph with its verified provenance (BBC, 2016, "Earliest Known Photograph of Jonathan the 182 Year Old Tortoise, Taken in 1886").

Jonathan's daily life on Saint Helena has been documented in detail through official statements and media coverage. AFP News Agency produced a video profile of Jonathan around the time of the 2022 coverage, noting that he had reportedly lost his sight due to cataracts and lost his sense of smell, but retained his hearing. His caretakers hand-feed him fruit and vegetables, including bananas, apples, and lettuce. Despite his visual and olfactory impairments, Jonathan reportedly continues to engage in mating behaviour with the other giant tortoises that share his enclosure — testimony to a vigour that has become a persistent element in coverage of his remarkable longevity.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes treatment verifies the basic facts accurately but does not engage with the more interesting epistemological dimensions of the record Jonathan holds. Three qualifications deserve serious examination: the meaning of "known," the meaning of "land animal," and the biology of verification for claims about extreme organismal longevity.

**The meaning of "known."** Jonathan's record is the oldest *known* land animal, which is a crucially different claim from "oldest land animal." The qualifier acknowledges that we have no complete record of all land animals alive on Earth, nor any mechanism for verifying the ages of unmonitored individuals. Tortoises of multiple giant tortoise species (*Aldabrachelys gigantea*, the Galápagos giant tortoises of the genus *Chelonoidis*) regularly live for over 100 years in both captivity and the wild. In the wild, individual tortoises are not tagged at birth and have no continuous record of their lives. There almost certainly exist wild Aldabra giant tortoises on Aldabra Atoll (population approximately 100,000 individuals) whose actual ages exceed Jonathan's estimated age, but who have never been formally documented or age-assessed. The record belongs to Jonathan not because he is necessarily the oldest living land animal, but because he is the oldest one with a documentable, externally verifiable age record.

**The meaning of "land animal."** "Land animal" is a category with fuzzy boundaries that excludes several other known record-holders in animal longevity. The ocean quahog clam (*Arctica islandica*), named "Ming," was dated at 507 years old when collected in 2006 from Icelandic waters based on growth ring counting (Butler et al., 2013, "Variability of Marine Climate on the North Icelandic Shelf in a 1357-Year Proxy Archive Based on Growth Increments in the Bivalve *Arctica islandica*," *Palaeogeography, Palaeoclimatology, Palaeoecology*, 373, 141–151). The Greenland shark (*Somniosus microcephalus*) has been estimated to live between 272 and 512 years, making individuals alive today potentially born in the 1500s (Nielsen et al., 2016, "Eye Lens Radiocarbon Reveals Centuries of Longevity in the Greenland Shark," *Science*, 353(6300), 702–704). Tube worms (*Escarpia laminata*) in the Gulf of Mexico have been estimated to live over 1,000 years (Roberts et al., 2018, "Observations of Deep-Sea Tube Worms as Extremely Long-Lived Animals," *The Science of Nature*, 105(5–6), 35). If the category "animal" is extended without qualification, Jonathan's record is not even close to the top.

The "land animal" qualifier is therefore not incidental but definitional to Jonathan's record. It excludes marine organisms specifically, and within terrestrial organisms, it focuses on individual (rather than colonial) animals. This is a defensible category, but it is a chosen category that shapes the claim. It is worth noting that the distinction between "land" and "marine" or "aquatic" is itself not biologically crisp — some tortoise species, including the Aldabra giant tortoise, regularly wade and swim in shallow water, though they remain fundamentally terrestrial air-breathers.

**Age verification in long-lived chelonians.** Tortoises do not have reliable internal age-clocking mechanisms easily accessible to external observation. Scute growth ring counting (analogous to tree ring counting) has been proposed but is unreliable for giant tortoises for several reasons: growth rate is highly dependent on resource availability and temperature, rings are not produced annually with consistent regularity, and the scutes can be worn smooth in older animals. Radiocarbon dating of bone tissue has been used for some individual age estimates but requires invasive sampling (Germano & Bury, 1998, "Age Determination of Turtles with Skeletochronology: Methodology and Applicability," *Journal of Herpetology*, 32(4), 557–568). For Jonathan specifically, age is estimated from the 1886 photograph (providing a minimum age) and from the approximate year of his arrival in Saint Helena (c. 1882, at which point he was described as "fully mature"); the confluence of these lines of evidence produces an estimated birth year of around 1832, but this is an estimate with a probable range of ±10–15 years. He might be 185; he might be 200. The Guinness record appropriately says "approximately" 190.


## 4. The Biology of Exceptional Longevity in Chelonians

The question of why tortoises, and particularly giant tortoises, achieve such extraordinary lifespans is one of the most active areas of comparative gerontology. Giant tortoises represent one of the extreme examples of what biologists call **negligible senescence** — a pattern of aging in which mortality rate does not increase appreciably with age, and in which some biomarkers of aging either decline very slowly or appear to plateau rather than progressively worsen (Finch, 1990, *Longevity, Senescence, and the Genome*, University of Chicago Press).

The mechanisms proposed for this extraordinary longevity include several overlapping factors. First, **low metabolic rate**: giant tortoises have exceptionally low metabolic rates relative to their body mass, consistent with the general inverse relationship between metabolic rate and lifespan in ectotherms (Hulbert et al., 2007, "Life and Death: Metabolic Rate, Membrane Composition, and Life Span of Animals," *Physiological Reviews*, 87(4), 1175–1213). The reduced rate of metabolic by-product accumulation — particularly reactive oxygen species (ROS) — reduces cellular damage rates and may slow the molecular clock of aging.

Second, **effective DNA repair mechanisms**: studies comparing the genomes of long-lived and short-lived reptiles have found enrichment of DNA repair pathways in long-lived species. A comparative genomic study of Galápagos giant tortoises (*Chelonoidis abingdonii* and related species) published in 2021 identified expansions and signatures of positive selection in genes associated with DNA repair and immune function, consistent with adaptations for exceptional longevity (Quesada et al., 2019, "Giant Tortoise Genomes Provide Insights Into Longevity and Age-Related Disease," *Nature Ecology & Evolution*, 3(1), 87–95).

Third, **cancer resistance**: large, long-lived animals pose a statistical puzzle — they have more cells and more cell divisions over their lifetime than small, short-lived animals, which should, naively, produce higher cancer rates (this is known as Peto's Paradox after the epidemiologist Richard Peto who formulated it). Giant tortoises appear to have evolved enhanced tumour suppressor mechanisms that counter this increased statistical risk, including duplication and functional redundancy of tumour suppressor genes like *TP53* (Caulin & Maley, 2011, "Peto's Paradox: Evolution's Prescription for Cancer Prevention," *Trends in Ecology & Evolution*, 26(4), 175–182).

Jonathan's continued competence and relative vigour at 190+ years is itself data. He has lost some sensory capacities, but his locomotor ability, feeding behaviour, and apparent interest in social and reproductive behaviours have been consistently reported by his caretakers. Whether his case constitutes "negligible senescence" in the strict biological sense — or whether there is a progressive but very slow decline in multiple organ systems that will eventually become clinically significant — is not determinable from current observational data.


## 5. Saint Helena as a Crossroads of Natural and Political History

The geographic context of Jonathan's home warrants more attention than it typically receives in coverage of his longevity. Saint Helena is a volcanic island of approximately 122 square kilometres, located in the South Atlantic approximately 1,900 kilometres west of the Angolan coast. It has extraordinary historical significance as a waypoint in the age of sail — a provisioning stop for ships transiting between Europe, Africa, and Asia — and as the site of Napoleon Bonaparte's final exile and death in 1821.

Jonathan arrived on Saint Helena around 1882, approximately 60 years after Napoleon's death. He has therefore lived through the entirety of the island's modern period as a British Overseas Territory, the transition from the age of sail to the age of steam, two world wars (the island served as a prisoner of war camp for Boer prisoners during the Second Anglo-Boer War, 1900–1902), the closure of the island's economy following the opening of the Suez Canal in 1869, and the island's long subsequent period of relative isolation and economic difficulty.

The isolation of Saint Helena, which remained accessible only by sea until the opening of Saint Helena Airport in 2016, created a peculiar inversion: one of the most remote places on Earth became one of the most visited in popular imagination because of Napoleon — and because of Jonathan. Tourism to see Jonathan is now one of the island's primary visitor attractions, contributing to the economy of a community with a population of approximately 4,500 people. His presence at Plantation House makes the governor's residence open to visitors specifically on Jonathan's account, creating an unusual structure where geopolitical institutional space (the governor's official residence) is made publicly accessible because of an animal resident.

Jonathan has also been used in a remarkable range of cultural contexts: featured on the Saint Helena five-pence coin, cited in natural history lectures and popular science writing, and referenced in discussions of extreme animal longevity in gerontological literature. He represents a rare case of a living organism whose continuous historical presence spans multiple historical eras that humans understand as discontinuously different — the era before railways, before telegraphs, before powered flight, before nuclear weapons.


## 6. Conclusions and Epistemic Assessment

Snopes correctly rated this claim as "True," and the basic facts are well established. Jonathan the Seychelles giant tortoise living on Saint Helena holds the Guinness World Record for oldest known living land animal. He is approximately 190 years old based on photographic evidence from 1886 and records of his arrival in Saint Helena around 1882 as a fully mature adult. His life at Plantation House is well documented, his caretakers are verifiable, and the photographic record is authentic.

The deeper picture, which the fact-check does not fully develop, involves significant epistemic qualification: "oldest known" reflects the limits of documentation rather than global biological fact; "land animal" is a chosen category that excludes marine organisms with far greater documented ages; and the age estimate carries uncertainty of perhaps ±10–15 years. None of these qualifications make the claim false — they make it more precise about what it actually asserts. Jonathan is not simply the oldest animal; he is the oldest animal with a documented terrestrial life history amenable to third-party age verification.

His extraordinary longevity also points toward genuinely active scientific questions about the mechanisms of negligible senescence in chelonians, the relationship between slow metabolism and extended lifespan, and the evolutionary genetics of cancer resistance in long-lived organisms. Jonathan is not merely a record-holder; he is a living data point in the comparative biology of aging whose molecular record has barely been accessed. Whether future genomic or physiological analysis of Jonathan himself would contribute to the gerontological literature remains an open possibility.
