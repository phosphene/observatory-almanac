## The Claim

In early July 2020, a meme began circulating across social media platforms with a message that could be parsed as two related assertions. First, that Disney had "hired" Colin Kaepernick. Second, and more provocatively, that this hiring was specifically for the purpose of teaching children about American history. The meme used the language of parental alarm: "Disney hired Colin Kaepernick to teach your kids about American history." Some versions appended commentary about what kind of history children would supposedly be receiving, with the implicit suggestion that Kaepernick's documented views on racism and police violence would constitute the pedagogical content.

The meme appeared against a highly charged backdrop. Only weeks earlier, the death of George Floyd at the hands of Minneapolis police officer Derek Chauvin had reignited national debate about systemic racism in law enforcement, producing one of the largest protest movements in US history. Corporations across sectors, including entertainment, were making public commitments regarding racial equity and representation. In that context, news about any major media company partnering with one of America's most high-profile racial justice activists was guaranteed to generate significant attention — and significant distortion.

Kaepernick was already a deeply polarising figure. Since 2016, when he began kneeling during the national anthem before NFL games as a protest against police brutality and systemic racism directed at Black Americans, Kaepernick had become a focal point of culture war arguments about patriotism, free speech, professional sports, and corporate America. Nike's 2018 endorsement deal with Kaepernick — announcing him as the face of a "Just Do It" campaign — had produced boycotts, social media firestorms, and parallel defences from free speech advocates and racial justice activists. Any subsequent major corporate involvement with Kaepernick would inevitably be processed through these pre-existing frames.

The Snopes fact-check by journalist Bethania Palma, published July 15, 2020, rated the meme's core claim as "Mixture" — acknowledging the real partnership while rejecting the specific characterisations that made the meme alarming to its intended audience.

## What's Actually True

On July 6, 2020, The Walt Disney Company and ESPN Films jointly announced a "first-look deal" with Colin Kaepernick for a multi-project documentary and scripted series development partnership. The announcement, made through an official ESPN press room press release, described the nature of the deal clearly: the first project in development was an exclusive docuseries chronicling Kaepernick's own journey, "using extensive new interviews and a vast never-before-seen archive that documents his last five years." Kaepernick would tell his story from his own perspective.

The broader partnership, per the press release, would "focus on telling scripted and unscripted stories that explore race, social injustice and the quest for equity, and will provide a new platform to showcase the work of Black and Brown directors and producers."

Bob Iger, Disney's executive chairman, was quoted in the press release: "Colin's experience gives him a unique perspective on the intersection of sports, culture and race, which will undoubtedly create compelling stories that will educate, enlighten and entertain, and we look forward to working with him on this important collaboration."

Industry trade publication Variety confirmed the deal on July 6, 2020, in a piece by Brian Steinberg.

So: the factual foundation of a Disney/Kaepernick partnership existed. The meme drew on a real event. What the meme got wrong — and wrong in ways that were not trivially incidental — was the characterisation: the use of "hired," the targeting of children, and the specific framing as history instruction.

**On "hired":** A "first-look deal" in the entertainment industry is a specific contractual arrangement whereby a production company or streamer has a right of first refusal on a creator's projects during the deal period. It is a common mechanism for securing access to talented or high-profile creators before competitors can sign them. Kaepernick was not hired as an employee of Disney. He was entering a production and development partnership. The distinction is substantively important: an "employee hired to teach" carries very different institutional authority and scope than "a creator with a first-look development deal for his own documentary projects."

**On "teach your kids":** Nothing in the Disney/ESPN press release, nothing in any reporting on the deal, and nothing in any subsequent project announcement stated or implied that the content developed under this partnership was intended for children. Disney operates multiple content divisions — Disney+, ESPN, ABC, Hulu, FX — serving enormously diverse audiences. A documentary about NFL racial justice activism produced through ESPN Films would self-evidently be aimed at a general or adult sports and culture audience, not children. The word "kids" appears nowhere in the primary documentation. It was inserted by the meme to invoke parental concern — a powerful but unsupported rhetorical escalation.

**On "American history":** The partnership deals with contemporary events — Kaepernick's own five-year experience in the NFL controversy and its racial justice context. This is not instruction in the historical sweep of American history. It is documentary storytelling about an ongoing contemporary dispute. The conflation with "American history education" suggests history class pedagogy to an audience primed to reject Kaepernick's particular perspective — a framing the underlying facts do not support.

## Epistemic Novelty

The Kaepernick-Disney meme is a specimen of what communications scholars call *frame amplification* operating through *motivated audience selection*. Understanding its mechanics illuminates a broader class of viral misinformation.

The meme's core strategy was to take a genuine, verifiable announcement and reframe it through an alternative semantic container. In frame analysis — developed in social science by Erving Goffman (1974, *Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience*, Harvard University Press) and subsequently applied extensively to media and political communication — a "frame" is the organising principle that gives events meaning, determining what aspects are made salient, what causal relationships are implied, and what evaluative conclusions are suggested. The same set of underlying facts can be accurately rendered through frames that produce radically different audience responses.

The actual frame: "ESPN Films, a sports media division, enters a development deal with a prominent athlete-activist to produce a documentary series about his experience and contemporary racial justice issues."

The meme's frame: "Disney is using your children's entertainment company to indoctrinate your kids with a particular activist's view of American history."

Both frames refer to the same factual substrate — the Disney/Kaepernick partnership announcement — but they activate entirely different evaluative responses. The second frame was illegitimate because it introduced false elements (children as audience, history instruction as content, employment as contractual relationship) while preserving the factual core (a real Disney partnership with Kaepernick) as its credibility foundation. This is a common architecture in meme misinformation: a real event serves as the "true" load-bearing element that makes the false additions seem plausible by association.

The "kids" addition deserves particular attention as an example of what scholars of moral foundations theory call an appeal to the *harm/care* foundation (Haidt, J., 2012, *The Righteous Mind: Why Good People Are Divided by Politics and Religion*, Pantheon Books). Research by Haidt, Graham, and colleagues has documented that conservatives tend to weight the purity, loyalty, and authority foundations more heavily than liberals, while liberals tend to weight harm/care and fairness more heavily. Framing the Disney-Kaepernick deal as a threat to children's minds activates both harm/care (threat to children) and authority (challenge to what institutions should do) — triggering a maximal response in the audience most likely to share the meme. The false "your kids" addition was not rhetorical accident; it was functionally optimised for emotional resonance with a specific audience.

The episode also illustrates how production deals — a routine contractual instrument in the entertainment industry — are poorly understood by general audiences. Most people have no model for what a "first-look deal" is, what it entails, what it means for the content, or how it differs from employment. This knowledge gap provides space for arbitrary framing, because without background understanding, audiences must rely entirely on the frame offered to them.

## The Evidence Base

**ESPN Press Room Press Release, July 6, 2020.** The primary documentary source: the official announcement of the first-look deal between The Walt Disney Company and Colin Kaepernick. It establishes the terms of the agreement as a development deal, describes the first project as a docuseries about Kaepernick's experience, and specifies the broader thematic scope (race, social injustice, equity, showcasing Black and Brown creators).

**Steinberg, B., Variety, "Colin Kaepernick Signs First-Look Deal With Walt Disney," July 6, 2020.** Independent trade press confirmation of the deal's existence and terms, providing industry-context framing on first-look arrangements.

**Goffman, E. (1974). *Frame Analysis: An Essay on the Organization of Experience*. Harvard University Press.** The foundational theoretical text for understanding how interpretive frames structure meaning-making. Goffman's concept of primary frameworks and their transformation through keying and fabrication provides the analytical vocabulary for how the meme restructured the Disney announcement.

**Haidt, J. (2012). *The Righteous Mind: Why Good People Are Divided by Politics and Religion*. Pantheon Books.** Haidt's synthesis of decades of moral psychology research documents the differential weighting of moral foundations across political groups. The "harm to children" framing in the meme directly targets foundations weighted heavily by politically conservative audiences.

**Palma, B., Snopes.com, "Did Disney 'Hire' Colin Kaepernick To 'Teach Your Kids About American History'?", July 15, 2020.** The fact-check establishing the original claim review and Mixture rating.

**Johnson, M., The Undefeated, "Colin Kaepernick Tried to Tell White America," May 30, 2020.** Provided context on the cultural reception of Kaepernick's 2016 protest, documenting the ongoing polarisation around his public role that made him a perpetual culture-war touchpoint.

**BBC, "Trump: NFL Kneelers 'Maybe Shouldn't Be in Country'," May 24, 2018.** Documents White House characterisation of kneeling protests, relevant to understanding the pre-existing political framework within which the Disney deal was received.

**Adler, D., Vanity Fair, "Our Never-Ending Culture Wars: Colin Kaepernick and Nike's Betsy Ross Air Maxes Edition," July 2, 2019.** Chronicles the ongoing culture war cycle around Kaepernick as a recurring flashpoint, providing context for why the Disney deal was immediately processed through protest/symbol frames.

## Why People Believe This

The Kaepernick-Disney meme succeeded because it exploited an architecture of plausible inference from real facts toward false conclusions. The meme worked on multiple concurrent channels.

**Channel 1: Real foundation.** The Disney deal was real. Anyone who looked it up found a genuine press release. This reality gave the meme a core of verified truth that made the embellishing additions more credible by proximity. Memes that are entirely fabricated are more easily rejected than memes that embed false additions in real events.

**Channel 2: Character priming.** Kaepernick is a figure about whom most conservative-leaning social media users already had formed strong negative views. Research on motivated reasoning — including Kahan et al. (2017, "Motivated Numeracy and Enlightened Self-Government," *Behavioural Public Policy*, Vol. 1, No. 1) — documents that in politically charged domains, prior attitudes dramatically shape how new information is processed, with information consistent with prior views more easily believed and better remembered. For an audience that associated Kaepernick negatively with NFL controversy, any claim about institutional empowerment of Kaepernick was pre-weighted as credible.

**Channel 3: Disney as trusted children's brand.** Disney's cultural identity as a company associated with childhood entertainment — theme parks, animated films, princesses, Star Wars merchandise — made the "teaching your kids" claim emotionally resonant in a way that, say, "ESPN Films" alone would not. Disney's non-children's programming (including ABC News, ESPN, Hulu, FX) is substantial but less salient in popular mental models. The meme exploited the gap between Disney's corporate reality and its public brand image.

**Channel 4: History education anxiety.** The summer of 2020 was a period of intense national debate about how American history is taught — particularly regarding slavery, race, and historical injustice. The 1619 Project, published by The New York Times Magazine in August 2019, had generated substantial political controversy by centering the history of slavery in a broader account of American origins. By mid-2020, debates about curricula, monument removals, and the appropriate scope of history education were near peak cultural salience. The meme's "teach your kids about American history" framing landed in a live wound — activating the existing anxiety about who gets to define historical narrative for the next generation.

These four channels operated in reinforcing combination: a real event, a pre-primed negative subject, a trusted-brand exploit, and a live cultural anxiety. The mixture was optimised, whether by design or by the evolutionary dynamics of social media sharing, for maximal propagation within a specific audience.

## The Verdict

The claim that Disney "hired" Colin Kaepernick to "teach your kids about American history" is a **mixture**: one element is true, two critical elements are false, and the overall framing is misleading in ways that materially distort the meaning of the real event.

**True:** Colin Kaepernick signed a first-look development deal with The Walt Disney Company and ESPN Films in July 2020 for a documentary series about race, social injustice, and his own experience.

**False:** Kaepernick was not "hired" in any conventional employment sense — he signed a production development partnership. The content was not directed at children — it was a documentary for general audiences developed through ESPN Films, a sports-programming division. And the project was not designed to teach history in any curricular sense — it was documentary storytelling about contemporary events.

The meme belongs to a well-documented category of political misinformation that derives plausibility from a real core while constructing false contextual additions calibrated for emotional resonance with a target audience. The "your kids" element was not incidental flavour; it was the load-bearing motivational hook designed to transform a documentary production deal into a perceived threat requiring viral transmission. Claims of this type — real event, false frame, emotionally loaded additions — are among the most difficult to counter because debunking requires nuance while the original claim is simple and viscerally compelling.
