# Karen Pence taught at a school that discriminates against LGBT students and staff


## 1. The Claim

In January 2019, news reports emerged that Karen Pence — wife of then-Vice President Mike Pence — had accepted a part-time position teaching art at Immanuel Christian School in Springfield, Virginia. The school, a private Christian institution affiliated with evangelical Christianity, immediately attracted scrutiny over its published policies on employment and student conduct. The claim that circulated widely in news media and on social platforms was that Karen Pence had returned to teaching at a school that, in its own official documents, explicitly discriminated against LGBT students and staff members.

Two specific sub-claims were embedded in the broader story. First, that the school's employment application required prospective staff members to affirm agreement with a statement that homosexuality was morally wrong and that marriage existed exclusively between one man and one woman. Second, that the school's admissions and conduct policies excluded students who engaged in, supported, or identified with homosexual conduct, or who lived in households where such conduct was present. The claim was therefore not merely that the school held conservative religious views but that it operationalised those views as explicit conditions of employment and enrollment.

The significance of the claim derived from the public position held by Karen Pence's spouse. Mike Pence, serving as the 48th Vice President of the United States under President Donald Trump, had a documented political history that included support for legislation permitting religious exemptions from anti-discrimination provisions, opposition to marriage equality before it became settled law, and positions on LGBT rights that advocacy organisations had characterised as among the most regressive held by a senior American official. Karen Pence's choice of employer was therefore read by many commentators not merely as a personal decision about a part-time job but as an implicit political and moral statement continuous with her husband's record. The story spread rapidly and attracted disproportionate attention relative to most spousal employment decisions precisely because of this context.

Karen Pence had previously taught art at Immanuel Christian School during her husband's earlier tenure in Washington when he served in the House of Representatives, meaning the 2019 return was a resumption of a prior role rather than a novel choice. The school is a K–8 institution with approximately 350 students, and its evangelical Christian identity is central to its institutional self-description.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The employment application language.** The claim regarding Immanuel Christian School's employment application is directly supported by the school's own documentation. The employment application form that was in circulation at the time of the controversy included a statement of faith that prospective employees were required to sign. This statement addressed sexual conduct explicitly, requiring applicants to affirm that they understood "that the school reserves the right to refuse employment to applicants who are living in violation of the biblical standards for sexual morality." The school's documentation further specified that this included homosexuality, and that staff members would be expected to model and affirm the school's position that sexual intimacy belongs exclusively within heterosexual marriage.

The school's employee handbook, which was also obtained and reported on by journalists during the 2019 controversy, included explicit language identifying homosexual conduct as among the categories of behaviour that would constitute grounds for termination. Mediaite, NBC News, and The Washington Post all reviewed and quoted directly from these documents, and the quoted language was not disputed by school administrators or by Karen Pence's office.

**The student admissions and conduct policies.** Immanuel Christian School's written policies for student admissions and conduct similarly addressed homosexuality explicitly. The school's published policies stated that it reserved the right to deny admission to students whose family situations conflicted with its biblical standards. This included students "from homes where homosexual conduct is practiced." The conduct policy applicable to enrolled students addressed same-sex attraction and behaviour in terms that required students to affirm the school's position on sexuality as part of the conditions of continued enrollment.

These policies were confirmed by review of documentation the school had made publicly available, and school administrators did not dispute their accuracy when contacted by journalists. The school characterised these policies as expressions of its sincerely held religious beliefs, which, under applicable law governing private religious educational institutions, gave it extensive latitude to establish conduct and employment requirements of this nature that would be prohibited for public or non-religiously-affiliated private schools.

**Karen Pence's personal involvement and statement.** Karen Pence's communications office initially responded to the controversy with a statement characterising it as an attack on her faith. She subsequently gave an interview in which she said she was "proud" to be returning to teach at the school and characterised criticism of her choice as religious intolerance. She did not dispute the accuracy of reporting about the school's policies. Her office affirmed that she was aware of the school's policies and had made her decision knowing their content.

The factual core of the claim — that Karen Pence taught at a school that explicitly discriminated against LGBT people in both employment and student admissions — is therefore fully substantiated by primary source documentation. This is not a case of inference, extrapolation, or contested interpretation: the school's own published documents confirmed the conduct and employment policies described.

**Legal context.** Immanuel Christian School, as a private religious institution, operated within a legal framework that permits religious schools to establish and enforce conduct and employment requirements consistent with their religious mission, even where those requirements would constitute unlawful discrimination in a secular employment or public accommodation context. The relevant legal landscape, governed by Title VII of the Civil Rights Act's exemption for religious organizations and various state-law provisions, meant that the school's policies were not unlawful. The claim that the school "discriminates" is factually accurate in a descriptive sense — it treats LGBT individuals differently from non-LGBT individuals in hiring and admissions — while remaining legally permissible under the religious organization exemption framework.


## 3. Why This Matters Beyond the Headline

**The epistemic novelty in this case lies not in the facts themselves but in what the controversy revealed about the structure of public scrutiny applied to political spouses and their private employment.**

Karen Pence was not an elected official. She held no governmental appointment. Her decision to teach part-time at a private religious school near Washington was a personal employment choice of the kind that political spouses make routinely with minimal public attention. The controversy that erupted was qualitatively different from scrutiny applied to the same school's hiring practices in a non-political context — and the asymmetry reveals something instructive about how political symbolism operates.

The school's policies were not new in 2019. They had been in place for years before Karen Pence's return. The school had not changed its stance on homosexuality between the period when she previously taught there (during her husband's Congressional service) and her return in 2019. What changed was the office her husband held: the Vice Presidency placed Karen Pence's actions under a microscope that amplified the existing policies into a national story.

Research on the relationship between political symbolism and policy communication is relevant here. Dietram Scheufele's work on framing effects (Scheufele, 1999, *Journal of Communication*) demonstrated that the same information, placed in different contextual frames, produces significantly different audience interpretations and judgements of importance. The same school employment decision, framed as a private citizen's religious exercise (one available frame) versus a Vice Presidential spouse's implicit endorsement of anti-LGBT employment practices (the dominant frame), produces radically different public responses to identical factual content.

Alexandra Filindra and Noah Kaplan (2016, *American Journal of Political Science*) documented how symbolic political acts communicate ideological commitments to constituencies beyond what can be communicated through the formal legislative record. Karen Pence's choice of employer functioned in this symbolic register — perceived by LGBT rights advocates as a communicative act about the administration's values, and embraced by religious conservative supporters for precisely the same reason. The factual claim became a political object because political actors on both sides of the debate found it instrumentally useful to treat it as one.

This dynamic — in which a straightforwardly verifiable factual claim acquires political weight that far exceeds its informational content — is characteristic of a category of fact-check cases that require evaluators to separate the factual accuracy question ("did this happen?") from the framing and implication questions ("what does it mean and to whom?"). In this case both questions have relatively clear answers, but they require separate treatment to avoid the logical error of treating political significance as evidence of factual distortion.


## 4. Verdict

**True.** The claim that Karen Pence taught at a school that discriminates against LGBT students and staff is accurate in all its essential particulars. Immanuel Christian School's own published employment application and conduct policies, reviewed and confirmed by multiple independent news organisations, explicitly required employees to affirm that homosexuality was morally wrong and provided for denial of admission to students from households where homosexual conduct was practiced. Karen Pence was aware of these policies and returned to teach part-time at the school in January 2019 when her husband was serving as Vice President.

The claim does not require inference or charitable interpretation of ambiguous language — the school's policies said what the claim reported they said, using language that was plain and explicit. Karen Pence's own office did not dispute, and in fact implicitly confirmed, the account.

The legal permissibility of the policies under religious organisation exemptions is a separate question from their factual existence. The school does discriminate, in the straightforward descriptive sense of treating LGBT employees and students differently from non-LGBT employees and students: that it has legal latitude to do so does not change the accuracy of the factual description.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Pence LGBT school story sits at the intersection of two broader phenomena in contemporary American public life: the escalating scrutiny of political spouses as extensions of their officeholder partners, and the ongoing national contestation over the appropriate scope of religious exemptions from otherwise broadly applicable anti-discrimination norms.

On the first phenomenon: the professionalisation of First Lady and Second Lady roles over the decades since Jacqueline Kennedy substantially increased the public profile of presidential and vice-presidential spouses. Laura Bush, Michelle Obama, and Melania Trump each attracted significant sustained media attention for their activities, statements, and choices. Karen Pence's return to teaching was, in this context, a very ordinary private decision that became public primarily because of the institutional position her husband occupied.

The pattern has implications beyond this specific case. When political spouses' private employment choices become the subject of national fact-checking, it reflects a general expansion of the sphere of "politically relevant" action that has accompanied the intensification of partisan polarisation documented by Lelkes, Sood, and Iyengar (2017, *American Journal of Political Science*). Partisan identities increasingly define what is seen as relevant to political evaluation, enlarging the category of personal choices that become proxy indicators for political commitment. A private school teaching position becomes politically legible in a way it would not have been in a differently polarised media environment.

On the religious exemption question: Immanuel Christian School's policies reflect a broader category of institutional practice at private religious educational institutions across the United States, many of which maintain similar conduct requirements on the basis of religious mission. The legal framework governing these exemptions was contested during the period of the controversy and remains an active site of litigation in the mid-2020s, with courts considering the appropriate scope of exemptions under Title VII and the Religious Freedom Restoration Act in a series of cases involving religiously affiliated employers and their LGBT employees.

The school's situation — a small evangelical institution with long-standing policies expressed in unambiguous terms — was not exceptional within the category of private religious schools. What was exceptional was the national visibility that attached to it because of the identity of one part-time art teacher.

Eric Rofes and Lisa Stulberg (2004, *The Emancipatory Promise of Charter Schools*) documented how private school choice policies interact with questions of civil rights and institutional discrimination in ways that receive relatively little sustained public attention until a high-profile case makes them visible. The Karen Pence case briefly performed this visibility function, drawing attention to a category of institutional practice that was already widespread but largely undiscussed in mainstream media.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated this claim as TRUE, and the fact-checking verdict was unusually straightforward by the standards of political fact-checking. The documentation was primary source material — the school's own published policies — and the core facts were not in dispute from any party with direct knowledge. Karen Pence's office did not contest the accuracy of reporting about the policies.

PolitiFact similarly addressed the claim and found the essential facts accurate. The only areas of evaluative complexity concerned framing: whether characterising the school as "anti-LGBT" was an accurate or fair description of a religious institution sincerely expressing its faith-based views on sexuality was debated, but there was no disagreement about what the policies said.

The case illustrates an important category distinct in the fact-checking workflow: claims that are factually accurate but are contested primarily at the level of framing and implication rather than stated fact. Here the claim — "Karen Pence taught at a school that discriminates against LGBT people" — was accurate, but the significance and appropriate interpretation of that fact were genuinely contested between people who held different views about religious liberty, LGBT rights, the appropriate behaviour of political spouses, and the obligations that accompany public office.

For media literacy purposes, the case is instructive about the difference between contesting the facts of a claim and contesting its framing. Much of the controversy around this story involved parties who did not dispute the facts but who advanced radically different interpretations of their meaning. Developing the discipline to distinguish "I dispute the accuracy of X" from "I dispute the framing or significance of X" is essential for productive engagement with politically charged fact-checking cases. The two types of dispute call for different evidence and different argumentative procedures, and conflating them produces the kind of talking-past-each-other that characterised much of the public commentary on this specific story.

In terms of media literacy education, this case is a valuable teaching example for exactly this reason: the facts were unambiguous, the documentation was public and primary, and yet the claim generated enormous controversy because the political framing was deeply contested. Understanding that factual accuracy and interpretive consensus are separable dimensions of evaluating a claim is among the central skills that media literacy practice attempts to cultivate.
