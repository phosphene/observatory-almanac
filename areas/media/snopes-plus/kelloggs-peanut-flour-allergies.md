# "Kellogg's to Add Peanut Flour to Products"

## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: Kellogg's is covertly adding traces of peanut flour to all of its products — including products never previously containing peanut ingredients — as a deliberate strategy to avoid compliance with the Food Safety Modernization Act (FSMA), at the direct expense of consumers with peanut allergies.

**Proximate origin**: On 5 May 2016, the website Snack Safely — which covers food allergy topics for the allergy community — published an article entitled "Kellogg's Now Adding Traces of Peanut Flour Across Product Lines." The piece argued that following Kellogg's addition of peanut flour to its Austin and Keebler sandwich cracker lines beginning in April 2016, the company was now extending this practice across additional products. As evidence, Snack Safely cited Kellogg's Nutri-Grain "Fruit Crunch" bars, which they noted now contained peanut flour. The article did not include any verified Kellogg's statement confirming that peanut flour was being added to new products; it characterized the pattern as a "surmise" about an ongoing plan.

**The FSMA circumvention theory**: The Snack Safely article offered a specific explanatory theory: Kellogg's was intentionally adding small amounts of peanut flour to otherwise non-peanut products specifically to list peanut ingredients on the label, which would allow the company to avoid the costs of complying with FSMA allergen-control requirements scheduled to go into effect in September 2016. The theory held that if peanut is already declared as an ingredient, separate FSMA allergen cross-contamination prevention obligations would not apply, allowing Kellogg's to avoid both the capital cost of allergen-segregated production and the compliance burden of FSMA. The article was accompanied by a petition that accumulated substantial signatures from the food allergy community.

**Secondary circulation**: The Snack Safely article and its associated petition were shared widely within food allergy advocacy communities, where peanut allergy — the most common cause of fatal anaphylaxis in the United States — is a high-stakes ongoing concern. Parents of children with peanut allergies, who regularly audit ingredient labels and maintain product safety lists, encountered the claim in a context of genuine alertness. The implied message — "Kellogg's is secretly adding peanuts to products you thought were safe" — triggered precisely the category of alarm that is most acute for the roughly 1–2% of the U.S. population with peanut allergy (Sicherer, S. H., et al., 2010, *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*).

**Why this claim is compelling in its specific community**: For food allergy families, the stakes of ingredient changes are not abstract. A child with peanut anaphylaxis who consumes a formerly-safe product that now contains peanut flour faces a life-threatening emergency. Within this community, vigilance about hidden or undisclosed ingredient changes is a survival-relevant behavior, not merely consumer preference. A claim that a major food company is adding peanut ingredients covertly activates well-justified vigilance in a community for whom supplier and manufacturer transparency is literally vital.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The real product changes at Kellogg's**: It is true that Kellogg's added peanut flour to some of its products beginning in April 2016. Specifically, Kellogg's publicly announced that four flavors of Austin brand sandwich crackers — Austin Cheddar Cheese Cracker Sandwiches, Austin Grilled Cheese Cracker Sandwiches, Austin Pepper Jack Cracker Sandwiches, and the Austin Variety Pack Cracker Sandwiches — would contain "Wheat, Milk, Soy, and Peanut ingredients" beginning in April 2016. A parallel announcement was made for affected Keebler sandwich cracker products. Both announcements were made publicly through Kellogg's online community forum.

**The announcements were public**: The Snack Safely article characterized Kellogg's as failing to announce the changes. This was misleading. Kellogg's posted statements on its online community regarding both Austin and Keebler product line changes in April 2016, well before the May 2016 Snack Safely piece. These statements explicitly named the products affected and specified the allergen changes. Kellogg's did not issue a formal press release, which was a legitimate critique — the food allergy community reasonably could argue that community forum posts are insufficient notification of changes with anaphylaxis risk. But "no press release" is meaningfully different from "no announcement" — the latter was false.

**The Nutri-Grain Fruit Crunch misidentification**: Snack Safely used Kellogg's Nutri-Grain "Fruit Crunch" bars as evidence that peanut flour was being added to new products beyond the cracker lines. Kellogg's confirmed and Snopes verified that the Fruit Crunch bars had already contained peanut flour as an ingredient since their introduction in 2012. More significantly, the Fruit Crunch bars had been discontinued and were no longer available in the market at the time the Snack Safely article was published. The use of a discontinued product that had always contained peanut flour as evidence of a new expansion of peanut flour use was an error that substantially undermined the article's factual basis.

**The FSMA circumvention theory lacked evidentiary support**: Snack Safely was explicit that the FSMA hypothesis was a "surmise" — an inference rather than a confirmed fact. No Kellogg's internal communications, regulatory filings, or other documentation supported the theory that peanut flour was being added specifically to avoid FSMA compliance. Kellogg's had a documented long history of making ingredient changes across its product portfolio for straightforward formulation reasons, and an Austin Grilled Cheese variant without peanut flour was explicitly announced for September 2016 release — a consumer-protective action directly inconsistent with the theory of using peanut flour addition to avoid allergy management obligations.

**The FSMA context**: The Food Safety Modernization Act (FSMA), signed into law in January 2011, represented the most comprehensive U.S. food safety legislation since the Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 (FDA, 2011). FSMA's Preventive Controls for Human Food rule, which was the rule with the September 2016 implementation date for larger manufacturers, required food facilities to implement preventive control measures for hazards that are "reasonably foreseeable," including allergen cross-contact. The Snack Safely hypothesis held that by declaring peanut as an ingredient, Kellogg's could reclassify what would have been "cross-contact" as intentional use, reducing compliance obligations. However, this theory was not confirmed by FDA officials or food safety lawyers, and Kellogg's stated it had no validity.

**The subsequent retraction**: On 11 May 2016 — six days after the original article — Snack Safely published a retraction stating that "Yesterday, we posted an article entitled 'Kellogg's Now Adding Traces of Peanut Flour Across Product Lines' that asserted that Kellogg's was adding peanut flour across their product lines," and acknowledging the error regarding the Nutri-Grain bars. The retraction was issued reasonably promptly after the errors were identified. However, the original article had accumulated significant shares and the petition had gathered substantial signatures before the retraction was issued. Retractions consistently fail to circulate as widely as the original debunked content (Lewandowsky et al., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*).

**Snopes verdict**: **Mixture**. It is true that Kellogg's added peanut flour to some of its products (Austin and Keebler crackers) in April 2016. It is false that this was done secretly; Kellogg's made public announcements. The scope-broadening evidence used by Snack Safely (Nutri-Grain bars) was factually wrong — those bars were discontinued and had always contained peanut flour. The FSMA circumvention theory was speculative and unsubstantiated.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — How Legitimate Advocacy Communities Amplify Misinformation

The Kellogg's peanut flour case presents an epistemically distinctive dynamic: a misinformation event generated not by a disreputable bad-faith publisher but by a specialized advocacy outlet with genuine domain expertise and community trust, operating under real community stakes, making errors of scope and inference that then travel through high-trust networks.

Snack Safely is not Before It's News. It is a legitimate resource for the food allergy community with substantial genuine value: the site tracks ingredient changes, compiles product safety databases, and alerts its readers to exactly the kind of manufacturer changes that the peanut allergy community needs to track. The site's credibility within its audience is earned and largely deserved. This creates an information hazard that is substantively different from the hazard posed by low-credibility viral click-bait sites.

The concept of **trust-amplified misinformation** describes the phenomenon in which information transmitted through high-trust channels spreads faster, receives less scrutiny, and corrects more slowly than the same information transmitted through low-trust channels. A claim from Before It's News is met with automatic skepticism by educated consumers who recognize the outlet. A claim from a respected outlet within a specialized community travels differently: it is pre-validated by community trust before any individual reader performs verification. Within the food allergy community, Snack Safely's word was — for much of its audience — more credible than Kellogg's own statements, because the community's prior included well-founded skepticism of manufacturer self-reporting.

The tragedy of trust-amplified misinformation is that the damage it does falls disproportionately on the most motivated and engaged members of a community. Casual readers encounter the Kellogg's story alongside many other pieces of content and may remember it only vaguely. Food allergy parents who actively follow Snack Safely as a practical safety resource updated their mental product safety lists based on the article. The retraction reached a smaller audience than the original claim, as is typical. The behavioral consequence — parents removing Kellogg's products from their "safe" lists, potentially unnecessarily — was concentrated in exactly the community the article most intended to serve.

There is also a structural accountability gap in specialized advocacy media. General journalism has conventions of verification and retraction that are imperfect but codified: editors review significant claims before publication, corrections are published prominently, and there are competitive incentives for rival outlets to identify errors. Specialized advocacy media operates with smaller staff, higher audience trust that can substitute for verification, and stronger prior agreement between writer and reader on the shape of the expected story ("corporation does something bad that harms our community") that can reduce skeptical scrutiny before publication. These are not criticisms of advocacy media as a category — they are features of its operating model that create specific vulnerability patterns.

The Snack Safely retraction is actually noteworthy as an example of the corrective mechanism functioning: the errors were identified, acknowledged promptly, and the retraction was issued publicly. But the long tail of the original misinformation — petition signatures collected before the retraction, social media shares that did not include the correction page — persisted beyond the correction event. Understanding this persistence is important for understanding why even prompt corrections do not fully neutralize misinformation effects (Ecker et al., 2011, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*).


## 4. The Real Complexity — Peanut Allergy, Allergen Labeling, and FSMA

The legitimate concerns that motivated the Snack Safely piece deserve careful attention even as its specific errors are corrected. The peanut allergy regulatory environment in 2016 was genuinely in transition, and the concerns of the food allergy community about manufacturer notification practices were substantively reasonable.

**Peanut allergy epidemiology**: Peanut allergy affects approximately 1.4–2.0% of the U.S. population (Sicherer, S. H., & Sampson, H. A., 2014, *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*). It is the most common cause of fatal food-induced anaphylaxis. Unlike some food allergies, peanut allergy typically does not resolve with age and is often severe, with reactions triggered by very small exposures — as low as hundredths of a milligram of peanut protein in highly sensitized individuals (Hourihane, J. O. B., et al., 1997, *British Medical Journal*). The stakes of product formulation changes are consequently asymmetric: a mislabeled or inadequately communicated ingredient change involving peanut can cause death.

**The FALCPA labeling requirement**: The Food Allergen Labeling and Consumer Protection Act of 2004 (FALCPA) requires that the eight major allergens (including peanuts) be declared on food labels in plain language. FALCPA's requirements apply to ingredients and to any protein derived from a major allergen added intentionally. If Kellogg's added peanut flour to a product under FALCPA, it was required to declare it on the label — which the Austin and Keebler announcements confirmed was the plan. From a regulatory labeling standpoint, the changes were compliant.

**The adequacy of notification**: The legitimate concern the food allergy community raised — and which the Snack Safely article captured imprecisely — was not primarily whether Kellogg's eventually complied with labeling requirements, but whether the company did enough to proactively notify the community of affected products before those products reached store shelves. FDA regulations do not require manufacturers to provide advance notice to allergen advocacy groups; the legal obligation is label compliance, not pre-market community notification. But the food allergy community has reasonable expectations that go beyond the regulatory minimum, and the practice of posting to a community forum rather than issuing a company-wide press release was legitimately subject to criticism as inadequate outreach for a change with anaphylaxis implications.

**The FSMA preventive controls**: Under the FSMA Preventive Controls for Human Food rule, facilities must conduct a hazard analysis and implement risk-based preventive controls for hazards that are "reasonably foreseeable." Allergen cross-contact (the inadvertent introduction of allergen protein into a product not intended to contain it) is one such hazard. The Snack Safely theory — that Kellogg's deliberately added peanut to avoid FSMA cross-contact controls — assumed that intentional peanut use would exempt the company from FSMA allergen control obligations for other products. FDA's actual interpretation was that FSMA allergen controls apply to all products with allergen hazards, whether from cross-contact or intentional use; intentionally adding peanut to some products does not reduce the preventive control obligation for products intended to be peanut-free. The theory was not consistent with FDA regulatory interpretation.

**Austin cracker without peanuts**: The announced September 2016 introduction of an Austin Grilled Cheese Sandwich Cracker variety specifically formulated without peanut is perhaps the clearest evidence against the FSMA circumvention theory. A company attempting to avoid allergen segregation costs by uniformly adding peanut to its product portfolio would not simultaneously be investing in a peanut-free alternative product line. The two actions are in direct tension; the peanut-free product introduction is most consistent with a company trying to serve consumers with varied allergen needs, not one systematically allergizing its portfolio.


## 5. Policy Implications and Consumer Practice

**What this case reveals about allergen change notification**: The Kellogg's episode highlighted a gap between legal compliance minimums and the expectations of the food allergy community regarding proactive notification. Food companies are legally required to label products accurately but are not required to proactively market the change to allergen communities. The allergy advocacy community has historically pushed for higher voluntary standards — advance notification to registered community members, direct communication with allergy advocacy organizations, and clear shelf placement during transition periods when old and new formulations may coexist.

**The allergy community's own information infrastructure**: The food allergy community has developed substantial information-sharing infrastructure to address this notification gap: platforms like Snack Safely, FARE (Food Allergy Research & Education), and various online communities serve as distributed monitoring systems that collectively identify label changes faster than any single family's personal monitoring could. This infrastructure is genuinely valuable. The Kellogg's case illustrates both its power (rapid community-wide awareness of a real change) and its vulnerability (rapid community-wide spread of supplementary claims that were not accurate).

**Practical guidance for allergy community members**: The event reinforced a practice the allergy community generally follows: checking the ingredient label of every product at every purchase, regardless of prior purchase history. This remains the most reliable protection against undisclosed or newly added allergens, since it is independent of any secondary information intermediary and relies on legally mandated label accuracy. Secondary information sources — advocacy sites, community forums, parent groups — are valuable supplements but should be cross-checked against the current product label.

**FARE's role as a more institutionally accountable information source**: The Food Allergy Research & Education organization maintains direct relationships with major food manufacturers and has established channels for receiving advance notification of formulation changes that affect major allergens. For allergy families, FARE's communications represent a higher-institutional-accountability source for allergen change information, complementing the faster but less vetted peer community channels.


## 6. Verdict and Takeaway

The claim is a **Mixture**. Kellogg's did add peanut flour to some products in April 2016 — the Austin and Keebler cracker changes were real and were a legitimate subject of concern for the peanut allergy community. The company did post public announcements, albeit through a community forum rather than a press release, contradicting the "secretive" framing. The scope-broadening evidence (Nutri-Grain Fruit Crunch bars) was factually wrong. The FSMA circumvention theory was unsupported speculation, refuted by the simultaneous development of a peanut-free Austin product variant. Snack Safely issued a retraction promptly after errors were identified.

The deeper story is about the structure of information in specialized advocacy communities, where high trust and high stakes create conditions that simultaneously make accurate information very impactful and misinformation unusually potent. The legitimate core concern — that large food companies do not always provide optimal advance notification of allergen changes to the communities most at risk — was real and remains worthy of ongoing advocacy. The specific claim as amplified was not accurate. Holding both of these truths simultaneously is the appropriate epistemic response.
