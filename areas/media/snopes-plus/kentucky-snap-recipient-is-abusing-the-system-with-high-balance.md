## §1 — Claim & Verdict Summary

In August 2016, a cluster of viral websites began circulating a story originating from Liberty, Kentucky, in which an unnamed shopper photographed a receipt dropped by another customer at a Save-A-Lot grocery store. The receipt appeared to show the customer had an EBT (Electronic Benefits Transfer) balance of $5,583.42 after spending approximately $40. The story was amplified as evidence of blatant abuse of the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), sometimes called "food stamps." Commenters and headline writers framed the high balance as proof that SNAP recipients were receiving far more taxpayer money than they needed and were not being held accountable.

Snopes rated the claim **Unproven** for two interrelated reasons: (1) the authenticity of the photograph and receipt has never been independently verified, making it impossible to confirm the balance actually existed; and (2) even if the balance were genuine, a high EBT balance is not inherently evidence of fraud or abuse under existing federal law. The verdict acknowledges a factual kernel — high EBT balances do occasionally exist — while refusing to validate the moral-legal conclusion that the viral narrative built upon it.

The claim exemplifies a recurring structure in welfare-skeptic viral content: a photograph of ambiguous origin is used to anchor an emotionally charged story that slides from description ("this person has a high balance") to moral condemnation ("they are abusing the system") without supplying any of the intermediate evidence that would make the second claim defensible.

## §2 — Evidential Foundation

The USDA's Food and Nutrition Service (FNS), the federal agency that oversees SNAP, confirmed to Snopes that high EBT balances are statistically rare but not structurally impossible. As of June 2015, FNS had identified 1,783 households nationwide with balances of $2,500 or higher, out of 22,436,441 participating households — representing approximately 0.008 percent of all active accounts (USDA FNS, 2015, internal data provided to Snopes). This figure is small in relative terms but meaningful: it establishes that high balances exist, that federal administrators are aware of them, and that the agency has reviewed whether they indicate fraud.

The FNS finding on high-balance accounts is critical: a government review determined that "few of these households exhibit behavior that suggests potential fraud." In case studies conducted by the states of Texas and Ohio, investigators found that large accumulated balances typically resulted from one of several non-fraudulent behaviors: (a) recipients using coupons or discount shopping strategies that stretched the benefit further than average; (b) recipients who made infrequent grocery trips due to transportation barriers, causing benefits to pile up between purchases; and (c) recipients whose life circumstances had recently changed in ways that reduced their consumption but whose formal annual recertification had not yet caught up with their changed income situation.

Existing law expressly permits benefit accumulation. Federal SNAP regulations allow recipients to carry unused benefits indefinitely, provided the account is used at least once within any 12-month rolling period. If an account goes 12 months without activity, benefits are expunged. There is no statutory cap on the amount of benefits that may accumulate (USDA, 7 C.F.R. § 274.2). This design was intentional: it was meant to prevent the perverse incentive of spending down benefits before month's end in order to avoid losing them, which could encourage imprudent food purchasing behavior.

SNAP households are required to report changes in income within six months of any change and to recertify their eligibility at least once every 12 months (with exceptions for elderly or disabled households with no earned income). Participants who fail to report income changes that would make them ineligible are subject to repayment demands. The existence of a high balance does not in itself indicate that any such reporting failure occurred — it is entirely consistent with legally compliant behavior.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Semiotics of Welfare Outrage

The Kentucky receipt story belongs to a well-documented genre of American political media: the welfare-outrage anecdote. These narratives have a characteristic epistemic structure that deserves careful analysis. They begin with a datum that is either unverified or partially verified (a photograph, a conversation overheard, a receipt seen in passing), attach an attributed moral transgression ("the taxpayers' money"), and then circulate the composite claim in a way that makes refutation feel beside the point. The outrage is the point.

What makes such stories epistemically durable is that they exploit several distinct cognitive biases simultaneously. First, there is the **availability heuristic** (Tversky & Kahneman, 1973, *Cognitive Psychology*): a vivid, concrete image of a single EBT balance is easier to mentally retrieve and reason about than the abstract statistical reality that such balances affect 0.008% of SNAP households. Second, there is the **just-world hypothesis** (Lerner, 1980, *The Belief in a Just World: A Fundamental Delusion*, Plenum Press): welfare recipients who appear to be doing "too well" generate moral discomfort because they seem to violate the expectation that need and benefit should be perfectly proportional. Third, there is **in-group/out-group attribution asymmetry** (Pettigrew, 1979, *Journal of Social Issues*): the unnamed recipient is implicitly coded as someone not like "working people," and the disparity between their balance and what "most working people have in savings" is presented as an injustice rather than a policy outcome.

The epistemic novelty here is that Snopes's analysis surfaces the gap between **legal fact** and **moral narrative**: the behavior described, if real, was entirely within the law. The law's design reflects a deliberate policy tradeoff that welfare-outrage media routinely elides. The question of whether accumulation policies are good policy is a legitimate political debate; the question of whether a recipient operating within those policies is "abusing the system" is a category error. Abuse, in SNAP policy terms, has a specific technical meaning — it involves misrepresentation, trafficking of benefits, or use of benefits for prohibited items — none of which is evidenced by a high balance.

An additional epistemic layer involves image authenticity. The photograph of the receipt was attributed to Glen Phillips, Jr., who claimed in a June 2016 Facebook post to have found it in his driveway. EBT receipts can be easily fabricated using standard photo editing software or by creating a mock transaction; no journalist or government agency independently inspected the original document. In the information environment of 2016, viral photographs routinely circulated without provenance verification. The willingness of hundreds of thousands of sharers to treat an unknown photograph as definitive evidence of systemic fraud reflects what researchers have called **motivated reasoning for congenial claims** (Kunda, 1990, *Psychological Bulletin*): people are far more credulous about evidence that confirms pre-existing beliefs than about evidence that would require them to revise those beliefs.

## §4 — Institutional & Legal Context

SNAP is administered by the USDA through the Food and Nutrition Service, with state-level agencies handling eligibility determination and distribution. As of fiscal year 2016, SNAP served approximately 44 million Americans, with an average monthly benefit of approximately $125 per person (USDA FNS, 2016, *SNAP Participation and Costs*). The program is means-tested and subject to periodic reauthorization through the Farm Bill, a legislative vehicle that addresses a broad range of agricultural and nutrition policy.

The EBT system replaced paper-based food stamp coupons in the early 2000s as part of a modernization effort designed to reduce stigma, improve security, and streamline administration. EBT cards function similarly to debit cards and can be used only at authorized retailers for SNAP-eligible food items. Unlike cash assistance (TANF), SNAP benefits cannot be withdrawn as cash and cannot be used for non-food items, alcohol, tobacco, vitamins, or prepared hot foods.

The policy question the Kentucky receipt story implicitly raises — whether there should be a cap on EBT benefit accumulation — is a real legislative question that has been discussed by food policy researchers. Arguments for a cap include the idea that benefits should be spent on current food needs, not banked indefinitely. Arguments against include the practical reality that many SNAP households have irregular shopping patterns due to transportation constraints, disability, or rural geography, and that a cap could inadvertently punish frugal behavior or create administrative complexity without meaningfully reducing fraud (Mabli et al., 2013, *Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) and Food Security Programs*, Mathematica Policy Research).

The story also needs to be situated within the political moment of August 2016: Presidential campaign season, during which welfare policy, immigration, and entitlement spending were heavily contested topics. Viral welfare-outrage stories acquired additional political potency during this period because they served as emotional mobilization tools for audiences already primed to view government assistance programs as corrupt or wasteful.

## §5 — What Remains Genuinely Uncertain

Several questions raised by the Kentucky EBT balance story are genuinely unresolved and deserve honest acknowledgment as open epistemic liabilities:

**The image's authenticity** remains unverified. Glen Phillips Jr.'s Facebook claim to have found the receipt has not been independently investigated, the receipt itself was never compared against actual transaction records, and the specific store whose loyalty-card system would have generated such a receipt was not confirmed. The receipt could be genuine, staged, or digitally altered. This is not a trivial gap: if the receipt was fabricated, the entire story collapses. If it was genuine, all of the USDA's contextual points about non-fraudulent explanations still apply.

**The reasons for the high balance**, if genuine, are unknown. The viral narrative presented no evidence of misreported income, fraudulent certification, or benefits trafficking. The USDA's own review of high-balance households found that few exhibited fraud-indicative behavior. It is conceivable that a particular household's balance reflected genuine program abuse, but that conclusion requires specific evidence rather than inference from the bare fact of a high number.

**Whether the existing accumulation policy is optimal** is a genuine policy science question that empirical research has not definitively settled. Behavioral economics research suggests that benefit "banking" behavior, while legally permitted, may not always serve recipients' nutritional interests (Shapiro, 2005, *Journal of Political Economy*), but other research indicates that flexible access to accumulated benefits helps households manage food insecurity over irregular income cycles (Leung & Villar, 2016, *American Journal of Agricultural Economics*).

## §6 — Synthesis & Gap Classification

The Kentucky SNAP receipt story is a canonical instance of the **unverified moral-outrage register** in American welfare discourse. Its power derives not from evidence but from the affective charge of visible inequality combined with an implicit assumption that any unusually large government benefit must be unearned or abused. Snopes correctly rates it Unproven because the foundational evidentiary question — whether the photograph is genuine — cannot be resolved, and because the additional inference — that a high balance equals abuse — is contradicted by federal law and by actual USDA fraud investigations.

**EARC classification: EC (Empirically constrained, Contextually dependent).** The factual question of whether a $5,583 EBT balance is "abuse" is empirically answerable given law and policy: under existing SNAP regulations, it is not inherently so. However, the contextual variables — whether the account holder had properly reported income changes, whether the account was actively used, whether the receipt was genuine — cannot be resolved from the available evidence. The claim therefore sits in a space where empirical frameworks provide the outer constraints but contextual specifics determine the final verdict.

**Gap:** The primary epistemic gap is verification of the image's authenticity — a task that was not undertaken by the outlets that spread the story. A secondary gap involves the broader policy literature on benefit accumulation caps as an anti-fraud measure, which remains underdeveloped relative to its political salience. Future Truth Vault entries in the welfare-misinformation cluster should catalogue the recurring structural features of EBT outrage stories (anonymous photograph, comparison to "working people's savings," use of SNAP/EBT/food stamps terminology interchangeably) as diagnostic heuristics for evaluating similar claims.
