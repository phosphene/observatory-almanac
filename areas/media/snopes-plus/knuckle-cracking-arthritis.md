# Cracking Your Knuckles Causes Arthritis


## 1. The Claim

Few bodily habits attract as much unsolicited parental and social disapproval as knuckle cracking. The sharp, sometimes loud popping sound produced by forcing a metacarpophalangeal or interphalangeal joint beyond its normal range of motion has been the subject of admonishment across generations, cultures, and households. The standard charge is practical and medical: cracking your knuckles is not merely annoying but harmful, and the specific harm alleged is arthritis — a painful, degenerative condition of joint tissue that would be a severe price to pay for what is essentially a nervous habit.

The claim appears in multiple formulations. The minimal version holds that habitual knuckle cracking increases the risk of developing arthritis in the cracked fingers. A more specific version targets osteoarthritis — the degenerative "wear and tear" form associated with aging and mechanical stress. Some versions specify that the cracking "damages the cartilage" or "wears down the joint surfaces," providing a mechanistic narrative that makes the claim feel grounded in basic biomechanics. The claim is typically invoked by parents addressing children, by colleagues responding to the auditory discomfort that other people's knuckle cracking creates, and in casual conversation as a received piece of medical folk wisdom.

The myth has significant cultural longevity. Knuckle cracking warnings appear in advice literature going back to at least the early 20th century in English-language sources, and analogous prohibitions exist in many other cultures. The claim's persistence through multiple generations of medical advancement suggests that it serves social functions beyond straightforward health advice — it is a vehicle for expressing disapproval of a habit many people find annoying, dressed in the credible authority of medical language.

Understanding why this particular medical myth has proved so durable requires engaging both with the genuine scientific evidence (which is clear) and with the social dynamics of how "medical" objections to annoying habits get constructed and maintained even in the absence of supporting evidence.


## 2. What's Actually True

The scientific evidence is clear: habitual knuckle cracking does not cause arthritis. This conclusion is supported by several independent lines of evidence spanning multiple decades of research, including a remarkable long-term self-experiment that has achieved near-legendary status in evidence-based medicine circles.

**The Unger self-experiment**: The most celebrated piece of evidence is the extraordinary controlled self-experiment conducted by California physician Donald Unger. For over 60 years — from approximately the 1950s to when he published his results in 1998 and received his Ig Nobel Prize in 2009 — Unger cracked the knuckles of his left hand at least twice daily while deliberately refraining from cracking the knuckles of his right hand, which served as the control. He estimated having cracked his left-hand knuckles at least 36,500 times over the study period. At the experiment's end, Unger had both hands evaluated using clinical examination and radiological imaging. His conclusion, published in a letter to *Arthritis and Rheumatism* (Unger, 1998, *Arthritis and Rheumatism*): "There was no apparent relationship between knuckle cracking and the subsequent development of arthritis of the fingers." The right and left hands showed no significant difference in arthritis prevalence. Unger noted pointedly that his 60-year experiment proved at minimum that his mother had been wrong to warn him about knuckle cracking — a conclusion for which the Ig Nobel Academy honoured him.

**Epidemiological studies**: The Unger self-experiment, while compelling as a parable, is a single case study (N=1). More statistically powered evidence comes from epidemiological studies examining knuckle-cracking habits in larger populations.

A 1990 study by Castellanos & Axelrod (1990, *Annals of the Rheumatic Diseases*) examined 300 people over 45 who had cracked their knuckles habitually for at least 35 years, comparing them to non-crackers. No increased prevalence of hand or finger osteoarthritis was found in the cracker group. The same study did find elevated rates of hand swelling and reduced grip strength in habitual crackers, though these findings remained controversial and have not been consistently replicated.

A more recent study by Deweber and colleagues (2011, *Journal of the American Board of Family Medicine*) used a case-control design, recruiting 215 individuals (including 135 with hand osteoarthritis and 80 without). The study found no significant association between habitual knuckle cracking history and the presence or severity of hand osteoarthritis, with odds ratios not significantly different from 1.0.

A 2017 study by Boutin and colleagues (2017, *PLOS ONE*) specifically examined grip strength in 30 habitual knuckle crackers and 30 non-crackers using standardized grip dynamometry, finding no significant difference between the groups — directly addressing and effectively failing to replicate the Castellanos & Axelrod (1990) grip-strength finding.

**The mechanism of the cracking sound**: A genuinely interesting open scientific question involves not the arthritis claim but the mechanism of the cracking sound itself — an area where the scientific consensus has been revised more recently than the arthritis research and that most public coverage still presents in outdated terms.

The long-dominant explanation, derived from a 1971 study by Unsworth, Dowson & Wright (*Annals of the Rheumatic Diseases*), held that the crack is produced by the sudden collapse of a gas-filled cavity in the synovial fluid upon joint distraction. The model proposed that when the joint surfaces are pulled apart, dissolved gases (primarily carbon dioxide) rapidly come out of solution to form a bubble, and the cracking sound is produced by the bubble's collapse (tribonucleation). The approximately 15-30 minute wait before a knuckle can be re-cracked was explained as the time required for gas to re-dissolve into the synovial fluid.

A 2015 study by Kawchuk and colleagues (Kawchuk et al., 2015, *PLOS ONE*) using real-time MRI imaging to observe knuckle cracking as it occurred produced a significant finding: the crack appeared to be associated with the rapid *formation* of a gas-filled cavity, not its collapse. The imaging clearly showed a bright spot of gas appearing in the joint at the moment of the audible crack, rather than a pre-existing bubble collapsing. This suggested a different mechanism — one in which the sound is produced by bubble formation (tribonucleation) rather than bubble collapse — and reopened what had appeared to be a settled question about basic joint mechanics.

However, the Kawchuk et al. (2015) interpretation was itself subsequently challenged by Chandran Suja & Bhattacharya (2018, *Scientific Reports*), who used mathematical modeling of bubble dynamics to argue that the observed MRI evidence was compatible with either formation or partial collapse of a gas cavity and did not definitely settle the mechanism. As of the most recent literature, the mechanism of the knuckle-cracking sound remains genuinely unsettled at a fine-grained mechanistic level — a minor but genuine open question in musculoskeletal science.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The knuckle cracking myth illustrates a distinctive category of medical folk belief: claims that serve clear social functions (disciplining an annoying habit) and are maintained not because they are substantiated but because their substantiation is difficult to check and the burden of proof is placed on the wrong party.

The social dynamics of the claim are worth unpacking. When a child cracks their knuckles and a parent says "that will give you arthritis," the child is almost never in a position to demand the evidence for this assertion. The parent's authority plus the medical-sounding nature of the claim plus the difficulty of disproving it in a casual setting means the claim propagates one generation at a time, embedded in parent-child interaction. This transmission mechanism is entirely divorced from empirical support — the claim's biological plausibility determines whether it spreads, not its accuracy.

The biological plausibility is genuine, which is critical. The intuitive logic -- forcing a joint beyond its normal range of movement, producing an audible physical event that sounds like something breaking, must surely cause wear and damage over time -- makes good sense as a general principle of mechanical systems. If you forced a door hinge past its designed range of motion 36,500 times, you would expect mechanical damage. The inference that joints work like door hinges is an availability-class error — applying a mechanical model to a biological system without accounting for the biological system's regenerative and adaptive properties — but it is not irrational by everyday standards of reasoning about physical systems.

Research on how people evaluate health claims suggests that biological plausibility is one of the most powerful endorsement mechanisms for medical folk beliefs. Studies by Kaptchuk and colleagues on the "therapeutic ritual" effect in medical contexts (Kaptchuk et al., 2008, *BMJ*) and research by Tversky & Kahneman on the "representativeness heuristic" suggest that people evaluate health claims primarily through story-coherence and physical plausibility rather than through explicit citation of evidence. A claim that tells a coherent causal story — crack causes mechanical stress causes wear causes arthritis — is cognitively privileged over even well-replicated negative findings that lack a compelling causal narrative.

**Epistemic novelty**: What is genuinely underappreciated in the popular debunking of this claim is that it contains a real but incorrectly identified signal. Habitual knuckle cracking *may* be associated with some hand functional changes over long periods — the Castellanos & Axelrod (1990) grip strength and hand swelling findings, though not strongly replicated, have not been decisively refuted. The myth incorrectly identifies the adverse outcome (osteoarthritis) but may be pointing vaguely toward real functional concerns (grip strength, soft tissue integrity) that warrant more investigation than they have received.

This distinction matters epistemologically: completely debunking the claim risks communicating "knuckle cracking is harmless" when the evidence more precisely says "knuckle cracking does not cause the specific joint pathology called arthritis." These are not identical claims, and precision about the distinction matters for anyone interested in long-term hand function. A habitual knuckle cracker who uses the arthritis debunking as license to dismiss all functional concern has overcorrected from the original myth.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** False  
**Confidence:** High  
**Epistemic Basis:** Multiple independent studies including one 60-year self-experiment; consistent negative findings  
**Phenomenological Status:** Not applicable (mechanical/biological claim)  
**Mechanistic Status:** Confirmed — no known mechanism links knuckle cracking to arthritis; cracking sound mechanism itself remains under investigation  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Low — the arthritis claim encodes a real social function (discouraging the sound) but is empirically false

The claim is false. No reliable evidence supports an association between habitual knuckle cracking and arthritis (osteoarthritis or any other form). The Unger self-experiment, while a single case, provides 60 years of controlled longitudinal evidence within one individual. Multiple epidemiological studies across different populations and time periods converge on the same negative finding. Biologically plausible mechanisms by which the mechanical forces involved in knuckle cracking could cause articular cartilage damage have not been identified.

The more interesting scientific questions lie elsewhere: the mechanism of the cracking sound itself (bubble formation versus collapse) remains genuinely debated by physicists and physiologists, and the minority signal on grip strength in long-term crackers merits higher-powered longitudinal study than it has received. These questions are more scientifically interesting than the arthritis question, which is effectively settled.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The knuckle cracking case opens onto several connected intellectual territories: the biomechanics of synovial joints, the sociology of medical folk belief transmission, the history of the Ig Nobel Prize as an institution, and the question of what it means to "settle" a scientific question at the level of folk knowledge.

**Synovial Joint Biomechanics**: Synovial joints — the class that includes the finger joints, knee, hip, shoulder, and most other major articular structures — are remarkable biological engineering achievements. The articulating surfaces are covered with hyaline cartilage, which is avascular and has very limited intrinsic regenerative capacity, but is maintained under normal conditions by the mechanical circulation of synovial fluid, which delivers nutrients and removes waste products. The joint cavity is enclosed in a fibrous capsule and lined with a synovial membrane that produces synovial fluid — a viscous lubricant with viscoelastic properties far exceeding any engineered lubricant at equivalent scales. Understanding this architecture contextualizes the knuckle cracking question: the concern about "damaging cartilage" would require that the forces involved in knuckle cracking exceed the cartilage's ability to remodel and recover, which available evidence suggests they do not.

The precise forces involved in voluntary knuckle cracking have been measured in biomechanical studies. The rapid separation of joint surfaces during cracking produces briefly elevated intra-articular pressures in a range that, based on comparison with occupational and athletic joint-loading data, is not expected to exceed the thresholds associated with cartilage injury (Protapapas & Cymet, 2002, *Journal of the American Osteopathic Association*). The forces are genuinely within the physiological range of normal joint use, not extraordinary.

**The Ig Nobel Prize and Citizen Science**: The Ig Nobel Prize, awarded since 1991 by the Annals of Improbable Research, specifically recognizes research that "first makes people laugh, and then makes them think." Unger's 2009 award is one of the prize's most appreciated choices among science communicators because it honors a genuine 60-year controlled experiment — remarkable for its duration, its controlled design (left hand cracked, right hand not), and the personal dedication required to maintain a behavioral protocol for six decades. The Unger case is occasionally used in science education as an example of good experimental thinking applied to an everyday question with rigorous personal commitment.

The prize also illuminates the institutional ecology of science: Unger's result could not easily have been published in a high-impact journal because the scientific question was considered too trivial to warrant academic attention. The letter to *Arthritis and Rheumatism* was a vehicle appropriate to the modest scale of the study, but it lacked the visibility or impact to penetrate public consciousness without the Ig Nobel Prize amplification. The case illustrates how genuine empirical contributions can fail to find their way into public knowledge through standard scientific communication pathways, and how alternative venues (like the Ig Nobel ceremony) can sometimes serve the science communication function better.

**The Controversy About Grip Strength**: The Castellanos & Axelrod (1990) finding that habitual knuckle crackers showed reduced grip strength and some hand swelling, independent of arthritis prevalence, represents the most persistent genuine empirical controversy in this area. The finding was based on a clinical assessment of 300 patients and achieved statistical significance, but it has not been consistently replicated. The 2017 Boutin et al. study directly targeted this question with standardized dynamometry and found no difference. However, the Boutin study used a shorter exposure history and a smaller sample, and the question of whether very long-term habitual cracking produces measurable grip changes has not been settled by a large-scale, properly powered longitudinal study.

The grip strength question is less emotionally resonant than the arthritis claim — partially because grip strength differences, if present, would be subtle compared to clinical osteoarthritis symptoms — but it is scientifically more interesting precisely because it is not settled. A thoughtful reader coming away from the standard debunking ("knuckle cracking doesn't cause arthritis — no evidence!") should understand that the *arthritis* claim is dismissed, not the broader question of whether habitual mechanical joint manipulation over decades has functional consequences.

**Transmission Mechanisms of Medical Myth**: The persistence of this myth serves as a useful model for understanding how medical folk beliefs are maintained in the face of contrary evidence. Research by Lewandowsky and colleagues on "misinformation and its corrections" (Lewandowsky et al., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) demonstrates that corrections of false beliefs are systematically less effective when the false belief has a plausible causal story attached and when the correction does not provide an alternative causal story. The arthritis myth has a clear story (cracking damages joints, damaged joints develop arthritis). The debunking says "no, the studies don't show this" — without offering an equally compelling narrative of *why* the cracking is harmless. Effective science communication on this topic requires not just the negative finding but also the positive alternative: the mechanism of the cracking sound (gas dynamics in synovial fluid), the resilience of synovial joint cartilage, and the distinction between mechanical force within the physiological range and force sufficient to cause tissue damage.

**Cross-Cultural Dimensions**: Prohibitions on joint cracking and associated negative consequences appear in multiple languages and cultures with localised variations. German folk medicine includes prohibitions analogous to the English arthritis claim; Japanese popular belief associates finger cracking with negative joint outcomes; variants appear across East Asian, South Asian, and West African popular health traditions. This cross-cultural distribution suggests that the claim may be generated independently from the observation that audible joint manipulation sounds injurious — an instinctive "if it sounds like something breaking it might be breaking" heuristic that operates across cultures without requiring transmission from a single source.

**Arthritis Type Distinctions**: A nuance almost universally absent from popular debunking of this claim concerns which *form* of arthritis is being discussed. "Arthritis" is a generic term covering over 100 distinct conditions including osteoarthritis (degenerative wear), rheumatoid arthritis (autoimmune), psoriatic arthritis, gout, and many others. The evidence on knuckle cracking specifically addresses osteoarthritis — the mechanical wear-and-tear form that would be the biologically plausible consequence of repeated joint stress. No study has examined whether habitual knuckle cracking might be associated with inflammatory forms of arthritis (which have immune-mediated rather than mechanical etiologies, making a cracking-arthritis connection even more biologically implausible). Universal dismissal — "knuckle cracking doesn't cause arthritis" — is not technically supported by studies that examined only osteoarthritis outcomes, though the biological plausibility of any connection to immune-mediated arthritis is essentially zero.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim as **False**, which is the correct verdict for the claim as typically stated. Their analysis cites the Unger self-experiment, notes the epidemiological studies on larger populations, and correctly explains the mechanism of the cracking sound (gas bubbles in synovial fluid) as a way of demonstrating that the pop is not a sign of tissue damage.

The Snopes treatment is accurate in its central finding and in most of its supporting evidence. The brief description of the Unger experiment conveys both the remarkable commitment the research required and the clarity of its conclusion. The reference to epidemiological studies appropriately notes the convergent evidence beyond the single case.

Where the Snopes piece could go deeper, for Observatory purposes, is in the following dimensions: the open scientific controversy over whether knuckle cracking causes grip strength changes (a more nuanced finding than the arthritis claim but not entirely settled); the mechanistic debate about *why* the cracking sound occurs (the bubble formation versus collapse question is genuinely unsettled as of 2018 onward); the social transmission dynamics that explain why this myth has survived decades of debunking; and the arthritis-type distinction that would make the blanket "doesn't cause arthritis" claim technically more precise. The Unger experiment also deserves celebration as a model of rigorous self-experimentation — a rare methodological template with broader value for understanding how individual-level controlled designs can contribute genuine scientific evidence.

The Observatory's contribution is not to alter the verdict — the claim is false and should remain rated as such — but to provide the richer scientific and social context that transforms a simple fact-check into a window onto joint biomechanics, the sociology of medical folk belief, and the genuine unresolved questions lurking at the edges of the settled central claim.
