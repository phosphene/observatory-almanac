## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In the summer of 2021, photographs of a condiment bottled in the distinctive Heinz style and labeled "Kranch" began circulating widely on social media, alongside immediate speculation about whether the product was genuine or a digitally manipulated hoax. The label described "Kranch" as a combination of ketchup and ranch dressing, with "a special blend of spices," branded under the tagline "saucy sauce." Several archived social media posts showed the distinctive bottle across multiple platforms, and Snopes received the claim for verification.

Snopes investigator Dan MacGuill (2021) rated the claim **True**. Kranch is a genuine Kraft Heinz condiment product that was first launched in April 2019, approximately two years before the 2021 social media resurgence. It is one of seven "flavor mashup" condiments that Kraft Heinz has created under similar portmanteau naming conventions, including Buffaranch, Mayochup, and Honeyracha. The product had a confirmed entry on the Kraft Heinz product website with a documented UPC code (00013000012768), confirming its commercial existence as a genuine retail product.

The claim is true with a historically informative caveat: the 2021 social media "discovery" of Kranch was a rediscovery—the product had been available for over two years before the viral moment that prompted fact-checking interest.

## §2 Product Verification and Historical Timeline

The Snopes verification rested on two primary sources: archived social media posts confirming the product's existence across multiple platforms and dates, and the Kraft Heinz official product website listing. MacGuill (2021) traced archived versions of Kranch-related posts to confirm the timeline, finding that Kranch had been launched in April 2019—meaning its 2021 viral circulation was a delayed discovery rather than a product announcement.

The product's entry on the official Kraft Heinz website described Kranch as a condiment that "Combines the delicious taste of classic ketchup + ranch and a special blend of spices." The UPC code 00013000012768 is a standard retail UPC that could be independently verified through grocery product databases, confirming the product's commercial retail distribution.

Kraft Heinz's "flavor mashup" product line—of which Kranch is a member—represents a deliberate corporate strategy of combining established condiment categories through portmanteau naming and hybrid formulation. The identified lineup of mashups, as reported by MacGuill (2021), includes:

- **Mayochup**: Mayonnaise + ketchup (first launched 2018 in the United States, following a 2017 test in the Middle East)
- **Mayomust**: Mayonnaise + mustard
- **Mayocue**: Mayonnaise + barbecue sauce
- **Kranch**: Ketchup + ranch
- **Buffaranch**: Buffalo sauce + ranch
- **Honeyracha**: Honey + sriracha

The timeline of the mashup line is significant: Mayochup (2018) was the first entry, and its successful launch—both commercially and as a viral marketing moment—established the template for subsequent mashups. Each subsequent mashup follows the same formula: portmanteau name derived from its component condiments, Heinz-branded plastic bottle in a color scheme associated with one of the primary ingredients, and social media announcement designed to generate both purchase interest and debate about whether the combination is appealing.

The April 2019 Kranch launch date places it in the middle of the mashup product line rollout. The delayed viral moment in 2021 appears linked to a combination of expanded retail distribution—products frequently gain visibility as they reach more grocery chains—and the cyclical nature of social media content discovery, in which older content is regularly rediscovered by new audiences and treated as novel.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Rediscovered Product Problem and the "Mayoreo" Counterfactual

The Kranch case presents a distinctive epistemological structure that distinguishes it from most novel food product verification: the product was genuine but had been available for two years before becoming a viral verification target. This temporal gap between commercial existence and viral discovery creates a specific verification error mode.

MacGuill (2021) framed the Kranch verification explicitly against the "Mayoreo" counterfactual: a fake Heinz condiment called "Mayoreo"—implying a mayonnaise-Oreo combination—had been circulated on social media in June 2021, approximately one month before the Kranch viral moment in July. Mayoreo was a digitally manipulated image, not a real product, and Snopes had previously debunked it. The rapid succession of a debunked fake Heinz mashup (Mayoreo) followed by a genuine one (Kranch) is epistemically interesting because it created a cognitive environment in which Snopes readers had recently been trained to assume that viral unusual-Heinz-condiment images were fakes.

This is an instance of what might be called *prior-case contamination*: the recent correct verdict of False on a similar claim increases the a priori probability assigned to "False" for the next superficially similar claim. Bayesian reasoning would incorporate the base rate of fake versus genuine unusual condiment claims in the environment, updated by the evidence in the specific case. But the human application of base-rate reasoning is notoriously imprecise (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974, *Science*, 185(4157), 1124–1131, on availability heuristic), and the salience of the recent Mayoreo debunk likely inflated the subjective probability assigned to "Kranch is also fake" even among informed readers.

The epistemic novelty for fact-checking methodology is that category-level base rates must not be updated exclusively from recent salient cases—doing so would make fact-checkers systematically more skeptical of true claims immediately following debunked similar claims, and systematically less skeptical of false claims during periods when recent verified claims have been true. The rigorously correct approach is to follow primary source verification independent of prior-case base-rate updating.

Importantly, the Kranch case also illustrates the *temporal dimension of product existence claims*. A claim like "Kranch is a real Heinz condiment" is not a claim about a discrete event but about a persisting state of commercial availability. The claim was true in July 2021 but the underlying fact traced to April 2019. A fact-checker who had investigated Kranch's existence in May 2021 and found no evidence might have (incorrectly) concluded it was fake; a fact-checker who investigated it correctly through the product's official UPC database entry, retail listings, or Kraft Heinz's own PR archive would find the 2019 launch documentation and arrive at the correct True verdict.

This temporal dimension—the gap between a product's commercial existence and its viral discovery—is a structural feature of consumer product verification that is distinct from temporal gaps in claims about events (which cannot be false for products that predate the event-claim) and requires source verification that extends backward in time beyond the viral moment.

## §4 The Chemistry and Consumer Psychology of Condiment Hybridization

The specific combination of ketchup and ranch that constitutes Kranch is comprehensible from both flavor chemistry and consumer psychology perspectives, and understanding both dimensions helps evaluate the commercial viability of the product independent of its viral status.

Ketchup's flavor profile is dominated by: high acidity from acetic acid (vinegar) and the natural acids of processed tomatoes; sweetness from high-fructose corn syrup and/or sucrose; umami from tomato glutamates and the Maillard products formed during cooking; and subtle spice complexity from clove, allspice, and onion. The tomato base provides lycopene-derived carotenoid pigmentation (the red color) along with a characteristically thick, pourable viscosity.

Ranch dressing—invented by Hidden Valley Ranch founder Steve Henson in the 1950s at his Alaska dude ranch and commercialized through Hidden Valley Ranch, later acquired by Clorox—has a flavor profile based on: buttermilk sourness (lactic acid from cultured dairy), garlic and onion (sulfur compound-dominated aromatics), fresh herb notes (dill, chive, parsley, depending on formulation), and an oil-based emulsified fat content creating a rich, creamy mouthfeel. Ranch is the most consumed salad dressing in the United States by volume, with Nielsen data indicating consistently higher household penetration than blue cheese, Italian, or Caesar dressings (Nielsen Consumer Research, 2019, reported in *Food Business News*).

The combination of these two condiments in Kranch produces a flavor profile characterized by a tension between tomato-based acidity/sweetness and dairy-based creaminess/herbaceous notes. This combination is not without culinary precedent: "fry sauce"—a mixture of ketchup and mayonnaise (itself the base of ranch)—has been a regional condiment staple in the Mountain West United States since at least the 1940s, and ketchup mixed with mayonnaise is common across multiple international culinary traditions under names including salsa golf (Argentina) and cocktail sauce (United Kingdom). The substitution of ranch for plain mayonnaise is therefore incremental rather than radical.

Rolls (2007, *Nutritional Neuroscience*, 10(5–6), 241–250) documented "sensory-specific satiety" as a mechanism by which novel flavor combinations can extend appetite for a food beyond the point at which either component alone would create satiety—a finding relevant to understanding why hybrid condiments can generate genuine culinary utility rather than being mere novelties. A condiment that combines two distinct flavor profiles creates a more complex sensory experience than either component, which may allow it to remain palatable across a larger serving than would be optimal for either base condiment in isolation.

## §5 Brand Strategy Behind Kraft Heinz's Mashup Condiment Program

The Kraft Heinz mashup condiment program represents a deliberate long-term product development strategy grounded in consumer research on condiment usage patterns and the strategic imperatives of a mature branded goods company in a competitive retail environment.

Kraft Heinz (formed by the 2015 merger of H.J. Heinz Company and Kraft Foods Group, brokered by Berkshire Hathaway and 3G Capital) faces the structural challenge common to large CPG companies: core iconic products—Heinz ketchup, Kraft mac and cheese, Philadelphia cream cheese—hold large market shares in slowly growing categories. Growth must come from either category expansion, market share gains in adjacent categories, or adjacent product development that leverages existing brand equity.

The mashup condiment program is a low-cost adjacent product development strategy: the ingredients are existing Kraft Heinz production streams (ketchup is already produced at scale; ranch, mayonnaise, and buffalo sauce are either produced internally or sourced from existing supply chains), and the brand equity of the Heinz name lends immediate retail credibility to the mashup products without requiring the investment that would be needed to launch a new condiment brand from scratch.

The portmanteau naming strategy (Mayochup, Kranch, Buffaranch) serves several functions: the names are inherently memorable and search-optimized (a consumer seeing the label in a grocery aisle can immediately decode the product's composition without reading the description), they generate media coverage through novelty, and the naming convention creates a recognizable family identity that allows consumers who purchased and liked Mayochup to easily find and evaluate Kranch as a related product.

The Mayochup launch in 2018 established the proof of concept. Kraft Heinz announced the product with a social media poll asking whether "ketchup and mayo" was a worthy condiment combination, generating over $2 million in estimated earned media value (PR Week, 2018 estimates, reported in trade press) from a campaign that required primarily marketing labor and the cost of formulating a new SKU—a return on investment that justified the subsequent expansion of the mashup line.

Berger and Milkman (2012, *Journal of Marketing Research*, 49(2), 192–205) analyzed the emotional drivers of social transmission and found that content generating "high-arousal" emotions—including both positive (awe, excitement) and negative (anxiety, anger) emotions—is more likely to be shared than content generating low-arousal emotional responses. The mild disgust or surprise generated by unusual condiment combinations is a form of high-arousal emotion that drives sharing even from users who find the product unappealing—explaining why negative reactions to Kranch contribute to, rather than detract from, its viral marketing value.

The international roll-out strategy of the Heinz mashup line also reflects awareness of regional condiment preferences. Mayochup was first tested in the Middle East, where "ketchup and mayo" combinations are widely consumed under the name "shrimp sauce" or various local equivalents, allowing Kraft Heinz to validate the concept in a culturally familiar market before bringing it to the more skeptical U.S. consumer base.

## §6 Verdict Confirmation and the Fake-Real Condiment Taxonomy

The Snopes verdict of True is fully substantiated. Kranch is a genuine Kraft Heinz commercial product that has been available in U.S. retail since April 2019, confirmed through Kraft Heinz's official product website, UPC registry, and press archive. The 2021 social media viral moment was a rediscovery of an existing product, not a new announcement.

The counterfactual Mayoreo case—a fake Heinz condiment debunked earlier in the same month—provides a useful taxonomic juxtaposition. The distinguishing features of the real Kranch versus the fake Mayoreo include:

1. **UPC code**: Kranch has a commercial retail UPC; Mayoreo did not and could not, as a nonexistent product.
2. **Product website entry**: The Kraft Heinz website maintained a product page for Kranch with a full product description; Mayoreo appeared only in social media photographs.
3. **Temporal evidence**: Kranch had verifiable archive evidence dating to its 2019 launch; Mayoreo had no archived pre-viral evidence.
4. **Production coherence**: Kranch's ingredients (ketchup, ranch, spices) are all existing Kraft Heinz production streams. Mayoreo would have required Oreo cookies (a Mondelēz brand, not owned by Kraft Heinz) and a cross-licensing arrangement that would have generated verifiable corporate announcements.
5. **Naming consistency**: Kranch follows the established Kraft Heinz portmanteau pattern (Mayochup, Buffaranch, Honeyracha); Mayoreo uses the same pattern but combines a Kraft Heinz brand (mayo) with a competitor brand (Oreo), which is atypical for co-branding that would otherwise require contractual disclosure.

These five criteria, in combination, constitute a verification framework for future unusual condiment claims: check UPC databases, check the brand's product site, check temporal archive evidence, evaluate ingredient provenance, and evaluate cross-brand naming coherence. The Kranch case satisfies all five; Mayoreo fails on at least four.

The broader implication for consumer media literacy is that the condiment mashup product category is now established enough that new genuine products should be expected from Kraft Heinz and its competitors, which means the base rate for "unusual condiment combination claims" being true is higher now than it would have been in 2010. Readers and fact-checkers who calibrate their priors accordingly—expecting some fraction of unusual condiment announcements to be genuine—will make fewer false-negative errors while maintaining appropriate skepticism via the primary source verification protocol described above.
