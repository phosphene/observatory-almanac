# Yes, LA County Granted a Young Girl's Request for a Unicorn License

**Snopes Verdict: TRUE** — In December 2022, the Los Angeles County Department of Animal Care and Control (DACC) officially responded to a handwritten letter from a child named Madeline, granting a pre-approved unicorn license under five whimsical conditions. The response was authentic, authored by DACC Director Marcia Mayeda, and publicly shared by the Department through its official social media channels.

---

## §1 — Claim and Verdict Summary

The claim is that the Los Angeles County Department of Animal Care and Control actually approved a young girl's written request to keep a unicorn in her backyard. Snopes fact-checker David Emery assessed the claim as **True** in a fact-check published December 17, 2022.

The story originated when the DACC published Madeline's handwritten letter and the department's formal written response on Instagram and Facebook on December 6, 2022, describing the exchange as bringing them "so much JOY." The exchange was subsequently covered by *The Washington Post* (December 9, 2022), which interviewed DACC Director Marcia Mayeda about the event and confirmed the letter's authenticity. The unicorn license included five conditions:

1. The unicorn must be cared for in compliance with all animal caretaking regulations in Los Angeles County Code Title 10.
2. The unicorn must have regular access to sunlight, moonbeams, and rainbows.
3. The unicorn must be fed watermelon at least once per week.
4. The unicorn's horn must be polished at least monthly with a soft cloth.
5. Any sparkles or glitter used on the unicorn must be nontoxic and biodegradable.

As an accompanying note for context, Snopes stipulated: "As fact-checkers, we're obliged to stipulate that unicorns are mythical creatures that, like Santa Claus, don't really exist. But we'd like to further stipulate that in addition to empirical truths there are also truths of the heart."

The "True" verdict applies to the factual claim that the license was officially issued. Unicorns themselves, Snopes is at pains to emphasize, remain mythological.

## §2 — Evidence and Source Analysis

The evidentiary basis for the TRUE verdict is unusually strong for a human-interest claim and rests on three pillars: primary official social media publication, independent news verification, and a named senior official as the author.

**Primary Source — Official DACC Social Media.** The DACC is a publicly accountable county department, and its Instagram and Facebook accounts are official channels. Snopes confirmed both the December 6 Instagram story (https://www.instagram.com/p/Cl2fINALCuc/) and the Facebook post (https://www.facebook.com/photo?fbid=525274226309242) as published by the official DACC account. The posts included publicly shared images of Madeline's handwritten letter and the department's formal typewritten response on official letterhead, signed by Director Mayeda. The letterhead, signature, departmental seal, and internal formatting of the response letter are consistent with authenticated DACC correspondence. The letters were reproduced in the Snopes article.

**Independent News Verification — The Washington Post.** The Washington Post published a feature on December 9, 2022 (archived at https://web.archive.org/web/20221209163529/https://www.washingtonpost.com/nation/2022/12/09/unicorn-license-girl-los-angeles/), in which reporter contacted and interviewed Marcia Mayeda directly. Mayeda confirmed the story, provided motivational context (the emotional toll of animal-care enforcement work and the morale-boosting quality of receiving a child's joyful letter), and described arrangements for Madeline to receive the response letter on her birthday along with an in-person visit to DACC headquarters. The *Washington Post* interview constitutes independent journalistic verification by a major national news organization, corroborating the official social media publication.

**Named Author — Director Marcia Mayeda.** The response letter was signed by Marcia Mayeda, whose role as DACC Director is publicly documented through the official Los Angeles County government website and LACOUNTYANIMALS.org. Her direct quotation in the *Washington Post* interview confirms personal authorship and intent. This removes any ambiguity about whether the letter was a subordinate staffer humor exercise that bypassed official oversight — Mayeda claimed the response as her own deliberate act.

**Contextual Corroboration — The "Yes, Virginia" Precedent.** The Snopes fact-check appropriately frames the episode within the tradition of the September 21, 1897 "Yes, Virginia, There Is a Santa Claus" editorial published in *The New York Sun*, in which editor Francis Pharcellus Church responded to eight-year-old Virginia O'Hanlon's question about Santa's existence with a celebrated affirmative editorial. This framing is analytically useful: it situates the DACC response within a recognized genre of institutional or journalistic responses to children's sincere questions about mythological or fantastical subjects, characterized by a rhetorical mode that takes the question's emotional meaning seriously while not literally affirming the factual existence of the subject.

## §3 — Scientific and Epistemic Context

While this story involves no directly contested scientific claims, it engages several intersecting epistemological and institutional dimensions of considerable interest.

**Regulatory Creativity and the Scope of Animal Licensing Law.** The five conditions in the unicorn license are simultaneously whimsical and technically sophisticated from a regulatory drafting perspective. Condition 1 — compliance with Los Angeles County Code Title 10 — is the only condition with genuine legal weight, and it is the cleverest element: it formally incorporates the entire animal care code into the unicorn license, meaning that if a unicorn were somehow obtained, the full regulatory framework for large exotic animal care would apply. Conditions 2–5 are non-legally-binding whimsy embedded within an official document format. This dual-register drafting (legally-grounded first condition, then whimsical conditions) reflects a sophisticated awareness of how official documents produce authority effects. The DACC's legal counsel presumably reviewed or was aware of the document; the county's public communication function amplified it. The document is a genuine artifact of institutional creativity within the bounds of official authority.

**The Institutional Psychology of Emotional Contrast.** Mayeda's explanation to the *Washington Post* of why the letter mattered — that animal control officers regularly confront violence, neglect, fatal disease, and dangerous animal decisions, and that Madeline's letter offered relief from that cumulative emotional burden — illuminates a significant feature of public service employee psychology. Research on compassion fatigue in animal care professionals (Sinclair, S., et al., *Veterinary Record*, 2017; Figley, C. R., & Roop, R. G., *Compassion Fatigue in the Animal-Care Community*, 2006, Humane Society Press) documents elevated rates of secondary traumatic stress, burnout, and compassion fatigue among animal control, veterinary, and shelter workers relative to comparable public servant roles. The morale-boosting function the letter served — acknowledged explicitly by Mayeda — is consistent with the broader literature on "meaning restoration" events in high-stress public service contexts: brief encounters with innocence, joy, or playful absurdity can provide disproportionate psychological recovery from accumulated vicarious trauma. The unicorn license episode is therefore not merely a charming human-interest story; it documents an adaptive psychological mechanism for maintaining professional well-being in high-stress animal care environments.

**Epistemic novelty for this entry:** The "unicorn license" story sits at a rarely examined intersection of institutional fact-verification and ontological whimsy. Fact-checkers routinely face claims about factual events (did X happen?) and factual entities (does Y exist?). This case poses a hybrid: the event (the license was issued) is demonstrably factual; the ontological referent (the unicorn) is demonstrably non-existent. The Snopes verdict of "True" applies only to the event-claim and explicitly disavows the ontological claim. This two-register structure — factual event + fictional subject — is pedagogically valuable for media literacy because it demonstrates that "True" verdicts do not require that all elements of a claim be factually real; they require only that the specific claim being evaluated be accurately described. The license was issued: True. Unicorns exist: False. The license could be issued for a unicorn: True (provision was made). These are independent truth-value assignments, and the episode illustrates why precision in claim-identification is prerequisite to accurate verdict assignment.

## §4 — Verification Methodology

The verification methodology for this claim is essentially straightforward: the DACC published the letters through its own official channels, a named senior public official confirmed the story to an independent major news organization, and the documents bear the formal markers of official correspondence. This is a high-confidence evidentiary base for a claim of this type.

Potential areas of fraudulent risk that the methodology implicitly guarded against: (1) the DACC social media accounts could have been unofficial fan accounts — Snopes' identification of them as the official accounts is defensible given their integration with LA County government online communications. (2) The *Washington Post* interview could have reached a different Marcia Mayeda — but Mayeda's public existence as DACC Director is independently verifiable through county government sources. (3) The letter could have been a staged internal production for PR purposes rather than a response to an actual child's letter — Mayeda's *Washington Post* description of arranging Madeline's birthday delivery and headquarters visit makes staged fabrication implausible. There is no claimed debunking or counter-narrative in the public record.

The weakest element of the verification record is that Madeline herself (a minor) was not independently verified as a real individual; her surname and full identifying details were redacted from the published letters for child privacy reasons. This is appropriate and not an investigative gap — the redaction is itself evidence of genuine institutional child-protection practice, which would not accompany a fictional child in a PR fabrication.

## §5 — Gap Analysis and Open Questions

**Gap 1 — Legal weight of the unicorn license.** The document is officially signed and bears institutional authority markers. In a strict administrative law sense, does a unicorn license issued by an official county director, under a signature of authority, create any legally cognizable expectation? The question is moot as a practical matter (unicorns are mythological), but it raises interesting questions in the philosophy of administrative law about performative speech acts by public officials: when an official acts within their authority, does a document have legal standing regardless of its object's existence? This is unexplored.

**Gap 2 — Comparable institutional responses.** The Snopes article draws the "Yes, Virginia" parallel but does not survey the broader landscape of comparable institutional responses to children's whimsical letters: NORAD Santa tracking, NASA responses to children's space questions, and similar cases. A comparative analysis of these institutional genres would substantiate the DACC episode's place within a recognizable tradition of institutional playfulness that serves genuine public-relations and morale functions.

**Gap 3 — Long-term follow-up with Madeline.** Mayeda described plans for Madeline to visit DACC headquarters; whether this visit occurred and what form it took was not reported in the fact-check or its updates. This is not a verification gap but a narrative completeness gap.

**Gap 4 — Replication in other counties.** Following the story's viral spread, it is plausible that similar requests were submitted to other county animal control departments. Whether any of these produced comparable responses — or whether the DACC inspired a new institutional practice genre — is untracked.

## §6 — Observatory Synthesis and Implications

The unicorn license story is among the most clearly epistemically resolved cases in the Observatory's inventory: it is True, the evidence is strong, the counter-claim surface area is essentially zero, and the sociological interest exceeds the fact-checking challenge. Its value to the Observatory lies primarily in two domains.

First, as an illustration of verdict-structure precision: the two-register analysis (factual event vs. ontological referent) demonstrates that accurate fact-checking requires specifying which sub-claim is being evaluated. Public communication of the "True" verdict without this specification creates room for misreading — audiences may infer a literal endorsement of unicorn existence if the claim framing is imprecise. Snopes' editorial choice to include the explicit unicorn-skepticism note ("unicorns are mythical creatures that, like Santa Claus, don't really exist") is a model of responsible verdict communication.

Second, as a case study in institutional humor as civic function: the DACC episode documents a real public agency acting in the public interest through creative, emotionally generous engagement with a child. This is an observable phenomenon in the behavioral sociology of public institutions — institutions occasionally produce genuinely whimsical, human-scaled responses that serve morale, public trust, and community functions simultaneously. Documenting and analyzing these cases provides evidence against the reductive cynicism that treats all government institutional action as purely bureaucratic or self-interested.
