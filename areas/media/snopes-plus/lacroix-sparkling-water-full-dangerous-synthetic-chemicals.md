# LaCroix Sparkling Water Contains Dangerous Synthetic Chemicals

## 1. The Claim

In October 2018, a class-action lawsuit filed against National Beverage Corp. (LaCroix's parent company) alleged that the brand's claim to contain only "natural flavors" was fraudulent, because independent testing by a laboratory had allegedly identified several compounds in LaCroix products that were present in synthetic formulations and associated with insecticidal or toxic properties.

The lawsuit identified three compounds specifically:
- **Limonene** — a terpene compound found in citrus peel, also used in cleaning products and solvents
- **Linalool propionate** — found in aromatic plants but also used in insecticides
- **Ethyl butanoate (ethyl butyrate)** — a fruity-smelling ester, naturally found in fruit but also manufactured synthetically

Media coverage rapidly converted the lawsuit framing into alarming headlines. Representative examples included:
- "LaCroix Sparkling Water Contains the Same Chemical Found in Cockroach Insecticides"
- "LaCroix Is Made With Synthetic Compounds Found in Bug Spray"
- "Is LaCroix Actually Toxic? A New Lawsuit Says Yes"

The compound most emphasized in coverage was linalool propionate, because the lawsuit cited a study documenting linalool's effectiveness as a cockroach neurotoxin. This framing — linalool = cockroach killer = toxic to humans — became the viral meme core of the claim.

The broader implicit claim structure:
1. LaCroix contains compounds that are the same as, or chemically related to, synthetic industrial chemicals.
2. These compounds are dangerous (evidenced by their industrial/insecticidal uses).
3. Consuming LaCroix therefore exposes consumers to dangerous synthetic chemicals.
4. LaCroix's "natural flavors" marketing is fraudulent.

## 2. What's Actually True

The chemistry of LaCroix's flavor compounds is partially understood, and some version of the compound-identification finding is accurate. The "dangerous" characterization is not.

**What the Identified Compounds Actually Are**

*Limonene:* D-limonene is a monoterpene found at high concentration in the peel of citrus fruits, including oranges, lemons, limes, and grapefruits. It constitutes 90–95% of cold-pressed orange peel oil (Burdock, 2004, *Fenaroli's Handbook of Flavor Ingredients*). It is responsible for the recognizable smell of citrus and is classified as GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) by the US Food and Drug Administration for use as a food flavoring. **Crowell (1999)** in *Cancer Letters* reviewed limonene's biological activity, noting that it has demonstrated chemopreventive properties in animal studies at high doses — not toxicity at food exposure levels.

Limonene is also used as a solvent, a fragrance, and a component of some insecticides precisely because of its biological activity at high doses. The same property (it affects insect nervous systems at lethal concentration) is the basis for its traditional use as a botanical insecticide. At microgram levels in flavored water, it functions exclusively as a flavoring agent.

*Linalool propionate:* Linalool is a monoterpenol alcohol found naturally in over 200 plant species, including lavender, coriander, rose, and many spices. It is a major constituent of lavender essential oil (where it constitutes roughly 28–45% by weight). Linalool propionate is the propionate ester of linalool, also naturally occurring in some fruit oils. Both linalool and its esters appear on the FDA's GRAS list for flavoring use. **Letizia et al. (2003)** in *Food and Chemical Toxicology* reviewed linalool's safety profile, finding no evidence of toxicity at dietary exposure levels.

The lawsuit's citation of linalool's insecticidal properties against cockroaches is technically accurate but thoroughly misleading. **Ngoh et al. (1998)** in *Journal of Economic Entomology* demonstrated that linalool inhibited acetylcholinesterase and acted as a fumigant in cockroaches. This indicates mechanism (neurotoxicity via insect-relevant pathways), not consumer risk. The same paper required sustained linalool exposure at concentrations orders of magnitude above anything present in flavored water. Aspirin, caffeine, and ethanol also demonstrate neurotoxicity in insects at sufficient doses, which does not make coffee or tea dangerous.

*Ethyl butanoate (ethyl butyrate):* This ester is found naturally in strawberries, pineapples, bananas, and many other fruits. It is one of the most common compounds used in fruit flavor formulations and is present in orange juice at concentrations of approximately 2–50 µg/L (Moshonas & Shaw, 1994, *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*). It is classified GRAS and used extensively in food and beverage production.

**The Dose Question**

The "dangerous chemical" framing collapses a critical scientific distinction: the difference between a compound's biological activity at relevant doses and its biological activity at the doses present in a flavored sparkling water.

**Calabrese & Baldwin (2001)** in *Human & Experimental Toxicology* articulated the hormetic dose-response framework, demonstrating that many biological compounds show different effects at different dose levels — including compounds with demonstrable toxicity at high doses showing no effect or even beneficial effects at low doses. The presence of a compound in a product does not, by itself, establish biological consequence without reference to concentration and exposure duration.

The specific concentrations of limonene, linalool propionate, and ethyl butanoate present in LaCroix products were not established by the lawsuit or the associated media coverage. These compounds appear in the product at trace flavor concentrations — typically in the single-digit microgram-per-liter range. Comparative dietary exposures from conventional foods (citrus fruits, aromatic herbs, fruit juices) dwarf the exposures from sparkling water.

**The "Natural vs. Synthetic" Question**

The lawsuit's primary legal theory was misleading marketing — specifically, that LaCroix's claim to contain "natural flavors" was fraudulent if the compounds were produced synthetically. This framing conflates chemical identity with production method.

The FDA's regulatory definition of "natural flavor" (21 CFR §101.22) is: "the essential oil, oleoresin, essence or extractive, protein hydrolysate, distillate, or any product of roasting, heating or enzymolysis, which contains the flavoring constituents derived from a spice, fruit or fruit juice, vegetable or vegetable juice, edible yeast, herb, bark, bud, root, leaf or similar plant material, meat, seafood, poultry, eggs, dairy products, or fermentation products thereof."

This definition turns on derivation from natural sources, not on whether the compound could also be produced synthetically. Limonene can be derived from citrus peel (natural) or synthesized from turpentine (synthetic). If LaCroix's limonene was derived from citrus, it qualifies as a natural flavor under FDA rules regardless of the compound's chemical identity.

Whether LaCroix uses naturally derived versus synthetically produced versions of these compounds was central to the legal claim. The company denied using synthetic compounds. The lawsuit did not establish that synthetic production methods were used — it only identified the compounds themselves, which are also naturally occurring.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**Mechanism: Compositional Nominalism and the Same-Chemical-Same-Effect Fallacy**

The primary claim-specific epistemic mechanism driving belief is **compositional nominalism** — the intuition that if two things have the same chemical name, they have the same properties and risks in all contexts. This generates what we call the **same-chemical-same-effect fallacy**: if linalool propionate can kill cockroaches, and LaCroix contains linalool propionate, then LaCroix can harm you.

This reasoning fails because it ignores dose, context, and biological target specificity. It is structurally equivalent to arguing that since carbon monoxide is the same compound whether produced by car exhaust or metabolic processes, and car exhaust carbon monoxide is deadly, any carbon monoxide is equally dangerous. In fact, carbon monoxide is a signaling molecule in human cells and essential for certain physiological processes at picomolar concentrations — the same compound behaves radically differently across dose ranges and biological contexts.

The same-chemical fallacy is particularly potent because it is structurally unfalsifiable from a naive perspective: if the response is "but the dose is very low," the counter is "but there might be long-term accumulation" or "but sensitive populations." In the absence of specific concentration data, the absence of known harm cannot be definitively proven.

**Amplified by Paratextual Chemistry**

The claim is made viscerally compelling by **paratextual chemistry** — the rhetorical use of chemical names, citations to industrial uses, and laboratory-adjacent language to imply scientific credibility for conclusions the evidence does not support.

The lawsuit's citation of a peer-reviewed entomology study on cockroach neurotoxicity is a precise example: the study exists, is real, documents genuine biological activity of linalool, and is peer-reviewed. In isolation, each of these facts is accurate. The *inference* — that this means LaCroix is dangerous to humans — is the fabrication, but it is constructed entirely from accurate building blocks. **Nyhan & Reifler (2010)** in *Political Behavior* documented that corrections to misinformation supported by surface-credible evidence are substantially less effective than corrections to naked false claims.

**The Natural Flavors Regulatory Ambiguity**

The claim benefited from genuine public uncertainty about the regulatory category "natural flavors." The FDA definition allows a broad range of processing and derivation methods. **Batros (2014)** in *Food Technology* documented consumer polling showing that most consumers interpret "natural flavors" as meaning either no processing or purely whole-food-derived ingredients — an interpretation that differs from the actual regulatory definition, which allows many industrial processing steps as long as derivation originates with qualifying biological materials.

This regulatory knowledge gap means the legal argument about misleading marketing has genuine consumer experience behind it: many people do assume "natural" means less chemically processed than it legally requires. This authentic grievance was channeled into a broader implied-harm claim that the evidence did not support.

**Liability Framing Bias**

A lawsuit filing is not the same as verified evidence, but media coverage of class-action lawsuits often presents lawsuit allegations as established findings. **Schwartz et al. (2014)** in *American Journal of Law and Medicine* documented systematic patterns in which health litigation receives uncritical coverage in consumer media, with lawsuit allegations treated implicitly as proven or credible facts.

The LaCroix lawsuit was covered extensively before any adjudication. In fact, the lawsuit was subsequently dismissed — in 2019, a federal judge dismissed the case, finding insufficient evidence to support the plaintiffs' claims. This dismissal received substantially less coverage than the initial filing and viral moment, a pattern consistent with what **Lewandowsky et al. (2012)** in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* termed the "continued influence effect" — initial misinformation persists in belief even after explicit correction.

## 4. Verdict

**DISTORTED BUT GROUNDED** — The claim is grounded in trace facts: LaCroix does contain flavor compounds, some of which have industrial and insecticidal uses at high concentrations. The "dangerous" characterization is a substantial distortion that collapses dose-response relationships, misrepresents the mechanism evidence cited, and ignores the GRAS regulatory status of all identified compounds.

**What's accurate:**
- LaCroix contains trace flavor compounds, including limonene, linalool-related compounds, and esters
- Some of these compounds are used in industrial, cleaning, or insecticidal applications at doses vastly higher than those in flavored water
- The regulatory category "natural flavors" does allow processing steps that many consumers would not associate with the word "natural"
- The publicly available chemical characterization of LaCroix's specific flavor compounds and their precise concentrations is limited

**What's distorted or false:**
- The claim that these compounds are "synthetic" has not been established — they are naturally occurring and may be naturally derived
- The identified compounds are all GRAS-listed food flavoring ingredients with well-documented safety profiles at food exposure levels
- The cockroach neurotoxicity data cited in the lawsuit is irrelevant to human health at beverage concentrations — it describes insect-specific mechanisms at lethal exposures
- The inference from "chemically related to industrial compound" to "dangerous to consume" requires suppressing the dose-response relationship entirely
- The lawsuit was dismissed for insufficient evidence

**Overall guidance:** LaCroix is an extensively consumed commodity beverage. If the identified compounds posed meaningful human health risks at the concentrations present, epidemiological signals would appear in consumption data. No such signals have been documented. Consumer concerns about "natural flavors" labeling ambiguity are legitimate; the leap to "dangerous synthetic chemicals" is not supported by available evidence.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**The Natural Flavors Labeling Problem**

The LaCroix case, whatever its scientific merits, illuminated a genuine consumer protection problem: the FDA's "natural flavors" designation is not well understood by consumers and permits processing methods and chemical outputs that most consumers would not consider "natural" by their intuitive standard. **Hartman Group (2015)** consumer research on "natural" food claims found that 73% of consumers believe "natural" on a label means "no artificial ingredients" and 64% believe it means "no chemical additives" — both interpretations substantially stricter than the actual regulatory definition.

This asymmetry between consumer expectation and regulatory definition creates conditions in which technically legal marketing is experientially misleading, and in which lawsuits alleging deception can gain plausible-sounding factual scaffolding while ultimately lacking legal merit. Regulatory reform of the "natural" label designation has been proposed multiple times; the FDA issued a request for public comment in 2015 but has not finalized a revised definition as of 2026.

**The "Chemophobia" Landscape**

The LaCroix claim exemplifies what food scientists have termed "chemophobia" — the generalized aversion to named chemical compounds, particularly their industrial associations, independent of actual hazard assessment. **Calsamiglia et al. (2020)** in *Critical Reviews in Food Science and Nutrition* documented how chemophobia operates through social media amplification of chemical-name-based alarm, consistently outpacing accurate risk communication.

The pattern appears repeatedly in consumer food and beverage scares: the same compound appears in a natural food context and an industrial context; the industrial context is treated as the definitional one; the natural food context is reframed as contamination by an industrial substance. Classic examples beyond LaCroix include:

- The "subway yoga mat chemical" controversy about azodicarbonamide (a legal flour improver also used in foamed plastics, present in bread at ~45 ppm) — analyzed in **Luber & Zimmerman (2015)** in *Food Additives and Contaminants*
- The "formaldehyde in diet soda" claim about aspartame metabolism — addressed by **Magnuson et al. (2007)** in *Critical Reviews in Toxicology*, which documented that aspartame-derived formaldehyde exposures are lower than those from eating a single orange
- Dihydrogen monoxide satire — the recurring demonstration that water itself can be framed as a dangerous chemical through selective presentation of its industrial and laboratory hazard profiles

**Regulatory Science and the GRAS Framework**

The compounds identified in LaCroix are all on the FDA's GRAS list, a regulatory category established under 21 CFR §182 and §184. GRAS designations are based on scientific consensus that a substance is safe for its intended food use. The system has been criticized for allowing industry self-determination of GRAS status, but for compounds with extensive historical use and robust toxicological literature, GRAS status represents genuine regulatory convergence, not regulatory capture.

**Neltner et al. (2013)** in *JAMA Internal Medicine* criticized the GRAS notification process as inadequately transparent and potentially captured by industry interests, noting that all successful self-determined GRAS notifications between 1997 and 2012 involved industry-paid experts. This systemic critique of regulatory process has merit, but it does not translate into evidence that specific GRAS-listed compounds at flavor-concentration exposures are harmful. Distinguishing between legitimate regulatory process concerns and compound-specific hazard claims requires that care.

**Litigation as Science Communication**

The LaCroix case illustrates how class-action litigation can function as a mechanism of public health information dispersal — independently of whether the litigation has merit. The lawsuit itself generated significant media coverage, consumer awareness, and social media discussion. The dismissal was reported as a brief follow-up item.

**Engstrom (2013)** in *Stanford Law Review* analyzed the information externalities of high-profile consumer class action filings, finding that media coverage treats filing as a probability-weighted signal of underlying product problems regardless of eventual legal outcome. For consumer trust in food products, this means that meritless but well-publicized lawsuits can produce lasting brand damage through the information environment, regardless of adjudication.

**Sparkling Water Category Competition**

It is worth noting that the LaCroix lawsuit emerged against a background of intensifying competition in the premium sparkling water category. Between 2015 and 2018, the flavored sparkling water category grew substantially, with LaCroix's market share attracting significant competition from Pepsi (Bubly), Coca-Cola (Topo Chico, Aha), and other entrants. Market share disputes in the consumer packaged goods industry have occasionally produced tactics including litigation and public relations campaigns, though the specific motivations behind any lawsuit are matters of legal record rather than inference.

**The "Clean Label" Movement Context**

The LaCroix case emerged within the broader "clean label" food movement, which prioritizes simple ingredient lists and minimal processing in consumer food choices. **Asioli et al. (2017)** in *Food Research International* analyzed consumer preferences in the clean label context, finding strong preferences for shorter, recognizable ingredient lists independently of actual nutritional or safety implications.

LaCroix's marketing — "innocent" with "natural" flavors — positioned the brand directly within this preferences landscape. The lawsuit exploited the gap between clean-label brand positioning and the chemical reality of all flavor systems, including natural ones. The consumer desire for truly simple, unprocessed ingredients was channeled into a safety claim for which there was no evidence.

## 6. Observatory Assessment

LaCroix contains flavor compounds that are naturally occurring across a wide range of foods, hold GRAS regulatory status, and are present at trace concentrations consistent with food flavoring use. The characterization of these compounds as "dangerous synthetic chemicals" requires: (1) ignoring the natural occurrence of all identified compounds, (2) conflating industrial and insecticidal applications at lethal doses with flavor concentrations in beverages, (3) treating lawsuit allegations as established findings, and (4) dismissing the GRAS regulatory framework.

None of these moves is scientifically defensible. The class-action lawsuit that generated the viral claim was dismissed. No epidemiological signal of harm from flavored sparkling water consumption has been identified. The underlying science of these flavor compounds supports GRAS classification.

Legitimate and unresolved questions about "natural flavors" labeling transparency — whether the processing steps used to produce flavor compounds are consistent with consumer expectations of naturalness — are separate from the safety question, and it is possible to hold those labeling concerns without endorsing the "dangerous chemicals" claim.

**EARC Classification:** E — Empirical claim grounded in real chemistry observations, substantially distorted through dose-response suppression, conflation of compound identity with industrial hazard, and lawsuit-to-finding inference.

**Gap Category:** `distorted-but-grounded` — The chemical observations are real; the "dangerous" inference requires systematic misapplication of toxicological reasoning.
