# "Despite Rumors, Land O'Lakes Butter Isn't Bringing Back Previous Logo"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: In October 2024, a rumor alleging that Minnesota-based dairy cooperative Land O'Lakes had announced the return of its previous logo — featuring a kneeling Indigenous woman holding a package of Land O'Lakes butter against a backdrop of green pine trees and a blue lake — circulated widely on social media. The alleged announcement quoted the company as saying: "Our customers made it clear that they respect tradition more than wokeness." The implicit framing was that consumer pressure, specifically organized boycotts or purchasing decisions, had forced Land O'Lakes to reverse its 2020 decision to remove the Indigenous woman from its packaging.

**Structure of the original claim**: The rumor contained several rhetorically effective elements designed for maximum sharing within conservative and culture-war-engaged social media communities: 
1. A corporate capitulation narrative (company reverses "woke" decision due to consumer pressure)
2. A specific quote attributed to the company that deployed polarizing language ("wokeness")
3. A call to action or celebration (the implied invitation to resume purchasing Land O'Lakes products or to vindicate a cultural position)
4. A clear antagonist/protagonist structure (the corporate "woke" decision versus the traditionalist consumer base)

This structure — corporate surrender to consumer traditionalism — is a recurrent template in the America's Last Line of Defense satirical network's content. The network has previously produced similar fake "capitulation" stories across multiple brands and cultural contexts. The template works because it flatters the core audience of the pages by confirming their belief that consumer pressure on cultural issues is effective and that corporations will respond to traditionalist pressure if it is sufficiently organized.

**Spread and reception**: The claim spread in Facebook posts with substantial engagement. One user commented: "When I see it then I will start to purchase their products!" Another sharer later self-corrected in comments: "i have egg on my face. Apparently, there is no proof that this is legit. Then again, there is no proof it isn't. Either way, I jumped the gun — sorry all." This self-correction, still embedded in the comment thread, captures the epistemically ambiguous middle position many readers occupied: aware the claim might be false, but also not fully certain, and having already shared the content. A separate Facebook post went further, showing a recently purchased Land O'Lakes package without the Indigenous woman image, reasoning: "Not here (yet). I bought it yesterday." This quasi-empirical approach — looking at physical product to assess the claim's truth — is epistemically sound in principle but was operating on a false premise: Land O'Lakes had removed the logo in 2020, so any current package lacking the image was simply what the brand currently looked like, not evidence that a reversion had not yet reached that store.

**Cultural and political context**: The rumor circulated at a moment of heightened debate about corporate DEI (diversity, equity, and inclusion) policies, pledges, and reversals. In late 2024, a number of major American corporations were reducing or modifying DEI commitments under pressure from conservative advocacy campaigns, political pressure from Republican-controlled state legislatures, and the threat of litigation following the Supreme Court's 2023 ruling in *Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard* restricting race-conscious admissions policies. The Land O'Lakes logo story fit neatly into a broader narrative of cultural rollback, which made it plausible to audiences who were consuming real stories of genuine corporate policy changes in adjacent areas.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Land O'Lakes removed the Indigenous woman logo in February 2020**: Land O'Lakes made the decision official via a February 6, 2020, news release, in which Beth Ford, President and CEO, stated: "As Land O'Lakes looks toward our 100th anniversary, we've recognized we need packaging that reflects the foundation and heart of our company culture — and nothing does that better than our farmer-owners whose milk is used to produce Land O'Lakes' dairy products." The new packaging removed the Indigenous woman figure and the lake-and-trees background design, replacing these with images foregrounding the word "Land O Lakes" with its founding year (1921) in bold, and eventually featuring farmer imagery that aligned with the cooperative's farmer-owner identity.

**The original logo's history and the image's cultural origin**: The Land O'Lakes Indigenous woman logo — formally known as "Mia" in some brand contexts, though this designation was not universally used — was introduced in 1928, designed by commercial artist Arthur C. Hanson. The image depicted a Native American woman in what appears to be Ojibwe or pan-tribal Plains regalia, kneeling on a lakeshore and holding a box of Land O'Lakes butter. The image was revised multiple times over the following decades — notably by Ojibwe artist Patrick DesJarlait in the 1950s, who updated the figure's appearance while maintaining the basic composition. Katherine J. Wu, writing in Smithsonian Magazine, noted that the DesJarlait redesign gave the image its most enduring and widely recognized form.

**The removal received minimal attention in February 2020 and major attention in April 2020**: CNN reported in April 2020 that the packaging change had been made in February but "received little notice until this week." The April coverage followed a wave of attention to Native American mascot and logo removals across major brands, universities, and sports teams — a wave catalyzed by renewed discussion of the Washington NFL team's name (which would change to the Washington Football Team in July 2020) and related movements. Land O'Lakes' decision, though made months earlier, entered the news cycle during this intensified cultural moment.

**The company confirmed no logo reversal had occurred**: There was no evidence that Land O'Lakes reversed its 2020 decision. Land O'Lakes' official communications, corporate press releases, and website contained no announcement of any logo change. No credible news outlet — including those specializing in brand and marketing coverage, consumer packaging, or agriculture and dairy industry news — reported any logo reversal. The claim did not meet the threshold of plausibility that would prompt serious journalistic coverage; it circulated entirely within social media ecosystems without any establishment media coverage.

**The source: America's Last Line of Defense network**: The claim originated with the "America — Love It or Leave It" Facebook page, and separately with America's Last Line of Defense (ALLOD), the parent network. Both pages' "bio" sections stated "Nothing on this page is real." The ALLOD network is a well-documented network of satirical websites and Facebook pages that publish content designed to appear as legitimate conservative news or outrage material. Snopes has tracked ALLOD content across multiple previous pieces, including the fake claims that Kid Rock shipped millions in hurricane relief supplies, that bread costs $11 in California due to minimum wage increases, and that Vice President Kamala Harris had been fired from McDonald's for stealing. The network's content consistently follows the pattern of provocative claims aligned with conservative cultural positions, presented in the visual format of real news, with satire disclosure buried in page metadata rather than in the content itself.

**Why the parent company quote was manufactured**: The quote attributed to Land O'Lakes — "Our customers made it clear that they respect tradition more than wokeness" — is tonally incompatible with actual corporate communication standards. Major corporations, including those navigating cultural controversies, do not use partisan political vocabulary ("wokeness") in official communications because doing so would alienate portions of their customer base and could generate reputational and legal liabilities. Beth Ford, Land O'Lakes' CEO, has given multiple interviews on the logo change using language of cooperative identity, farmer representation, and forward-looking brand positioning — the precise opposite of reactive defensive traditionalism. Any communications professional familiar with corporate communication norms would immediately flag the alleged quote as invented.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The epistemic novelty: confirmation frames and corporate behavior expectations**: The Land O'Lakes logo rumor is epistemically interesting because it succeeds not through making the false claim difficult to check but through making it emotionally and politically satisfying to believe without checking. Research on motivated reasoning — the tendency to evaluate evidence differently based on whether the conclusion is consistent with prior beliefs and preferences — documents that individuals are substantially less likely to deploy critical evaluation tools against information that confirms what they already believe or want to be true (Kunda, Z., 1990, "The Case for Motivated Reasoning," *Psychological Bulletin*, 108(3), 480–498). The Land O'Lakes reversal claim confirmed, for its target audience, a cherished belief: that consumer action against "woke" corporate decisions is effective, that cultural traditionalism is a winning public position, and that corporations' occasional concessions to cultural progressivism are superficial and reversible under pressure.

This pattern — the "confirmation claim" that inflates the apparent success of one's own cultural coalition — is a consistent feature of the ALLOD network's content strategy. Unlike misinformation that targets facts about external events (elections, health crises, crimes), ALLOD content frequently manufactures evidence for the cultural and political efficacy of conservative consumer behavior. This type of misinformation is distinctly motivating to share because it functions as victory news within the relevant community.

**The "woke capitalism" discourse frame and its information effects**: By October 2024, the discourse frame of "woke capitalism" — the idea that major corporations had adopted progressive cultural positions under social pressure and that countervailing conservative consumer pressure could reverse these positions — had been extensively developed in conservative media, think tank publications, and political rhetoric. Peter Roff (Roff, P., 2022, "Woke capitalism and what to do about it," *American Conservative*) and others had articulated theoretical frameworks for understanding corporate cultural positioning as both commercially calculated and politically consequential. Within this frame, a Land O'Lakes logo reversal was not merely possible but expected — it would confirm the theory's predictions. False confirmatory evidence for a widely held theory within a community circulates with particular velocity because it satisfies the confirmatory hunger of community members who are waiting for such evidence.

**The absence of verification infrastructure in partisan social media ecosystems**: Research by Guess, A., Nagler, J., and Tucker, J. (Guess, A., Nagler, J., & Tucker, J., 2019, "Less than you think: Prevalence and predictors of fake news dissemination on Facebook," *Science Advances*, 5(1)) documented that fake news exposure on Facebook was concentrated in specific demographic and political groups, and that within those groups, fact-checking behavior was correlated with media literacy habits that were themselves unevenly distributed. Within conservative social media ecosystems specifically, Snopes and other mainstream fact-checkers are frequently perceived as politically biased, reducing their effectiveness as correction sources even when they produce accurate corrections. The Land O'Lakes rumor's primary audience was therefore the audience most likely to share it and least likely to fact-check it through sources that would challenge the claim.

**Physical evidence misinterpretation**: One notable feature of the Land O'Lakes rumor's reception was the response of at least one Facebook user who posted a photograph of a recently purchased Land O'Lakes product as evidence against the rumor ("Not here yet"). This misidentification of normal current packaging as evidence of the alleged reversion not having occurred yet — rather than evidence that the alleged reversion had not been announced at all — demonstrates a particular failure mode of empirical reasoning: treating the absence of the promised change as corroboratory of the original claim's potential truth ("maybe it just hasn't arrived in stores"). The logical structure is: "They announced they'd bring it back [false] → I don't see it yet → They must not have rolled it out everywhere yet." This chains a false premise to a real observation and produces a false conclusion, while the reasoning process itself looks like verification.


## 4. The Broader Context

**Land O'Lakes' cooperative identity and the logo decision**: Land O'Lakes is structured as a farmer-owned dairy cooperative, founded in 1921. Its governance and marketing decisions are made within a framework of representing its approximately 1,700 member dairy farmers and 10,000-plus agricultural members. Beth Ford's explanation of the logo change — that the company wanted packaging that "reflects the foundation and heart of our company culture" and represents its "farmer-to-fork structure" — was consistent with the cooperative's historical identity and its centennial public positioning. The removal of the Indigenous woman logo was framed not as a concession to cultural pressure but as an affirmative choice to foreground the cooperative's actual farmers.

The historical record on the logo change demonstrates that Land O'Lakes was not reacting to a specific boycott campaign or public pressure moment in February 2020; the decision was made and announced before the April media attention it received. This timeline undermined the "corporate capitulation to consumer pressure" interpretation of the change even at the time. Christine Hauser's New York Times coverage (Hauser, C., 2020, "Land O'Lakes Removes Native American Woman From Its Products," *New York Times*, 17 April 2020) noted the absence of evidence of significant organized pressure preceding the decision.

**Native American imagery in corporate branding: a broader pattern**: The Land O'Lakes case was one of many corporate logo and imagery reviews concerning Native American representation across the 2010s and 2020s. The Washington NFL team, Cleveland baseball team (then Indians, later Guardians), the Chicago Blackhawks (NHL), and multiple university athletic programs underwent reviews or made changes to Native American imagery during this period. These decisions were consistently contested within the culture war frame, with proponents arguing they corrected historical stereotyping and opponents arguing they erased tradition and were driven by political correctness. Research by Fryberg, S.A. and colleagues (Fryberg, S.A., Markus, H.R., Oyserman, D., & Stone, J.M., 2008, "Of Warrior Chiefs and Indian Princesses: The Psychological Consequences of American Indian Mascots," *Basic and Applied Social Psychology*, 30(3), 208–218) documented that Native American stereotyping in mascots and imagery produced measurable negative psychological effects on self-concept for Native American adolescent participants and activated stereotypical associations about Native Americans among non-Native participants — providing empirical basis for the scholarship supporting removal decisions.

**The ALLOD satire network as a recurring misinformation vector**: The America's Last Line of Defense network has been documented by Snopes and other media across multiple fact-checks since at least 2016. Its business model appears to involve generating high-engagement shareable content that circulates within conservative social media communities, generating advertising revenue through the traffic the content produces. The "self-labeling satire" strategy — placing "Nothing on this page is real" in page metadata while producing content that visually and contextually mimics real news — provides a degree of legal and platform-terms protection while maximizing the content's viral potential among audiences who do not encounter the disclaimer. Research on this specific type of content strategy by Allcott, H. and Gentzkow, M. (Allcott, H. & Gentzkow, M., 2017, "Social media and fake news in the 2016 election," *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 31(2), 211–236) documented the commercial incentive structures underlying fake news production, including satirical content that monetizes political audience engagement.

**The 2024 DEI rollback landscape**: The cultural moment in which the Land O'Lakes rumor circulated — October 2024, in the run-up to the U.S. presidential election — was characterized by genuine and well-documented corporate DEI policy changes. Major companies including John Deere, Harley-Davidson, Jack Daniel's parent Brown-Forman, and Ford Motor Company had made public statements reducing their DEI commitments during 2024 under pressure from conservative consumer campaigns and investor activism. This genuine background of real corporate cultural reversals made the Land O'Lakes claim more plausible within the broader information environment: if John Deere really did distance itself from DEI statements, why not Land O'Lakes from its logo?


## 5. The Verdict

**Snopes rating — FALSE**. No credible evidence supports the claim that Land O'Lakes announced the return of its previous packaging featuring an Indigenous woman. The rumor originated with satirical Facebook pages in the America's Last Line of Defense network, which explicitly label their content as non-real. Land O'Lakes removed the Indigenous woman logo in February 2020 as part of a rebranding tied to its centennial approaching year and its cooperative farmer-owner identity, and has not reversed this decision. No credible news outlet reported any logo reversal. The alleged company quote using the term "wokeness" is tonally inconsistent with actual corporate communication practice and definitively not found in any official Land O'Lakes communication.


## 6. Knowledge Gaps and Open Questions

**Gap 1 — Psychological and market effects of the logo change on Native American communities**: The Land O'Lakes logo removal was broadly welcomed by Native American advocates, but formal research on how the change was perceived within Native American communities — and whether it had any measurable effect on Land O'Lakes brand perception or purchase behavior among Native American consumers — does not appear to have been published. Research on the effects of mascot removals on Native American community sentiment (Staurowsky, E.J., 2007, "You Know, We Are All Indian: Exploring White Power and Privilege in Reactions to the NCAA Native American Mascot Policy," *Journal of Sport and Social Issues*, 31(1), 61–76) provides a framework but focuses on mascot contexts rather than commercial imagery.

**Gap 2 — The ALLOD network's reach and impact attribution**: While Snopes and others have documented numerous individual ALLOD content items, no published comprehensive study of the network's total reach, audience demographics, geographic distribution, or measurable political impact appears to exist. Academic content on pseudo-news partisan satire networks has grown substantially since 2016 (Lazer et al., 2018, *Science*, "The science of fake news") but tends to address the category rather than specific networks, and the ALLOD network specifically is understudied relative to its documented reach.

**Gap 3 — Corporate communication strategies for pre-emptive satire correction**: Given the ALLOD network's documented pattern of generating fake quotes attributed to real corporations, an open question is whether corporations like Land O'Lakes have developed specific communication strategies to pre-emptively or rapidly correct ALLOD-originated rumors. The speed and clarity of a corporate correction matters enormously for limiting spread; understanding whether and how corporate communications teams monitor satirical networks for attributions would be valuable for understanding the media ecosystem landscape.

**Gap 4 — Boomerang effects of logo-change-reversal rumors on brand perception**: Some research on false positive brand news (Bambauer-Sachse, S. & Mangold, S., 2011, "Brand equity dilution through negative online word-of-mouth communication," *Journal of Retailing and Consumer Services*, 18(1), 38–45) documents that false positive brand information can create expectation gaps that produce consumer disappointment and backlash when the promised change does not appear. For the Land O'Lakes case, some portion of the audience that believed the logo was being reinstated may have subsequently experienced disappointment or frustration at its continued absence. Whether this produced any measurable effect on Land O'Lakes' brand perception, sales, or relationship with the conservative consumer segment is unknown.
