# Is This the Largest Crocodile Skull Ever Found?


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: In April 2021, a photograph began circulating on social media that showed a human figure kneeling beside an enormous crocodilian skull. The photograph was captioned with the identification of the skull as belonging to Purussaurus — described in the caption and in associated commentary as the largest crocodile to ever live. The implication was that this represented an actual paleontological specimen: either a genuine fossil or a photographic record of one. The claim asked viewers to believe they were looking at physical evidence of the most massive crocodilian predator in evolutionary history.

**Why the claim attracted attention**: The photograph is visually spectacular. The skull depicted is massive — large enough that the kneeling human figure is dwarfed by it, providing an immediate and visceral sense of scale. This kind of scale-relative photography is a well-established trope of paleontological illustration: museum visitors photographed next to diplodocus skeletons, scientists posing beside unearthed dinosaur femurs. The human figure in the photograph provides a reference point that makes the scale of the object immediately comprehensible to viewers without paleontological training. The visual design of the photograph is optimized for emotional impact.

**What Purussaurus actually was**: Purussaurus is a genuine genus of extinct giant caiman that lived during the Late Miocene period, approximately 5–8 million years ago, in what is now South America. Some reconstructed individuals exceeded 40 feet (12 meters) in total length. It was a genuine megapredator — an apex predator of Late Miocene South American aquatic ecosystems — with documented feeding ecology that included large vertebrates. A 2015 study by Aureliano, Ghilardi, Souza, Guilherme, Cavalcanti & Riff published in *PLOS ONE* estimated Purussaurus' bite force at approximately 11.5 tons of pressure — described as 20 times the strength of a great white shark's bite force, and potentially the most powerful bite force of any animal to have ever lived. It is therefore entirely plausible that Purussaurus could possess a skull of the dimensions depicted.

**What the photograph actually shows**: According to Snopes' investigation and direct contact with the relevant company, the photograph does not show either a Purussaurus fossil or a Deinosuchus fossil. It shows a fiberglass model — a prop fabricated for use in television production — created by Crawley Creatures, a UK-based model design company that specializes in paleontological models, museum displays, and film props.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The skull is a model, not a fossil**: Snopes investigated the photograph and identified the skull as a creation of Crawley Creatures, a model design company based in the United Kingdom. Creative director Jez Gibson-Harris confirmed directly to Snopes that the model was fabricated using a clay sculpting technique followed by fiberglass casting. Gibson-Harris stated: "sculpted in clay, moulded and a fibre glass cast was made which is what you see." The photograph therefore does not document a paleontological discovery or a museum specimen — it documents a television or museum prop.

**The skull models Deinosuchus, not Purussaurus**: Even setting aside the model-vs.-fossil distinction, the skull was not modeled on Purussaurus at all. According to Gibson-Harris, the skull was sculpted as a representation of Deinosuchus — a different extinct crocodilian species from a completely different geological era. Deinosuchus (*Deinosuchus riograndensis*) was a giant relative of alligators that lived during the Late Cretaceous period approximately 80 million years ago, substantially predating Purussaurus by more than 70 million years. The photograph has been circulating online for years with the Purussaurus caption, but the underlying model was built as Deinosuchus from the outset.

**The production context**: Gibson-Harris told Snopes that the model was likely made for either *Prehistoric Park* (2005, ITV/Animal Planet) or *Walking with Monsters* (2005, BBC) — both docu-fiction natural history productions that featured animatronic and model representations of prehistoric animals. The Deinosuchus scene in *Prehistoric Park* has been publicly identified and posted online, providing corroborating context for the skull's production origin.

**What Deinosuchus actually was**: Deinosuchus ("[terrible crocodile]") was a giant crocodilian apex predator of Late Cretaceous North America. The Western Australia Museum describes it as having grown to approximately 11 metres (about 36 feet) in length and weighing 6–7 tonnes — roughly twice the mass of the largest tyrannosaurs of its time. Bite mark evidence preserved on fossil dinosaur bones indicates that Deinosuchus preyed on dinosaurs. It was the top predator in the rivers and estuaries along the eastern coast of Laramidia (the western island of a bisected Late Cretaceous North America). A smaller population on the eastern island (Appalachia) appears to have been a different species with different prey preferences, including turtles.

**Genuine Deinosuchus fossils exist**: Actual Deinosuchus fossil material is on display at institutions including the Natural History Museum of Utah, where a cast of *Deinosuchus riograndensis* skull material is exhibited. The comparison between the genuine fossil cast and the Crawley Creatures model provides a useful calibration of the model's accuracy and scale.

**The Purussaurus attribution has been circulating for years**: The social media circulation of this photograph with a Purussaurus caption predates the 2021 viral surge — variations of the caption have been attached to the image online since at least the mid-2010s. The inaccurate attribution persists across multiple platforms and sharing cycles, each iteration reinforcing the erroneous identification.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What This Case Reveals Beyond the Verdict

The verdict here is Miscaptioned: the photograph is real, the object photographed is real, but the identification attached to it is wrong in two independent respects (wrong species, wrong category of object). The fact that the error is double compounds its resistance to correction, because a correction that fixes only one layer — saying "this is Deinosuchus, not Purussaurus" — leaves the model/fossil confusion in place, and a correction that addresses only the model/fossil distinction leaves the species misidentification in place. Both layers require correction simultaneously.

**The fossil replica economy and its verification challenges**: The production of high-quality paleontological models — whether for museums, film productions, or theme parks — creates a complex verification environment for paleontological claims on social media. A good model made by skilled artisans like Crawley Creatures is designed to be visually indistinguishable from a fossil cast. When such models circulate on social media stripped of their production context, they enter an information environment with no standard mechanism for distinguishing genuine fossils from props and replicas. The Deinosuchus/Purussaurus case is an instance of this broader problem: a prop made for broadcast television, circulated on social media without attribution, is interpreted by viewers as a genuine paleontological specimen. This epistemological problem is not trivially solvable — the photograph cannot, on its own, disclose whether the object depicted is a fossil, a cast of a fossil, a model, or a digital composite.

**Amateur paleontology, social media, and species misidentification**: The case also illustrates a systematic challenge in online science communication: species misidentification of both extant and extinct organisms. For extant species, naturalist communities and identification tools (iNaturalist, Merlin Bird ID, etc.) provide accessible correction mechanisms. For extinct species, correction requires specialist knowledge of taxa that most users do not possess. Purussaurus and Deinosuchus are both real genera known primarily to paleontologists and paleontology enthusiasts — but the enthusiasm that drives viral sharing of "giant prehistoric predator" content is not typically accompanied by precise taxonomic and chronological knowledge that would flag the distinction. The resulting caption error propagates because the error's correction requires knowledge that the sharers who amplify the content do not have.

**Purussaurus, Deinosuchus, and the ecology of extreme body size in crocodilians**: An interesting epistemic dimension of this case is that the answer to "was there a giant extinct crocodilian predator?" is emphatically yes — twice. Both Purussaurus and Deinosuchus were genuine megapredators exceeding 10 meters in length, with bite forces that substantially exceeded any modern crocodilian. The claim being corrected is not that giant extinct crocodilians existed — they did — but that a specific photograph shows a specific species. The underlying zoological fact — the existence of crocodilians of extraordinary size in evolutionary history — is fully true. The confusion is misattribution and categorical error, not the invention of a nonexistent animal.

**Bite force and the physics of ancient megapredation**: The 2015 Aureliano et al. study on Purussaurus bite force is a scientifically significant paper in its own right. Using finite element analysis of a reconstructed Purussaurus skull — not the model depicted in the photograph — and comparative bite force data from extant large crocodilians, the researchers estimated a maximum bite force of approximately 11.5 metric tons. This figure has been widely cited in popular science contexts, sometimes with the additional claim that it exceeds the bite force of any other animal known to have existed, including larger theropod dinosaurs. The comparison is methodologically complex: bite force estimates for both extinct crocodilians and extinct theropods (including *T. rex*) carry significant modeling uncertainty, and direct comparison depends on assumptions about muscle attachment, jaw geometry, and bite mechanics that vary across studies (Erickson, Lappin & Vliet, 2003, *Journal of Experimental Biology*). The scientific reality is that both Purussaurus and large tyrannosaurids produced extraordinary bite forces; precise relative ranking requires more detailed biomechanical modeling than currently available.

**The role of paleontological television drama in shaping public knowledge**: *Walking with Dinosaurs* (1999), *Walking with Monsters* (2005), *Prehistoric Park* (2005), and the broader BBC/ITV "Walking with..." franchise produced a generation of visually compelling representations of prehistoric animal life that shaped general public knowledge of paleontology. Crawley Creatures' contributions to these productions are thus part of the public visual vocabulary for prehistoric animals — they shaped what these animals "look like" in popular imagination, sometimes in advance of the fossil record's resolution of those animals' actual appearance. When a model from one of these productions is stripped of its production context and recirculated as a "real fossil," it is in a sense cannibalizing its own source: the props built to visualize paleontological knowledge are being reused to generate false claims about paleontological specimens.


## 4. Evidence Assessment and Source Quality

**Crawley Creatures direct statement**: The most important evidentiary element in this fact-check is the direct statement from Jez Gibson-Harris, creative director of Crawley Creatures, confirming that the model depicted in the photograph is a Deinosuchus skull created by his company, sculpted in clay and cast in fiberglass. This is a named primary source with direct relevant knowledge — the person who supervised the creation of the object in the photograph. No higher-quality confirmation of the model's origin is possible.

**The production context (Prehistoric Park / Walking with Monsters)**: Gibson-Harris's recollection that the model was made for one of these two productions is supported by the independently verifiable existence of Deinosuchus scenes in *Prehistoric Park*. This secondary corroboration is consistent with the primary statement.

**Western Australia Museum Deinosuchus description**: The museum's detailed description of Deinosuchus — its dimensions, estimated weight, ecological role, and geological timing — provides accurate baseline information against which to evaluate the claims made about the model. This is a credible institutional source for natural history information.

**2015 Purussaurus bite force study**: Aureliano, T., Ghilardi, A.M., Souza, R.G., Guilherme, E., Cavalcanti, M.J. & Riff, D. (2015). Morphological, Paleohistological, and Phylogenetic Approaches to the Diet of Five Species of Purussaurus. *PLOS ONE* provides the scientific basis for the bite force claims. *PLOS ONE* is a peer-reviewed open-access journal; the finite element methodology used is standard for paleontological biomechanical studies. The 11.5-ton bite force estimate is methodologically defensible as an order-of-magnitude estimate, with appropriate uncertainty bounds.

**Species distribution of paleontological expertise**: This fact-check required not just identification of the photograph's content but knowledge of two distinct extinct crocodilian genera from different geological periods and continents. The double-specificity of the error — species misidentification plus model/fossil confusion — demanded specialist input that general-audience fact-checking resources are not typically organized to provide efficiently. The involvement of Crawley Creatures' creative director resolved the model identification cleanly; species identification for the authentic Deinosuchus character of the model required additional paleontological context.

**Gap assigned**: **E (Epistemic Gap)** — The broader category of error illustrated here — high-quality prop or replica images misidentified as genuine fossils or specimens on social media — lacks any systematic tracking or correction infrastructure. Paleontological models and fossil casts are routinely circulated without production attribution; once attribution is lost, the visual identity of the object is indistinguishable from the fossil original to non-specialist viewers. No published study has surveyed the frequency or taxonomic distribution of paleontological misidentification on major social media platforms.


## 5. Broader Context — Giant Extinct Crocodilians, Paleoecology, and the Economy of Prehistoric Spectacle

Giant extinct crocodilians occupy a distinctive position in popular natural history communication. They combine the visceral familiarity of modern crocodilians — animals widely known as dangerous and archaic in appearance — with extraordinary scale that exceeds modern animals of the same lineage. This combination makes them ideal subjects for popular science spectacle: they are recognizable (crocodile) but unimaginably large (40 feet, 11.5 tons of bite force), creating the kind of scalar shock that effective natural history communication exploits.

Both Purussaurus and Deinosuchus represent real evolutionary extremes of body size in Crocodyliformes. The ecological conditions that allowed such extreme size evolution are themselves scientifically interesting. For Deinosuchus, the Late Cretaceous coastal habitats of North America provided rich prey resources, warm temperatures, and — critically — an absence of competing aquatic megapredators large enough to challenge the genus. For Purussaurus, the Late Miocene Amazon Basin provided a shallow, warm, prey-rich inland sea environment that supported the largest body mass possible for an ectothermic crocodilian.

Modern crocodilians, while large by extant tetrapod standards, are substantially smaller than their Mesozoic and Miocene relatives. The largest modern crocodilian — the saltwater crocodile (*Crocodylus porosus*) — reaches lengths of approximately 6 meters under exceptional conditions, less than half the reconstructed length of Deinosuchus or Purussaurus. This size differential reflects post-Cretaceous changes in large vertebrate prey availability, climatic shifts, and competitive pressures from expanding large mammal faunas. The ecological conditions that allowed crocodilians to reach 40-foot body lengths no longer exist.

Research on the biomechanics of giant crocodilian skulls has contributed to broader understanding of crocodilian feeding mechanics, skull loading patterns, and the constraints on maximum achievable bite force in archosaurs. Studies using finite element analysis of crocodilian skull geometry (Metzger, Daniel & Ross, 2005, *Journal of Experimental Biology*) provide the methodological toolkit that subsequent work on fossil taxa — including the Purussaurus study — applied and extended. This methodological lineage illustrates how biomechanical techniques developed in the context of modern extant animals are subsequently applied to generate testable estimates for extinct forms.

The Crawley Creatures production history also illuminates the economics of paleontological visual media. Creating a high-quality, life-size skull model of an extinct megapredator for broadcast television requires substantial investment in clay sculpting, material expertise, structural engineering (to make the final fiberglass cast transportable and displayable), and paleontological consultation to ensure visual plausibility. That investment produces an artifact — the model itself — that outlives its television production context and takes on a second life as a museum display prop, a photographic subject, and, in cases like this, a social media specimen that generates its own independent information life.


## 6. Verdict Summary and Takeaways

**Snopes Verdict: Miscaptioned**

The photograph shows a real object — a high-quality fiberglass skull model created by Crawley Creatures — but the object is (1) not a fossil and (2) not a model of Purussaurus. It is a Deinosuchus skull model, likely created for TV productions in 2005–2006. The Purussaurus caption that has accompanied the photograph since its first viral circulation is doubly wrong: wrong species and wrong category of artifact. Purussaurus and Deinosuchus were both real giant extinct crocodilians; neither is depicted by a genuine fossil in this photograph.

**Key takeaways**:

1. **Two independent errors**: The caption is wrong in species identification (Purussaurus vs. Deinosuchus) and in artifact category (fossil vs. fiberglass model). Both corrections are necessary for an accurate understanding of the photograph.

2. **Both animals were real megapredators**: Purussaurus (Late Miocene, South America, ~8 million years ago, >40 feet) and Deinosuchus (Late Cretaceous, North America, ~80 million years ago, ~36 feet) were genuine apex predators of their respective ecosystems. The excitement about the photograph's scale is not misplaced — it just latched onto the wrong animal and the wrong type of object.

3. **The model vs. fossil verification problem**: High-quality paleontological models and fossil casts are visually indistinguishable from genuine fossils in photographs. Without provenance information, social media audiences cannot reliably distinguish artifacts from specimens. This is a systemic problem in online paleontology communication with no general solution currently deployed at scale.

4. **Production context creates information orphans**: When props or models created for film or television circulate stripped of their production attribution, they generate a category of misinformation that is highly resistant to organic correction: the object depicted is real, the image is genuine, but the caption is wrong in ways that require specialist knowledge to identify.

5. **Epistemic gap**: No systematic infrastructure exists for tracking or correcting paleontological misidentification of model/prop images on social media. Individual fact-checks like this one provide targeted corrections but do not solve the underlying category confusion at scale.
