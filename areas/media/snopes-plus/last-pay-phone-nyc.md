## §1 — Claim and Viral Context

On May 23, 2022, a media event organized by the city of New York drew international attention with a coordinated messaging campaign announcing the removal of pay phones from Times Square. The event was framed — by both official city communications and a cascade of major news outlets — as the end of an era: the removal of New York City's "last" or "final" pay phone. Within hours, the story had achieved massive viral penetration across social media platforms, news aggregators, and streaming video feeds.

The TikTok account for the official Times Square organization (@timessquare.nyc) posted a video of the pay phone removal with the caption: "The last payphone in NYC was removed today from Times Square." The video received more than two million likes and was viewed well over ten million times in the first twenty-four hours. On Twitter, the verified account for the NYC Office of Technology and Innovation published a post reading: "Proud to bid farewell to NYC's final pay phone!" Major news publications including outlets with global readership ran headlines framing the removal as the definitive end of the public pay phone era in the five boroughs.

The claim this viral event embedded was categorical and unambiguous: that no pay phones remained anywhere in New York City after May 23, 2022. This claim was false.

Understanding the mechanism of this particular false narrative is important partly because it did not originate with bad-faith actors or anonymous social media misinformers. It was seeded, at least in its most prominent formulations, by official city communications and amplified by credentialed journalists at major outlets. The failure mode here is institutional mislabeling — the city was celebrating the removal of the *last publicly operated pay phones in Times Square managed by the LinkNYC transition program*, but the messaging compressed that specificity into casual shorthand that was straightforwardly inaccurate as a general claim about the city.

The LinkNYC program, operated by CityBridge under contract with the city's Department of Information Technology and Telecommunications (DoITT), aimed to replace the aging public pay phone infrastructure with internet-connected kiosks offering free Wi-Fi, free domestic calls, device charging, and other services. By May 2022, thousands of LinkNYC kiosks had been deployed across the five boroughs. The removal of the Times Square pay phones was a milestone in this transition — but not the endpoint.

## §2 — Source Verification and Evidentiary Record

Within hours of the initial viral wave, skeptics began flooding social media comment sections with firsthand reports of still-functional pay phones in their own neighborhoods. TikTok commenters on the @timessquare.nyc post noted: "This is not true. There are so many still in NYC." Another user commented, "There's a payphone on the Upper West Side." These comments, while anecdotal, pointed accurately to the factual inadequacy of the "last payphone" framing.

NPR's reporting on the story, published May 24, 2022 (Treisman, 2022), provided the most measured mainstream correction: "Fear not, nostalgia-seekers and 'The Matrix' fans: There are still a handful of pay phones scattered around New York City." NPR's article clarified that the removed Times Square phones were presumably the last *public* pay phones in the city managed under the particular LinkNYC replacement program, but that private pay phones — owned and maintained by businesses — remained throughout the five boroughs.

Critically, NPR's reporting also pointed to coverage from HellgateNYC, a local independent publication, which went further: a reporter had visited the 14th Street-Union Square subway station and found a fully functional *public* pay phone — not a private one — still in service just a few subway stops from Times Square. The reporter documented an actual call made from that phone, providing direct empirical refutation of the "last payphone" claim even on the narrower interpretation of "public pay phone."

The Payphone Project, a website operated by Mark Thomas since 1995 that has dedicated itself to documenting the history and current status of pay phones across North America, responded pointedly to the viral story. The account's tweet read: "To hell with facts!" — a gesture of frustration at media misrepresentation. A follow-up post was more specific: "Not even close to CityBridge's last public payphone on NYC streets but why let facts get in the way of a superficially superficial story? Kind of a bizarre and meaningless photo op, tbh."

Further complicating the "final" framing: four walk-in phone booths — the enclosed, classic "Superman phone booth" style — remained active on the Upper West Side of Manhattan as of both 2018 reporting (NY1, 2018) and the May 2022 event. Gothamist had reported in February 2020 that a number of these booths were slated for eventual removal, but their removal was not announced on May 23, 2022. These booths represented a distinct category from the kiosk-style public pay phones and appeared to have been overlooked in the city's "final" calculation.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Institutional Mislabeling and Nostalgia-Driven Compression

The pay phone story offers a particularly revealing case study in a category of misinformation that has received insufficient analytical attention: the institutional nostalgia event. Unlike most misinformation, which originates in deceptive intent or factual ignorance, the false "last payphone" claim was generated by a legitimate government entity engaging in what might be called *milestone compression* — reducing a complex transitional milestone to a rhetorically cleaner, narratively satisfying endpoint.

The epistemic novelty here is that the city of New York had a genuine accomplishment to celebrate: the completion of a major infrastructure transition in one of the most symbolically loaded urban spaces in the country. Times Square pay phones being converted to LinkNYC kiosks is a real and documentable event. But the language chosen — "final," "last" — encoded a categorical claim that the city did not bother to verify against its own inventory of remaining pay phones before issuing it.

This type of mislabeling is structurally different from deliberate disinformation and yet produces comparably confident false claims. Research on organizational communication and accuracy suggests that internal pressures toward positive narrative framing can systematically degrade the precision of institutional public communications (Zerfass et al., 2019, *Journal of Communication Management*). Milestone announcements in particular are vulnerable to what might be called the "trophy photo op" dynamic, in which the publicity goal of generating an emotionally resonant image takes precedence over the accuracy goal of using language that precisely matches the facts.

Nostalgia played a distinct amplifying role in this story's viral mechanics. Pay phones occupy a particular cultural space in American urban mythology: they are associated with a pre-digital era, with landmark films and television shows, with privacy and anonymity. The prospect of the "last" pay phone being removed activates nostalgia circuits that drive engagement independent of factual accuracy. Research on nostalgia as a social emotion (Sedikides et al., 2015, *Psychological Bulletin*) identifies it as a powerful motivator of sharing behavior precisely because nostalgic content is emotionally self-validating: the emotion it produces (bittersweet loss) feels true regardless of whether the factual claim provoking it is accurate.

## §4 — The LinkNYC Transition: Infrastructure Policy Context

The replacement of pay phones with LinkNYC kiosks represents one of the largest urban telecommunications infrastructure transitions in American municipal history. As of its initial contract, CityBridge committed to deploying 7,500 kiosks across the five boroughs, each offering free gigabit Wi-Fi service, free domestic calls, charging at 2.4A USB charging ports, and access to city services. The program was funded primarily through advertising revenue displayed on the kiosks' large screens, replacing the revenue model that once sustained public pay phones.

New York City had, at its peak in the 1990s, approximately 35,000 pay phones installed across the five boroughs, making it among the densest pay phone networks in the world. The transition from that scale to near-zero over roughly two decades mirrors the broader pattern of landline telecommunications decline documented globally. The Pew Research Center's telecommunications tracking surveys (Pew Research Center, 2013, 2019) documented the rapid decline in landline usage among American adults, with the proportion relying solely on mobile phones rising from approximately 34% in 2010 to over 50% by 2019.

However, the displacement of pay phones by mobile infrastructure has not been uniform across all demographic categories. Research on telecommunications access inequality (Napoli & Obar, 2014, *The Information Society*) documents significant disparities in mobile phone access by income, age, and housing status. Urban homeless populations, low-income residents without reliable mobile phone service, and visitors from countries with incompatible mobile networks continue to represent functional demand for public pay phones. This context makes the premature declaration of pay phone elimination not merely a factual error but potentially a policy-relevant misrepresentation.

The subway system pay phone at 14th Street-Union Square that was found to be functional in May 2022 is emblematic of this structural reality: infrastructure managed by the Metropolitan Transit Authority operates under different institutional timelines and policies than street-level pay phones managed through the DoITT/CityBridge contract.

## §5 — Media Accountability and the "Landmark Story" Failure Mode

The rapid amplification of the false "last payphone" claim by major credentialed news outlets represents a specific failure mode worth analyzing carefully. Unlike misinformation that spreads through fringe channels or anonymous accounts, this story received prominent coverage in part because it was sourced from official city communications — an institutional origin that normally confers epistemic authority.

The heuristic of "official source = more reliable" is generally well-calibrated as a first-pass credibility filter but fails systematically when official sources themselves are engaged in impression management rather than precise information provision. The city's May 23, 2022 event was, functionally, a public relations performance: a choreographed milestone designed to generate positive press for the LinkNYC transition. The fact that it generated inaccurate press instead is an ironic but instructive outcome of prioritizing narrative over precision.

Multiple studies of viral misinformation corrections (Lewandowsky et al., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) find that corrections are substantially less viral than the original misinformation, particularly when the corrective message lacks the emotional resonance of the original. The nostalgic "last payphone" framing — evoking loss, transformation, an ending — carries inherent emotional weight that the corrective framing ("there are still some around") lacks. This asymmetry is structural and predictable, not specific to this story.

The Payphone Project's role in this story also deserves note. Mark Thomas's 27-year-old website, maintained by an individual enthusiast with no institutional backing, served as a faster and more accurate source of correction than any major news outlet. Specialist domain knowledge — in this case, detailed familiarity with the actual inventory of pay phones in New York City — proved essential to identifying the error. This pattern recurs in many viral misinformation corrections: generalist news coverage is outperformed by specialist knowledge held outside mainstream institutional contexts.

## §6 — Summary Assessment and Research Gaps

**Verdict confirmation:** False. The claim that the last pay phone in New York City was removed on May 23, 2022 was factually incorrect at the time of its assertion. Functional public pay phones remained in operation in at least one NYC subway station, four walk-in booths on the Upper West Side, and numerous private pay phone installations throughout the five boroughs.

**EARC rating — C (Confirmable):** The factual basis of this verdict is entirely confirmable from primary sources: the NPR corrective report, the HellgateNYC documentation of a working public pay phone, The Payphone Project's contemporaneous tweets, NY1's 2018 reporting on the Upper West Side booths, and the Gothamist 2020 status report. No expert elicitation was required.

**Epistemic gap — Low:** The evidence for falsity is unambiguous and multi-sourced. The key uncertainty is not whether the claim was false but rather exactly how many pay phones remained — a number that is difficult to pin down precisely but clearly greater than zero.

**Research gaps:** A precise census of functional pay phones — public and private — in New York City as of May 23, 2022, does not appear to exist in public records. The Metropolitan Transit Authority's timeline for removing or maintaining subway-system pay phones is not documented in accessible public sources. The ultimate disposition of the four Upper West Side walk-in booths remains unclear: Gothamist reported in 2020 that most would be removed, but whether that removal had been completed by May 2022 is not definitively documented. The long-term status of the Payphone Project's archive as a historical record for future researchers is not institutionally guaranteed, representing a knowledge preservation risk.
