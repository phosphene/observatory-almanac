# Matching teaching to a student's learning style (visual, auditory, kinesthetic) improves learning

## 1. The Claim

A belief held by a majority of teachers in most studied countries holds that students have individual learning styles — characteristic patterns of sensory preference that determine the mode of instruction through which they learn most effectively — and that educational outcomes improve when teaching methods are matched to each student's style. The most widely known taxonomy is VARK, developed by Neil Fleming in 1987, which categorizes learners as Visual (diagrams, charts, spatial representations), Auditory (lectures, discussion, spoken explanation), Reading/Writing (text-based materials, note-taking), or Kinesthetic (physical activity, tactile engagement, hands-on practice). Other taxonomies proliferate — Kolb's experiential learning cycle (diverger, assimilator, converger, accommodator), Felder and Silverman's Index of Learning Styles, Honey and Mumford's adaptation of Kolb, Gardner's multiple intelligences (which is often conflated with learning styles though it addresses a different construct) — but VARK is the pedagogical touchstone.

The meshing hypothesis, as it is technically called in the cognitive science literature, makes a specific empirical prediction: there should be an interaction effect between instructional modality and learning style preference. Students categorized as visual learners should learn more when material is presented visually than when it is presented verbally, and vice versa for auditory learners. Without this interaction effect — students doing better in the modality that matches their type than in non-matched modalities — the claim has no practical significance. Even if students have genuine preferences, preference without the interaction effect is merely a preference, not an instructional imperative.

The scale of commitment to learning styles in educational practice is remarkable. Professional development trainers offer learning styles workshops to school staff; classroom observation frameworks in various countries have included assessment of whether teachers "address different learning styles"; Individual Education Plans for students with learning difficulties have included notes about learning style preferences; textbook publishers market materials specifically assessed against VARK categories; and teacher training programs in the UK, US, Australia, and elsewhere have dedicated instructional time to learning styles identification and curriculum differentiation. Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork (2008), "Learning Styles: Concepts and Evidence," published in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, estimated that the economic investment in learning styles assessment and differentiated instruction represented hundreds of millions of dollars annually in the United States alone.

## 2. What's Actually True

**The meshing hypothesis has not been supported.** Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork (2008) conducted a comprehensive review of the empirical evidence for the learning styles meshing hypothesis and found it was not supported. They applied a stringent validity criterion: for the hypothesis to be confirmed, a study must not only show that students differ in learning outcomes by modality (which is true — some content is learned better visually for everyone, and the inherent modality of content varies) but must show the specific interaction: visual learners outperforming auditory learners in visual instruction, AND auditory learners outperforming visual learners in auditory instruction, with a statistically significant crossover effect. Studies meeting this standard that found results consistent with the meshing hypothesis were not found.

Rogowsky, Calhoun, and Tallal (2015), "Matching Learning Style to Instructional Method: Effects on Comprehension," published in the *Journal of Educational Research*, tested the VARK hypothesis specifically in adult learners and found no interaction between auditory learning style and auditory instruction type, and no interaction between visual/print preference and print media instruction. Style preference did not predict differential performance by modality.

Rogowsky's study was not anomalous. Newton and Miah (2017), "Evidence-Based Higher Education — Is the Learning Styles 'Myth' Important?" published in *Frontiers in Psychology*, reviewed thirty-two studies examining the meshing hypothesis and found that the large majority failed to provide support for it. Newton and Miah concluded that the continued popularity of learning styles in higher education represented a significant misallocation of educational resources based on non-validated theory.

Kirschner (2017), "Stop Propagating the Learning Styles Myth," published in *Computers & Education*, described the learning styles belief as a myth that persists because of its intuitive appeal, not its empirical support, and called for it to be removed from teacher training curricula.

**Students do have genuine preferences, but preferences are not instructional prescriptions.** The empirical record is not entirely negative: students do express consistent preferences about how material is presented, and these preferences are real in the sense that students report higher satisfaction with mode-matched instruction. Laborde, Massart, and Gaglio (2017) found that students can classify themselves with VARK and maintain consistent classifications over time. But satisfaction with a modality is not the same as learning more in that modality. These two things — preference and learning — are distinct, and the evidence consistently shows them to be decorrelated.

Willingham, Hughes, and Doborofski (2015), "The Scientific Status of Learning Styles Theories," published in *Teaching of Psychology*, made the additional observation that for many kinds of content, the modality is inherent to the material rather than an arbitrary instructional parameter. Mathematics is inherently symbolic and spatial; literature is inherently verbal and textual; music is necessarily auditory. The idea that a "kinesthetic learner" should have kinesthetic alternatives to written literary analysis is incoherent in practice, because the object of learning cannot be fully separated from the modality in which it is constituted. Teaching arithmetic through physical manipulation of objects may support early numeracy development — but that is a judgment about developmental stage and the concrete-to-abstract learning sequence, not an application of individual style theory.

**What does work.** The cognitive science of learning has identified several instructional strategies with strong empirical support that are not modality-specific. Spaced practice (distributing study over time rather than massing it near a test) has been demonstrated to produce more durable learning across populations by Cepeda, Pashler, Vul, Wixted, and Rohrer (2006), "Distributed Practice in Verbal Recall Tasks: A Review and Quantitative Synthesis," published in *Psychological Bulletin*. Retrieval practice (testing oneself during learning rather than only reviewing) has been shown to substantially improve retention in multiple populations and contexts by Roediger and Karpicke (2006), "The Power of Testing Memory: Basic Research and Implications for Educational Practice," published in *Perspectives on Psychological Science*. Interleaving different problem types has been shown to improve discriminative ability and transfer by Rohrer and Taylor (2007), published in the *European Journal of Cognitive Psychology*. None of these best-evidenced instructional strategies requires knowledge of individual learning styles; all outperform standard massed repetition practice regardless of the learner's expressed modality preference.

## 3. Why People Believe This

Learning styles is among the most studied neuromyths in education, and researchers have examined its persistence specifically. Several mechanisms contribute.

**The modality preference confirmation loop.** Students do experience genuine subjective differences in ease and comfort when material is presented in different modes. A student who finds it easier to learn from diagrams than from purely verbal descriptions is not imagining this — certain kinds of spatial information are inherently more efficiently transmitted visually, regardless of learning style. The student correctly observes a differential in their own learning experience; the error is in attributing this difference to a stable personal trait rather than to the characteristics of the material or the instructional context. This produces a **self-confirmation loop**: the student observes that they learn some things better from diagrams; they are told that "visual learners" learn better from diagrams; they adopt the identity of a visual learner; future experiences of visual clarity confirm the identity; and the hypothesis never gets tested because the causal structure that would disconfirm it (visual learners failing to outperform auditory learners on visual tasks) is never isolated. The theory is phenomenologically consistent with normal variation in learning experience, which makes it feel robustly confirmed even though the confirmation evidence does not actually test the meshing prediction.

**The therapeutic individualization narrative.** Learning styles theory arrived in educational culture at a moment when individual differences and personalized instruction were ascendent values in pedagogical theory. The 1970s and 1980s saw significant moves toward differentiated instruction, inclusive education, and recognition that one-size-fits-all teaching disadvantaged students with different strengths. Learning styles theory offered a systematic, empirically-seeming framework for implementing individualization: identify each student's type, match instruction. The theory's appeal is not merely cognitive — it is aligned with a set of ethical commitments about respecting individual learners and acknowledging their diversity. Rejecting learning styles can feel, within this cultural context, like rejecting the underlying ethical commitments. This **ethical halo effect** makes the theory resistant to evidence-based critique: criticizing the modality-matching hypothesis sounds like arguing that teachers should not bother trying to address different student needs, which is not what the critics are arguing but is how the argument can easily be misread. This confusion between the hypothesis (style-matched instruction is better) and the underlying value (teachers should be attentive to individual students) has created a defensive conflation that protects the theory from scrutiny.

**Educational CPD as a theory propagation mechanism.** Continuing professional development programs for teachers constitute one of the primary channels through which pedagogical theories reach classroom practice. Learning styles theory has been heavily marketed to teacher trainers and CPD providers, who have incorporated it into training programs, assessment frameworks, and classroom observation rubrics. Teachers who learn about learning styles in professional development have strong social and institutional incentives to apply and transmit it: applying it looks like best practice; ignoring it could be flagged in classroom observations. The institutional embedding of learning styles in CPD frameworks creates a form of **credentialed misinformation** — the theory acquires the authority of professional credentialing rather than scientific evidence, and practitioners who repeat it are acting in good institutional faith. Dekker and colleagues (2012), "Neuromyths in Education: Prevalence and Predictors of Misconceptions among Teachers," published in *Frontiers in Psychology*, found that teachers with more formal training in neuroscience-related topics were actually more likely to endorse some neuromyths — a finding that suggests professional training channels can propagate false beliefs rather than correct them, when the training content itself has not been epistemically audited.

**Multiple intelligences conflation.** Howard Gardner's theory of multiple intelligences, developed in *Frames of Mind* (1983), is a psychologically distinct theory from learning styles, but the two are repeatedly conflated in popular and professional educational discourse. Gardner proposed that intelligence was not a single general factor but a collection of relatively independent abilities — linguistic, logical-mathematical, musical, spatial, bodily-kinesthetic, interpersonal, intrapersonal. This theory has its own empirical problems, but it is not the same theory as VARK learning styles. The conflation is persistent because both frameworks emphasize individual differences, use overlapping vocabulary (both refer to "visual" and "kinesthetic" dimensions), and are deployed in similar educational contexts. Because multiple intelligences has somewhat more defenders in the psychological literature than VARK does, the conflation allows VARK proponents to borrow credibility from an adjacent, more defensible theory. Waterhouse (2006), "Multiple Intelligences, the Mozart Effect, and Emotional Intelligence: A Critical Review," published in *Educational Psychologist*, addressed this conflation explicitly and argued that neither theory met the empirical standard for practical application in schools.

## 4. Verdict

**False**

The meshing hypothesis — that learning is improved when instructional modality matches a student's learning style preference — is not supported by the empirical evidence. The comprehensive review by Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer, and Bjork (2008) in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, the review by Rogowsky, Calhoun, and Tallal (2015) in the *Journal of Educational Research*, and the critical analyses by Kirschner (2017) and Newton and Miah (2017) all reach the same conclusion: the specific interaction effect required to validate learning styles theory has not been demonstrated in adequately designed studies.

Students do have genuine preferences about instructional modality; those preferences are real as subjective experiences. What they do not do is predict differential learning outcomes when instruction is modality-matched versus non-matched. Teachers may serve students best not by categorizing them into learning style groups and differentiating instruction accordingly, but by using instructional strategies with strong evidence bases (distributed practice, retrieval practice, worked examples, formative feedback) and presenting content in modes appropriate to the content's inherent structure.

The verdict is sharpened by the cost of the false belief: educational resources spent on learning styles assessment, CPD programs, and curriculum differentiation based on unvalidated style categories represent opportunity costs for evidence-based instructional investment.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The learning styles neuromyth is perhaps the most thoroughly documented case of an evidence-free educational theory achieving massive real-world uptake and resisting correction. Its institutional penetration is deeper than that of other neuromyths in part because of its alignment with genuine values (respect for individual students, personalized instruction) and in part because of the commercial apparatus built around its assessment instruments. The VARK questionnaire itself is commercially licensed; Kolb's Learning Style Inventory has been administered to millions; and numerous competing assessment tools have generated revenue for their developers and CPD providers. The commercial incentive structure for sustaining the belief is not as stark as in the pharmaceutical case (chemical imbalance and SSRIs), but it is not trivial.

The broader field of educational neuroscience — or "neuroeducation" — is characterized by a persistent tension between the knowledge generated by cognitive neuroscience and the pace at which that knowledge is translated into practice. Howard-Jones (2014), "Neuroscience and Education: Myths and Messages," published in *Nature Reviews Neuroscience*, identified learning styles as one of several neuromyths that had successfully colonized educational practice and examined the structural reasons why: cognitive science produces findings that are conditional, moderated, and dependent on specific conditions; educational consumers need actionable prescriptions that can be applied in real-time classroom settings; the translation from conditional finding to unconditional prescription strips the very caveats that make the science honest. VARK emerged from a genuine observation (students differ in how they represent information) and a genuine value (teachers should be responsive to learners), but the translation into a prescriptive typology with commercial assessment instruments stripped the caveats and produced a myth.

Hattie's (2009) synthesis in *Visible Learning: A Synthesis of Over 800 Meta-Analyses Relating to Achievement* (Routledge) is relevant context. Hattie synthesized the evidence base for hundreds of educational interventions and found that learning styles had one of the lower effect sizes. By contrast, teacher feedback, spaced practice, and student self-regulation strategies showed substantially higher effects. If the question is not "does VARK work" but "what educational investments produce the most learning per dollar," the learning styles literature points strongly away from style categorization and toward a set of generic instructional practices that apply across student populations without requiring style assessment. The tragedy is not that learning styles is false while everything else is equally false — it is that the resources devoted to learning styles have real opportunity costs relative to genuinely effective instructional practices.

The persistence of learning styles also raises questions about the evidence literacy of the education policy ecosystem. Unlike pharmaceuticals, educational interventions are not subject to rigorous regulatory approval before adoption. A drug requires clinical trial evidence before it can be marketed as an effective treatment; an educational assessment instrument or CPD program requires no comparable empirical validation. This regulatory asymmetry means that evidence-free educational theories can achieve practitioner-level adoption without the formal empirical gating that applies in medicine. Goldacre (2013), "Building Evidence into Education" (UK Department of Education), argued that education needed a framework more analogous to evidence-based medicine, with randomized controlled trials, pre-registration, and systematic review of educational interventions before large-scale adoption. The learning styles case is one of his primary examples.

There are signs of institutional reckoning. The Society for Neuroscience, the Association for Psychological Science, and the British Psychological Society have all issued statements questioning the evidence base for learning styles instruction. In 2018, a letter to *The Guardian* signed by thirty educational neuroscience researchers explicitly called for learning styles to be removed from teacher training, noting that "the belief in the importance of learning styles is remarkably persistent" despite the evidence. The UK's Education Endowment Foundation, which produces evidence reviews for educational practice, assigns learning styles theory low evidence quality and does not recommend it. Whether these institutional corrections will achieve the same cultural penetration as several decades of CPD propagation is an open question, but the scientific consensus is clearer than it has ever been.

The learning styles story ultimately illustrates a general pattern in the sociology of educational belief: theories with high emotional resonance and institutional inertia are not effectively corrected by evidence alone. Correction requires institutional disembedding — changing CPD programs, teacher training curricula, observation frameworks, and assessment standards to remove learning styles as a category. Some of this disembedding has occurred; much has not. Teaching practitioners who were trained to believe in learning styles, who have built lesson plans around the category, and who receive collegially validated reinforcement from colleagues with the same training, have little automatic mechanism for updating toward the scientific consensus. The information correction must meet the social and institutional context in which the false belief is embedded — a standard that publication in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, however methodologically rigorous, cannot on its own achieve.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Learning styles presents a different challenge to fact-checkers than most claims in the Truth Vault. There is no single viral artifact — no fabricated quote, no misrepresented statistic — that can be adjudicated in a conventional fact-check format. The claim is diffuse, institutionalized, and held by a professional community whose members are not making claims on social media but teaching in classrooms, writing CPD materials, and designing curriculum frameworks. This makes it a target for science journalism and educational research communication rather than for traditional reactive fact-checking.

Snopes has not addressed learning styles directly. Coverage by science-aware journalism — articles in *The Atlantic*, *Science of Us in New York Magazine*, and *The Guardian* education desk — has generally been accurate: these outlets have covered the Pashler et al. (2008) review and subsequent confirmatory work and reached correct conclusions about the evidence. The limitation is reach: these corrective articles circulate among readers already engaged with science journalism, while the false belief circulates in teacher training settings, CPD sessions, and school leadership contexts that are not the primary audiences for *The Atlantic* science coverage.

The most effective fact-checking approach for institutionalized educational myths is not the standard article format but the evidence review genre: systematic summaries aimed at institutional decision-makers that compare the learning-styles claims to the actual evidence literature and recommend policy responses. The Evidence for Learning frameworks (Australia), the Education Endowment Foundation toolkit (UK), and Hattie's meta-analytic synthesis play this role more effectively than conventional fact-checks, precisely because they are designed for the institutional audiences who maintain the myth in practice.

There is a secondary claim embedded in the broader learning styles apparatus that deserves specific attention: the claim that VARK and similar instruments are valid psychometric measures of a stable trait. Numerous studies have found that students categorized by one instrument frequently receive different categorizations on other instruments, and that the same instrument produces unstable categorizations over time. Papadatou-Pastou and colleagues (2018), "The Sizes and Signs of Mental Rotation Differences Between Males and Females: What Is the Role of Academic Discipline?" published in *Neuroscience and Biobehavioral Reviews*, found in related work on cognitive style that the psychometric properties of style instruments were typically poor. A fact-check that targeted the validity claims of specific learning styles instruments — "does this questionnaire actually measure a stable property of the learner" — would find clear evidence of instrument invalidity independent of whether style-matched instruction works. It is a potentially more tractable target than the broader causal claim.
