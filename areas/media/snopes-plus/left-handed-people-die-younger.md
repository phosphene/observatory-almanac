# Left-Handed People Die Younger Than Right-Handed People

## 1. The Claim

In 1991, psychologists Stanley Coren and Diane Halpern published a study in the prestigious *New England Journal of Medicine* claiming that left-handed people died an average of 9 years earlier than right-handed people. According to their research, right-handed individuals lived to an average age of 75, while left-handed people died at an average age of 66.

The study gained massive media attention and entered popular consciousness as a striking example of how biological differences might affect longevity. The finding seemed to suggest that handedness — a fundamental aspect of human neurological organization — carried significant mortality implications.

Coren and Halpern's research was based on data collected from next-of-kin reporting the handedness of deceased individuals. They contacted families of people who had recently died and asked whether their deceased relative had been left-handed or right-handed. From this data, they calculated average lifespans for each group and found the dramatic 9-year difference.

The study appeared to provide scientific backing for long-standing cultural superstitions about left-handedness. Throughout history, left-handedness has been associated with negative connotations — the Latin word "sinister" originally meant "left" — and this research seemed to offer modern scientific evidence that left-handedness was indeed associated with disadvantage, albeit mortality rather than moral character.

The findings were widely reported in newspapers, magazines, and television news programs. The stark numerical difference — 9 years — was both easy to understand and alarming enough to capture public attention. For left-handed individuals, the study suggested they might face significantly shortened lifespans simply due to their neurological wiring.

## 2. What's Actually True

The 1991 Coren and Halpern study was fundamentally flawed and has been thoroughly debunked by subsequent research. The dramatic 9-year lifespan difference they reported was an artifact of methodological errors, particularly birth cohort bias, rather than a real biological phenomenon.

The critical flaw in their methodology was relying on next-of-kin reports about deceased individuals' handedness without accounting for historical changes in social treatment of left-handedness. Throughout much of the 20th century, left-handed children were routinely forced to switch to using their right hands in schools, at home, and in social settings. This practice was most common in earlier generations and gradually declined over time.

This created a systematic bias in the data: older people were much more likely to have been forced to switch from left-handed to right-handed behavior during childhood, meaning they would be reported as "right-handed" by family members even though they were naturally left-handed. Younger people, by contrast, were more likely to have been allowed to remain left-handed throughout their lives.

The result was that the pool of people identified as "left-handed" in death records was artificially skewed toward younger individuals, not because left-handedness caused early death, but because older left-handed people had been systematically misclassified as right-handed due to forced switching in childhood.

Subsequent longitudinal studies that followed living people over time, rather than relying on retrospective family reports, found no significant difference in lifespan between left-handed and right-handed individuals. These prospective studies, which tracked people from youth into old age, eliminated the birth cohort bias that had contaminated the original research.

A comprehensive analysis by Martin et al. (1993) reexamined Coren and Halpern's data and demonstrated how birth cohort effects could completely explain the observed mortality difference. They showed that when you account for changing social practices around handedness across generations, the apparent lifespan difference disappears.

Large-scale population studies conducted since the 1990s have consistently found no meaningful association between handedness and mortality. Research following tens of thousands of individuals for decades has failed to replicate the dramatic findings of the original study.

Modern research does suggest some small statistical associations between left-handedness and certain health conditions, but these associations are modest and don't translate into significant lifespan differences. For example, left-handed people show slightly higher rates of some autoimmune conditions and slightly lower rates of others, but these differences are too small to create measurable mortality impacts.

The scientific consensus today is clear: handedness does not significantly affect lifespan. Left-handed people can expect to live just as long as right-handed people, and the 1991 study's findings represent one of the most striking examples of how methodological flaws can produce dramatic but entirely spurious results.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The left-handedness mortality claim gained traction because it appeared to provide scientific validation for existing cultural anxieties and stereotypes about left-handedness. Throughout history, left-handed people have faced discrimination and suspicion, so a study suggesting biological disadvantage resonated with pre-existing biases.

The research came from respected institutions and was published in one of the world's most prestigious medical journals. The *New England Journal of Medicine*'s reputation lent credibility to findings that might have been more skeptically received if published in a lesser-known venue. This demonstrates how institutional authority can amplify even flawed research.

The study's numerical precision — exactly 9 years difference — made it particularly memorable and quotable. Specific statistics feel more authoritative than vague claims, and the round number made it easy to remember and repeat. Media coverage emphasized this stark numerical difference rather than examining the methodology that produced it.

For some people, the finding confirmed suspicions that left-handedness represented a biological abnormality or disadvantage. This interpretation aligned with historical prejudices and provided seemingly objective justification for treating left-handedness as undesirable.

The study also appealed to broader fascination with biological determinism — the idea that fundamental aspects of our biology shape major life outcomes. The notion that something as basic as handedness could predict lifespan fit into popular narratives about genetic destiny and biological fate.

Media coverage of the research was generally uncritical, focusing on the dramatic findings rather than examining the methodology. Journalists typically lack the statistical training necessary to identify problems with research design, and the study's publication in a prestigious journal discouraged skeptical scrutiny.

The finding spread through popular culture, appearing in trivia books, conversation starters, and general knowledge collections. Once established in popular consciousness, it became self-perpetuating, with each repetition lending it additional apparent credibility.

Some left-handed individuals embraced the finding as evidence of their uniqueness or as explanation for perceived disadvantages they'd experienced. This created emotional investment in the claim that made subsequent debunking less psychologically acceptable.

The persistence of belief in this debunked finding also reflects broader problems with scientific literacy and understanding of research methodology. Many people lack the background necessary to evaluate statistical claims or identify methodological problems, making them vulnerable to accepting flawed research if it comes from authoritative sources.

## 4. Verdict

**Status: DEFINITIVELY FALSE** — The claim that left-handed people die 9 years younger than right-handed people is based on fundamentally flawed research and has been thoroughly debunked by subsequent studies.

The evidence against this claim is overwhelming and multifaceted:

**Methodological critique**: The original study's reliance on next-of-kin reporting without accounting for historical changes in handedness treatment created systematic bias that entirely explains the observed difference.

**Replication failure**: Multiple attempts to replicate the findings using more rigorous methodologies have failed to find any significant mortality differences between handedness groups.

**Longitudinal evidence**: Prospective studies following people over decades show no meaningful lifespan differences between left-handed and right-handed individuals.

**Mechanism absence**: No plausible biological mechanism has been identified that would cause handedness to significantly affect mortality, and proposed explanations have not withstood scientific scrutiny.

The original Coren and Halpern study represents a textbook case of how methodological flaws can produce dramatic but entirely spurious findings. The birth cohort bias in their data was so severe that it created an apparent 9-year lifespan difference where none actually existed.

This case demonstrates the importance of research methodology in producing reliable scientific knowledge. Even when conducted by competent researchers and published in prestigious journals, studies can produce completely incorrect conclusions if their methodological approach contains fundamental flaws.

The scientific community's response to this flawed research — rapid identification of methodological problems, replication attempts, and eventual consensus rejection — illustrates how the scientific process works to correct errors, even when initial findings receive widespread attention and acceptance.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The left-handedness mortality myth reveals important dynamics in how scientific misinformation spreads and persists, particularly when research findings intersect with cultural prejudices and institutional authority. This case demonstrates how even prestigious publication venues can amplify flawed research when peer review fails to catch methodological problems.

The study's initial acceptance and widespread media coverage illustrate the challenge of post-publication error correction in science. Once findings are published and publicized, correcting them requires additional research, publication of critiques, and sustained effort to communicate corrections — a process that often fails to achieve the reach of the original, incorrect findings.

This case also highlights how historical context affects research interpretation. The changing social treatment of left-handedness across generations created confounding variables that the original researchers failed to consider. This demonstrates the importance of understanding social and historical factors when interpreting biological or psychological research.

The persistence of belief in debunked findings reflects broader challenges in scientific communication and education. The general public often lacks the methodological knowledge necessary to evaluate research claims, making them dependent on institutional authority and media interpretation rather than independent assessment of evidence quality.

From a statistical perspective, this case serves as an excellent example of how confounding variables and selection bias can produce dramatic but entirely spurious associations. It's frequently used in statistics and research methodology courses to illustrate the importance of careful study design and the dangers of drawing causal conclusions from correlational data.

The left-handedness study also connects to broader questions about biological determinism and the tendency to seek biological explanations for complex social phenomena. The appeal of simple biological explanations for life outcomes often overrides careful consideration of alternative explanations and methodological limitations.

The case demonstrates how scientific authority can both spread misinformation and eventually correct it. While the prestigious journal publication initially lent credibility to flawed findings, the scientific community's eventual consensus against those findings also carries authority that supports the correction.

This example illustrates the importance of distinguishing between statistical associations and causal relationships. Even if left-handed people had shown different mortality patterns in the data, this wouldn't necessarily indicate that handedness causes mortality differences — other factors could explain the association.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Traditional fact-checking organizations have rarely addressed the left-handedness mortality claim, despite its persistence in popular culture and occasional resurfacing in social media. This absence reflects fact-checkers' typical focus on political claims and current controversies rather than persistent scientific misconceptions rooted in academic literature.

When the claim does receive attention from verification organizations, it's usually in the context of broader "myth-busting" articles rather than dedicated fact-checks. These treatments often correctly identify the claim as false but may not provide sufficient detail about the methodological problems that created the spurious findings.

Science journalism has been more effective at addressing this particular misconception, with several publications producing detailed explanations of why the original study was flawed. However, these corrections typically reach scientifically interested audiences rather than the general public who might encounter the original claim through casual conversation or social media.

Academic journals have published numerous critiques and corrections of the original research, but these remain largely inaccessible to general audiences. The scientific literature contains clear refutations of the handedness-mortality association, but translating this academic consensus into public understanding requires additional communication efforts.

Popular science writers and skeptical organizations have addressed the left-handedness myth more consistently than traditional fact-checkers. Writers like Ben Goldacre and organizations like the Committee for Skeptical Inquiry have included this case in broader discussions of scientific methodology and common misconceptions.

The periodic resurfacing of this claim on social media platforms creates opportunities for real-time correction, but these corrections often fail to achieve the reach of the original, more sensational claim. The asymmetry between misinformation spread and correction effectiveness remains a persistent challenge.

Educational materials in statistics and research methods courses frequently use this case as an example of methodological problems, potentially reaching future researchers, journalists, and informed citizens who might be better equipped to identify and resist similar flawed claims.

Professional medical organizations have occasionally issued statements clarifying that handedness doesn not affect mortality, but these corrections typically target healthcare providers rather than general audiences. The medical community's consensus against the original findings is clear, but translating this professional knowledge into public understanding requires additional effort.

The left-handedness case demonstrates how different types of scientific misinformation require different correction strategies. While viral false claims might be addressed through rapid fact-checking, persistent misconceptions rooted in published research require sustained educational efforts and ongoing vigilance against resurging incorrect information.

The relative success of scientific community corrections in this case — most scientists and science-literate individuals now recognize the original study as flawed — suggests that systematic debunking can be effective when the scientific consensus is clear and the methodological problems are well-understood. However, translating this expert consensus into broader public understanding remains an ongoing challenge that requires continued communication efforts across multiple platforms and formats.
