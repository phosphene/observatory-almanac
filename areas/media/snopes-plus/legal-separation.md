## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In September 2010, a widely shared email and social media post presented readers with a viral description and photograph of mechanically separated chicken (MSC). The text described a product created by pressing the entirety of a chicken carcass — "bones, eyes, guts, and all" — through a sieve, producing a pink, paste-like substance. It further claimed that this product is treated with ammonia because it is "crawling with bacteria," then artificially re-flavored and artificially re-colored before being used in chicken nuggets and frozen chicken products. The implied conclusion was that consumers are unknowingly eating a disgusting, chemically-treated industrial byproduct every time they eat fast food chicken.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2012) evaluated the claim and assigned it the verdict **Mixture** — meaning that some significant elements are true, some are substantially exaggerated or false, and some are accurate in a technical sense but misleading in context. The overall picture painted by the email is more alarming than the factual reality warrants, but the underlying subject matter is real and the regulatory, safety, and labeling environment around mechanically separated meat and lean finely textured beef is genuinely complex.

**What is true:** Mechanically separated poultry (MSP) and mechanically separated meat (MSM) are real industrial processes. They produce paste-like products used in cheaper meat products including hot dogs. They involve forcing bones with attached meat through pressure extrusion or sieves. The products may have altered texture, color, and flavor compared to whole-cut meat. Beef-based mechanically separated meat is banned from human food in the U.S. (as of 2004). Lean finely textured beef (LFTB), colloquially called "pink slime," is a distinct product that has been treated with ammonium hydroxide gas.

**What is false or exaggerated:** MSP does not involve grinding "bones, eyes, guts, and all" into an undifferentiated mass of the whole animal. It uses carcass material after primary butchering, not intact whole animals including organs. MSP and MSM are not routinely soaked in ammonia; ammonium hydroxide treatment as an anti-microbial measure is specifically associated with LFTB (lean beef trimmings). The photograph that circulated with the email depicted soft-serve ice cream machinery, not MSP/MSM production. McDonald's Chicken McNuggets specifically have not been made with MSP since 2003 (well before the 2010 email circulated), when the company switched to all white meat.

## §2 The Science of Mechanical Separation: Process, Products, and Regulation

Mechanically separated meat (MSM) and mechanically separated poultry (MSP) are defined product categories in U.S. federal food law with distinct regulatory histories. Understanding the actual process — as opposed to the email's grotesque characterization — is essential to accurate assessment.

The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) defines MSP as "a paste-like and batter-like poultry product produced by forcing bones, with attached edible tissue, through a sieve or similar device under high pressure to separate bone from the edible tissue." The critical phrase is "bones, with attached edible tissue" — meaning the process operates on skeletal material after primary butchering, not on intact animal carcasses including visceral organs. Eyes, digestive organs, intestinal content, and undesirable tissues are removed in prior processing steps; MSP uses the back, neck, and rib cage bones that retain residual meat after conventional cuts have been removed. The resulting product is indeed paste-like; the pressure extrusion causes cellular damage and release of marrow, which alters the texture significantly from intact meat.

The recovery of edible tissue from bones that would otherwise be discarded as waste has a straightforward economic and environmental logic. Prior to the development of mechanical separation technology in the 1960s — as Mikkelson (2012) notes — significant quantities of residual meat adhered to bones and were discarded. Mechanical recovery reduces waste in the production chain. The ethical judgment of whether this is desirable is a separate question from whether the product is (a) what the email claims it is and (b) regulatory safe.

On the regulatory record, the FSIS's 1995 final rule established that MSP is safe for human consumption and may be used without restriction, but must be labeled as "mechanically separated chicken" or "mechanically separated turkey" in the product's ingredient list (FSIS, *Federal Register*, Vol. 60, No. 23, 6 February 1995). This labeling requirement provides transparency for informed consumer choice.

For MSM derived from beef, the regulatory history diverged sharply from poultry after the emergence of Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy (BSE, popularly called "mad cow disease") in the United Kingdom and subsequent identification of variant Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease (vCJD) in humans who had consumed BSE-affected beef products. The BSE prion abnormally accumulates in neural tissue; mechanically separated beef was considered high-risk because bone marrow and neural-adjacent tissue could be included in the product. The FSIS enacted regulations in 2004 that defined mechanically separated beef as "inedible" and prohibited its use in human food products (FSIS, Enhanced Measures to Protect Against Bovine Spongiform Encephalopathy, *Federal Register*, Vol. 69, No. 4, 7 January 2004). This is not a consumer safety failure but rather a regulatory system functioning as intended — identifying elevated risk and responding with restriction.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Disgust Narrative as a Regulatory Bypass Mechanism

The description of mechanically separated chicken as "bones, eyes, guts, and all" represents a specific rhetorical and epistemic structure that deserves close examination. Rather than misrepresenting the product's safety record or regulatory status — both of which are complicated and in some cases legitimately concerning — the claim's primary mechanism is to trigger a pre-cognitive disgust response that bypasses rational evaluation of the regulatory and safety evidence.

Rozin, Haidt, and McCauley (1993, *Psychological Review*, vol. 100, no. 4) established the theoretical framework for disgust as an adaptive behavior system that evolved to regulate food choice in environments where visible cues of decomposition, contamination, or inappropriate category membership (such as animal-origin materials identified with viscera or excretion) reliably predicted danger. The critical feature of this regulatory system from an epistemic standpoint is that it is *pre-rational* — the disgust evaluation precedes and tends to override analytical deliberation. Fessler and Haley (2006, *Social Behavior*, vol. 21, no. 3) confirmed experimentally that disgust-eliciting descriptions of products lower purchasing intentions and increase risk perception even when explicitly presented information indicates the product is safe.

The email's rhetorical construction — "bones, eyes, guts, and all" — specifically activates what Rozin (1987, *Learning and Motivation*, vol. 18, no. 2) called the "contamination heuristic": the perception that contact with a disgusting substance renders the resulting product disgusting regardless of any transformation or removal. Even if a reader were told that chicken nuggets are made through a hygienic, regulated process that excludes eyes and intestinal material, the activation of the contamination frame by the vivid image of whole-organism processing tends to persist and to color evaluation of subsequent factual information.

The epistemic novelty here is that this disgust-activation mechanism constitutes what might be called a *narrative regulatory bypass*: it achieves a consumer behavior outcome (avoidance of MSP products) through the manipulation of a pre-rational psychological system rather than through accurate information about the product's actual safety profile or labeling status. This is consequential because the real regulatory and safety issues in the MSP/LFTB space — the ammonia treatment applied to LFTB, the BSE-based ban on mechanically separated beef, the absence of LFTB labeling requirements, the taste test evidence of quality differentials — are *genuine* issues that warrant consumer and regulatory attention. By substituting a false and disgust-maximizing narrative for the actual, more complicated story, the viral email potentially displaced more targeted and accurate consumer advocacy with generalized revulsion that is not well calibrated to specific actual risks.

This displacement mechanism is discussed in the context of risk communication by Sandman (1987, *Proceedings of the 87th Annual Meeting, Air Pollution Control Association*), who coined the term "outrage factor" to describe the systematic observation that public risk responses correlate more strongly with factors that generate moral indignation (dread, inequity, involuntary exposure, lack of familiarity) than with technical risk magnitude. The MSP email maximizes outrage factors — involuntary exposure ("you're eating this without knowing it"), industrial opacity ("they don't tell you"), physical disgust ("bones and eyes") — rather than calibrating to actual harm probability.

## §4 Lean Finely Textured Beef ("Pink Slime"): A Distinct Product with Distinct Regulatory Issues

Lean finely textured beef (LFTB), pejoratively called "pink slime" in media coverage beginning with a 2009 *New York Times* article by Michael Moss, is frequently conflated with mechanically separated meat in popular discourse. It is a distinct product with distinct manufacturing process, distinct regulatory status, and distinct safety profile.

LFTB is produced from beef trimmings — the fatty remnants left after whole cuts are removed from a carcass — by centrifugal separation of fat from lean tissue. The resulting lean beef is then squeezed through tubes and briefly exposed to a fine ammonia gas mist (producing ammonium hydroxide in contact with moisture), which serves as an anti-microbial intervention targeting pathogens, particularly *E. coli* O157:H7. Ammonia is classified as GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) by the FDA. The USDA's Economic Research Service has noted that LFTB can constitute up to 25% of the final blend in ground beef products sold at retail and used in institutional food service.

The legitimate consumer concerns about LFTB center on three issues. First, **labeling**: unlike MSP, LFTB is not required to be disclosed on product labels. American beef consumers who see "ground beef" or "100% beef" on packaging have no regulatory mandate requiring disclosure of LFTB content. This is a genuine transparency gap that was debated — and in some states acted upon — in the period following the 2012 media coverage. Major retailers including Kroger, Safeway, Supervalu, Bi-Lo, and Winn-Dixie voluntarily ceased purchasing LFTB-containing products in March 2012 following consumer pressure (Simpson, *Reuters*, 22 March 2012), and fast-food chains including McDonald's and Burger King announced cessation of LFTB use in February 2012 (Johnson, MSNBC, 31 January 2012). Second, **quality**: J.M. Hirsch (2012, *The Huffington Post*, 16 March 2012) conducted a side-by-side sensory comparison of LFTB-containing and non-LFTB hamburgers and found the LFTB burger to be "flat," "one-dimensional," and "mealy," with fewer detectable juices. This is consistent with the structural characteristic of LFTB: it is composed largely of connective tissue and lean tissue from trimming areas close to the hide, which have higher collagen content and lower myofibrillar protein density than interior cuts. Third, **sourcing proximity to fecal contamination**: Moss (2009, *New York Times*, 30 December 2009) and Shin (2008, *Washington Post*, 12 June 2008) documented that trim from areas near the hide and peritoneum has higher exposure to fecal contamination than interior cuts, which is precisely why the ammonium hydroxide anti-microbial treatment was developed as a processing control. The American Meat Institute disputes characterizations of LFTB as sub-standard, arguing that the end product is nutritious lean beef regardless of starting material, and that the process is analogous to cream separation from milk.

## §5 Fast Food: The McDonald's McNugget Exception

A specific and factually significant component of the email is the assertion that "all fast-food chicken" is made from MSP. This assertion was already false when the email circulated in 2010.

McDonald's Chicken McNuggets, the iconic fast food chicken product most associated in popular culture with processed chicken, transitioned away from mechanically separated poultry in 2003. A McDonald's spokesperson confirmed to multiple media outlets that since 2003, Chicken McNuggets have been made with USDA-inspected boneless white meat chicken. This transition predates the viral email by seven years and had been publicly documented, including in McDonald's own promotional materials. Mikkelson (2012) specifically noted this fact in the Snopes analysis.

This matters epistemically because the email used McDonald's Chicken McNuggets as the foremost example of MSP deployment in fast food, yet this assertion had been false for nearly a decade at the point of maximum circulation. The longevity of factual errors in viral food claims is documented extensively in the food misinformation literature; once a food product is associated with a contamination or disgust narrative in the popular imagination, the association tends to persist long after the underlying factual predicate has changed (see Smith, 2006, *Food Scares, Culture and Power*, in *Sociology of Health and Illness*, vol. 28, no. 4).

Hot dogs present a genuinely mixed case with respect to MSP: under current USDA regulations, hot dogs may contain unlimited amounts of mechanically separated chicken or turkey, provided the MSC/MST is labeled as such in the ingredient list. Hot dogs that are labeled as "all beef" or "premium beef" are by definition not MSP-containing, but lower-price options may contain MSP in addition to other meat materials. The regulatory framework therefore provides labeling transparency — a consumer who reads ingredient labels can determine whether MSP is present — but this requires active label reading and knowledge of what ingredient terms to look for.

## §6 Conclusion: Accurate Disgust, Inaccurate Details, and the Value of Precision in Food Advocacy

The mechanical separation email exemplifies an important tension in food system advocacy: the use of emotionally compelling but factually inaccurate content to achieve legitimate consumer policy objectives. Some of the policy objectives advanced by opposition to MSP and LFTB are defensible: greater transparency in labeling, clearer consumer disclosure of processing methods, and quality standards in institutional food procurement are all reasonable advocacy positions. But the path to these objectives via a false and disgust-maximizing narrative creates several epistemic harms.

First, corrections to the false specific claims (McNuggets contain whole white meat; MSP does not include eyes and guts; ammonia soap does not saturate MSP) tend to be read as institutional defense of the entire industrial meat processing system, reducing the salience of the genuine regulatory concerns that exist. Second, once a claim is established as factually mixture/false, partisans on both sides overextend: advocates for the meat processing industry use the fact-checking of the exaggerated claims to discredit all concern about MSP and LFTB, while critics overextend the valid transparency concerns to all chicken products regardless of actual composition.

Alexander (2009, *Charlotte Observer*, 23 June 2009) documented chemical safety concerns specifically at poultry processing facilities relating to ammonia and other chemical handling — a genuine occupational and environmental safety issue that is substantially more accurate than the email's depiction. The accurate story is that industrialized meat processing involves real regulatory complexity, genuine transparency gaps in labeling (specific to LFTB), and a historical record that includes legitimate food safety governance responses (the 2004 MSM beef ban). That story is adequate to support informed consumer advocacy without recourse to false descriptions of production processes.

The Snopes verdict of Mixture captures this accurately: some is true, some important, some false, some misleading. The epistemic discipline required is to hold those categories distinct rather than collapsing them into either total endorsement or total dismissal.
