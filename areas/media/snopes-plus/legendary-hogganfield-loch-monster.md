## The Claim

In early May 2024, a photograph began circulating on social media platforms — primarily Threads, where it originated, and then TikTok, Facebook, Imgur, and iFunny — with a specific and richly detailed claim attached to it. A Threads post by the account @historicillustrations provided the framing: "The famous photo taken on this day in 1930 of 'Hoggie,' the legendary Hogganfield Loch monster which was finally captured after many years of folk doubting its existence."

The claim had multiple layered elements: that the photograph was historic, dating to 1930; that it depicted a real creature called "Hoggie"; that this creature was a "legendary" monster with an established folklore tradition attached to a specific Scottish loch; that its existence had been a matter of folk doubt over "many years"; and that the photograph represented definitive confirmation of this legend's underlying reality. Some resharing on platforms like 9GAG simplified the claim further, just captioning the image "Lochness Monster" and leveraging the more famous Loch Ness legend as implicit verification context.

Each of these layered claims is false. The photograph was not taken in 1930. There is no legend of "Hoggie" attaching to Hogganfield Loch with any documented pre-2023 existence. The image was not a photograph of a real creature. It was an AI-generated image created using artificial intelligence software and first shared online in October 2023 — months before its viral circulation as a supposed historical document.

## What's Actually True

The image is AI-generated. Snopes fact-checker Aleksandra Wrona established this through a convergence of evidence that is worth detailing in full, as it illustrates the current standard toolkit for synthetic media detection.

A TinEye reverse-image search established that the image's earliest traceable online appearance was in October 2023 (TinEye, accessed May 2024). This timeline is immediately incompatible with the claimed 1930 date: no authenticated 1930 photograph would have its first traceable digital appearance in October 2023 without some documented chain of digitisation, archive discovery, or publication history. The absence of any such chain is a strong preliminary indicator of fabrication.

Visual analysis of the image provided direct forensic evidence. When the photograph is zoomed in on the human subjects depicted — the people supposedly observing or capturing the "monster" — characteristic AI distortion becomes apparent in their facial features. AI image generators operating on diffusion-model architectures have a well-documented tendency to produce coherent facial structures at low resolution that become distorted, anatomically incorrect, or incoherent when examined at higher magnification. Fingers, ears, and background details are also frequently unstable. Wrona noted that "the more we zoomed in the photograph, the more evident it was that it was created using AI-powered software," with facial features blurred and deformed in patterns consistent with generative model artifacts.

Two AI detection tools were applied. The tool Is It AI? (isitai.com) indicated the image was "highly likely to have been created using artificial intelligence." More decisively, Hive Moderation's AI-generated content detection tool concluded with 100% certainty that the image was AI-generated (Hive Moderation, accessed May 2024). The Hive tool, which uses neural classifiers trained on large corpora of known authentic and synthetic imagery, was categorical in this assessment.

Beyond the authentication problem, Wrona's research established a second, arguably more important fact: there is no documented "Hoggie" legend attaching to Hogganfield Loch in any pre-2023 source that Snopes could locate. Hogganfield Loch is a real location — a 51-hectare urban park lake in the northeast of Glasgow — but it carries no tradition of monster sightings, no folklore tradition of a resident creature, no historical reports of "Hoggie" or any equivalent. "Nessie" — the Loch Ness Monster — has a centuries-long, densely documented legendary tradition, with the earliest modern-era sighting famously attributed to John Mackay in 1933 and the creature appearing in the context of older historical records (Witchell, N., 1989, *The Loch Ness Story*, Corgi Books). "Hoggie" has none of this. The viral claim did not merely fabricate a photograph; it appears to have fabricated the legend the photograph purported to illustrate.

## The Epistemic Architecture of Retroactive Myth

The Hogganfield Loch case is unusual among AI-generated image hoaxes in that the misinformation operates on two distinct epistemic registers simultaneously. The first register involves the standard mechanisms of AI image fabrication: a synthetic image is given false documentary weight through the assignment of a specific historical date, a named photographer-context, and a platform-specific retro-historical aesthetic that mimics early 20th-century photographic grain and contrast. This is the layer most commonly analysed in discussions of synthetic media.

The second register is less commonly examined but more epistemically significant: the fabrication of a legend to which the synthetic image is attached. The post did not claim to show an existing well-known cryptid — in which case audiences could at least cross-check the claim against the established folklore. It claimed to show a *localised* legend of which most viewers, even those familiar with Scottish folklore, would have no independent knowledge. This strategic epistemic positioning exploits the same mechanism that enables the fabrication of specialist-knowledge claims: viewers cannot disprove what they cannot verify, and the absence of contradictory knowledge is silently recruited as positive evidence.

This is what philosophers of testimony call *knowledge by acquaintance* failure. When a claim concerns something a viewer has direct experience of — the shape of their home city, the existence of a famous public figure — fabrications are immediately and easily detected. When a claim concerns something local, historical, and specific to a community the viewer does not belong to — a regional Scottish folklore tradition from 1930 — the viewer has no direct basis for disconfirmation. The hoax therefore exploits the limits of personal knowledge by targeting the precise category of claims most resistant to first-hand refutation.

The specific framing — "this day in 1930" from an account named @historicillustrations — activates what cognitive psychologists call the *source heuristic*: the tendency to assign higher credibility to content that *looks like* it comes from an authoritative, archive-oriented source (Metzger, M.J. & Flanagin, A.J., 2013, *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication*). An account whose name and documented posting history revolves around "historical illustrations" benefits from a genre-coherence cue: this is the kind of content that kind of account posts, so the claim is pre-consistent with the source's brand. The heuristic is correct in many cases — specialist sources are often reliable within their domain — but it fails when the specialist designation is itself constructed to exploit the heuristic.

The epistemic novelty of this case is the explicit mythology manufacture: not a hoax that falsely documents a real creature, but one that falsely constructs the creature's legendary status before documenting it. This inverts the standard cryptozoological evidence sequence (legend → claimed sighting → claimed photography). Here the sequence is: synthetic image → retroactive legend → viral spread with legend as context. The legend exists *because* the image claims it does.

## Why People Believe This

The Hogganfield Loch Monster image circulated extensively and generated substantial engagement before being debunked. The mechanisms that enabled its spread are worth examining, as they are partially distinct from those that drive simpler photographic hoaxes.

**The Nessie heuristic.** Scotland's association with lake monsters is culturally entrenched to a degree unusual even within the broader cryptozoology tradition. "Nessie" has been the subject of major newspaper coverage since 1933, multiple scientific investigations (including sonar surveys by the Loch Ness Investigation Bureau in the 1960s–70s), and at least one famous photograph later retracted as a hoax — the "Surgeon's Photograph" of 1934, which Robert Wilson claimed to have taken but which Marmaduke Wetherell confessed in 1994 to have fabricated. This cultural saturation means that "creature in a Scottish loch photographed in the 1930s" is not a claim pattern that triggers immediate implausibility — it is, if anything, a familiar trope. The Nessie context lends a genre familiarity that lowers critical thresholds.

**Vintage aesthetics and temporal distance.** AI image generators have become increasingly adept at simulating the visual characteristics of early 20th-century photography: the grain, the tonal compression, the contrast ratios, the depth of field characteristics of large-format glass plate cameras. These aesthetic markers function as authenticity cues that are difficult for non-specialist viewers to distinguish from genuine archival material. The temporal distance of "1930" additionally insulates the claim from easy verification: there are no living witnesses, no one expects to remember the event personally, and the absence of immediate personal evidence is compatible with the claim.

**Horror-adjacent engagement.** The image triggers a mild but real aesthetic of the uncanny — a creature lurking in dark water, surrounded by observers, photographed at close range in a period when such documentation technology was limited. This uncanny quality is engaging independent of credibility: the image is arresting because it resonates with a cultural template (the lake monster photograph) that has aesthetic power derived from decades of exposure to genuine mythology-adjacent material. Engagement and credibility are related but distinct; this image likely attracted clicks and shares from viewers who were aesthetically engaged regardless of whether they were fully convinced of the claim.

**Platform velocity.** The jump from Threads to TikTok to Facebook to Imgur and iFunny within a short window represents a platform cascade characteristic of content with high engagement potential. Each re-posting adds implicit credibility: "this is appearing on multiple platforms" functions as a distributed social proof signal. Platforms have different user demographics and moderation approaches; content that crosses multiple platforms appears to have passed multiple independent credibility filters, even when those filters are all equally absent or ineffective.

## Verdict

**Fake.** The image is AI-generated, confirmed by multiple independent detection methods including Hive Moderation's 100% certainty assessment and independent visual forensic analysis. The image's earliest traceable online appearance is October 2023, not the claimed date of 1930. The specific claim that "Hoggie" is a "legendary" Hogganfield Loch Monster with a documented pre-existing folklore tradition could not be verified: no such tradition has been found in any source predating the viral circulation of this image. The most parsimonious interpretation is that the legend itself was fabricated as context for the synthetic image.

## The Wider Picture

The Hogganfield Loch fake exists at the intersection of several important and rapidly evolving research areas: AI image generation forensics, the sociology of cryptozoology, and what media scholars are beginning to call "legend manufacturing" — the deliberate construction of pseudo-folkloric claim-contexts for synthetic content.

**AI image detection methodology.** The use of two independent detection tools — Is It AI? and Hive Moderation — reflects emerging best practice in AI image forensics. Sensity AI (now Hive Moderation) published benchmark evaluations in 2023 showing that ensemble detection approaches consistently outperform single-classifier methods, with lower false-negative rates on diffusion-model generated content (Korshunov, P. & Marcel, S., 2023, *Proceedings of the IEEE International Conference on Acoustics, Speech and Signal Processing*). The visual artifact approach — pixel-level analysis of faces and fine detail at high magnification — remains a valuable complement to neural classifiers, particularly for flagging content types underrepresented in classifier training data. Wrona's combination of both approaches in this fact-check exemplifies the current gold standard.

**Temporal evidence in image provenance.** The TinEye trace establishing October 2023 as the image's first appearance is a clean example of temporal evidence use in provenance determination. Genuine historical photographs that circulate online have chains of prior online appearances traceable through reverse-image search and archival databases: they appear in historical blogs, genealogy sites, newspaper digital archives, or museum collections long before they go viral. Fabricated "historical" images typically have no such chain; their digital birth is simultaneous with their claim. The absence of pre-October-2023 digital appearances constitutes strong negative evidence against the 1930 date attribution.

**The sociology of cryptozoology.** The Loch Ness Monster has been studied sociologically as well as scientifically. Bord, J. & Bord, C. (1980, *Alien Animals*, Granada Publishing) documented the role of the 1933–34 media cycle in crystallising and amplifying earlier, diffuse reports of strange animals in Loch Ness into a coherent "monster" tradition. More recently, Clark, J. (2012, *Unexplained! Strange Sightings, Incredible Occurrences, and Puzzling Physical Phenomena*, Visible Ink Press) traced how local legends become national and international phenomena through media amplification. The Hogganfield case is a digital-age inversion of this process: rather than organic local legend being amplified outward, a synthetic image constructed an artificial local legend for global viral distribution, explicitly referencing the Nessie template to provide genre-familiarity.

**The manufactured legend as misinformation category.** Academics studying computational propaganda have focused primarily on false claims about real events (disinformation), false claims that are genuinely believed by their originators (misinformation), and synthetic media depicting real people in false contexts (deepfakes). The Hogganfield case occupies a somewhat different space: a synthetic image generating a synthetic legend about a synthetic cryptid attributed to a real place. This category — call it *fabricated mythology* or *legend manufacturing* — deserves specific analytical attention because its refutation requires not just image authentication but also negative folklore research: establishing that a claimed legend does not exist in documented form. That is a different and in some ways harder task than showing that an image is synthetic, because the absence of evidence for a legend is not itself evidence of non-existence — it may simply indicate poor documentation, regional obscurity, or oral tradition that has not been transcribed. In this case the absence is striking enough to be informative, but the evidentiary standard for "there is no Hoggie legend" is genuinely more demanding than "this image is AI-generated."

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes' approach to the Hogganfield Loch claim is efficient and technically competent on the image authentication side, with one notable gap and one notable achievement.

**Tool convergence.** The use of TinEye for provenance dating, Is It AI? for initial classification, and Hive Moderation for high-confidence classification represents appropriate tool redundancy. No single AI detection tool should be treated as definitive given documented variance in cross-generator generalisation performance (Wang, S.Y. et al., 2020, *Proceedings of the IEEE/CVF Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition*). The convergence of multiple independent tools on a "fake" verdict substantially reduces the risk of false positive detection. Combined with the visual forensic analysis — the face-distortion evidence — the authentication case is robust.

**The legend gap.** Wrona's observation that no "Hoggie" legend was findable in any pre-2023 source is the genuinely novel contribution of this fact-check beyond standard AI image authentication. It shifts the analysis from "was this image genuine?" to "was the claim-context genuine?" — a more demanding and more illuminating question. However, the fact-check appropriately notes the absence without claiming definitive proof: the phrasing "we weren't able to find any evidence" is honest about the evidentiary status of negative search results.

**What the check does not address.** The fact-check does not investigate the @historicillustrations account more broadly — its posting history, evident purpose, or whether it is an AI-content farm operating across multiple platforms. This investigation, while beyond the scope of a single fact-check, would be valuable for understanding the information environment in which this fabrication circulated. Accounts dedicated to producing attractive historical-seeming content are an underanalysed category of synthetic media source; their content is often not politically motivated (unlike disinformation operations) but can achieve substantial viral reach through the aesthetic power of faux-archival aesthetics.

The Hogganfield Loch Monster is a case study in the limits of collective memory as a verification mechanism. Nobody remembered "Hoggie" because there was nothing to remember. The image is a sharp illustration of a lesson becoming increasingly urgent in the AI era: the absence of personal counter-evidence is not confirmation of a claim. Legends that nobody has heard of deserve precisely the scepticism that legends everybody has heard of have largely escaped.
