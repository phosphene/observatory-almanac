## The Claim

In October 2021, a song called "Let's Go Brandon" — a coded rallying cry expressing antipathy toward President Joe Biden — reached the top of the iTunes digital song sales chart. Rapper Loza Alexander first achieved this position on or around October 16, 2021; his version was later displaced near the top of the chart by a version released by Bryson Gray. The accomplishment was real and documented. But the claims that rapidly attached themselves to this fact were not.

On social media and across conservative news outlets, the chart performance was reported, amplified, and reframed in increasingly expansive terms. Multiple commentators, news aggregators, and prominent social media accounts declared that "Let's Go Brandon" was the number-one song in America. Others crossed an additional line and claimed it was the number-one song on Apple Music. Screenshots of the iTunes chart circulated alongside these assertions, positioned as evidential support for the broader claim. The clear implication was that the song had captured a cross-cutting, majority-of-mainstream-America kind of popularity — the sort of cultural saturation that "number one" has historically implied.

These claims were false. The song was not the number-one song in America by any credible cross-platform measure. It was not number one on Apple Music. It was number one on a specific sub-metric — the iTunes digital song sales chart — that, by 2021, measured a small and rapidly shrinking slice of music consumption. Understanding exactly why this distinction matters, and why it was so easily blurred in the telling, requires understanding the structural transformation that has overtaken the recorded music industry over the previous decade.

The broader claim also elided an important qualifier: several different artists released songs titled "Let's Go Brandon," and the chart performances of these various versions were frequently conflated. Loza Alexander's version and Bryson Gray's version were two distinct recordings that each charted at different points. The excitement surrounding "Let's Go Brandon" was real and measurable. The specific claims made to characterise that excitement exceeded what the evidence supported.

## What's Actually True

The core factual anchor is straightforward: Loza Alexander's "Let's Go Brandon" did reach number one on the iTunes chart for digital song sales, and separately, Bryson Gray's version achieved a similar position. These were genuine chart placements, verifiable through the iTunes store itself, and Fox News reported Loza Alexander's achievement on October 16, 2021. The songs also charted on the Billboard platform, with Alexander's version debuting at number 45 on the Billboard Hot 100 — a real accomplishment for an independent artist. That same release topped two specialised Billboard charts: "Rap Digital Song Sales" and "R&B/Hip-Hop Digital Song Sales."

Billboard reported (via its chart dated October 30, 2021) that Alexander's song accumulated 1.2 million US streams and 35,200 downloads sold during its first week of tracking. These are real numbers representing a genuinely notable debut for an independent political recording.

Simultaneously, Adele's "Easy on Me" was the unambiguous number-one song in America by every cross-platform measure. According to Billboard, "Easy on Me" during the same peak period generated 65 million radio airplay audience impressions, 53.9 million US streams, and 74,000 downloads — outpacing "Let's Go Brandon" by roughly 50 million streams and more than 40,000 downloads in that first tracking week. "Easy on Me" was number one on the Billboard Hot 100, the industry's standard composite chart incorporating streams, digital sales, and radio airplay, normalised to reflect national consumption patterns. No version of "Let's Go Brandon" was in Apple Music's top 100 songs at the relevant time, because Apple Music is a streaming service with entirely separate charting from the iTunes download store.

The Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA) data, drawn from its US Sales Database, provides the structural context: digital single sales fell from approximately 1.4 billion in 2012 to just 257 million in 2020 — an 80% collapse over eight years. By 2021, iTunes digital song purchasing was a vestigial behaviour primarily among older demographics, users who had not migrated to subscription streaming, and niche enthusiasts for particular formats. Being "number one on iTunes" in 2021 carried a fraction of the cultural weight it would have carried in 2010.

Snopes journalist Dan Evon published a careful breakdown of these distinctions on October 29, 2021, noting the difference between iTunes (a media library and digital store for purchasing individual songs) and Apple Music (a streaming subscription service). These are two different products under the Apple umbrella with different audiences, chartings, and consumption patterns. The confusion between them was either genuine ignorance of how these services differ or deliberate elision for rhetorical effect.

## Epistemic Novelty

The "Let's Go Brandon" chart controversy is a genuinely instructive case study in what might be called *metric laundering* — the practice of selecting a real but non-representative measurement to make a misleading claim about a larger phenomenon. The tactic exploits several structural features of how ordinary people relate to quantification.

First, there is *metric unfamiliarity*. Most people do not have detailed knowledge of what the iTunes chart measures versus what the Billboard Hot 100 measures versus what the Apple Music charts measure. These are distinct instruments with different methodologies, different input data, and different relationships to actual listening behaviour. The rhetoric of "number one" effaces all these distinctions: "number one" sounds absolute, comprehensive, and unambiguous. The phrase appears to designate a position in a total ordering over all music. In practice, it designates a position on a specific chart using a specific weighting formula for a specific universe of transactions.

Second, there is *temporal lag in public mental models*. The iTunes Store opened in 2003 and was genuinely dominant through roughly 2012–2014. During those years, "number one on iTunes" was a meaningful proxy for broad popularity because that was where most digital music consumption occurred. The public mental model formed during that era persisted long after the actual music economy had shifted to streaming. By 2021, it was the equivalent of claiming a film had record-breaking "VHS rental sales" — technically meaningful within a narrowing niche, but not a measure of broad cultural impact.

Third, there is a *motivated amplification loop*. Political media ecosystems reinforce internally generated signals. A conservative audience sees conservative media first reporting the iTunes chart position, then celebrating it, then citing each other's celebration as evidence of importance. The repetition within the system generates a sense of cultural saturation that is decoupled from external reality. Research by Benkler, Faris, and Roberts (2018, *Network Propaganda: Manipulation, Disinformation, and Radicalization in American Politics*, Oxford University Press) demonstrated through large-scale mapping of US media network topology that the right-wing media ecosystem exhibits significantly higher internal cross-citation and significantly lower bridging to mainstream or counter-narrative sources than left-wing equivalents. The "Let's Go Brandon" chart phenomenon fits this pattern: the claim propagated rapidly within the internal network and encountered friction only when it crossed into cross-partisan or fact-checking contexts.

Fourth, the episode illustrates how *low-cost political expression* exploits cultural infrastructure. Purchasing a download on iTunes is a low-friction, small-dollar act that can be performed as political signalling with negligible cost. This creates an asymmetry: people who strongly wished to signal opposition to Biden were highly motivated to purchase the song, creating a spike in a metric that was easily interpreted as evidence of mass popular sentiment. But the metric was not sampled from the general population — it was sampled from politically motivated purchasers of a specific format. A similar dynamic appeared during the 2020 TikTok campaign to inflate registration numbers for the Trump Tulsa rally: coordinated low-cost digital actions can produce real numbers in real metrics without those numbers reflecting what they appear to reflect about broader sentiment.

## The Evidence Base

**Dan Evon, Snopes.com, October 29, 2021.** The original fact-check that broke down the iTunes/Apple Music distinction and provided the Billboard comparison, establishing the metric context for the claim.

**Recording Industry Association of America (RIAA), US Sales Database.** The authoritative source on digital single sales volume trends. The figures cited in Evon's piece — 1.4 billion digital single sales in 2012, 257 million in 2020 — are drawn from the RIAA's public-facing database.

**Billboard Hot 100 Charts, Week of October 30, 2021.** The primary evidence source for Adele's "Easy on Me" performance metrics and for Loza Alexander's debut at position 45.

**Fox News, "Let's Go Brandon! Goes from Social Media Meme to the Top of the iTunes Charts," October 16, 2021.** Michael Lee's article in Fox News was among the first major outlet reports on the chart achievement, establishing the story's media timeline.

**Benkler, Y., Faris, R., and Roberts, H. (2018). *Network Propaganda: Manipulation, Disinformation, and Radicalization in American Politics*. Oxford University Press.** This large-scale analysis of partisan media networks provides the structural framework for understanding asymmetric echo-chamber amplification. The authors used computational methods to analyse millions of media citations during the 2016 US election cycle and documented how internal cross-citation patterns within the right-wing media ecosystem create self-reinforcing information environments.

**Casey Newton, The Verge, "Music Sales Decline for the First Time since the iTunes Store Opened," January 3, 2014.** Established the early trendline of the streaming-driven decline in digital purchases.

**Karp, H., Wall Street Journal, "Apple iTunes Sees Big Drop in Music Sales," October 24, 2014.** WSJ contemporaneous reporting on the acceleration of the iTunes sales decline, noting more than 13% decline in digital music sales within the year.

**Kim, E., Business Insider, "The Reason Apple Needs a Music Streaming Service Explained in One Chart," 2015.** Provided the consumer behaviour data underlying the shift to streaming subscription models, relevant to understanding why iTunes download sales no longer proxy for overall music popularity.

## Why People Believe This

The appeal of the "Let's Go Brandon" chart story, beyond its pure political function, reflects something accurate: a real cultural moment was happening. The phrase had emerged organically at an October 2, 2021 NASCAR race at Talladega Superspeedway, when NBC Sports reporter Kelli Stavast, struggling to hear the chanting crowd clearly, described them as chanting "Let's Go Brandon" for winning driver Brandon Brown — when in fact the crowd was chanting a crude anti-Biden slogan. The incident became immediately iconic in certain political spheres as a symbol of perceived media cover-up, and the phrase took on a second life as political merchandise, social media shorthand, and, promptly, music.

The organic energy was real. People did purchase the song as political expression. The song did chart. In this sense, the cultural moment was not fabricated — something genuinely was happening in grassroots political expression that music charts registered. The error was in what the charts were being made to evidence. The claim was not merely "this song charted" but "this is the most popular song in America" — a much stronger claim that the evidence did not support.

Psychologically, selection of a favourable metric is a well-documented persuasion technique. Researchers Schwarz and Strack (1991, "Context Effects in Attitude Surveys: Applying Cognitive Theory to Social Research," *European Review of Social Psychology*, Vol. 2) demonstrated that framing effects in how a question is presented or a datum is labelled dramatically alter the inferences people draw. "Number one" carries strong priming — it activates associations with dominant cultural relevance — while "number one on a subcategory of a declining purchase platform" does not. Communicators who understood or intuited this difference chose the simpler, more powerful framing even if it was technically incomplete.

There is also a generational factor in metric literacy. Audiences who were heavy iTunes users in its 2003–2012 heyday had those chart rankings as their baseline reference for music popularity. For these users, the mental model "iTunes = the music store = what everyone buys" was accurate during the period they formed it. The transition to streaming subscriptions was a regime change in music consumption, but mental models do not update uniformly or automatically. For many older listeners, "making it on iTunes" retained the cultural weight it had earned in 2008 — even though the landscape had fundamentally changed.

Finally, there is motivated reasoning on both political poles. Enthusiasts for "Let's Go Brandon" were motivated to believe the song was number one in America because that would confirm their sense that anti-Biden sentiment was majority culture. Critics of the song were motivated to emphasise its actual metrics because that would confirm their sense that it was a niche expression. Both motivations introduced distortion into how facts were processed and communicated. The neutral position — the song had a real, notable achievement in a narrowing market, while a much larger achievement simultaneously belonged to Adele — was neither exciting to its fans nor satisfying to its critics.

## The Verdict

The claim that "Let's Go Brandon" was the number-one song in America in October 2021 is **false**. Multiple versions of the song did reach the top of the iTunes digital song sales chart, which is a genuine accomplishment within a sharply declining market segment. The song debuted at number 45 on the Billboard Hot 100 with 1.2 million US streams and 35,200 downloads, compared to Adele's "Easy on Me" at number one with 53.9 million streams and 74,000 downloads in the same tracking period. No version of "Let's Go Brandon" appeared in Apple Music's top 100 songs — Apple Music is a separate service from iTunes and a more accurate proxy for current listening behaviour.

The episode belongs to a recognisable genre of political metric laundering: the selection of a real but non-representative measurement to support a claim about broader cultural dominance. iTunes was the correct metric for the mid-2000s. By 2021, with digital single sales having fallen 80% since 2012, it was a vestigial market niche — real, but no longer representative. The excitement around the song was not fabricated. The interpretation placed on its chart performance was.

For media-literate observers, the incident is a textbook illustration of why chart literacy matters. The Billboard Hot 100 synthesises streams, digital sales, and radio plays into a weighted composite because no single metric alone captures listening behaviour across the population. A song can dominate one metric dramatically while remaining invisible in others. "Number one" is only meaningful relative to a defined, appropriate universe — and the universe of iTunes download purchasers in 2021 was not the universe of American music listeners.
