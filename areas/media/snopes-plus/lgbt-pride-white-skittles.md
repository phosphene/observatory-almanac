## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

During June 2021—LGBTQ Pride Month in the United States, the United Kingdom, and numerous other countries—a photograph circulated widely across social media platforms including Twitter, Reddit, and Facebook that appeared to show a bag of all-white Skittles candy sitting beneath a supermarket shelf label reading "Skittles White Pride." The visual juxtaposition—Pride-solidarity candy branded with the most recognizable phrase of white nationalist rhetoric—provoked an immediate and heated response. The two-word label appeared, at first encounter, to suggest that either Mars Incorporated (the manufacturer of Skittles) or the supermarket chain carrying the product had intentionally placed a racially loaded message on candy explicitly designed to celebrate and support the LGBTQ community.

Snopes investigator Dan MacGuill (2021) reviewed the image and assigned the composite verdict **Mixture**. The constituent parts of the verdict are carefully delineated: the photograph itself was authentic—no digital manipulation of the kind commonly used to fabricate scandalous supermarket shelf labels was detected—but the "White Pride" label did not represent any intentional corporate or retail policy, racially motivated or otherwise. It was, instead, the result of an isolated labelling error at a specific store in a UK SPAR supermarket chain outlet, an error that had been identified and corrected approximately one year before the photograph re-emerged online in June 2021.

The SPAR UK Twitter account responded on the same day the photograph began recirculating (June 3, 2021), stating: "Hello, this was a labelling error last year that was quickly rectified. We will make sure it never happens again. Many thanks!" This constitutes corporate acknowledgement of both the authenticity of the incident and its accidental nature. The label was the result of a supermarket employee generating a shelf-identification label for the white Skittles product. The expected label text would have drawn from the product's official name and marketing. The Mars promotional campaign associated with the white Skittles release—"During Pride, only one rainbow matters"—intentionally employs the word "Pride" in a non-racial sense. The construction "Skittles White Pride," however, was apparently produced when whoever created the shelf label concatenated the product's colour descriptor ("White") with the word "Pride" drawn from the campaign's promotional language, yielding the inadvertent phrase without any apparent awareness of its white supremacist resonance.

## §2 The White Skittles Campaign: Corporate LGBTQ Solidarity and Its Controversy

To fully understand the context in which this labelling error occurred and re-circulated, it is necessary to appreciate the underlying Mars-Skittles LGBTQ Pride campaign and the controversy that had already surrounded it prior to the SPAR shelf-label incident.

Skittles are among the world's most recognizable candy brands. Their flagship slogan—"Taste the Rainbow"—invokes a vivid spectrum of colours that has, since the brand's introduction in the 1970s, been a central element of their identity. The candy's individual pieces come in bright distinct hues (red, orange, yellow, green, purple), and the rainbow theme pervades their packaging, advertising, and cultural identity. The fact that the LGBTQ Pride flag also employs a rainbow symbol is therefore not incidental to the Mars corporate partnership decision: it made Skittles an unusually natural corporate fit for Pride solidarity marketing.

Beginning in 2017, Mars began releasing limited-edition all-white Skittles packages during Pride Month. The creative logic, as articulated in the campaign's promotional materials, was that Skittles would surrender its own iconic rainbow as an act of symbolic deference to the LGBTQ Pride rainbow. The campaign slogan—"During Pride, only one rainbow matters"—makes this logic explicit. The candy product itself is functionally identical to regular Skittles in terms of flavour, but all exterior colouring is removed from the candy shells, producing uniformly white pieces. The packaging is similarly stripped of its usual riot of colour in favour of monochrome or minimal-colour branding. From a marketing perspective, the campaign was designed to generate both positive brand association through perceived LGBTQ allyship and media coverage through the novelty of the design decision.

Despite this apparently straightforward pro-LGBTQ gesture, the campaign attracted significant criticism since its initial launch in 2017. Critics from within LGBTQ communities and from marketing analysts raised several objections. First, some argued that the removal of colour from candy sold under the premise of supporting a community frequently described as celebrating visibility and colourful self-expression produced an aesthetically and symbolically confused product: it was whiteness, not rainbows, that the product actually embodied. Second, scholars of corporate allyship and "rainbow capitalism"—the co-optation of Pride symbolism by commercial interests during June each year—raised questions about whether purchasing colourless candy constituted any meaningful mechanism of support for LGBTQ organizations, communities, or political causes (Chasin, 2000, *Selling Out: The Gay and Lesbian Movement Goes to Market*, St. Martin's Press). Third, some commentators noted that Mars's commitment to LGBTQ causes was not transparently disclosed in the campaign—the specific charities or organizational recipients of any portion of proceeds were not made prominently clear to consumers.

This pre-existing critical context is directly relevant to why the shelf label photograph spread as quickly as it did in June 2021. The image did not emerge into a neutral interpretive environment. It emerged into a discourse that had already been primed with scepticism and irony about corporate LGBTQ marketing during Pride. Some of the most significant sharing appeared to arise not from genuine belief that Mars or SPAR had made a racist corporate statement, but from an ironic reading of the image as inadvertently capturing something structurally critical about the contradictions of corporate allyship marketing—that a product stripped of all colour, sold as solidarity with a rainbow community, should accidentally bear the label "White Pride" struck many commentators as darkly apt rather than literally incriminating.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Inadvertent Semiotics, Labelling Systems, and Viral Context-Stripping

The epistemic novelty presented by this case lies in the intersection of three distinct phenomena that each carry implications beyond the individual fact-check: the mechanics of how retail labelling systems generate inadvertent semantic combinations; the dynamics of context-stripping that accelerate viral spread of material with high emotional charge; and the specific semiotic instability of commercial products that borrow their primary identity markers from contested political and cultural vocabularies.

Retail shelf-labelling systems are, in most large-format supermarket operations, semi-automated. Product information—brand name, variant descriptor, campaign name—is frequently pulled from internal product management databases that may be populated by data entry staff with no authority or incentive to flag potential semantic collisions. The SPAR employee who generated the "Skittles White Pride" label likely entered the product colour descriptor ("White") and the campaign word ("Pride") in a field that produced a label concatenating these strings. This is not an unfamiliar class of error in computational linguistics and human-computer interaction research. Whittle et al. (2020), writing in *Behaviour & Information Technology* (Taylor & Francis), documented systematic categories of user-generated text that produce unintended semantic readings when natural-language segments are concatenated in automated display systems—a class of error particularly common in contexts where staff generating the inputs are operating in high-throughput, low-attention conditions typical of retail stock management.

The broader epistemological issue is that this labelling error constitutes what might be called *incidental misinformation*: not fabricated, not politically motivated, not structurally misleading in its primary generation, but virally amplified in a stripped context that removed the very features—the physical store setting, the accompanying verbal clarification, the date—that would have disambiguated its accidental nature. Tandoc et al. (2018), in their influential taxonomy published in *Digital Journalism* (Taylor & Francis), proposed a distinction between fabricated content, manipulated content, and content that is technically authentic but misleadingly framed through selective context removal. The "Skittles White Pride" photograph is an almost ideal example of the third category: the image is not altered, but its viral circulation consistently stripped the temporal context (this happened in 2020, not June 2021), the geographical specificity (a single store, since corrected), and the corporate acknowledgement (SPAR had already corrected the error and expressed regret).

The semiotic instability dimension is equally significant. The word "Pride" now carries such dense and contested ideological loading—operating simultaneously as the name of a specific LGBTQ cultural-political movement, as a general English noun connoting self-esteem, and, in certain compound forms, as a component of white nationalist vocabulary—that any product labelling system that introduces "Pride" into an automated concatenation field alongside a colour descriptor invites exactly this kind of collision. This case therefore presents a genuine epistemic gap in how corporations manage the semiotic risks of commercial pride campaigns: there is, to date, no documented systematic protocol within Mars, SPAR, or similar corporate entities for auditing shelf-label systems downstream of Pride campaigns to catch precisely this class of inadvertent combination.

## §4 Corporate LGBTQ Allyship: Mechanisms, Critiques, and the Rainbow Capitalism Debate

The SPAR/Skittles incident cannot be adequately understood outside the broader literature on corporate LGBTQ allyship and its critics. The term "rainbow capitalism" has gained increasing currency in academic and activist discourse to describe a mode of commercial engagement with Pride that many scholars argue functions primarily as brand equity extraction rather than as material support for LGBTQ liberation.

McDonald (2019), in a review article in *Journal of Consumer Culture* (SAGE), traced the historical trajectory of corporate Pride engagement from the early 1990s—when most major corporations explicitly avoided any association with LGBTQ events for fear of alienating mainstream consumers—through the post–*Lawrence v. Texas* (2003) and post–*Obergefell v. Hodges* (2015) periods, when legal and political normalization of LGBTQ rights increasingly made overt consumer homophobia commercially costly. McDonald (2019) documented that as of 2019, more than 70% of Fortune 500 companies had adopted public LGBTQ non-discrimination policies, and Pride Month had become one of the most commercially saturated retail moments in the American consumer calendar, with brands ranging from Skittles to major financial institutions deploying rainbow-themed marketing in June.

Johnston and Taylor (2008), in *Gender, Place & Culture* (Taylor & Francis), offered an early critical analysis of what they termed the "corporatization of the gay and lesbian movement," arguing that the growing dependence of Pride events on corporate sponsorship revenue created structural pressures to prioritize legible, consumption-friendly narratives of LGBTQ identity over more politically radical organizing against homophobia, transphobia, or economic precarity. The Skittles campaign—premised on the idea that purchasing colourless candy constitutes a meaningful act of LGBTQ solidarity—sits squarely within this critique.

Chasin (2000), in *Selling Out: The Gay and Lesbian Movement Goes to Market* (St. Martin's Press), made an earlier version of this argument with particular force: that the integration of LGBTQ identity into mainstream consumer culture required the construction of an image of gay and lesbian consumers as affluent, brand-loyal, and politically moderate—an image that erased the structural marginalization of, particularly, queer people of colour, transgender people, and those at the economic margins of LGBTQ communities.

The "white Skittles" case is therefore, from a critical marketing studies perspective, a useful test case: the product literally performs whiteness as LGBTQ solidarity, via a marketing logic that, however symbolically intended, strips colour from the candy in a gesture constructed as generous rather than as an erasure. The coincidental irony of the "Skittles White Pride" shelf label produced a moment in which an underlying tension in the campaign's aesthetic and political logic became, briefly and accidentally, legible to a mass audience.

## §5 Source Analysis and Verification Chain

MacGuill's (2021) verification methodology for the Snopes fact-check relied on four primary evidentiary elements. First, a reverse image search and photographic authenticity assessment established that the image had not been digitally fabricated—the shelf label was consistent with genuine SPAR-format point-of-sale labelling, including typeface, label dimensions, and retail shelf context visible in the background. Second, MacGuill traced the earliest verifiable circulation of the image to a 2020 origin point, predating the June 2021 viral wave by approximately one year. Third, the SPAR UK corporate Twitter account directly acknowledged the image's authenticity and characterized it as a prior labelling error on June 3, 2021—the same day as the most intense period of viral spreading—providing a corporate primary source response synchronized with the circulation event. Fourth, the original Mars campaign materials were reviewed to establish the slogan "During Pride, only one rainbow matters," providing the source text from which the shelf label's "Pride" component was drawn.

The epistemological power of the "Mixture" rating in this case derives precisely from its refusal to collapse the distinction between the image's authenticity and the meaning imputed to it in its viral context. A verdict of "False" would be inaccurate because the photograph was not fabricated. A verdict of "True"—meaning that SPAR or Mars had deliberately labelled Pride Skittles as "White Pride"—would be equally inaccurate. The "Mixture" rating captures the correct epistemic structure: the image is an authentic document of a real event (the mislabelled shelf), but the event was an accidental labelling error of limited scope, already corrected before the image began circulating, rather than a deliberate ideological statement by corporate actors.

The temporal gap between the original error (2020) and the viral spread (June 2021) is itself analytically significant. It suggests that the photograph was deliberately or incidentally held and then released or re-circulated during Pride Month specifically to maximize its emotional impact—a timing that transformed an already-corrected retail error into an apparent contemporaneous corporate scandal. Whether this re-circulation was deliberate provocation, algorithmic amplification of high-engagement content, or coincidental rediscovery remains undetermined by available evidence.

## §6 Synthesis: Verdict Architecture and Lessons for Misinformation Epistemology

The "Skittles White Pride" case offers several durable lessons that extend beyond the specific fact-check. For misinformation scholarship, it illustrates the category of viral claim that is simultaneously true in its evidentiary core and false in its imputed significance—a category that binary true/false rating systems systematically mishandle. MacGuill's (2021) "Mixture" rating, supported by explicit differentiation between "What's True" and "What's False," models a best practice: genuine complexity of a claim should be faithfully represented in the verdict structure rather than forced into an artificially simple category.

For corporate communications and crisis response, the SPAR Twitter response—rapid, direct, factually specific, and appropriately contrite without being excessively self-flagellating—represents a reasonably effective model for addressing viral incidents involving inadvertent but politically sensitive errors. The acknowledgement that the error would not happen again ("We will make sure it never happens again") constitutes a forward-looking commitment without admitting deliberate wrongdoing.

For scholars of LGBTQ consumer culture and rainbow capitalism, the incident reveals the semiotic fragility of commercial Pride campaigns that rely on colour puns and wordplay in product naming. The "During Pride, only one rainbow matters" campaign was designed to be clever about colour and identity; the inadvertent "White Pride" label was a downstream consequence of the same wordplay logic. Companies that build marketing campaigns around contested political vocabulary—whose double meanings are part of their communicative strategy—bear heightened responsibility for auditing all labelling and downstream communications for unintended combinations of the same vocabulary with racial or political meanings.

Finally, the case provides an instructive example of how the virality of an image can substantially exceed its factual significance: by June 2021, the SPAR UK labelling error had already been corrected, acknowledged, and resolved for approximately a year. The viral image's June 2021 circui was an afterlife phenomenon—the image was re-activated in a context timed to maximize its emotional resonance, in a month when audiences were already primed by the discourse around corporate Pride marketing. The most significant misinformation in the incident was not the content of the shelf label itself, but the strips of temporal and contextual information that were shed from the photograph as it circulated, transforming an archived historical correction into an apparently live scandal.
