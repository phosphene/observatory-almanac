# Lia Thomas and Réka György Were Both Banned from Twitter over the Trans Athlete Debate

## 1. The Claim

In March 2022, following Lia Thomas's history-making victory at the NCAA Division I Women's Swimming and Diving Championships, two parallel claims circulated widely on social media and in right-leaning media outlets. The first, and most specific, held that Virginia Tech competitive swimmer and 2016 Rio Olympian Réka György had been suspended by Twitter after posting criticism of Thomas's eligibility to compete in women's events. The second, broader claim used this alleged suspension as evidence that Twitter was systematically censoring female athletes who dared speak out against transgender inclusion policies in competitive sport. Together, these threads coalesced into a single narrative: that women who questioned the fairness of Lia Thomas's participation were being silenced by Big Tech—with György cast as the martyr figure and Thomas positioned, depending on the version, as either a tool of the censorship regime or herself a target.

The impersonation account at the center of the controversy carried the handle @RekaGyorgy_ and had gathered over 100,000 likes on a single post that read: "My finals spot was stolen by Lia Thomas, who is a biological male. Until we all refuse to compete nothing will change. Thanks for all the support retweets and follows I wont stop fighting." This message circulated so widely and so fast—shared by prominent influencers and political commentators across the ideological spectrum—that tens of thousands of users encountered it as authentic testimony from György herself. When the account was subsequently suspended, commentators declared it proof positive of what they had always suspected: that platforms were enforcing ideological conformity on gender issues by removing accounts that pushed back against transgender inclusion.

The claim spread through a media ecosystem already supercharged by Thomas's actual win. On March 17, 2022, she became the first openly transgender athlete to win an NCAA Division I national championship in any sport, finishing the 500-yard freestyle in 4 minutes, 33.24 seconds at Atlanta's McAquatic Center. Protests took place outside the event. Teammate testimonials circulated in fragments. Multiple athletes, coaches, and parents submitted open letters to the NCAA. The entire information environment was primed to receive a story mixing platform censorship with the trans athlete controversy, and this claim arrived ready-made, pre-packaged with what appeared to be a direct testimonial from a directly affected competitor.

## 2. What's Actually True

The account that was suspended—bearing the handle @RekaGyorgy_—was not Réka György's account. It was an impersonation account created by an unknown third party and suspended for violating Twitter's platform rules against impersonating real people, a category of violation entirely distinct from viewpoint-based enforcement.

György herself confirmed this directly to Reuters News Agency in March 2022. She stated: "I don't have Twitter." The Reuters fact-check, published within days of the claim going viral (Hartnett, Mackinnon, & O'Brien, 2022, *Reuters Fact Check*), established that no account belonging to György existed at any point, that no such tweet was published by her, and that the suspension of @RekaGyorgy_ was a routine enforcement action against a fake account, not a censorship of a real athlete.

Lia Thomas was not banned from Twitter during or after the controversy. She maintained active social media accounts throughout the period, and no credible reporting from any news organization—including those critical of her participation in women's swimming—documented any suspension of her profiles.

What is documented and verifiable: Réka György did issue a real, signed public statement regarding Lia Thomas and the NCAA's policies, published in Swimming World Magazine (Swimming World, 2022). That statement was substantially more nuanced than the fake tweet. György wrote that she respects and fully stands with Thomas as a human being, acknowledging that Thomas, like all fellow swimmers, worked hard and sacrificed in order to compete at that level. However, György also criticized the NCAA's rules "that allow her to compete against us, who are biological women," and called for policy reform. The real statement—measured, complex, and attributing no malice to Thomas personally—was already in public circulation before or alongside the impersonation account's explosion. The fake tweet, amplified to millions, functioned to replace this nuanced statement with a cruder, more inflammatory substitute.

Thomas competed at the championship under the NCAA's then-current policy, which required transgender women to complete one year of hormone therapy before competing in women's events. World Aquatics (then known as FINA) subsequently adopted stricter rules in June 2022, prohibiting transgender women who had undergone male puberty from competing in the elite women's category regardless of hormone therapy duration. In June 2024, the Court of Arbitration for Sport dismissed Thomas's challenge to World Aquatics' eligibility framework, finding that she lacked standing as she was no longer a member of USA Swimming for World Aquatics competition purposes (CAS, 2024, Arbitral Award 2023/A/9839).

## 3. Why People Believe This

The speed and reach of this claim illustrate a specific misinformation mechanism that had matured considerably by 2022: what might be called the **impersonation inversion**. An impersonator constructs an account modeled on a real person, crafts a message calibrated precisely to what the target is assumed to believe but would not say in polished public language, and the resulting document circulates not as a provocation but as testimony. When the platform then acts correctly—removing the impersonation account for the mundane violation it represents—the removal is read through the lens of the controversy itself. "Of course they banned her" becomes the operative interpretation, and the platform's routine enforcement of identity rules is transformed into evidence of viewpoint suppression. The impersonator's account has done double work: first generating outrage as a fake document, then generating conspiracy as a deleted document.

The psychological mechanism underlying this reinterpretation is what researchers term **motivated reasoning under ambiguity** (Kunda, 1990, *Psychological Bulletin*, 108(3):480–498). When people observe an ambiguous event whose actual explanation is inaccessible—a Twitter account being suspended—they default to the explanation most consistent with their existing beliefs and emotional commitments. For someone primed to believe that social media platforms systematically suppress trans-skeptical voices, a suspension event will be interpreted as censorship without requiring any additional evidence. The alternative explanation—impersonation—requires knowing the internal origin of the account, which is opaque to any outside observer. Motivated reasoning effectively closes the inquiry at the point where the evidence confirms the preferred hypothesis: the account was banned, I expected banning, therefore the banning was for the reason I expected.

There is also a deeper epistemic problem operating simultaneously: **source-monitoring failure**. Research by Johnson, Hashtroudi, and Lindsay (1993, *Psychological Bulletin*, 114(1):3–28) on source monitoring demonstrates that people frequently retain information—a statement, a quote, a claim—while losing or confusing its provenance. Tens of thousands of people encountered the fake post before the suspension and accepted it as authentic. Even after the suspension and subsequent fact-checking, those who had already read and encoded the fake statement retained its content as memory. The later correction—"this was an impersonation"—arrived after initial encoding and failed to overwrite it. The statement "Réka György said this" had been registered before the qualification "but she didn't say it" existed as information in circulation.

The narrative also succeeded because it mapped onto a pre-existing cultural template with extremely high activation energy: **the silenced woman**. This template—a courageous female athlete speaks uncomfortable truths about a politically powerful doctrine, only to be erased by faceless corporate authority—follows the classical whistleblower suppression narrative that activates deep intuitions about fairness, voice, and institutional bad faith. People across the political spectrum had been watching the Thomas controversy build for months; they expected female athletes to speak out, and they expected them to face consequences. The fake account delivered both pieces of the expected narrative simultaneously and on schedule.

A critical epistemic feature of this episode—one that distinguishes it from simpler misinformation events—is that the true events were objectively more complex and interesting than the false ones, yet the false version thoroughly dominated initial discourse. The real Réka György statement expressed simultaneous solidarity with Thomas as a person and policy dissent from the NCAA's eligibility framework. It resisted the culture-war binary that required either total support or total opposition. The fake tweet, by contrast, collapsed the situation into a single tribal assertion. This is an instance of **narrative legibility bias**: structurally simpler narratives (she was banned for what she said) outcompete structurally accurate but multi-layered ones (an impersonator was removed for identity fraud, and the real athlete's actual views don't fit the frame you've been given). The true version requires holding two independent facts in mind at once—György's genuine critical views coexist with the falsity of the specific tweet—while the misinformation requires only one.

György's biographical positioning reinforced plausibility. A 2016 Rio Olympian representing Hungary, a two-time ACC champion, a three-time All-American at Virginia Tech, someone who had competed in the same 500-yard freestyle event as Thomas: she had the most visible and direct stake of any competitor. The inference "of course she would say this" was nearly irresistible. Personal stake was converted into predicted speech, and the impersonation was retroactively backstopped by genuine grievance.

Secondary confirmation bias then intensified the claim's spread through conservative media ecosystems. Outlets that had been intensively covering transgender athletics controversies were primed to amplify a story combining two themes—Big Tech censorship and trans athlete controversy—that they had been separately covering for months. Several published pieces citing the fake account before the impersonation was established; when corrections were issued, they typically appeared as appended updates to articles whose original headlines had already been screenshotted, cropped, and circulated in isolation. The correction existed; the correction did not reach most of the people who had seen the headline.

The broader claim that Lia Thomas herself was banned from Twitter appears to have originated through what Kuran and Sunstein (1999, *Stanford Law Review*, 51(4):683–788) describe as **availability cascades**: once the generalized belief that "Twitter is banning people in the trans athlete debate" was activated by the impersonator suspension, specific names were plugged into the narrative on minimal or zero evidence. The Thomas variant required no single viral post of its own; it assembled itself from the ambient informational noise of a controversy in which everyone expected enforcement, and specific Twitter suspensions from unrelated controversies were misattributed.

## 4. Verdict

**False**

The suspension that occurred was of an impersonation account, not of Réka György, who confirmed she has no Twitter account. Twitter's enforcement action was standard platform policy against impersonation—entirely distinct from viewpoint-based censorship. Lia Thomas was not banned from Twitter. No credible evidence supports any component of the claim that either athlete was silenced on social media over the trans athlete debate.

György's real public position, as expressed in her signed letter to the NCAA published in Swimming World Magazine, includes both personal respect for Thomas as a competitor and human being, and institutional dissent from the NCAA's eligibility rules. Those real views were publicly available and widely covered. The viral claim misrepresented both the platform action and the substance of György's actual public position.

The gap classification of *distorted-but-grounded* is appropriate: the distortion is operated on a real event (a real suspension occurred) and real participants (György and Thomas are real people engaged in a real controversy), but the mechanism, target, and meaning of the enforcement action were all fundamentally misrepresented. The grounded real event—a suspension taking place during a moment of heated controversy—provided structural plausibility for the false interpretation.

## 5. The Wider Picture

This episode belongs to a well-documented class of platform-manipulation events in which impersonation is weaponized not primarily to deceive the target's followers, but to manufacture an outrage event whose removal becomes self-generating controversy. By 2022, impersonation attacks of this kind had been observed across many politically charged domains—climate science, vaccine policy, election integrity—and gender and sports had emerged as particularly hospitable terrain because both activate deeply held identity commitments across multiple communities simultaneously.

Research on misinformation diffusion has consistently documented that emotionally activating content spreads substantially faster than neutral content of equivalent factual interest. Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018, *Science*, 359(6380):1146–1151) analyzed approximately 126,000 Twitter rumor cascades between 2006 and 2017 and found that false news reached more people, faster, and spread more broadly than true news at every level of content analysis—and that the emotional novelty of false content, not bot amplification, was the primary driver. The fake György tweet was engineered, consciously or otherwise, to hit precisely the affective register most likely to cascade: an Olympian claiming her literal competition results had been stolen. That framing was not true but it was maximally activating.

The underlying dispute about transgender athletes in competitive sport involves genuinely contested science, genuine institutional uncertainty, and genuine competing values. Research on post-transition athletic performance remains ongoing; the IOC, World Aquatics, the NCAA, and national governing bodies have adopted different and rapidly evolving policies throughout this period. In a domain where authoritative answers are uncertain and where passionate disagreement is widespread, simple false narratives find especially fertile ground. The György impersonation succeeded not because it was sophisticated but because the background conditions—genuine controversy, genuine uncertainty, genuine emotional investment—had already done the work of making audiences receptive.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated this claim "False" in a March 2022 article by Dan Evon, providing substantial documentation of the impersonation account, György's confirmation that she has no Twitter account, and the text of her real NCAA statement. Snopes explicitly noted the critical distinction between the impersonation account's suspension and any viewpoint-based enforcement action.

PolitiFact independently reached the same conclusion, rating the claim "False" in two separate articles published within the same week, one focused specifically on the @RekaGyorgy_ account and one on the secondary claim that György had tweeted that Thomas stole her finals spot. Reuters published an independent fact-check documenting the same sequence and quoting György directly. AP Fact Check confirmed findings aligned with the other outlets.

The consensus across major English-language fact-checking organizations was unusually clean and unanimous—an uncommon alignment in a domain where complex events frequently generate divergent verdicts. The impersonation mechanism was clear-cut enough that multiple independent investigations reached identical findings within 72 hours of the claim's initial spread, with documentary evidence from the platform, direct confirmation from the real person, and side-by-side comparison of the fake tweet versus György's actual public statement all available to establish the record.
