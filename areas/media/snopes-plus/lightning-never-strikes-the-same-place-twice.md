# Lightning never strikes the same place twice


## 1. The Claim

Among the most durable pieces of folk meteorology in the English-speaking world is the proverb "lightning never strikes the same place twice." The saying appears in newspapers, novels, self-help literature, and everyday speech across several centuries, and it functions in two quite different registers. Used literally, it expresses a meteorological claim: that a location or object struck by lightning is statistically protected from future lightning contact. Used figuratively, it expresses a philosophical position about the non-recurrence of fortuitous or catastrophic events: that good luck (or bad luck) of a particular kind will not visit the same person or place a second time. The figurative use has generated an entire family of proverbial variants — "lightning never strikes twice in the same place" and "fool's luck comes around but once" — that persist in popular speech as expressions about probability, fate, and the supposed uniqueness of dramatic events.

In its literal meteorological form, the claim implies that a specific location — say, a particular tree, a rooftop, a hilltop, or a person — having once been struck by lightning is somehow spent as a target, that the electrical conditions which caused the initial strike will not recur, or that some as-yet-unspecified physics of lightning makes same-site returns impossible. This is not a fringe misunderstanding; surveys of public science literacy consistently find lightning-behaviour myths among the most widely believed meteorological misconceptions (Crum, L.A., 2012, *Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society*, 93(6), 783–796). The claim has resisted correction partly because it never receives focal attention — it arrives as an aside, a reassurance, a rhetorical flourish — and partly because the intuition it expresses about uniqueness and probability is one that humans find generally congenial.

Its cultural persistence also benefits from its rhetorical versatility. In consolatory contexts it reassures someone who has survived a trauma that the exact same catastrophe is unlikely to befall them again. In gambling and decision-making contexts it expresses the idea that a successful or unsuccessful pattern will not repeat. In religious discourse it has occasionally been invoked to gesture at divine providence distributing its attentions broadly. None of these uses require the underlying meteorological claim to be examined closely; the saying derives its authority from its age and familiarity rather than from empirical evaluation.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim is demonstrably and completely false as a statement of meteorological physics. Lightning not only can strike the same place twice — it almost certainly will do so, for any sufficiently tall, conductive, or exposed object, and will do so repeatedly throughout that object's lifetime. The physical mechanisms of lightning formation make same-location strikes not merely possible but, in many cases, statistically inevitable.

**How lightning attachment works.** A lightning discharge begins with the development of charge separation inside a cumulonimbus cloud, driven by differential movement of hydrometeors — ice crystals, graupel, and water droplets — within the cloud's convective updraft (Illingworth, A.J., 1985, *Journal of Atmospheric and Terrestrial Physics*, 47(1-3), 193–198). As the negative charge region at the cloud's base accumulates, an equal and opposite positive charge is induced in the ground and in objects on the ground below it. This charge induction is not uniformly distributed: it concentrates most intensely at points of high geometry — the tips of towers, trees, antenna masts, rooftops, mountain peaks, and any object that protrudes above its surroundings. The concentration of induced charge at elevated points causes those points to emit faint upward leaders — ionised plasma channels extending skyward toward the descending stepped leader from the cloud. When a downward-propagating stepped leader connects with an upward-propagating leader, the return stroke — what we observe as lightning — follows the completed channel (Rakov, V.A. & Uman, M.A., 2003, *Lightning: Physics and Effects*, Cambridge University Press, pp. 61–104).

The critical physical consequence of this mechanism is that the objects which most strongly attract lightning are those with the greatest induced charge concentration — which are precisely the same objects that attracted it the first time. A tall steel tower does not become less electrically prominent after being struck; its geometry, conductivity, and elevation remain identical. The following storm will produce the same charge induction in the same places, the same upward streamers from the same towers, and the same return strokes to the same locations.

**Empirical data: the Empire State Building.** The most widely cited and best-documented example of repeat lightning strikes on a single structure is the Empire State Building in New York City. Instrumented lightning studies of the building's mast have recorded strike frequencies ranging from approximately fifteen to forty-five strikes per year depending on the seasonal storm cycle, with a commonly cited long-term average of approximately twenty-three strikes per year (Krider, E.P., Noggle, R.C., Uman, M.A. & Orville, R.E., 1980, *Journal of Applied Meteorology*, 19(1), 27–35; also discussed in Rakov & Uman, 2003, p. 107). The building's antenna mast has been struck multiple times in a single thunderstorm on numerous occasions. This is not anomalous luck; it is the predictable outcome of placing the tallest structure in the region in a climatically active zone.

The Empire State Building case is particularly instructive because the building has instruments that can detect and record low-amplitude upward lightning discharges invisible to the naked eye, not just the dramatic downward-stroke lightning that observers notice. Instrumented studies by Rakov et al. (1995, *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres*, 100(D12), 25,695–25,706) found that upward-initiated discharges from tall structures are extremely common, occurring at rates that dwarf the downward-stroke frequency. The same-location concentration of lightning is, if anything, more extreme than visible observation suggests.

**The lightning rod proves the claim false.** The most elegant refutation of the lightning-never-strikes-twice claim lies in the device invented to protect buildings from lightning: the lightning rod. Benjamin Franklin proposed the lightning rod in 1752, explicitly on the physical principle that a well-grounded pointed conductor would attract lightning and safely conduct its energy to ground, thereby protecting adjacent structures (Franklin, B., 1751, *Experiments and Observations on Electricity*, London: E. Cave; discussion in Cohen, I.B., 1990, *Benjamin Franklin's Science*, Harvard University Press, pp. 75–98). The lightning rod works precisely because lightning reliably returns to the same attractively positioned, well-grounded conductor. A lightning rod that attracted lightning only once and thereafter became immune to strikes would be a useless safety device. The hundreds of millions of lightning rods protecting structures around the world have functioned effectively for nearly three centuries on the explicit physical premise that lightning repeatedly strikes the same elevated conductive points. The popular saying, taken literally, would require every one of those rods to become ineffective after its first strike.

**What lightning actually targets.** Statistical studies of ground lightning strike distribution consistently show strong non-uniformity. Isolated tall trees in open landscapes are struck at dramatically higher rates than surrounding lower vegetation (Bech, J., & Gayà, M., 2013, *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres*, 118(24), 13,600–13,615). Mountain summits are hotspots for cloud-to-ground lightning concentrated by orography (Soula, S. & Chauzy, S., 2001, *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres*, 106(D11), 11,589–11,601). Communication towers, broadcast masts, and tall buildings attract lightning in proportion to their height advantage over surroundings. Humans in open landscapes are high-priority strike targets compared to humans sheltered in structures precisely because they become relatively tall, exposed, and conductive in those environments. The physics guarantees recurrence to the same high-priority targets.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The gambler's fallacy applied to meteorology.** The most direct cognitive mechanism behind the lightning myth is a variant of the gambler's fallacy — the erroneous belief that prior outcomes in an independent random process affect future probabilities. In its classic casino form, this becomes: "the roulette wheel has come up red five times in a row, so black is now 'due.'" Applied to lightning: "this tree was struck last year, so it has used up its strike quota." The fallacy arises from a misattribution of the law of large numbers, which governs the distribution of outcomes across a suitably long sequence, to individual events where no such depletion mechanism exists. Lightning strikes are not drawn from a finite pool. The cloud does not remember striking a particular tree and skip it the next time.

The gambler's fallacy is robustly documented in human probabilistic reasoning across many domains (Tversky, A. & Kahneman, D., 1971, *Psychological Bulletin*, 76(2), 105–110). It appears to be a product of the brain's representation of random processes as inherently self-correcting — an intuition that works reasonably well for biological population dynamics and resource competition but breaks down completely for memoryless physical processes. Lightning physics is memoryless: the charge separation that determines where the stepped leader descends is indifferent to the strike history of any location.

**The uniqueness intuition.** Beyond the gambler's fallacy, the saying also taps a broader intuition about the uniqueness of dramatic events. Psychologically, human beings tend to encode significant events as specific, located, individual occurrences that do not repeat in exactly the same form. A car accident at a particular intersection, a fire in a specific building, a flood along a distinct stretch of river — these feel like discrete historical events that have "happened" to their locations and are in some sense resolved. This uniqueness encoding may reflect genuinely useful cognitive economics when applied to some categories of events (a predator that hunts successfully in one location may not return), but it is a distortion when applied to physical attractors that remain structurally identical across successive storms.

**The consolation function as epidemiological advantage.** An important piece of epistemic novelty in understanding why this particular false claim survives so effectively is its emotional and social function. The saying is almost always deployed as a form of reassurance — offered to someone who has already experienced a bad event, or to defuse anxiety about recurring catastrophe. This consolatory function confers strong social epidemiological advantages on the claim: it is useful in a social interaction context, it feels kind to say and pleasant to hear, and rejecting it (by pointing out its factual falsity) requires delivering a more frightening message about the actual physics. The claim thus benefits from what might be called motivated tolerance — listeners who might otherwise scrutinise it are disinclined to do so because believing it feels better than not believing it. Claims that serve comfort are empirically stickier than claims that merely describe the world accurately (Heath, C., Bell, C. & Sternberg, E., 2001, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 81(6), 1028–1041, on transmissibility of emotionally resonant information).

**Proverb authority and formulaic language.** Proverbs occupy a peculiar epistemic position in most cultures: they arrive pre-tagged as collective wisdom, carrying the implicit authority of ancestral validation. When something is presented as a proverb — recognisable by its rhythmic structure, its brevity, its familiar phrasing — recipients process it as received wisdom rather than as a factual claim requiring evaluation. This proverb-processing mode suppresses the normal schema for evaluating assertions. "Lightning never strikes twice" activates the listener's proverb-recognition system before it activates the fact-checking system, and once processed as proverbial wisdom, the claim enjoys a form of cognitive immunity to contradiction that novel factual claims do not.

**Survivorship asymmetry in personal experience.** A final reinforcing mechanism is the asymmetry between people's relevant personal experience. Almost no one personally knows an individual who has been struck by lightning twice, and very few people have direct observation of a physical location struck twice in their presence. The rarity of personal counter-experience leaves the field open for the proverb's authority to go unchallenged. The counter-evidence that would most effectively dislodge the belief — direct repetition of a dramatic event — is precisely the evidence least accessible to most individuals. Population-level observation (the Empire State Building; lightning rod statistics) requires that one actively seek information rather than rely on available personal experience, a step most people never take.


## 4. Verdict

**Refuted.** The claim that lightning never strikes the same place twice is meteorologically false. The physical mechanism of lightning formation — charge induction concentrated at geometrically prominent, conductive points — guarantees that the same elevated or exposed locations will attract lightning repeatedly through successive storms. The Empire State Building's documented strike average of approximately twenty-three per year per year (Rakov & Uman, 2003; Krider et al., 1980, *Journal of Applied Meteorology*) constitutes definitive empirical refutation of the literal claim. The invention and multi-century successful operation of the lightning rod depends entirely on the opposite physical principle: that lightning reliably returns to the same attractively positioned conductor.

The saying is false as meteorology. It retains expressive value as a figure of speech about the non-recurrence of certain kinds of good or bad fortune, but even in that figurative sense its accuracy depends on context, and its meteorological authority is nil. No safety behaviour based on the literal claim — such as sheltering under a previously struck tree — is defensible.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The lightning-never-strikes-twice myth sits within a broader family of weather folk beliefs that combine genuine observation with faulty extrapolation. Both the false claim that lightning never strikes twice and the false claim that rubber tyres protect car occupants from lightning (they do not; it is the metal frame that provides a Faraday cage effect) reflect the same pattern: folk knowledge attaches to a memorable formulation, simplified past the threshold of accuracy, that then circulates with the authority of received wisdom. Neither claim is malicious; both are genuinely trying to describe a real feature of weather behaviour; both oversimplify in a way that produces factual inversion.

The lightning-twice myth is also notable as a case where a well-known proverb is trivially refuted by one of history's most famous scientific inventions, created by one of history's best-known scientists. Benjamin Franklin's lightning rod is taught in primary school curricula around the world, usually in the same contexts — electricity, weather, early American history — where the lightning myth also circulates. The cognitive coexistence of these two incompatible pieces of knowledge in the same head, without generating a contradiction that forces resolution, illustrates how well-compartmentalised folk knowledge can be from formally acquired scientific knowledge. The proverb lives in one compartment; the lightning rod lives in another; they do not meet.

From a pedagogy and science communication standpoint, the lightning-rod refutation represents an ideal teaching intervention: no specialised knowledge is required, the refutation is memorable, and it reverses the intuitive direction of the myth rather than simply denying it. Telling someone "lightning prefers the same places — that is why lightning rods work" is both accurate and surprisingly instructive.

The myth also has practical safety implications, since a person who believes lightning will not restrike a location may underestimate the danger of remaining in an exposed elevated position after an initial nearby strike during an ongoing storm. The National Weather Service and equivalent agencies in other countries consistently emphasise that the correct response to any nearby lightning includes seeking durable shelter and relocating away from elevated, isolated, or conductive objects — with no exception for locations already struck (NOAA National Weather Service, Lightning Safety Guidelines, 2022, updated annually at weather.gov/safety/lightning).


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has addressed the lightning-strikes-twice claim and rates it **False**, noting the extensive empirical documentation of repeat lightning strikes on structures including the Empire State Building, lightning rods, and tall trees (*Snopes*, "Does Lightning Strike The Same Place Twice?", snopes.com/fact-check/lightning-strikes-twice/). The Snopes entry emphasises both the physical mechanisms and the observational record.

The claim is also addressed by NOAA's public science communication resources, which explicitly debunk it as a dangerous misconception that could lead to unsafe shelter decisions, and by the American Meteorological Society's weather literacy materials. The fact-checking literature on this claim is thus both clear and consistent: the verdict is false without qualification.

The broader challenge for science communicators addressing this claim is the disjunction between the figurative and literal versions. Any correction of the meteorological falsehood risks being read as a rejection of the proverbial wisdom about the non-recurrence of dramatic life events, which is a different and more contextually complicated claim. The most effective corrections are those that explicitly distinguish the figurative meaning — which may or may not be useful counsel depending on context — from the meteorological claim, which is simply wrong.
