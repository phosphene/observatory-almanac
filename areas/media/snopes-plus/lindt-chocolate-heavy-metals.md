# The Facts Behind Accusations That Lindt Chocolate Contains Dangerous Heavy Metals


## 1. The Claim

For several years, and with renewed intensity following a wave of TikTok videos in late 2024 and a 2023 class-action lawsuit, claims have circulated that Lindt dark chocolate bars — specifically the Excellence Dark Chocolate 70% Cocoa and Excellence Dark Chocolate 85% Cocoa variants — contain unsafe levels of the heavy metals lead and cadmium. The claim originated in consumer testing conducted in 2022 by two organisations: Consumer Reports, a veteran nonprofit consumer advocacy organisation, and As You Sow, an environmental and social corporate accountability advocacy group. Both entities reported that their testing found the Lindt bars contained concentrations of at least one heavy metal exceeding California's maximum allowable dose levels under the state's Proposition 65 regulatory framework.

In November 2024, a TikTok video that attracted significant circulation described the situation in blunt terms: "Lindt chocolate... apparently they've got lead and cadmium... not supposed to be there... they wanted to say well we never said lead wasn't in our chocolate." The video exemplified a common amplification pattern: the accurate core of the claim (testing found elevated metal levels) is preserved while the nuance is stripped (California standards vs. federal standards; contested methodology; ongoing litigation without verdict; the near-universal presence of these metals at some level in many foods). The claim has been escalating in visibility since the 2023 lawsuit survived a dismissal attempt, making it more likely to generate additional media coverage through late 2024 and into 2025.

Lindt has consistently disputed the claims, with a spokesperson asserting that the company's products "comply with all applicable safety standards and declaration requirements and are safe to consume." The company's position highlights that under California's Proposition 65, there is a disclosure requirement for products exceeding defined thresholds — and Lindt maintains its products do not exceed those thresholds. The fact that two independent watchdog organisations reported contrary testing results, and that the resulting lawsuit was not dismissed, indicates that the dispute is substantive rather than frivolous.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Two independent consumer organisations conducted testing and reported elevated metal levels.** Consumer Reports published its findings in 2022, analysing the heavy metal content of dark chocolate products from multiple manufacturers using what its methodology documentation describes as "triple quadrupole inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-QQQ-MS)" — a highly sensitive analytical technique capable of detecting trace elements at extremely low concentrations. Agilent, a laboratory equipment manufacturer, has described ICP-QQQ-MS as providing "around ten times lower detection limits for most elements" compared to standard ICP-MS. As You Sow conducted parallel testing using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and published its results in the same period.

Both organisations tested against California's maximum allowable dose levels (MADLs) under Proposition 65. According to the California Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment (OEHHA), the MADL for cadmium is 4.1 micrograms per day and for lead is 0.5 micrograms per day. Consumer Reports tested a one-ounce serving; As You Sow used one serving size as defined on product packaging. Their findings were comparable:

- The Lindt Excellence 70% Cocoa bar tested below the MADL for lead but above the MADL for cadmium in one-serving quantities.
- The Lindt Excellence 85% Cocoa bar tested above the MADL for lead but below the MADL for cadmium in one-serving quantities.

These findings do not claim that a single serving would cause immediate harm. California's Proposition 65 MADLs are designed as disclosure thresholds — amounts at which manufacturers are required to warn consumers — rather than as levels at which a discrete health event is expected to occur. The logic of the framework is cumulative exposure management: if a person regularly consumes products that each contribute trace amounts of a heavy metal, the aggregate daily exposure across diet matters. MADLs are set conservatively to account for the cumulative contribution from all sources, not just chocolate.

**The scientific context: heavy metals in cocoa are systemic, not unique to Lindt.** Several peer-reviewed studies establish that heavy metal contamination in chocolate and cocoa products is a widespread phenomenon of the food supply rather than a company-specific failing.

A 2018 study by Abt et al. (*Food Additives & Contaminants: Part B*) — conducted by FDA researchers — tested cadmium and lead levels across cocoa powder, dark chocolate, milk chocolate, and cocoa nib products available in the U.S. market, and identified items across multiple brands with cadmium levels up to 3.15 micrograms per kilogram and lead levels up to 0.38 micrograms per kilogram, establishing that Lindt is not an exceptional case but part of a broader pattern.

A 2019 peer-reviewed study by Kim et al. (*Nutrients*) estimated that Americans consume an average of 4.63 micrograms of cadmium per day from all dietary sources — a figure that already exceeds California's MADL of 4.1 micrograms per day before any chocolate consumption is considered. The cadmium comes from a diverse range of common foods including rice, bread, spinach, and sunflower seeds. An FDA Total Diet Study survey (2018–2020) found that the foods with the highest cadmium concentrations included spinach, sunflower seeds, and cocoa powder; the highest lead concentrations were in baking powder and cocoa powder.

**Why cacao is particularly prone to heavy metal accumulation.** The biogeochemical mechanisms by which cocoa beans accumulate cadmium and lead are well understood in the food science literature. Cadmium is absorbed by cocoa bean plants through the same root uptake pathways used to absorb essential micronutrients like zinc. Soils in the primary cocoa-growing regions of South America and West Africa contain naturally occurring cadmium at varying levels; some regions, particularly in South American growing areas, have volcanic soils with elevated cadmium concentrations that bioaccumulate in cocoa beans regardless of agricultural practice. Lead enters chocolate through post-harvest processing rather than plant absorption — during fermentation, drying, and transport, cocoa beans can be exposed to environmental lead, particularly in processing environments in regions where lead in dust and soil remains elevated due to historical industrial activity or leaded fuel use.

**The regulatory landscape: no U.S. national standard.** As of the time of Snopes' reporting, the United States had no federal standards for maximum allowable levels of cadmium or lead in food. California's Proposition 65 MADLs are the most protective standards available in the U.S. context, which is why Consumer Reports and As You Sow chose them as the comparison benchmark. The FDA has acknowledged the gap and launched its "Closer to Zero" initiative, which aims to develop action levels — amounts at which the FDA would direct food manufacturers to reduce contamination — for lead, cadmium, arsenic, and mercury in foods, with a priority emphasis on foods commonly consumed by infants and young children.

**The litigation status.** The class-action lawsuit (*Gralia v. Lindt & Sprüngli USA Inc.*, Eastern District of New York) filed in 2023 alleged that Lindt failed to disclose that its 70% and 85% cocoa bars contained unsafe levels of lead and cadmium, and that consumers were injured by this omission. Lindt sought to have the lawsuit dismissed; that attempt failed. As of the most recent update cited in Snopes' reporting — November 2024 — the case was in early stages of discovery. A lawsuit in discovery does not constitute evidence of wrongdoing; it constitutes evidence that the allegations survived preliminary legal scrutiny, which is a lower standard than proof.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Consumer Reports as credibility anchor.** Consumer Reports is among the most trusted consumer advocacy organisations in the United States, with a decades-long track record of product testing and a methodology that explicitly excludes commercial advertising relationships. When Consumer Reports says a product contains heavy metals, its audience is conditioned by experience to take that seriously. The 2022 report on dark chocolate and heavy metals was not a fringe finding but a systematically conducted analysis across multiple brands, and Lindt appeared alongside other major manufacturers. The credibility transfer from Consumer Reports to the claim about Lindt is well-founded: this is not the same as an anonymous TikTok video; it represents documented testing by a credible institution.

**The information deficit about food safety regulation.** Many consumers have limited familiarity with the distinction between California's Proposition 65 standards and federal FDA standards, or with the difference between disclosure thresholds and toxicity thresholds. When claims say Lindt products "exceed safe levels," the implicit framework for most consumers is that there is a single objective threshold of safety, and that the product fails it. The actual situation — multiple regulatory standards with different philosophical bases, some without federal equivalents, all contested by the manufacturer — is considerably more complex and does not resolve into a clean binary. The information deficit about how food safety regulation actually works creates a zone in which "unsafe levels" sounds more categorical than it is.

**The genuinely contested nature of heavy metal exposure science.** Unlike some food safety claims where the science is settled and the claim is simply false, the health effects of low-level chronic dietary exposure to cadmium and lead are genuinely contested areas of nutritional epidemiology. Lead has no known safe exposure level in children (as established by the CDC, which in 2024 updated its blood lead reference value to reflect this understanding). Cadmium is a known carcinogen under OSHA classification. Neither of these facts means that a serving of Lindt dark chocolate causes measurable harm to a healthy adult, but they mean that the claim "these heavy metals are dangerous" is not simply fabricated — it corresponds to real toxicological properties of these substances.

This is the epistemic novelty most important to surface in this case: heavy metals in chocolate are not a fabricated health scare. Lead and cadmium have documented mechanisms of harm at sufficient doses; the scientific questions are about what "sufficient doses" means in the context of typical consumption patterns, cumulative dietary exposure, and individual vulnerability factors (age, weight, existing exposure burden). Claims that oscillate between "dangerous levels found in chocolate" and "chocolate is safe to eat" are both partly right, depending on which questions are being asked at which resolution. The claim is not false in the way that claims about, say, chocolate *causing* cancer are false; it is contested in the way that empirically grounded regulatory disputes are contested.

**Social media amplification via the lawsuit.** The 2023 lawsuit provides a particularly powerful amplification mechanism because it moves the claim from the realm of consumer advocacy testing — which audiences may devalue as advocacy rather than science — into the legal domain, where claims are subject to judicial scrutiny. The survival of the lawsuit past the dismissal motion signals to many audiences that the claims have more than frivolous basis. This judicial plausibility attribution is a separate mechanism from the scientific plausibility attribution provided by Consumer Reports, and the two compound each other in the social media environment where the claim circulates.


## 4. Verdict

**Unproven — real testing by credible organizations found elevated metal levels against California's standards, but Lindt contests the findings, the litigation is ongoing, and independent proof of unsafe levels under all applicable standards cannot be established.** Consumer Reports and As You Sow conducted legitimate testing using recognised analytical methods and found Lindt's 70% and 85% cocoa bars exceeded at least one California Proposition 65 MADL in one-serving quantities. This is a real finding and deserves consumer awareness. However, Lindt disputes the findings and maintains compliance with all applicable regulations; the U.S. has no federal standard against which to evaluate the claim; the class-action lawsuit is in discovery without verdict; and neither watchdog organisation's testing has been subjected to formal independent peer review. The claim is appropriately rated unproven because the evidence is real but contested in ways that do not permit a categorical conclusion.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Lindt heavy metals case is a microcosm of a broader structural problem in U.S. food safety regulation: the absence of enforceable federal standards for heavy metal contamination in foods consumed by adults. The Closer to Zero initiative indicates FDA awareness of the gap, but the initiative is primarily focused on children's foods and has not produced binding action levels for most food categories as of the reporting period. In the absence of federal standards, California's Proposition 65 becomes the de facto national standard for consumer advocates — which is both understandable and imperfect, given that California's standards were designed for disclosure rather than as absolute safety thresholds, and that their levels are set to be protective even for daily consumption of a single product, without accounting for the degree to which most people consume many different foods.

The broader question of heavy metal contamination in cocoa and chocolate deserves sustained public health attention beyond the specific Lindt controversy. Abt et al.'s (2018) FDA study established that contamination is present across the market; Kim et al.'s (2019) dietary intake study established that Americans already exceed California's cadmium MADL from food sources other than chocolate. These findings suggest that addressing heavy metals in the food supply requires systemic interventions — soil management practices in cocoa-growing regions, processing environment controls, coordinated international standards that incentivise cocoa producers to manage cadmium accumulation through fertilisation and pH management — rather than just litigation against individual brands. The Lindt lawsuit may generate settlement pressure that creates incentives for the company to reduce heavy metal levels, but it will not address the systemic agricultural and processing conditions that generate the contamination across the industry.

For consumers seeking practical guidance, the most calibrated position available from the published science is Dr. Melissa Melough's (2019, as quoted to the *New York Times*) suggestion that consumers concerned about lead and cadmium in dark chocolate could consider limiting consumption to approximately one ounce per day and seeking products with lower measured heavy metal levels where that information is available.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published a detailed examination of this claim in December 2024, written by Laerke Christensen, and assigned an "unproven" rating that reflects the genuine evidentiary complexity. The rating represents an intellectually honest position in a case where the available evidence does not resolve cleanly into true or false: consumer testing by a credible organisation found results that exceed California standards; the manufacturer disputes the findings; U.S. federal standards do not exist against which to calibrate the dispute; and the litigation is ongoing.

For fact-checkers encountering food safety claims of this type, several analytical moves are particularly valuable: (1) distinguish between the testing finding (what a specific organisation measured) and the public health claim (whether those measured levels are definitively unsafe); (2) identify which regulatory standard is being used as the benchmark, and whether that standard is contested, state-specific, or otherwise exceptional; (3) check whether the claimed contamination is specific to one manufacturer or industry-wide — industry-wide contamination radically changes the claim's implications; (4) examine whether the manufacturer has issued a substantive technical response or merely a corporate denial; and (5) note the litigation status and be precise about what legal survival of a dismissal motion does and does not establish. The discipline of separating "real testing found real results" from "those results definitively prove the food is unsafe" is foundational to accurate reporting on food safety claims where the science is genuinely contested and regulatory frameworks are both fragmented and evolving.
