## The Claim

On June 10, 2021, a Facebook post by Sandra Fan circulated widely on social media, showing two photographs of a man crouched beside a large male lion, one hand pressed into the animal's extended paw in what appeared to be a gentle massage. The caption accompanying the post informed viewers that the man, a Briton named Alex Larenty, lived on a game reserve in South Africa and spent his days giving lions foot massages. It explained that he had first discovered lions' enjoyment of paw-handling while applying cream to treat infections, and that the behaviour had evolved into a daily ritual that bonded him with the animals. The photos, the post emphasised, were not hunting photographs.

The explicit claim embedded in the post is a double-component one: these are authentic photographs (not doctored, not from a staged event, not hunting-related), and they show what they purport to show — a man with an established relationship of trust with a lion, engaging in behaviour that the lion appears to enjoy. Snopes journalist Dan MacGuill, writing on June 21, 2021, confirmed both components. The rating is "True."

---

## What's Actually True

The photographs are authentic and unedited. The two images circulating in the viral post have documented provenance. The first, taken in May 2010, is held by Getty Images with a credit to photographer Matthew Tabaccos of Barcroft Media. The Getty caption identifies the man as "Trainer Alex Larenty" and the lion as "Jamu," a nine-year-old male lion residing at the Lion Park near Johannesburg, South Africa. The caption provides specific detail: the game Larenty plays with Jamu is called "This Little Piggy," involving the gentle manipulation of the lion's toes and paw pads, and has become the animal's "favourite game." The second photograph was originally published in the Daily Mail in 2009 via the Caters licensing agency, and also reportedly depicts Jamu.

Alex Larenty emigrated from Chipping Norton in the United Kingdom to South Africa and became a handler and keeper at the Lion Park outside Johannesburg. The Lion Park, subsequently rebranded and relocated as the Lion and Safari Park, was a commercial facility that allowed visitors to interact with and observe lions, cheetahs, and other large African predators. Larenty's relationship with Jamu began through a practical veterinary procedure: the application of insect-repellent cream to protect lions from parasites and biting insects. Larenty described to Getty Images how he discovered that Jamu enjoyed being scratched and tickled during this process, that the lion would visibly relax and display what Larenty characterised as enjoyment, and that the "This Little Piggy" massage game evolved from those early interactions.

The caption's claim — that Larenty had "built up a relationship with Jamu through complete mutual trust" and could safely enter the pride and interact closely with large adult male lions — was contextualised by Larenty himself: such interactions are possible only because of a specific, long-cultivated bilateral relationship built through years of contact from the animal's birth, not a general property of lion behaviour toward humans. A stranger attempting the same interaction would, as the Getty caption notes, "end up as lunch."

## Why People Believe This

The Facebook post's framing — "This is not a hunting photo" — reveals a specific semiotic environment in which these images circulated in mid-2021. The qualifier was not arbitrary. It responded to a documented pattern in which photographs of humans in close physical contact with large African predators were assumed, sometimes correctly, to be connected to South Africa's controversial "canned hunting" industry — the practice of raising lions in captivity and then releasing them into fenced enclosures to be shot by paying hunters.

Canned hunting, which was documented in South Africa through the 2000s and 2010s, operates through a pipeline that begins with exactly the kind of human-habituated, hand-raised lion that Jamu represents. Lions raised in facilities where human contact is normalised become docile toward humans in a way that makes them easy, predictable targets. Conservation groups and journalists documented the path from "lion cub petting" at tourist facilities through to adult lions sold to canned hunting operators and shot in enclosed spaces by hunters purchasing what were often described as guaranteed trophies.

The social context surrounding the viral post was shaped by public awareness of this pipeline. Sandra Fan's explicit "not a hunting photo" declaration was pre-emptive — addressing a specific, reasonable suspicion that viewers familiar with canned hunting discourse might bring to the images. This framing was necessary precisely because the images do come from a facility — the Lion Park — that was itself implicated in the canned hunting pipeline.

As Dan MacGuill (2021) reported in the Snopes entry, CBS's "60 Minutes" broadcast in 2014 documented that the Lion Park had been selling lions to traders known to supply canned hunting operations. The Lion Park responded to the "60 Minutes" investigation by stating the sales had been inadvertent — they did not know the lions were destined for canned hunting — and that they had changed their practices to prevent recurrence. MacGuill notes that Larenty himself appears to have no involvement in hunting. The photographs show a genuine and evidently affectionate relationship between a specific man and a specific lion, both of whom were real, at a specific facility with a complicated record.

## The Epistemic Gap

The Snopes verdict addresses the authenticity question cleanly and correctly. The images are real, Larenty is real, Jamu is real, and the foot massage is real. The gap_category here is "captive-wildlife-ethics-complexity" because the full context of the story cannot be reduced to an authentication question.

The epistemic territory Snopes does not enter — appropriately, given its focus — concerns the ethical architecture within which photographs like these exist and circulate. The Lion Park's involvement in the canned hunting pipeline, however inadvertent, places Jamu and Larenty's relationship inside a system that has been extensively documented as causing significant harm to the very animals it presents as objects of affection.

The mechanism is one that Masson and McCarthy (1996, *When Elephants Weep: The Emotional Lives of Animals*, Delta Books) describe in other captive contexts: the close bond between keeper and animal creates an authentic emotional reality that coexists with — and can be instrumentalised by — institutional structures that treat the same animals as commodities. Jamu's relationship with Larenty may have been genuinely affectionate and mutually beneficial. The fact that the facility housing Jamu sold lions to canned hunting operations does not retroactively contaminate the specific relationship documented in those photographs. But it does mean that the circulation of the photographs without that context is incomplete in a way that matters to people who care about lion conservation.

The post's "not a hunting photo" framing reveals an awareness of this complexity without addressing it. It says: don't interpret this through the canned hunting lens. But it doesn't say: the facility where this happened had its own problems. The Snopes entry provides that missing context in its closing paragraphs, which is exemplary — but the viral story's continued circulation removes those paragraphs, leaving the uplifting human-lion bonding narrative floating free of the institutional context that complicates it.

This gap is epistemic in the sense that a full understanding of the claim requires contextual knowledge — about canned hunting, about the specific facility, about how captive lion facilities in South Africa operate — that most viewers sharing the photograph lack, and that the photograph itself does not provide.

## Broader Scientific Context

The interaction between Larenty and Jamu can be situated within the broader scientific literature on human-animal bonds and their behavioural mechanisms in captive feline settings. Cats of all sizes, domestic and wild, have touch-sensitive facial and body regions richly innervated with nerve endings that respond to gentle contact with measurable relaxation responses — reduced heart rate, lowered cortisol levels, and behavioural signs of affiliative engagement (Vitale et al., 2019, *Current Biology*; for domestic felids; analogous mechanisms operate in the Felidae broadly).

The specific phenomenon Larenty describes — Jamu appearing to "smile" and relax during paw handling — is consistent with documented responses of large felids to targeted tactile stimulation in keeper relationships. Studies of cheetah (*Acinonyx jubatus*) and lion welfare in captive settings have documented that tactile enrichment by familiar keepers — particularly grooming-adjacent contact — produces measurable reductions in stress indicators (Leighty et al., 2010, *Zoo Biology*). The paw massage in the photographs may appear whimsical, but it operationalises a legitimate enrichment principle: maintaining affiliative tactile contact with familiar individuals reduces baseline stress in social carnivores accustomed to allogrooming.

Larenty's description of the relationship as built on "complete mutual trust" through years of contact from Jamu's birth reflects the critical role of early socialisation and the sensitive period for human imprinting in large felids. Lions raised with consistent human contact from early life develop behavioural dispositions toward humans that are qualitatively different from those of wild-born or minimally socialised animals. This is not a general cognitive phenomenon but a developmental one: the window in which such bonds form is limited, and the bond formed during it is durable and specific to the individuals involved.

The Lion and Safari Park relocated from its original Gauteng site to North West province in 2016. As of 2021, the park had maintained a public commitment to conservation and education following the 2014 controversy, though independent monitoring of its current practices regarding animal sales and disposals is limited in the publicly available literature.

## Verdict and Reflection

The Snopes verdict of "True" is accurate and appropriate. The photographs are authentic, the subjects are real, and the relationship depicted is genuine. The images do show Alex Larenty giving Jamu a foot massage at the Lion Park in South Africa, and the photographs were taken in 2009 and 2010.

The value of the Snopes entry — beyond the authentication — is its willingness to include the canned hunting context in the closing paragraphs: not to retroactively invalidate the relationship depicted, but to ensure that the full institutional backdrop is legible to readers who encounter the story only through the photographs. That contextualization is part of what good fact-checking offers: not just "is this real?" but "what else do you need to know to understand what you're looking at?"

The photographs remain striking and the relationship they document appears genuine. What is also genuine, and requires acknowledgement, is the larger system in which that relationship was embedded — one that the Lion Park's own admission in 2014 confirms was not uniformly aligned with the welfare of the animals in its care. Both things are true. That's the honest result of clear-eyed engagement with a complicated image.

## The Lion Park's History and the Canned Hunting Industry

Understanding the Lion Park's context requires some background on what "canned hunting" means in practice in South Africa, because the term is sometimes misunderstood as referring to hunting that happens very easily — which it is — but the ethical problem it represents is more specific. Canned hunting refers to the shooting of lions (and occasionally other animals) in enclosed spaces from which the animal cannot escape. The animal is typically habituated to human presence, fed, and deprived of the normal flight distances that wild lions use to avoid humans. Hunters pay substantial sums — fees in the tens of thousands of US dollars are commonly cited — for a guaranteed or near-guaranteed trophy kill in conditions that require minimal hunting skill.

The supply chain for canned hunting operations in South Africa runs through captive breeding facilities, including legitimate tourist and educational facilities that breed lions, raise cubs, and then sell adult or sub-adult animals when they are no longer commercially useful as cub-petting or walking-with-lions attractions. Investigative journalism by Bloomberg, the BBC, and CBS's "60 Minutes" (which produced the 2014 report cited by MacGuill) documented this pipeline in detail: cubs are bred, handled extensively, used for cub-petting and tourist photography, then sold onward through a chain of traders who ultimately supply fenced hunting operations.

The Lion Park, when confronted with CBS's documentation that its lions had entered this pipeline, maintained that the sales were inadvertent — that they had sold animals to traders without knowing the animals' ultimate fate. This is plausible as a general claim: the intermediate traders in the canned hunting supply chain do not always disclose the end use of purchased animals. It is also a claim that is difficult to verify and difficult to disprove. The Lion Park's 2014 response stated that practices had been changed to ensure traceability of animal sales and prevent recurrence.

The post-2016 relocation of the facility to North West province and its rebranding as the Lion and Safari Park represent an institutional discontinuity in the public record. The social responsibilities and practices of the relocated facility are not independently verified in the academic or investigative journalism literature as of the time of writing.

What is clear is that Alex Larenty's relationship with Jamu existed within a facility that was both genuinely the site of authentic human-lion bonds and, at least until 2014, a participant in a supply chain that led to lion deaths in enclosed hunting contexts. The photographs of Larenty and Jamu are not diminished by this context in terms of their authenticity or their accurate depiction of a real relationship. But the circulation of those photographs without that context — particularly with the "not a hunting photo" framing that was designed to ward off the specific suspicion that canned hunting awareness creates — reflects the genuine difficulty of communicating complex institutional realities through individual images.
