## §1 — Claim & Verdict Overview

In early April 2021, social media users across multiple platforms circulated a photograph of a handwritten sign apparently posted on the door of a Little Caesars pizza restaurant. The sign read: *"To the person going through our trash for their next meal, you're a human being and worth more than a meal from a dumpster. Please come in during operating hours for a couple slices of hot pizza and a cup of water at no charge. No questions asked."* Posts sharing the image invariably presented it as a heartwarming demonstration of corporate or individual generosity, and many users implied or stated explicitly that the offer was currently active at Little Caesars locations.

Snopes investigator Bethania Palma (2021) rated this claim **Outdated**. The sign, and the gesture it represented, were real — but they dated from late August and early September 2017, not from 2021. The sign had been posted at a specific franchise location in Fargo, North Dakota, and had been covered by local television station KVLY at the time. By 2021, the sign was no longer posted. A spokesperson for Little Caesars confirmed this to Snopes: "The sign is no longer posted in the store, but the story is true and the franchisee did provide free pizza to people." The episode therefore sits at the junction of confirmed historical truth and actively misleading present-tense viral circulation — a class of misinformation that Snopes's "Outdated" rating is specifically designed to capture.

What makes this case notable beyond its simple timing is the speed and recurrence of its viral cycles, the additional context that was stripped with each resharing (including a burglary at the same location just days after the original story broke), and the epistemological questions it raises about how audiences receive and evaluate time-indexed claims in photo-based social media environments.

## §2 — The Full Story: Fargo, 2017

The sign was the initiative of Michelle Lussier, the general manager of a Little Caesars franchise in Fargo, North Dakota. According to reporting by KVLY television (August 2017), the restaurant had noticed evidence of someone going through its dumpster to find food, and Lussier decided that rather than simply accepting this or taking preventive measures, she would address the underlying need directly. The handwritten sign was posted on the restaurant's front door during operating hours.

Lussier told KVLY: "Don't feel embarrassed if you're hungry and you don't want to do what others do, you know stand on the corner for handouts. You feel down about that, you're more than welcome to come here and grab a couple slices." The gesture was explicitly framed around the dignity of the person who had been searching the dumpster — an acknowledgment that dumpster-diving for food is typically not a choice but a response to circumstances severe enough that the risk of social stigma and potential legal consequence (dumpster diving is illegal in some jurisdictions) is outweighed by the immediate need for food.

The KVLY story about Lussier's sign went viral almost immediately, generating wide coverage nationally and internationally. A donation box was set up at the restaurant to collect money to further support local homeless-assistance efforts. Less than ten days after the original story aired, KVLY ran a follow-up report: the donation box had been stolen. Lussier confirmed to KVLY that despite the theft, the restaurant was continuing to donate leftover pizzas to local food shelters and was still accepting donations by other means.

This follow-up story — the theft — received considerably less viral circulation than the original. The structure of its coverage was thus asymmetric: the heartwarming sign reached millions, the theft and its aftermath reached far fewer. By 2021, when the meme resurfaced, users sharing it had no access to either the timeline or the theft context unless they actively sought it out. The stripped image circulated on its own emotional valence, presenting an apparently current and ongoing offer that had ended years before.

The episode's broader food-insecurity context is significant. Fargo, North Dakota, like many mid-sized American cities, has experienced persistent challenges with homelessness and food insecurity. The USDA Economic Research Service (Coleman-Jensen et al., 2020, *Food Security in the U.S., Economic Information Bulletin*) found that in any given year, approximately 10–12% of U.S. households experience food insecurity, defined as limited or uncertain access to adequate food. North Dakota's rate has generally tracked below the national average but is not negligible, and urban-rural differences within the state mean that Fargo, as the state's largest city, concentrates a disproportionate share of the state's food-insecure population.

The Little Caesars corporation itself operates primarily through franchising, meaning that the decision made by Lussier was a franchise-level management decision rather than a corporate policy. The specifics of Little Caesars franchise agreements — which govern what franchisees may and must do with unsold food — are not public, but the broader context of fast-food franchise "food recovery" policies is relevant. The Food Recovery Network and similar organizations have documented that fast-food franchise agreements have historically imposed significant obstacles to food donation, including liability concerns, brand standards, and logistical requirements (Feeding America, *Waste and Hunger in America*, 2018). Lussier's sign represented a workaround: by inviting the person in and serving them during operating hours as a customer would be served, the transaction fit within typical franchise operational parameters.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Temporal Decontextualization Problem in Affective Virality

The Little Caesars dumpster divers case sits at the center of a methodologically important subclass of misinformation: **temporally decontextualized affective content**. This class differs from straightforward fabrication, manipulated imagery, or misleading statistics in that the underlying content is factually true — a real person, a real sign, a real gesture documented by credible local journalism. What is false, or at minimum actively misleading, is the implied temporal frame: the suggestion, never stated explicitly but effectively conveyed by present-tense sharing, that the sign is currently posted and the offer currently available.

Affective content — content whose primary mechanism of transmission is emotional resonance rather than informational novelty — is particularly vulnerable to temporal decontextualization. Research on viral content characteristics identified emotional arousal (specifically positive valence combined with moderate surprise) as a leading predictor of sharing behavior (Berger & Milkman, 2012, *Journal of Marketing Research*). Heartwarming corporate-generosity stories specifically engage several transmission-favorable features simultaneously: they provide positive affect, create a sense of social membership through shared appreciation, and generate the desire to spread the feeling by sharing the content. None of these transmission motivations depend on the content being current or the offer being available — they depend only on the content being emotionally resonant.

This creates a predictable failure mode in digital information ecosystems: content that was true at time T but is no longer accurate at time T+N circulates robustly precisely because the mechanisms that sustain its circulation are indifferent to its temporal status. A viral fact about a currently existing sign and a viral fact about a sign that existed four years ago are emotionally equivalent for the sharer, but they are informationally quite different for anyone who might make a real-world decision based on the information (such as traveling to a Little Caesars to request free food, or citing the case as evidence of a current corporate policy).

The "Outdated" rating that Snopes developed partly in response to cases like this represents an important epistemic innovation in fact-checking practice. Traditional fact-check verdicts — True, False, Mixed, Unproven — implicitly treat claims as atemporal propositions about a static world. But a significant proportion of circulating viral content consists of claims that were once true and are no longer accurately describable by their initial rating. The "Outdated" category creates a verdict dimension along the timeline axis rather than only the accuracy axis: it says "this was true, and is being shared as though it is still true, but the situation has changed." This is a meaningful and underexplored area where information forensics and epistemological classification intersect.

A further epistemic dimension is the **asymmetric follow-up circulation** documented in this case. The donation-box theft received minimal viral attention compared to the original sign story. Research on negativity bias in news consumption (Soroka et al., 2019, *American Journal of Political Science*) might predict otherwise — negative news is usually more attention-grabbing than positive. But feel-good stories function differently: the audience that shares them is motivated by the positive affect, and a follow-up story of theft and complication disrupts that affect without substituting a comparably shareable positive alternative. The result is systematic within-story informational attrition: the heartwarming initial event retains its maximum shareability while the complicating later events have no comparable virality driver.

This asymmetry means that the public record of the event, as reconstructed from social media circulation, is structurally more positive and less complicated than the actual event. Researchers studying public memory formation in the social media era should note that this kind of selective circulation is not random error but systematically affectively biased: the warm stories survive, the complications drop out.

## §4 — Food Insecurity, Homelessness, and Franchise Goodwill Economics

The dumpster-diving context for the Little Caesars sign requires some elaboration to understand fully. Dumpster diving — the practice of retrieving discarded food or other items from commercial waste containers — is practiced by a diverse population that does not map cleanly onto any single demographic or housing status. Studies of urban food recovery via municipal waste streams found that dumpster divers included homeless individuals, food-insecure housed individuals, freegans (individuals who recover salvageable food as an ideological act against food waste), and opportunistic scavengers of various economic backgrounds (Heynen, 2010, *Environment and Planning A*).

The legal status of dumpster diving in the United States is complex. While the U.S. Supreme Court's 1988 ruling in *California v. Greenwood* held that garbage placed at the curb for collection has no Fourth Amendment privacy protection (establishing that police could search discarded trash without a warrant), municipal ordinances commonly prohibit the practice on other grounds, including trespass (if the dumpster is behind a locked fence or gate), disorderly conduct, and civil or criminal statutes governing scavenging from commercial waste containers. A 2016 survey by Feeding America found that laws restricting dumpster diving from commercial containers were on the books in a substantial fraction of large U.S. cities, though enforcement was highly variable.

The economic context for fast-food restaurant food waste is also relevant. Restaurant-generated food waste is a substantial contributor to U.S. total food waste, which the ReFED organization estimated at approximately 119 million tons annually as of 2019 (*ReFED Insights Engine*, 2021). Fast-food pizza chains specifically operate on high-margin, high-throughput models that produce consistent daily leftover volume: pizzas prepared speculatively for the lunch or dinner rush that are unsold by closing time. The alternative to donation is typically dumpster disposal.

The donation of leftover food from commercial establishments is governed in the United States by the Bill Emerson Good Samaritan Food Donation Act (1996), which provides liability protection to donors who donate food in good faith that is not purposely adulterated or mislabeled. Despite this federal protection, franchise agreement liability provisions have historically operated as a practical barrier to donation, as Lussier's case illustrates through its workaround: she served the person as a customer rather than donating prepared food outside the established chain of custody.

## §5 — The "Outdated" Category in Fact-Check Epistemology

The Little Caesars case illustrates why Snopes's development of the "Outdated" verdict category represents a genuine methodological contribution to fact-checking practice. Several comparable cases illuminate the category's scope.

Historical corporate-goodwill claims that have been stripped of temporal context form a recurring subtype of "Outdated" content: a company's one-time charitable policy, a restaurant's now-discontinued free program, an individual franchise manager's personal initiative that ended when the manager left or the situation changed. These stories are structurally resistant to correction because their temporal decontextualization is invisible in the image itself — the sign, the storefront, the handwritten note does not come with a timestamp visible to casual viewers.

Palma (2021) noted that the Little Caesars sign story had "stayed viral" since 2017—resurgent in at least 2018 and again in April 2021. This multi-cycle virality is characteristic of affectively resonant "outdated" content: the content's emotional appeal is stable across years, so new audiences encountering it for the first time are indistinguishable from repeat audiences in terms of their sharing motivation. Each cycle delivers a new cohort of first-time encounterters, sufficiently large to drive viral metrics even if the core enthusiast audience has already seen it.

Research on correction effectiveness in misinformation contexts suggests that "Outdated"-class claims may be among the hardest to correct, precisely because the underlying claim is not false. Lewandowsky and colleagues (2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) documented the "continued influence effect" — the tendency for misinformation to influence reasoning even after explicit correction. This effect appears to be stronger when the original information is emotionally engaging and when the correction introduces complexity without substituting a comparably compelling alternative narrative. A correction that says "this was true but the sign is gone now" disrupts the feel-good story without substituting an equivalently resonant narrative, making it relatively unsatisfying as a resolution and potentially less memorable than the original viral content.

## §6 — Research Gaps and EARC Assignment

**EARC Assignment: A (Analytical)**

The Little Caesars dumpster divers case raises several research questions that remain underdeveloped in the literature on digital misinformation and food-system sociology.

**Gap 1: Temporal decontextualization as a misinformation category.** While there is substantial literature on fabricated content, manipulated imagery, and out-of-context repurposing of factual events, the specific dynamics of *temporally decontextualized true content* — content that was accurate at time T but is actively misleading at T+N — has not been the subject of focused empirical study. Key questions include: How does the time gap between original event and resurgent circulation affect the magnitude of the misleading effect? Does explicit dating of original content (timestamps, datelines) reduce the spread of temporally decontextualized content? How does the "Outdated" verdict category compare to other verdicts in its ability to reduce the claim's subsequent circulation?

**Gap 2: Asymmetric follow-up circulation.** The pattern documented here — heartwarming initial story reaches millions while complicating follow-up reaches far fewer — has been anecdotally noted by journalists but not systematically studied with cross-platform tracking data. Quantifying this asymmetry across a large sample of feel-good viral stories with complicating follow-ups would yield precise estimates of the within-story information attrition rate attributable to affective selection biases.

**Gap 3: Fast-food franchise culture and informal neighbor-assistance practices.** The literature on corporate social responsibility in franchise contexts (Dahlsrud, 2008, *Corporate Social Responsibility and Environmental Management*) primarily examines formal programs. The Lussier case represents informal, manager-initiated local philanthropy that falls outside formal CSR frameworks. The frequency, geographic distribution, and longevity of such informal franchise-level goodwill practices is unstudied.

**Gap 4: Food insecurity and dignity framing.** Lussier's sign was distinguished by its explicit dignity framing — "you're a human being and worth more than a meal from a dumpster." Research on behavioral interventions in food insecurity contexts (Wansink, 2014, *Slim by Design*; though note replication concerns around Wansink's work generally) has examined how framing affects uptake of food assistance, but the specific effect of dignity-foregrounding language on whether food-insecure individuals access available assistance has not been rigorously studied.
