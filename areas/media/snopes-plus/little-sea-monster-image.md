## The Claim

In October 2025, a photograph began recirculating widely on Facebook, Reddit, X (formerly Twitter), Threads, and TikTok. The image showed a small, pinkish creature with trailing tentacles and, most strikingly, what appeared to be a neat circle of human-like teeth — an upper row and a lower row, arranged in an oval mouth at the centre of the animal. The posts accompanying the image described the creature as a "little sea monster" and drew particular attention to its "teeth like a human." The image had previously circulated on social media in 2023, 2018, and as far back as 2013, always generating the same mixture of astonishment and discomfort. Its most prominent feature — the apparent dental row — produces an immediate and visceral uncanny response, the sense that something deeply wrong has appeared in the natural world: a small ocean creature staring back at the viewer through a very human-looking smile.

The claim embedded in the posts was twofold. First, that the photograph was genuine — that this was a real creature and a real photograph rather than a digital fabrication or a staged novelty. Second, and more implicitly, that the most striking feature of the creature — those teeth — were genuinely teeth, genuinely human-like in structure, and that this made the animal monstrous in some meaningful biological sense.

## What's Actually True

The photograph is genuine, and the creature depicted is real. It is *Promachoteuthis sulcus*, a species of deep-sea squid known only from a single specimen — a holotype captured in March 1971 by a trawling vessel operating in the South Atlantic Ocean, at a depth of approximately 1.85 kilometres (roughly 1.15 miles). The capture was accidental, as deep-sea biological specimens often are; the animal was brought up in a commercial or research trawl and preserved. It was not formally described and named as a new species until decades later.

The black-and-white version of the now-famous photograph — showing the creature's distinctive oral region — first appeared in a research paper published online in 2007 by Richard Young, Michael Vecchione, and Katharina Mangold. The paper, "A New Genus and Three New Species of Decapodiform Cephalopods (Mollusca: Cephalopoda)," was published in the journal *Reviews in Fish Biology and Fisheries* (Young et al., 2007, vol. 17, no. 2, pp. 353–365). The paper described three new cephalopod species, with *Promachoteuthis sulcus* being one of them. The photograph appeared on page six of the paper, depicting what the authors identified as the holotype specimen's mantle length of 25 millimetres — the animal in question was approximately the size of an adult human thumb.

Two of the paper's three authors, Young and Vecchione, subsequently placed a full-colour version of the photograph on the species' page on the Tree of Life Web Project, a collaborative online taxonomy database. In a later update to that page, they provided a caption that has since become the definitive scientific clarification of the image: "The squid seems to have a set of dentures. The 'dentures' are the circular, folded lips, of which only the upper and lower portions can be seen. The lips surround the beaks which are not visible in this photograph."

This explanation is the crux of the fact-check. What appears to be teeth is not teeth. Cephalopods — the class of molluscs that includes squid, octopuses, cuttlefish, and nautiluses — possess a beak, not teeth (Vendetti, 2006, University of California Museum of Palaeontology). The beak is a hard, chitinous structure, analogous in function to a parrot's beak, located at the centre of the oral apparatus and used to bite and manipulate prey. It is surrounded by soft lip tissue. In the photograph of *Promachoteuthis sulcus*, the beak is entirely hidden behind the folded lips, and only the upper and lower folds of the lip tissue are visible. The photograph's perspective, lighting, and the particular way the lips are positioned around the unseen beak conspire to create a remarkably convincing impression of two rows of flat, slightly rounded teeth.

The resemblance is, in technical terms, a pareidolia of anatomical structure — the observer's pattern-recognition systems, trained by millions of years of social primate evolution to read face-like features with extraordinary speed and sensitivity, process the image and immediately return an interpretation: mouth, teeth, face-like. This interpretation is wrong-footed by the actual anatomy, but the wrongness is not immediately legible from the photograph alone, which is why the image has circulated persistently for more than a decade without losing its power to disturb.

As of the Snopes fact-check published in November 2025 (Winter, Emery, Snopes, 4 November 2025), no additional specimens of *Promachoteuthis sulcus* have been documented. The sole known individual of the species, captured more than fifty years ago and preserved as a holotype, remains the only physical evidence of the species' existence. The animal in the photograph is, in a quite literal sense, the entire known population of its species from the perspective of formal taxonomy.

## Why People Believe This

The "little sea monster" image succeeds at generating belief — in the sense of a vivid, immediate, visceral conviction that something monstrous has been discovered — through several compounding mechanisms that are worth examining in sequence, because they reveal interesting things about how visual information is processed and how knowledge about the natural world is typically distributed.

**The deep sea as unknown territory.** Perhaps the most significant background condition enabling belief in the sea monster framing is the genuine depth of public ignorance about deep-sea biology. This is not irrationality — it is entirely reasonable. The deep ocean is genuinely inaccessible by ordinary means, its contents genuinely strange by the standards of any environment the vast majority of humans will ever physically encounter, and systematic knowledge about it is technically demanding and expensive to acquire. When people encounter a deep-sea creature they have never seen before, the correct epistemic response is substantial uncertainty. The ocean is real, large, and genuinely full of animals that look bizarre by surface standards. The reaction "I don't know what this is and it looks frightening" is right more often than it is wrong when applied to novel deep-sea imagery.

**Epistemic novelty: the category error of anthropomorphism.** Here is the genuinely interesting cognitive question the image poses. The immediate "teeth like a human" response to the photograph is not merely a failure of knowledge — it is the activation of a very specific cognitive module. Human social cognition is heavily specialized for face and oral-region processing. We detect face-like configurations at near-zero attentional cost, faster than we detect almost any other category of stimulus. We assign emotional and intentional content to face-like configurations automatically, in ways that are partly under explicit cognitive control and partly not. The photograph of *Promachoteuthis sulcus* triggers this module with unusual force because the oral region of the image presents with high fidelity to the internal template for "open human mouth with teeth visible." The fidelity is accidental — the result of a specific anatomical configuration, a specific photographic angle, and a specific lighting condition — but the brain does not know that it is accidental. It returns a verdict of "teeth" with the same confidence it would return for an actual human mouth.

What is epistemically novel about this case is the pairing of accurate bottom-level processing with inaccurate top-level interpretation. The visual data is processed correctly — the image does contain something that produces the same spatial frequency signature as teeth, in roughly the right position for a mouth. The error is in the domain knowledge that would reinterpret that signal: absent a mental model of cephalopod oral anatomy, the brain has no basis for overriding its initial read. The "teeth" interpretation is what you get when you apply normal human pattern recognition to an animal whose anatomy was not designed by the same evolutionary pressures that shaped ours.

**The viral image survival advantage of the uncanny.** Images that trigger the uncanny valley response — the slightly-wrong, familiar-yet-strange quality that produces discomfort rather than comfort — are strongly selected for in social media circulation. They generate shares, comments, and engagement at higher rates than pleasant or merely interesting images, because the emotional response they produce is unresolved: viewers do not close the loop of their reaction by looking and moving on; they share the image because looking at it alone is unsatisfying. The "teeth like a human" framing of the squid image amplifies its uncanny quality by verbally directing attention to the most unresolved element. The result is an image that has circulated for twelve-plus years without exhausting its capacity to disturb new viewers encountering it for the first time.

**The single-specimen mystery.** The fact that *Promachoteuthis sulcus* is known from only one specimen adds a layer of productive mystery that sustains belief in the "monster" framing. An animal documented by thousands of specimens becomes, over time, a known quantity. An animal known from a single preserved example from fifty years ago retains a quality of open-ended strangeness. We do not know how common the species is. We do not know whether that one specimen was typical of its kind. We do not know what colours the living animal displayed (preserved squid frequently lose colour). We do not know exactly how it moved through the water, what it ate, or what ate it. This cloud of unknowing is entirely appropriate — the science genuinely is sparse — but it is also narratively generative. The "monster" framing fills the unknown space with menace rather than ignorance.

## Verdict

The photograph is genuine, and the creature is real: *Promachoteuthis sulcus*, a deep-sea squid species first captured in 1971 and formally described in 2007 by Young, Vecchione, and Mangold in *Reviews in Fish Biology and Fisheries*. What appear to be human-like teeth are in fact the circular, folded lips of the squid's oral apparatus, partially obscuring a cephalopod beak that is typical of the class and not visible in the photograph. Snopes rated the underlying claim "True" while clearly establishing that the "teeth" interpretation is incorrect: the feature consists of lips, not teeth, and the resemblance to a human dental row is an anatomical coincidence produced by perspective and lighting conditions in a single photograph.

The honest summary is a disambiguation: the animal is real; the photograph is authentic; the "monster" framing is understandable given the image's visual properties; but the specific attribute most cited — human-like teeth — is a misidentification. The creature does not have teeth. It has lips, and they happened to look like teeth in the one photograph taken of the only known specimen of the species.

## The Wider Picture

The *Promachoteuthis sulcus* image is, stripped of its uncanny affect, a window into several active and genuinely fascinating areas of marine biology and cognitive science.

**Deep-sea discovery rates and the baseline of the unknown.** Despite centuries of ocean exploration and decades of systematic deep-sea research using remotely operated vehicles (ROVs) and advanced submersibles, new species from the deep ocean are still being formally described at a substantial rate. A 2017 analysis by Mora et al. estimated that the majority of marine species remain undiscovered and undescribed (*PLOS Biology*, 2011, 9(8): e1001127, Mora et al.). For deep-sea species specifically — those inhabiting mesopelagic (200–1,000 m), bathypelagic (1,000–4,000 m), and abyssopelagic (4,000–6,000 m) zones — the uncertainty is even larger, because accessing these zones requires specialised equipment and is logistically expensive. An animal like *Promachoteuthis sulcus*, known only from a single accidentally-captured specimen, represents the extreme end of the rarity distribution, but it is not categorically isolated — there are many deep-sea species with similarly sparse documentation. The image therefore accurately represents the deep ocean as a zone where genuinely novel organisms exist, and where ordinary assumptions about what animals look like can fail.

**Cephalopod biology and the beak.** The cephalopod beak is a remarkable structure. Unlike vertebrate teeth, which are made of mineralised tissue growing directly from the jaw, cephalopod beaks are made primarily of chitin and protein — the same material as insect exoskeletons — and are embedded in muscular tissue rather than bony jaw structures. The beak's mechanical properties have been extensively studied because it represents an unusual engineering challenge: it is the hardest structure in the soft-bodied squid, embedded in tissue that is much softer, and it must be able to bite prey without the interface producing stress concentrations that would tear the surrounding muscle. Research published in *Science* in 2008 by Miserez et al. ("The Transition from Stiff to Compliant Materials in Squid Beaks," *Science*, 2008, vol. 319, pp. 1816–1819) described the graded mechanical properties of the squid beak — from hard chitin at the tip to soft tissue at the base — as a lesson in functional materials science with potential implications for the design of prosthetics and surgical implants.

**The Tree of Life Web Project and open taxonomy.** The context in which the definitive scientific clarification of the *Promachoteuthis sulcus* photograph was published — the Tree of Life Web Project — is worth noting. The project was conceived in the 1990s as a collaborative effort to assemble accessible, accurate taxonomic information for all known species, maintained by biologists and accessible to the general public. The caption added by Young and Vecchione explaining the "dentures" is an example of scientists directly addressing the public misidentification generated by their own published image: having published the original black-and-white photograph in a behind-paywall academic paper, they went to a freely accessible public science platform to explain what the photograph actually showed. This is a small instance of open science practice functioning as misinformation correction — scientists intervening in the public circulation of their own work to prevent misunderstanding. Its effectiveness was partial, as the twelve-plus-year viral persistence of the "monster" framing demonstrates, but the intent and mechanism are instructive.

**The limits of the single-specimen record.** The fact that *Promachoteuthis sulcus* is known from one specimen raises genuine scientific questions that are not merely rhetorical. Single-specimen species descriptions are contested in taxonomy because they limit the ability to characterise variation within the species and because they leave open the question of whether the described features are universal or idiosyncratic. The squid's "folded lips" might look different in other individuals of the species; the apparent resemblance to human teeth might be a feature of this particular specimen's preserved state rather than a consistent anatomical feature. These are not reasons to disbelieve the species identification — the other morphological features of the animal are sufficient to place it in a valid taxon within the family Mastigoteuthidae — but they are reasons to hold the full picture with appropriate epistemic humility. The most honest statement is also the simplest: we know very little about this animal.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by Emery Winter, published 4 November 2025, represents a straightforward and well-executed example of authentic-image verification with anatomical qualification.

**Source tracing to original scientific literature.** Winter traced the photograph to its point of origin — the 2007 Young et al. paper in *Reviews in Fish Biology and Fisheries* — and properly attributed the clarifying "lips, not teeth" caption to its source on the Tree of Life Web Project. This is the methodologically correct approach: the primary scientific record rather than secondary journalism was the right point of reference, and the fact-checker reached it directly. Many viral image fact-checks stop at the level of confirming or denying digital manipulation; this one went to the taxonomy literature, which was the appropriate domain.

**The "True" rating with contextual qualification.** The Snopes verdict of "True" reflects the primary claim — this is a real animal, the photograph is not fabricated. The contextual note that the "teeth like a human" description is technically incorrect (the feature consists of lips) is embedded in the article rather than reflected in the rating, which is a standard limitation of the fact-check format: the rating must give a single verdict on the primary claim. A reader who only reads the verdict without the article will come away thinking the full claim is correct; a reader who reads the article will understand the specific anatomical correction.

**What this fact-check cannot address.** The Snopes article appropriately notes that no additional specimens have been found. This limitation is worth emphasising from a science communication standpoint: the absence of subsequent specimens means that virtually everything we might want to know about this animal — its behaviour, its population, its distribution, its life cycle — is unknown. The fact-check verifies the image and corrects the "teeth" misconception, but the deeper mystery of the species remains entirely open. This is one of those unusual cases where the fact-checkers' verdict ("True: it's real, but not teeth") is the beginning rather than the end of the interesting scientific story.

The "little sea monster" is one of the deep ocean's most successful ambassadors to the internet — an accidental icon that has spent over a decade generating a specific flavour of visceral discomfort in millions of viewers. The discomfort is real. The animal is real. The teeth are not teeth. The mystery of what *Promachoteuthis sulcus* actually does with those lips — and how many of its relatives it has in the dark below — remains genuinely and productively open.
