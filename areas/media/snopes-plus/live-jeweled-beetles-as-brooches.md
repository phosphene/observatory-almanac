## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A viral Facebook video published in June 2018 by the platform In the Know by AOL attracted millions of views with the claim that in Mexico, a tradition exists of wearing live beetles decorated with gold and semi-precious gemstones as functional jewelry — beetles that serve as brooches, affixed to the wearer's clothing by a tiny gold chain and leash to prevent the living ornament from escaping. The video described the practice as a culturally rooted phenomenon specific to Mexico, positioned the beetles as a kind of "pet-cessory," and closed by asking viewers whether they would wear such a thing. Thousands of commenters replied, with terms including "gross," "creepy," "cruel," and "inhumane" among the most common.

Snopes investigator David Emery (2018) assigned this claim the verdict **True**. The practice of adorning live beetles — specifically of the species *Zopherus chilensis*, known in Mexican Spanish as the *makech* or *maquech* — with precious metals and gemstones and wearing them as living jewelry is real, documented, and of considerable antiquity. The practice is primarily associated with the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico and is connected by oral tradition to pre-Columbian indigenous culture, though the precise historical origins are contested by scholars. The earliest print reference located by Emery (2018) dates to 1887, in a syndicated newspaper article from the New Haven Register, describing a jeweled beetle received from Mérida, Yucatán as "a high distinction."

This entry examines the documentary and historical record of the makech tradition, the biology and ethology of *Zopherus chilensis*, the contested mythological origin narrative, the genuine animal welfare debates surrounding the practice, and the anthropological and ethnotaxonomic context that situates this tradition within a broader global history of living ornaments.

## §2 Documentary and Historical Record

Emery (2018) cited a syndicated newspaper article from 1887 — attributed to the New Haven Register — describing a jeweled bug received from a Señora Fuentes of Mérida, Yucatán, as a high-distinction gift to a Miss Emily Nelson of Bridgeport, Connecticut. The article provides a remarkably detailed description that comports closely with modern accounts: a gold band fixed around the beetle's body, tiny jewels set in gold on the back, a fine gold chain attached to the underside connecting to a clasp brooch. The article noted that the beetle "does not seem to be timid, but crawls tranquilly oblivious to the admiration he creates" — a behavioral observation consistent with the docility of *Zopherus chilensis*, which is noted for sluggish movement and a tendency to remain still when disturbed.

An April 2015 article on Smithsonian.com, cited by Emery (2018), articulated both the mythological origin narrative and scholarly skepticism about its authenticity. According to the most common version of the legend, a pre-Columbian Maya princess fell in love with a young man forbidden to her; when their relationship was discovered, he was sentenced to death, and a shaman transformed him into a brilliant beetle that could be decorated and worn over the princess's heart as a perpetual reminder of their bond. Charles Hogue and other beetle curators are cited as reporting this legend in connection with the makech tradition.

The Smithsonian article also quoted Raul Aguilar, a San Francisco artist who grew up in Mexico and once owned a makech, expressing skepticism about the legend's authenticity. Aguilar noted that Maya culture was extensively documented in writing, stelae, and books, and that a tradition as distinctive as the makech would be expected to appear in the documentary record if it were genuinely pre-Columbian. He characterized the legend as likely a vendor narrative — a story told to curious buyers to add prestige and cultural weight to the item — though acknowledging that its elements do echo genuine aspects of pre-Columbian Yucatecan culture, including the concept of the shape-shifting shaman and the use of living animals in religious ceremony.

Aguilar's own encounter with the makech occurred in the 1970s in Veracruz, where he purchased one from a plaza vendor during a family trip. His description — "It's not really a fashion trend so much as a curiosity. You wouldn't wear it to a party" — provides a useful corrective to the viral video's framing, which implied the practice was either widespread or trending. The makech is in practice a specialty craft item sold in tourist markets in the Yucatán and occasionally elsewhere in Mexico, not a mainstream fashion accessory even within the region of its origin.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty — Living Ornament Traditions and the Ethnography of Insect Adornment

The makech tradition belongs to a broader ethnographic category that has received surprisingly little systematic scholarly attention: living ornaments — the use of living organisms, particularly invertebrates, as wearable objects. This category is analytically distinct from both taxidermy jewelry (which uses preserved specimens) and symbolic animal adornment (which represents animals in metal or gemstone without using the animal itself). Living ornament traditions have been documented across multiple cultures and ecological zones, and their epistemological status in Western discourse is consistently one of unsettled exoticism — simultaneously fascinating and disturbing, which explains much of the viral quality of the 2018 video.

Raffles (2010, *Insectopedia*) examined human-insect relationships across cultural contexts and documented multiple traditions of insect adornment, including Japanese cricket-keeping as a prestige practice and the use of beetle elytra (wing cases) as decorative elements in textiles across South and Southeast Asian cultures. Raffles noted that these traditions persistently challenge Western frameworks of animal instrumentalization by positioning insects as participants in relationships of aesthetic cohabitation rather than mere raw materials for decoration. The makech represents a particularly intense version of this challenge because the insect remains alive and mobile.

Conway Morris (2003, *Life's Solution: Inevitable Humans in a Lonely Universe*) does not address makech specifically but offers theoretical resources for understanding why certain animal forms — particularly those that combine alien morphology with unexpected behavioral docility — produce the uncanny responses documented in the comment threads of the 2018 viral video. The beetle's willingness to remain on the wearer's clothing, its glittering decorated surface, and its movement create a form of living aesthetic object that fits no comfortable Western category: neither pet, nor ornament, nor clothing accessory, but some unstable combination of all three.

The ethical debate surrounding the makech illustrates a broader challenge in applied animal ethics: determining the welfare implications of practices involving species with poorly understood sentience and pain responses. Tye (2017, *Tense Bees and Shell-Shocked Crabs: Are Animals Conscious?*) reviewed the evidence for invertebrate consciousness and concluded that the evidence for sentience in beetles and other Coleoptera is considerably weaker than for vertebrates but not negligible — a nuanced position that neither definitively condemns nor definitively exonerates the makech tradition from a welfare perspective. The gold and gemstone decorations are mechanically fixed to the beetle's wing cases (elytra) without (at least in traditional practice) penetrating to living tissue; the chain is attached to the ventral surface. Whether this constitutes significant suffering is genuinely uncertain under current scientific understanding.

The viral response to the practice — overwhelmingly negative in terms of welfare concern — is itself epistemically interesting as a data point about the asymmetric extension of moral consideration across different animal taxa. The same commenters who expressed horror at the jeweled beetle likely had far less visceral concern about the treatment of the beetle's arthropod relatives in food production. Roth & Dicke (2005, *Trends in Neurosciences*) documented that the assumption of pain or suffering in invertebrates is modulated by morphological similarity to vertebrates, meaning that insects and beetles, which lack the physiognomic cues associated with mammalian pain expression, trigger lower baseline concern even when their nociceptive biology may be comparable to animals that receive greater welfare protection.

## §4 The Biology of *Zopherus chilensis*

The species at the center of the makech tradition is *Zopherus chilensis* (formerly classified within various genus arrangements within the family Zopheridae), a large, slow-moving beetle native to Mexico and Central America with a particularly robust exoskeleton. The Animal Diversity Web entry for *Zopherus chilensis*, cited indirectly by Emery (2018) via the Smithsonian article, documents that the species is characterized by extreme sclerotization — an unusually hard, dense exoskeleton that functions as passive armor against predators and desiccation in its arid native habitat.

This extraordinary hardness is what makes the makech beetle specifically suitable for the adornment tradition. The beetle's elytra are sufficiently robust to hold the adhesives and settings used to fix gemstones without cracking under normal handling, and the beetle can survive the attachment process in a way that softer-bodied insect species could not. The species has been documented to live for several years when kept in favorable conditions — the 1887 newspaper article claimed the beetle could live "for a number of years if not exposed to the rigors of northern climes," and this longevity is consistent with the known biology of the genus.

*Zopherus chilensis* feeds primarily on fungal material found in decaying wood and has modest metabolic requirements, making it relatively straightforward to maintain in captivity as a long-lived pet or ornament. In traditional makech practice, the beetle is provided with food (typically small quantities of organic matter or fruit) and water, and its husbandry requirements are minimal compared with vertebrate pets of comparable size. This biological profile — longevity, hardness, slow movement, modest care requirements — is likely what drove the selection of this species for the adornment tradition in the first place.

The beetle's natural coloration is notable: *Zopherus chilensis* possesses a subtly iridescent black and dark grey exoskeleton that provides an aesthetically compelling base for gold and gem decoration. Unlike many tropical beetles exploited for decorative purposes, the visual appeal of the makech in adornment contexts is the combination of the beetle's own surface with the applied materials, rather than the replacement of the beetle's natural appearance. This hybrid aesthetic — natural iridescence augmented by precious metals — recurs in other insect adornment traditions documented by Raffles (2010).

The genus name *Zopherus* is derived from the Greek for "darkness" or "gloomy," reflecting the beetle's coloration; the species epithet *chilensis* is geographically misleading, as the species' primary range is Mexico and Central America rather than Chile, likely reflecting a historical taxonomic error in the original description. This nomenclatural quirk is a small illustration of the broader challenges of insect taxonomy in the colonial period, when specimens were frequently mislabeled with geographic data reflecting the port of export rather than the site of collection.

## §5 Cultural and Anthropological Context

The makech tradition sits at the intersection of several cultural currents that are important for contextualizing the viral response to the 2018 video. First, the tradition is specifically Yucatecan — it comes from a region with a complex and distinctive cultural history that blends indigenous Maya heritage with Spanish colonial influence and contemporary Mexican identity. Framing the practice as simply "Mexican" in the viral video, as Emery (2018) implicitly did in his fact-check setup, elides this specificity. The makech is not a pan-Mexican tradition; it is a regional specialty with documented roots in the Yucatán market economy and tourist trade.

Fabian (2014, *Time and the Other: How Anthropology Makes Its Object*, Columbia University Press) discussed the tendency in Western reception of non-Western cultural practices to treat them as representative of an undifferentiated cultural Other — a tendency visible in the viral video's framing of the makech as exotic and alien to a Western audience while its Yucatecan context was reduced to "Mexican." This flattening of cultural specificity is a structural feature of viral content production rather than a deliberate distortion, but it contributes to the misinformation effect: viewers came away with a more homogenized and exoticized picture of Mexican culture than the facts warranted.

Second, the legend of the Maya princess and her transformed lover — whatever its precise origins — belongs to a well-documented category of artifact-explanation myths: stories that retroactively provide cultural weight and mythological grounding for existing practices or objects. García Canclini (1995, *Hybrid Cultures: Strategies for Entering and Leaving Modernity*, University of Minnesota Press) analyzed the role of origin myths in Mexican artisanal commerce and found that market-oriented narratives of pre-Columbian origin are a widespread commercial strategy for positioning craft items as culturally authentic rather than merely tourist novelties. The skepticism of Raul Aguilar, cited in the Smithsonian article, is consistent with García Canclini's analytical framework: the legend may be a commercial narrative rather than an authenticated historical tradition, though this does not necessarily negate the practice's genuine cultural continuity.

Third, the question of whether the makech tradition constitutes animal cruelty must be situated relative to the much larger global insect farming and exploitation industry. Globally, insects are farmed for food, feed, cosmetics, and materials at a scale that dwarfs the niche makech market; the moral calculus applied to the makech is therefore asymmetrically scrutinized relative to species with more commercial importance. This asymmetry reflects the role of novelty and exoticism in driving moral outrage — the beetle brooch is unusual, visible, and socially performing in a way that industrial cricket farming is not, making it a more tractable target for viral moral concern.

## §6 The Claim's Truth and Its Implications for Media Literacy

Emery's (2018) verdict of True is unambiguous and well-supported by the historical and contemporary documentary record. The practice of wearing live jeweled beetles as brooches is real, documented since at least the 1880s, and continues in contemporary Yucatán market contexts. The viral video's core claim was accurate.

What the fact-check illuminates, however, is the significant difference between the accuracy of a factual claim and the completeness of the cultural context in which it is embedded. The viral video framed the makech primarily as a spectacle of the unusual — would you wear one? — rather than as a documented cultural practice with genuine historical depth, an attached mythology of contested provenance, a specific species ecology, and a genuine (if unresolved) animal welfare debate. Viewers came away with accurate basic information but a significantly impoverished cultural picture.

Mihailidis & Viotty (2017, *Media, Culture & Society*) found that viral fact-checking content, even when accurate, tends to flatten the epistemic texture of complex cultural phenomena in favor of binary verdict framing. This tendency is inherent in the fact-checking genre, which optimizes for rapid verdict delivery rather than cultural depth. The makech case illustrates the cost: viewers who accepted the video's "True" verdict had an accurate belief about the existence of the practice but lacked the contextual frameworks — historical, ecological, ethical, anthropological — needed to situate that belief meaningfully.

For media consumers, the makech case suggests that the interesting questions about a "True" verdict often begin rather than end at the verdict itself. True that the practice exists; also true that it is genuinely regional rather than pan-Mexican; also true that its origin legends are commercially mediated and scholarly-contested; also true that the welfare debate is unresolved under current invertebrate sentience science; also true that the beetle is specifically *Zopherus chilensis*, with documented biological properties that explain its selection for the tradition. Each of these additional true propositions enriches rather than contradicts the core verdict, illustrating that verification is a threshold, not a destination, in epistemic engagement with complex cultural facts.
