# Taika Waititi and Timothée Chalamet are remaking The Lost Boys


## 1. The Claim

In April 2024, a claim spread across social media — primarily from Facebook through to Twitter/X, Reddit entertainment communities, and film fan forums — asserting that director Taika Waititi was developing a remake of the 1987 horror-comedy film *The Lost Boys* and that Timothée Chalamet would star in the project. Variants of the claim added additional cast members: Evan Peters and Noah Schnapp were mentioned in some versions. The most elaborated versions of the claim included a fabricated film poster, release schedule speculation, and commentary on the pairing of Waititi and Chalamet as a creative collaboration.

The claim emerged in a form that was readily believable to entertainment-literate audiences: Taika Waititi is a commercially successful and culturally prominent director known for genre-inflected projects (*Thor: Ragnarok*, *Jojo Rabbit*, *What We Do in the Shadows*) and for working with contemporary young male stars in tonally playful films. Timothée Chalamet is among the most commercially significant and critically acclaimed actors of his generation, associated with both prestige productions and franchise films, and recently prominent as Paul Atreides in *Dune* and as Willy Wonka in *Wonka* (2023). The pairing was not obviously implausible, and *The Lost Boys* — a beloved cult classic about teenage vampires in Santa Cruz, California — is exactly the kind of IP that the current Hollywood remake-and-franchise cycle regularly revisits.

The claim was shared as entertainment news rather than as political opinion, which shaped how it circulated: many recipients responded with enthusiasm, casting speculation, aesthetic commentary, and nostalgia for the original, all of which propagated the claim further without any verification impulse. Entertainment rumour occupies a social media niche where sharing precedes checking even more thoroughly than in political news, because the emotional stakes of being wrong are perceived as low.

Snopes investigated the claim and rated it false.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The fabricated claim's source.** The Waititi-Chalamet *Lost Boys* claim originated from a Facebook post published on April 19, 2024, by an account identified as "YODA BBY ABY." This account explicitly describes itself as a satire and fake news account in its account metadata. The post included a fabricated film poster and a fictional plot description. Because the fabricated poster was formatted to resemble professionally designed entertainment media — using fonts and design conventions associated with genuine film marketing — it read as credible to many people who encountered it outside its original satirical context (Snopes, 2024).

The account had produced similar fabricated entertainment news items previously. The *Lost Boys* item was not intended, at the account level, as malicious misinformation: its satirical origin is disclosed. But when content produced by disclosed satire accounts escapes its original context and circulates as news, the satirical framing is lost, and the content functions as misinformation regardless of intent.

**The actual Lost Boys remake in development.** A *Lost Boys* remake is genuinely in development at Warner Bros. Entertainment, which owns the franchise. The real project, reported by Collider and other entertainment industry outlets, stars Noah Jupe and Jaeden Martell in the lead roles and is directed by Jonathan Entwistle (*The End of the F***ing World*, *I Am Not Okay With This*). The screenplay was written by Randy McKinnon. This project has been in development since at least September 2021 and represents the continuation of Warner Bros.' sustained interest in the franchise following a failed CW television series adaptation that was piloted but not picked up circa 2016.

Neither Taika Waititi, Timothée Chalamet, Evan Peters, nor Noah Schnapp is attached to the genuine Warner Bros. *Lost Boys* remake in any capacity confirmed by the studio, the production, or the artists' representatives.

**Taika Waititi's actual project slate.** As of 2024–2026, Waititi's confirmed directing projects include *Klara and the Sun*, an adaptation of Kazuo Ishiguro's novel scheduled for release in 2026; a Netflix animated adaptation of *Charlie and the Chocolate Factory*; and development work on several other projects including adaptations of *The Incal* and *Akira*, and a Star Wars film. A *Lost Boys* remake with Chalamet does not appear on any version of his publicly confirmed project list.

**Timothée Chalamet's actual project slate.** Chalamet's confirmed upcoming projects include *Dune: Part Three* (expected December 2026), *Marty Supreme* (2026), *Not Alone* (2027), and *Playground*, an adaptation of a Booker Prize-longlisted novel he is attached to produce and potentially star in. None of these is associated with Taika Waititi. He is not attached to a *Lost Boys* remake.

**Why the original film is a revival target.** *The Lost Boys*, directed by Joel Schumacher from a screenplay by Jan Fischer, James Jeremias, and Jeffrey Boam, was released by Warner Bros. on July 31, 1987. It starred Jason Patric, Corey Haim, Kiefer Sutherland, Corey Feldman, Dianne Wiest, and others, and was a significant commercial and critical success, establishing a template for the teen-horror-comedy genre of the late 1980s. The film attracted a devoted cult following and has remained culturally visible through its soundtrack, its cast (particularly the Corey duo), and its aesthetics. Its vampires-in-a-California-beach-town premise is tonally distinct from most contemporary vampire narratives and offers identifiable nostalgic appeal. The property's ongoing commercial viability for Warner Bros. makes revival attempts structurally predictable.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Credible-sounding attachment of real names to real IP.** The fabricated claim derived its plausibility from combining three genuine and publicly known facts: (1) Taika Waititi is a real director with a sustained track record of genre-playful productions; (2) Timothée Chalamet is a real actor with enormous commercial drawing power and genre versatility; (3) *The Lost Boys* is a real IP in genuine active development at Warner Bros. None of these components was invented. The fabricated claim was the specific combination — attaching Waititi and Chalamet as director and star of the genuine franchise revival project. The falsification was a relational claim, not an invented entity claim.

Research on misinformation and plausibility has established that false claims that incorporate true elements are systematically harder to detect than purely fabricated claims (Lewandowsky et al., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*). The "kernel of truth" in the *Lost Boys* fabrication — the genuine development project, the genuine existence of both named talents, the genuine compatibility of their known work styles with the genre — made the composite claim read as credible entertainment reporting rather than as fabrication.

**The epistemic novelty: entertainment rumour as a low-friction misinformation vector.** The deeper and less commonly articulated issue is the distinctive epistemic architecture of entertainment fan communities as misinformation vectors. In political news contexts, recipients are often actively motivated to fact-check claims because the stakes of being wrong are felt as meaningful: wrongly believing a political claim may lead to advocating for mistaken positions, spreading falsehoods in serious conversations, or making electoral decisions on bad information. The social costs of being fooled are perceived as significant.

In entertainment fan communities, the perceived social cost of believing and sharing a false casting announcement is near zero. Being excited about a fake movie is harmless, and the emotional payoff of sharing exciting creative news — especially when it constructs an appealing fantasy collaboration — is immediate. The result is that the sharing decision is made before the checking decision virtually every time, producing a propagation pattern in which the false claim reaches a very large audience before any correction is generated. When corrections do arrive, many recipients have already invested emotional engagement in the fake version, producing mild resistance to the correction (Guilbeault, Becker & Centola, 2018, *Nature Communications*).

This pattern is not unique to Lost Boys; it is the characteristic dynamic of fabricated entertainment news. Fake casting announcements for Marvel films, fake franchise revival claims, fake celebrity relationship reveals, and similar items circulate continuously in entertainment community social media, largely without correction, because the appetite for confirmation is large and the cost of false belief is perceived as low. The mechanism is structural to fan culture's relationship with entertainment news.

**The satire-to-misinformation pipeline.** The "YODA BBY ABY" Facebook account was disclosed as satire. Its disclaimers appear in the account's about section, which most people do not read when they encounter a single shared post. The decontextualisation of satirical content — in which the satirical disclaimer is left behind when the content is screenshot and reshared — is a well-documented dynamic (Tandoc, Lim & Ling, 2018, *Journalism Theory, Practice & Criticism*). The satirical intent of the original creator does not prevent the content from functioning as misinformation once it leaves the original context.

The *Lost Boys* fake claim was not malicious misinformation in origin. It became misinformation through the platform mechanics of decontextualised viral sharing. Understanding this pipeline — from knowingly satirical creation to unknowing misinformation consumption — is important for calibrating appropriate responses. Blaming the creator is not the most useful analysis when the mechanism is structural.

**The Hollywood remake cycle as background noise.** The contemporary entertainment industry's extensive investment in franchise revivals, reboots, remakes, and legacy IP exploitation creates a background environment in which new remake announcements are so frequent and so expected that they do not automatically trigger heightened scepticism. Audiences are accustomed to learning that a beloved property from the 1980s is being remade; the news arrives so regularly that a fake announcement fits cleanly into an established cognitive category. The remake announcement has become a recognised genre of entertainment news, and the Lost Boys fabrication was formatted as a conventional specimen of that genre.

Had the fabrication claimed that *The Lost Boys* was being adapted as a musical or transformed into a limited series about tax accountants, the strangeness of the claim would have prompted scepticism. Because a straight remake announcement is exactly what the Hollywood remake machine continually produces, the claim encountered no cognitive friction from its basic premise.


## 4. Verdict

**False.** Taika Waititi is not directing a remake of *The Lost Boys*. Timothée Chalamet is not attached to a *Lost Boys* remake. The claim originated from a Facebook account that self-identifies as a satire and fake news source, and no corroborating evidence from the film industry, the named artists' representatives, Warner Bros., or any reliable entertainment journalism source has confirmed any element of the specific claim.

A *Lost Boys* remake is genuinely in development at Warner Bros., directed by Jonathan Entwistle and starring Noah Jupe and Jaeden Martell. This is the real project to which the fake claim is adjacent without being accurate.

Snopes confirmed in 2024 that the claim is false after tracing it to the disclosed satire account and finding no independent confirmation from any film industry source.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The *Lost Boys* fake casting claim is a specimen of a recurring format in entertainment misinformation: the fabricated casting announcement for a real IP. This format proliferates because it requires minimal creative work (attaching desirable names to a recognisable property), produces a product that is immediately emotionally engaging to fans of both the property and the named talent, and travels in environments where checking is not culturally expected behaviour.

The underlying IP ecology of contemporary Hollywood creates the conditions for this format. The studio system's heavy investment in legacy IP revival — driven by franchise economics, the global growth of the superhero and fantasy genres, and the commercial success of nostalgia-anchored titles — means that virtually any beloved film from the 1970s through 2000s is plausibly in development for remake. This ambient plausibility is exploited by fabricated casting items: there is almost always a real basis (the real development of the real IP) onto which a false specificity (the false casting) can be attached.

The attachment of Waititi and Chalamet in particular reflects a coherent creative logic: both have demonstrated capacity for genre-inflected, tonally complex work that would suit the *Lost Boys* aesthetic. Waititi's *What We Do in the Shadows* (2014) is itself a vampire horror-comedy; his *Thor: Ragnarok* (2017) demonstrated his capacity to work within large studio genre frameworks while maintaining a distinctive directorial voice. Chalamet's genre range, demonstrated in *Dune* and *Wonka*, made him a plausible star for a horror-adjacent studio project. The fake claim was not arbitrary — it was built from a creative logic that made it feel like something a real entertainment journalist might actually report.

This construction of plausible-but-false entertainment news is worth distinguishing analytically from purely arbitrary fabrication. Research on news credibility has found that claims that fit prior expectations and that demonstrate internal narrative coherence are evaluated as more credible than claims that lack these properties (Amazeen & Bucy, 2019, *Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly*). The fabricated *Lost Boys* claim was built with narrative coherence: the director's vampire-comedy background, the star's genre versatility, the franchise's genuine revival status. These components assembled into a claim that passed the informal credibility test most readers apply to entertainment news before sharing.

The larger structural question is what the appetite for Waititi-Chalamet collaboration reveals about contemporary entertainment culture. The fake claim generated enthusiasm not primarily because people believe everything they read, but because the combination genuinely appealed to audiences who would want to see this version of the film. The falsification disappointed rather than embarrassed those who had shared it. This emotional architecture — the fabrication as wish-fulfilment — is worth tracking as a pattern in entertainment misinformation specifically, because it produces social dynamics distinct from politically motivated misinformation. Corrections in this space tend to be received with "what a shame" rather than "how dare you."

The actual remake, with Entwistle, Jupe, and Martell, remains a genuinely anticipated project in fan communities, and its eventual release will close the gap between the real and fabricated versions of the *Lost Boys* revival story.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes investigated the claim in 2024, tracing it to the April 19, 2024 Facebook post from the "YODA BBY ABY" account, verifying the account's self-identification as a satire and fake news source, and checking against Warner Bros.' actual *Lost Boys* development announcements, which name Jonathan Entwistle as director. The Snopes verdict was false.

The fact-checking methodology for fabricated entertainment casting claims follows a relatively standardised pathway: (1) search entertainment industry trade publications (Variety, The Hollywood Reporter, Deadline Hollywood, Collider) for any report of the specific attachment; (2) check the original source to identify whether it is a recognised satirical or fake news account; (3) verify the named artists' confirmed project slates with known industry reporting; (4) contact the artists' representatives and/or the studio if necessary for direct confirmation or denial; (5) trace the earliest instance of the claim to determine whether it originated in a satirical context.

In the *Lost Boys* case, step (1) alone is sufficient to distinguish the claim: no entertainment trade publication reported a Waititi-Chalamet *Lost Boys* project. Major real casting announcements are reported within hours of deals closing by the Hollywood trades, meaning a genuine Waititi-Chalamet-*Lost Boys* deal would have been in Variety and Deadline within a day. Its absence from those sources is close to determinative.

The media literacy lesson from the *Lost Boys* case is about context collapse in social media sharing architecture. Satire that reaches its designated audience within its original context is, by definition, not misinformation. It is when satirical content escapes its original context — when the satirical framing is lost in transit — that it begins to function as misinformation regardless of original intent. Platform design does not currently provide mechanically reliable indicators of satire/fake-news disclosure for decontextualised shared items. Users encountering screenshots or second-hand shares of content have no reliable visual cue distinguishing "this began as satire" from "this is genuine entertainment news." Developing or advocating for such indicators is an open challenge in media literacy and platform design, and the *Lost Boys* case is a clean example of why it matters.

For fans and casual entertainment news consumers, the practical heuristic that follows from this analysis is straightforward: before sharing a casting announcement that seems exciting or surprising, check whether Variety, Deadline, or The Hollywood Reporter has reported it. These publications have industry relationships that make them the first point of coverage for genuine deals. A casting announcement that exists only as a social media post, without any presence in the trades, should be treated as unverified at best.
