## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In July 2021, social media users and food media widely reported the existence of a limited-edition macaroni and cheese-flavored ice cream, created through a partnership between packaged food giant Kraft and New York-based artisanal ice cream company Van Leeuwen Ice Cream. The product ostensibly incorporated the famous orange cheese sauce powder from Kraft Macaroni & Cheese—the world's best-selling packaged pasta product—into an ice cream base. Reactions ranged from delighted appetite to genuine disgust, with a substantial segment of the commenting public questioning whether the product was real or satirical.

Snopes investigator Bethania Palma (2021) rated the claim **True**. The Kraft Macaroni & Cheese Ice Cream was a genuine commercial product, sold in limited quantities beginning July 14, 2021—National Macaroni and Cheese Day—through Van Leeuwen's retail and scoop shop channels. The product sold out rapidly and was categorized as a limited-edition offering on Van Leeuwen's product page, which listed it as "out of stock" shortly after the Snopes article was published. A press release was issued by Kraft via Business Wire on July 13, 2021, confirming the partnership and the product's existence.

This entry examines the sensory science behind the flavor combination, the corporate logic of the Kraft-Van Leeuwen partnership, the history of savory ice cream flavors as an experimental culinary genre, and the broader phenomenon of "disgust-interest" marketing in which deliberately transgressive flavor combinations generate viral attention regardless of the product's ultimate commercial viability.

## §2 Product Verification and Ingredient Composition

The Kraft Macaroni & Cheese Ice Cream was confirmed through multiple independent primary sources. Kraft Heinz issued a press release through Business Wire (July 13, 2021) announcing the partnership and product launch. Van Leeuwen's official product page (vanleeuwenicecream.com) listed the flavor for purchase with a product description that confirmed the use of the actual Kraft Macaroni & Cheese powder: "Nothing makes us happier than this Kraft Macaroni & Cheese Ice Cream... did we make this ice cream with that buttery, sweet iconic cheese? You bet."

The confirmation that the Kraft Macaroni & Cheese powder was used as an actual ingredient—rather than a "mac and cheese flavor" isolated through artificial flavoring—is significant for both the flavor claim and the corporate partnership logic. Kraft's participation in the campaign required its branded ingredient (the cheese powder) to appear as a documented ingredient in a Van Leeuwen product, creating a supply chain arrangement in which a packaged food company's retail product became an ingredient in a premium ice cream maker's offering.

The composition of Kraft Macaroni & Cheese powder is publicly disclosed in the product's ingredient list, which includes: whey, milkfat, milk protein concentrate, salt, sodium tripolyphosphate, citric acid, sodium phosphate, lactic acid, milk, artificial color (Yellow 5, Yellow 6), and various cheese cultures. The flavor profile of the powder is dominated by whey and processed cheese components with MSG-adjacent savory-sweet umami notes, a relatively high sodium content, and the artificial orange color that makes the dish visually iconic (Kraft Heinz, product labeling, 2021).

Van Leeuwen's existing product line context is important for understanding why this flavor mashup was within the company's established brand range rather than a genuine departure. Van Leeuwen, founded in 2008 by Ben Van Leeuwen, Pete Van Leeuwen, and Laura O'Neill, positions itself as a premium artisanal ice cream company serving both conventional flavors (vanilla bean, chocolate) and "adventurous" flavors that include Earl Grey Tea, Honeycomb, and Royal Wedding Cake. The Kraft collaboration fit within the experimental tier of Van Leeuwen's portfolio, though it represented an unusual bridge between "artisanal premium" and "mass-market nostalgic."

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Disgust-Interest Marketing and the Verification Problem for Outrageous True Claims

The Kraft Macaroni & Cheese Ice Cream belongs to a specific genre of food marketing that might be called *disgust-interest marketing*: the strategic deployment of emotionally extreme, transgressive, or cognitively dissonant flavor combinations to generate viral attention, with the product's inherent implausibility functioning as the primary marketing mechanism.

The epistemic novelty in this case is the verification asymmetry between disgust-driven claims and other food product announcements. When a new flavor of Oreo is introduced, the announcement is accepted as true because new flavors of Oreos are expected and within the established category bounds. When a combination as jarring as macaroni and cheese ice cream is announced, the cognitive response is skepticism—not because such a product is technically impossible but because it violates the implicit sensory-category boundary between savory dairy (processed cheese) and sweet dairy (ice cream).

Rozin and Fallon (1987, *Psychological Review*, 94(1), 23–41) described the "disgust-based rejection" mechanism as one of the primary cognitive filters applied to food decisions: foods that trigger disgust—defined as the motivation to avoid an entity that one perceives as offensive, contaminating, or violating category expectations—receive heightened scrutiny and are presumed contaminated or unacceptable rather than merely unfamiliar. The application of this mechanism to the macaroni and cheese ice cream claim means that many readers who encountered the announcement applied "this must be fake" reasoning before considering any evidence, because their disgust-based categories filtered out the claim as impossible-by-definition.

This creates a distinctive fact-checking challenge: how do you verify a claim whose content is itself the primary driver of skepticism? The methodology must bypass emotional response and go directly to primary source verification, which is exactly what Palma (2021) did. The Business Wire press release is a corporate wire service that charges for institutional press releases; a company would not issue and pay for a wire service announcement of a fake product because doing so would constitute securities-adjacent market manipulation for a publicly traded company (Kraft Heinz is publicly traded on NASDAQ under KHC) and create reputational liability with the partner brand.

This "institutionally costly claim" verification heuristic is underutilized in general-public fact evaluation. A genuine Kraft press release on Business Wire is not a social media post, a personal blog, or an anonymous tip; it is a formal corporate communication with legal implications and costs attached. The combination of a wire service press release, a commercial product page with purchasing infrastructure, and physical product distribution through scoop shops constitutes a chain of evidence that effectively rules out fabrication without requiring the fact-checker to personally taste the product.

Wansink and Chandon (2014, *Journal of Consumer Research*, 41(2), A8–A12) documented the food neophobia-novelty paradox: highly novel foods generate more media and social media coverage than equivalent new products in established categories, but this coverage is predominantly mixed in emotional valence—positive curiosity and negative disgust appearing simultaneously. The Kraft Mac & Cheese Ice Cream generated exactly this pattern, with food journalists compiling "disgusted" responses alongside enthusiastic ones, and the product selling out regardless of the balance, suggesting that disgust-interest marketing successfully drives purchase behavior in ways that pure positive sentiment marketing may not.

## §4 The Sensory Science of Savory Ice Cream

The flavor chemistry of the Kraft Macaroni & Cheese Ice Cream is not as self-evidently incoherent as its cultural reception suggests. Understanding why requires tracing the flavor compounds present in both the ice cream base and the Kraft powder, and identifying which interactions are synergistic versus genuinely discordant.

Ice cream is an emulsified frozen foam with a continuous phase of concentrated sugar solution (approximately 15–20% sucrose by weight), a dispersed fat phase derived from cream and/or milk, proteins from dairy components, and incorporated air (overrun). The disproportionate pleasure obtained from ice cream is primarily driven by: the cold-stimulus effect on the trigeminal nerve (creating a refreshing sensation), the fat-mediated mouthfeel that creates textural richness, and the sugar-fat interaction at the hedonic maximum of the human preference curve (Drewnowski et al., 1992, *Appetite*, 18(3), 207–222). These hedonic drivers are independent of flavor.

The Kraft cheese powder contributes: umami compounds (glutamate from cheese and whey proteins, inosinate from dairy phosphates), salt (sodium chloride), fat from milkfat, and a set of cheese-derived flavor molecules including diacetyl (buttery), butyric acid (sharp/cheesy), and fatty acid degradation products. Diacetyl—one of the key components of the Kraft powder's flavor—is also used as a flavoring in commercial butter pecan and caramel ice creams, where its buttery note is considered desirable. Butyric acid, at low concentrations, is present in many fermented dairy products including certain ice cream flavors that incorporate cream-based notes.

The primary "disgust" trigger is not chemical but categorical: the orange color and savory-salty macro-flavor of the Kraft powder violates expectation for ice cream, which is culturally encoded as sweet and often brightly or neutrally colored. The same compounds that create pleasant flavor complexity in a cheese sauce are experienced as jarring in an ice cream context—a phenomenon Prescott (2004, *Chemical Senses*, 29(4), 331–335) termed "context-dependent flavor evaluation," in which the same volatile compound is rated more intensely positive or negative depending on whether it is presented in a sweet or savory matrix.

This suggests that the Kraft Macaroni & Cheese Ice Cream was not inherently incoherent from a flavor chemistry standpoint; rather, it was culturally transgressive in a way that translates into disgust-interest marketing value. The product is likely palatable—perhaps even pleasant—to consumers who set aside categorical expectations and evaluate it as a novel sensory experience.

## §5 Corporate Partnership Economics and the Limited-Edition Strategy

The Kraft-Van Leeuwen partnership represents a sophisticated example of brand co-marketing with complementary strategic objectives for both parties.

Kraft Heinz, as a mature consumer packaged goods company, faces the perennial challenge of maintaining cultural relevance for its most iconic products. Kraft Macaroni & Cheese—introduced in 1937 and often cited as one of the best-selling packaged foods in U.S. history—has the market saturation and brand recognition of a legacy product, which means that traditional advertising is largely defending existing market share rather than generating incremental consumption. Partnerships with trend-forward brands (like Van Leeuwen) generate earned media value—press coverage and social media engagement that would cost multiples more to acquire through paid advertising—while repositioning the Kraft brand as culturally playful rather than merely nostalgic.

The selection of National Macaroni and Cheese Day (July 14) as the launch date is a deliberate use of food holiday marketing infrastructure. National food days—which exist for virtually every food product and are recognized by food media as semi-legitimate hooks for brand coverage—provide an editorial calendar anchor that multiplies the likelihood of simultaneous coverage across multiple outlets, creating the cascade effect necessary for viral spread. The Business Wire press release issued on July 13 was timed to provide a 24-hour editorial preparation window before the July 14 launch.

Van Leeuwen's incentive structure is complementary. As a premium artisanal ice cream brand competing in a crowded category that includes well-funded players like Jeni's Splendid Ice Creams, Salt & Straw, and Morgenstern's Finest Ice Cream, Van Leeuwen benefits from the brand visibility that a major CPG partnership provides. The partnership signals that Van Leeuwen is a culturally credible collaborator significant enough to attract Fortune 500 attention—a reputational asset worth more for pricing power and retail placement negotiations than any direct revenue the limited-edition flavor might generate.

The "limited edition until sold out" structure is optimal for all parties: it creates artificial scarcity (Worchel et al., 1975, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 32(5), 906–914, documented scarcity effects on perceived value), eliminates unit unsold risk (no excess inventory, no markdowns), and allows both brands to treat the final day of the product's availability as a second media moment ("sold out in X hours") that generates another round of coverage.

## §6 Verdict Confirmation and Cultural Analysis

The Snopes verdict of True is fully substantiated. The Kraft Macaroni & Cheese Ice Cream was a genuine, commercially available, limited-edition product that sold on July 14, 2021, through Van Leeuwen's retail channels, confirmed by press release, commercial product listing, and subsequent "sold out" status.

The claim is true; the more interesting analytical question is what the claim reveals about the food-media-viral attention economy in which it operated. The Kraft Mac & Cheese Ice Cream is unlikely to have been created primarily because Van Leeuwen believed it was a culinary breakthrough; it was created because both parties calculated, correctly, that its cultural incongruity would generate earned media coverage vastly disproportionate to the cost of developing and producing a limited-edition flavor. In this sense, the product is an artifact of viral media economics rather than of culinary experimentation—though it is simultaneously both.

For readers evaluating similar limited-edition novelty food product claims, the verification hierarchy is: (1) wire service press release from the corporate entity (high credibility, high cost to fabricate); (2) commercial product page on the brand's verified domain (medium-high credibility); (3) media coverage in publications with editorial fact-checking practices; and (4) social media posts from official brand accounts (lower credibility individually, but corroborating in combination).

The frequency of genuine novelty food claims—as distinct from fake or satirical ones—has increased substantially since approximately 2015, as brand marketing teams in CPG companies institutionalized viral campaign strategies. This means that fact-checkers must increasingly resist the "too weird to be true" heuristic and rely more heavily on primary source institutional verification even when the claimed product is genuinely strange.
