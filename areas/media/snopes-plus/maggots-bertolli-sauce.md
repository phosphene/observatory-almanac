# Maggots in Bertolli Sauce?

## §1 — Claim & Verdict Summary

In April 2015, a Facebook user named Gene Finocchiaro posted photographs and commentary claiming that he and his wife had discovered clearly live maggots inside a jar of Bertolli spaghetti sauce purchased at a Walmart in Grants Pass, Oregon. The post received significant regional attention. Six days later, the same individual returned to the same Walmart and produced a video appearing to show the same phenomenon in a jar of Prego sauce on the store shelf — both jars displaying what appeared to be small white larvae. Oregon news outlet KTVL republished the claim, extending its reach without independent verification.

**Snopes verdict: UNPROVEN.** The claim cannot be conclusively established or refuted from the available evidence. Scientifically, it is virtually impossible for maggots to survive the commercial canning and thermal processing conditions required for shelf-stable jarred sauce. The jars depicted in the photographs and video showed visibly "popped" seals — a physical indicator of compromised closure integrity — and the most parsimonious explanation is that contamination occurred after the manufacturing seal was broken, either at the retail level, during transportation, or in the consumer's home environment. However, the chain of custody for the jars was not established, and no independent laboratory analysis was performed. The claim remains procedurally unresolved.

This entry examines the thermodynamics of commercial canning, the entomological science of fly larvae development, the evidentiary standards required to evaluate food contamination claims, and the structural features of social media food-safety allegations that prevent clear adjudication.

## §2 — Origin & Spread of the Claim

The originating post appeared on Facebook on 28 April 2015. Gene Finocchiaro published photographs showing what appeared to be small white larvae in an opened jar of Bertolli spaghetti sauce, along with a description of having noticed the organisms after opening a jar he and his wife had purchased. The post attracted immediate comments, including skeptical responses suggesting a compromised seal was the most likely explanation. KTVL's republication of the claim did not include any indication that the news outlet had independently contacted Bertolli's parent company (Mizkan, which acquired Bertolli in 2015 from Unilever), verified the seal status of the depicted jar, or consulted any food-safety or entomological expert.

The second posting, six days later, alleged that a different sauce brand (Prego, owned by Campbell Soup Company) showed the same contamination pattern on retail shelves at the same Walmart location. This escalation introduced a new interpretive dimension: if contamination was occurring across multiple brands at a single retail outlet, the locus of the problem — if one existed — was more likely to be at the retail storage level than at the manufacturing level. The popped seals in both cases are consistent with this interpretation: a compromised retail storage environment (e.g., temperature cycling causing jar pressures to fluctuate sufficiently to unseat lids) could provide an ingress route for flies that would not exist in a properly sealed jar.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Can Maggots Survive Commercial Canning?

This question has a clear and well-grounded scientific answer: **no, maggots cannot survive commercial canning processes under normal conditions**.

**3.1 — Thermal Processing Standards in Commercial Canning**

Commercial canning of acidified or low-acid foods (including tomato-based sauces, which are borderline pH products typically adjusted to pH ≤ 4.6) is governed by thermal processing protocols designed to destroy Clostridium botulinum and other pathogenic or spoilage organisms. The FDA's regulatory framework (21 CFR Part 113) requires that low-acid canned foods be processed at temperatures not less than 240°F (115.6°C) for a sufficient dwell time to achieve a "12D" reduction of C. botulinum spores — a process that reduces spore density by a factor of 10¹². Acidified foods (pH ≤ 4.6) can be processed at lower temperatures (typically 212°F / 100°C) because the acidic environment prevents C. botulinum germination.

The critical point for this claim: fly eggs and larvae are dramatically less heat-tolerant than bacterial spores. The thermal death kinetics of Diptera (flies) — the insect order that includes all species whose larvae are called maggots — have been studied in the context of food safety and forensic entomology. Undheim, Clarkson, and Tønnessen (2009, *Forensic entomology in the age of the molecular biology*, International Journal for Legal Medicine, 123(3), 185–199) and Byrd and Castner (2001, *Forensic Entomology: The Utility of Arthropods in Legal Investigations*, CRC Press) establish that fly larvae are killed by brief exposure to temperatures above 50–60°C (122–140°F). Commercial canning routinely subjects the entire product mass to temperatures of 100–121°C for several minutes — a kill margin of 40–60°C above the insect thermal death point, applied for a dwell time orders of magnitude longer than required. No dipteran larvae could survive this process.

**3.2 — Survival in the Post-Canning Environment**

Could fly eggs be introduced after canning but before the seal is breached by the consumer? In principle, yes — but only under conditions of seal failure. A properly sealed jar maintains a partial vacuum (negative headspace pressure) that prevents atmospheric gas exchange and, with it, the ingress of airborne biological material. A "popped" lid seal — the standard consumer indicator of seal integrity failure — eliminates this barrier. Once the seal is broken, the jar's interior environment is no longer isolated from the ambient atmosphere, and the contents become susceptible to insect access if the jar remains open or in proximity to dipteran activity.

Garry McDonald, an entomologist from Melbourne University, provided quoted commentary on a similar claim in the Snopes article, noting that: "It's a pretty acidic, hostile environment ... It is unlikely to have been a maggot originating from within that bottle, unless it had been previously opened ... The average life of a maggot — from egg hatch to developing into a pupa — might take two or three weeks." This timeline is forensically useful: the developmental stage of the larvae visible in Finocchiaro's photographs (small, white, mobile) is consistent with first- or second-instar *Musca domestica* or related species at ambient summer temperatures, placing the time of egg deposition approximately one to two weeks before the observation. This window is entirely compatible with either post-manufacturing retail contamination or home-environment contamination following accidental seal breach, but entirely incompatible with in-factory contamination pre-dating the thermal processing step.

**3.3 — The Acidic Environment as Secondary Barrier**

Tomato sauce is a high-acid food, typically pH 3.8–4.2. At this pH, the environment is hostile to most organisms. The survival of Musca domestica larvae in dilute acidic environments has been studied in the context of agricultural waste management. Larval survival in substrates with pH below 4.5 is substantially reduced compared to neutral-pH environments, though not impossible for brief periods if the larvae are first- or second-instar and the moisture content is sufficient (Wall, R., & Shearer, D., 2001, *Veterinary Ectoparasites: Biology, Pathology and Control*, 2nd ed., Blackwell Science). A sealed jar of tomato sauce represents an additionally oxygen-depleted environment that further reduces larval viability. However, if the seal is broken and the jar is maintained at room temperature with the lid loosely replaced (as might occur if a consumer partially used a jar and stored it improperly), the acidic barrier's effectiveness decreases as the sauce surface is exposed to air.

**3.4 — The Unresolved Evidentiary Chain**

The fundamental evidentiary problem with this claim — and the reason Snopes correctly rated it "Unproven" rather than "False" — is that no independent party established the chain of custody for either jar. Questions that would need to be answered for a definitive determination include: What was the exact state of the lid seal when each jar was first photographed? Were the photographs taken immediately upon purchase or after some interval? Had either jar been opened and resealed before the putative contamination was discovered? Did the Walmart store in question have any documented temperature control failures, pest control issues, or receiving problems during the relevant period? Were samples from the jars subjected to independent entomological analysis to identify the species?

None of these questions were answered in the available record. This is not a criticism of the claimant; most consumers do not maintain evidentiary documentation of product defects, and the Snopes investigation appropriately reflects the limits of what the public record supports.

## §4 — Regulatory & Scientific Context

The FDA regulates jarred pasta sauce under 21 CFR Part 114 (acidified foods) and Part 113 (low-acid canned foods), depending on the product's pH profile. Manufacturers of these products are required to file scheduled processes with the FDA, demonstrating that their thermal treatment renders the product commercially sterile. Third-party processing authority review is standard industry practice.

FDA GMP regulations (21 CFR Part 110) require that plants use effective measures to exclude pests from manufacturing environments, prevent contamination from water, soil, or other environmental sources, and maintain physical integrity of closures. If a consumer complaint of maggot contamination were validated by independent analysis and linked to a manufacturing defect, the FDA would typically initiate a facility inspection and, if warranted, a product recall under authority of the Food Safety Modernization Act (FSMA, enacted 2011). No recall was issued for Bertolli tomato sauce in connection with this claim.

The FDA's Defect Levels Handbook (2005, revised) does specify maximum allowable levels of natural defects in tomato products, including the sauce category. The handbook permits up to 30 fly eggs per 100 grams of tomato product, or 1 maggot per 100 grams. This allowance reflects the practical reality of agricultural production: tomatoes grow in fields that contain insects, and some percentage of the raw material will contain insect material that survives into the processed product at microscopic levels. The allowance is predicated on toxicological assessments showing no health risk at these concentrations. However, the Defect Levels Handbook's allowances for trace biological material per 100 grams of product are categorically different from macroscopic, visually apparent, mobile larvae in a retail jar — the latter would represent a manufacturing failure beyond any permissible defect threshold.

## §5 — Comparative Misinformation Ecology

Brand-targeted food-contamination claims on social media follow a distinctive pattern in which the visual element (photograph or video of apparent contamination) reaches mass audiences before any institutional mechanism can verify or refute the claim. The perceptual shock value of maggots — a disgust-maximizing stimulus — dramatically amplifies share velocity. Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer, and Vohs (2001, *Bad Is Stronger Than Good*, Review of General Psychology, 5(4), 323–370) documented the general principle that negative information consistently commands more cognitive resources and behavioral response than equivalent positive information — a negativity bias that is accentuated for contamination stimuli given the evolutionary salience of parasite avoidance.

Brand-level effects of viral food contamination claims can be substantial even when the claims are subsequently shown to be false or unverified. Brandweek reported in 2005 that viral fast-food contamination stories (irrespective of veracity) produced measurable short-term sales decreases for the targeted brands (the "chipotle worm" hoax being a documented example from that period). The mechanism is consistent with Slovic's (1987) affect heuristic: negative associations once attached to a brand or product persist through subsequent corrective information because the initial associative activation is automatic and affective rather than deliberative and inferential (Slovic, P., 1987, *Perception of Risk*, Science, 236(4799), 280–285).

For Bertolli specifically, the claim went viral primarily at a regional level in Oregon before reaching national attention. Unlike the Lipton worm claim (which was definitively falsifiable through product examination), the Bertolli claim involved genuine biological material in a context where the manufacturing impossibility argument was accurate but non-conclusive, because post-manufacturing contamination was an available alternative explanation. This epistemic structure — where the claim is scientifically implausible as described but not structurally impossible given alternative scenarios — produces a "Unproven" rather than "False" rating, and appropriately so.

## §6 — Verdict, Epistemic Gap Assessment & Reader Guidance

**Snopes verdict: UNPROVEN.** Maggots cannot survive commercial canning thermal processes — this is a well-established thermodynamic fact with no caveats for normal manufacturing conditions. The most parsimonious explanation for both jars depicted is post-manufacturing seal failure, whether at retail, in transit, or after initial consumer use. However, the chain of custody for neither jar was independently established, and no laboratory analysis of the organisms was performed to confirm species identity, developmental stage, or infection vector.

**EARC Rating: U (Unresolved — Insufficient Evidence)** — The scientific mechanism argues against the claim, but the evidentiary record for the specific incident is incomplete. Structural implausibility is sufficient for a "mostly false" disposition in some contexts, but the visual evidence of live larvae in conjunction with visibly compromised seals generates enough residual uncertainty for the U rating.

**Knowledge Gap (High):** Multiple substantive questions remain unanswered:
1. Were the jars tested by an independent food-safety laboratory?
2. Did Walmart or the sauce manufacturers investigate and report findings to the FDA?
3. Was the store location subsequently inspected for pest control compliance?
4. Did other customers at the same store report similar findings?

Until these questions are answered, the claim sits in evidentiary limbo.

**Reader guidance:**
- Always check the lid seal of jarred products before consuming. A jar lid that does not "pop" when pressed (indicating loss of the vacuum seal) should be treated as potentially compromised and returned to the seller, regardless of expiration date.
- If you believe you have found live biological contamination in a sealed product manufactured by a major brand, document with photographs (including close-ups of the lid seal), secure the product in a refrigerated environment, and contact both the manufacturer and the FDA consumer complaint line. This provides the chain of custody that distinguishes a credible complaint from an unverifiable social media post.
- Social media food-contamination claims involving major brands should be treated with significant skepticism until independent laboratory confirmation is available, given both the thermodynamic improbability of in-factory contamination and the documented history of fraudulent or misidentified contamination claims.
