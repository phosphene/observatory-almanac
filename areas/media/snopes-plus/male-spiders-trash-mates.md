## The Claim

A social media post from @upworthyscience on X (formerly Twitter), originally published August 19, 2022, stated: "SCIENCE FACT: Male spiders will often wrap up bugs and gift them to female spiders to increase their chances of mating. Sometimes, though, male spiders will wrap up random trash, hoping the female spiders don't notice until after they get busy." Similar formulations of the claim had circulated on Facebook and Reddit as early as 2018.

The claim packages a genuinely interesting piece of animal behavior into an anthropomorphic frame — the male spider as a gift-giving schemer hoping to slip a worthless present past a discerning recipient — and the framing's appeal is clear. It maps spider reproductive behavior onto familiar human social experiences with gift-giving, courtship performance, and the detection of insincerity. The viral success of the post rested partly on the claim's comedy and partly on its apparent factual grounding.

Snopes fact-checker Madison Dapcevich investigated the claim in January 2024, consulting two spider experts and reviewing the scientific literature. The verdict was "True" — with the contextual note that calling such material "trash" is colloquially imprecise, since the "worthless" gifts described in scientific literature are not garbage but more precisely unusable biological waste or plant material.

## What's Actually True

The nuptial gift-giving behavior of certain spider families has been documented in the scientific literature for decades, and the specific phenomenon of "worthless gifts" — male spiders presenting wrapped inedible or nutritionally void material during courtship — has been confirmed by multiple peer-reviewed studies spanning from 2011 to 2023.

The biological basis of the behavior requires some contextual setup. Nuptial gift-giving is not universal among spiders. Only a few families have evolved courtship systems centered on prey gifts, most notably **Pisauridae** (nursery web spiders) and **Trechaleid** spiders. In the canonical form of the behavior, a male captures prey, wraps it in silk, and presents the wrapped package to a potential mate. Mating typically occurs — or is at least facilitated — while the female is engaged in unwrapping and consuming the gift. Jason Bond, an entomologist and professor in the Department of Entomology and Nematology at the University of California, Davis, confirmed this to Snopes: scientists have indeed observed the behavior, and multiple scientific studies confirmed the phenomena.

Sebastian Echeverri, a spider scientist and science communicator, told Snopes that only "a few types of spiders wrap food as a courtship gift to the female" — correcting any impression that this behavior is widespread across the roughly 45,000 known spider species. Within those families, the function of gift-giving appears to serve multiple non-mutually exclusive purposes. As Echeverri explained: "Gift giving seems to have evolved to serve non-mutually exclusive goals. Giving gifts tends to increase males' likelihood of mating, so they are part of the courtship itself. In addition, food gifts can give the female more nutrients to make eggs."

The peer-reviewed literature on worthless gifts is the core factual underpinning of the viral claim. Five research papers are directly relevant:

**Albo, María J., and Fernando G. Costa (2010).** "Nuptial Gift-Giving Behaviour and Male Mating Effort in the Neotropical Spider *Paratrechalea ornata* (Trechaleidae)." *Animal Behaviour*, vol. 79, no. 5, pp. 1031-36 (ScienceDirect). This study examined the trechaleid spider *Paratrechalea ornata* and found that females respond to gifts even when male silk cues are modified, with female silk cues eliciting gift-construction behavior in males.

**Albo, María J., Gudrun Winther, et al. (2011).** "Worthless Donations: Male Deception and Female Counter Play in a Nuptial Gift-Giving Spider." *BMC Evolutionary Biology*, vol. 11, no. 1, p. 329 (BioMed Central). This is one of the groundbreaking papers on deceptive gift-giving. It found that male spiders providing worthless gifts — nutritionally void packages — are not significantly less successful at mating than males offering genuinely nutritional gifts. The research concluded that females don't appear to have strong preferences for nutritional gifts over worthless ones in most cases: "males with worthless gifts appear to have as much success with mating and survival as those offering nutritional gifts."

**A 2017 study** published in *Behavioral Ecology* (vol. 28, no. 3, pp. 744, Oxford University Press) found that worthless scraps are often wrapped in *more* silk than edible gifts — a counterintuitive result. The additional silk wrapping extends the time required for the female to unwrap the gift, giving the male more time to mate before the deception is discovered. This finding suggests active co-evolutionary dynamics: the wrapping strategy is not simply lazy substitution but a calibrated deception that exploits the female's unwrapping behavior.

**Martínez-Villar, Mauro, et al. (2020).** "Empty Nuptial Gifts: A Further Step in the Evolution of Deception in Spiders?" *The Journal of Arachnology*, vol. 48, no. 2, pp. 214-17 (BioOne). This paper documented empty nuptial gifts — silk balls containing nothing at all — as a further extension of the deception strategy, suggesting an evolutionary trajectory in which the gift degrades from nutritional prey to prey remnants to plant material to empty silk. The authors interpreted empty gifts as evidence of ongoing selection pressure favoring even more economical forms of the deceptive strategy.

**Martínez Villar, M., et al. (2023).** "Lack of Female Preference for Nuptial Gifts May Have Led to Loss of the Male Sexual Trait." *Evolutionary Biology*, vol. 50, no. 3, pp. 318-31 (Springer Link). This 2023 study confirmed that "males may also produce worthless gifts" and found that worthless gifts — including prey leftovers, plant parts, and in some cases empty silk balls — are accepted by females during courtship. The study's finding that females accept both worthless and nutritional gifts (apparently unable to distinguish under silk wrapping) provides the proximate mechanism for why the deception strategy works.

**Albo, María J., Camila Pavón-Peláez, et al. (2023).** "Stressful Environments Favor Deceptive Alternative Mating Tactics to Become Dominant." *BMC Biology*, vol. 21, no. 1, p. 162 (BioMed Central). This study added an environmental contingency dimension to the picture: males are more likely to resort to worthless gifts during stressful environmental conditions, particularly when food availability is reduced. Under food-limited conditions, the "cheater strategy" — retaining the prey for personal nutrition while using the scraps for courtship — becomes adaptively dominant. This suggests that the deceptive strategy is not a fixed behavioral trait but an environmentally modulated alternative mating tactic.

Taken together, these studies document the behavior comprehensively: the nuptial gift tradition exists, male spiders do substitute worthless material for genuine prey, the deception succeeds because females cannot distinguish wrapped prey from wrapped non-prey, and the strategy is partly explained by the conservation of male energetic resources under food scarcity conditions.

## Why People Believe This

The viral success of the spider nuptial gift claim illustrates several dynamics that produce rapid and broad uptake of true (or substantially true) animal behavior claims on social media.

**The anthropomorphic narrative template.** The claim works because it maps onto a human social template that is instantly recognisable: the partner who presents a fraudulent gift, hoping the deception isn't discovered before the desired outcome is secured. This template is familiar from interpersonal experience, from comedy traditions, from romance critique. When animal behavior maps cleanly onto a human social template, it simultaneously seems surprising (animals do this?) and immediately comprehensible (of course they do, I've seen humans do it). The surprise of novelty and the pleasure of recognition combine to produce strong engagement. Bond's confirmation — "It's not entirely clear that females always 'don't notice' but in some taxa that may be the case" — preserves the essential truth of the claim while adding scientific precision without undermining its appeal.

**The epistemic novelty of evolved deception.** The existence of systematic, evolved deception within a single species — where male members of the species reliably deceive female members through ritualised false gift presentation, and where the females have evolved no reliable counter-detection mechanism — is genuinely surprising from an evolutionary naïve perspective. Students of evolutionary biology who have encountered arms race dynamics, honest signaling theory, and the literature on sexual conflict will find the result expected; but for general audiences, the idea that evolution produced consistent intrasexual deception as a stable strategy is counterintuitive and therefore attention-worthy. This represents a genuine epistemic novelty: the popular understanding of gift-giving behavior, even when people know it exists in other species, tends to assume it involves honest signals of quality. The "worthless gift" literature complicates this picture significantly.

**The truth value as amplifier, not just the frame.** Unlike many viral claims that spread because they confirm preconceptions or validate anxieties, the spider nuptial gift claim spread partly because it is substantially true and because truth of this kind — confirmed by peer-reviewed research, testified to by expert sources — carries a different emotional valence than rumour. Sharing it carries a certain epistemic pride: "look what science found." The fact-check's "True" verdict is not merely permission to believe the claim — it retrospectively validates the shared instinct that this was genuinely worth sharing. Claims confirmed by expert scientific literature and verified by fact-checkers acquire a different kind of social currency than unverified viral content.

**The compressed truth-complexity of viral formatting.** The @upworthyscience framing — "male spiders wrap random trash, hoping the female doesn't notice until after they get busy" — is a significant simplification of the actual scientific picture. Real nuptial gift fraud is not precisely "random trash" (it's usually prey leftovers or plant material), and "hoping the female doesn't notice" anthropomorphises a process that is better described as an evolved behavioral strategy that persists because females systematically fail to distinguish worthless from nutritional gifts under silk wrapping. But these simplifications do not falsify the claim — they domesticate it for general audiences. The core factual content is preserved through the simplification, which is why the "True" verdict is warranted even for the colloquially framed version.

## Verdict

**The claim is true.** Male spiders of certain species — primarily within the nuptial gift-giving families Pisauridae and Trechaleid — do present wrapped "worthless gifts" to females during courtship. These worthless gifts contain prey remnants, plant material, or in some documented cases nothing at all. Females accept them because they cannot reliably distinguish genuine prey gifts from worthless ones under silk wrapping. The deception is an evolved strategy that provides the male with mating access while conserving his energetic resources. Multiple peer-reviewed studies spanning 2010-2023 confirm the behavior's existence and explore its mechanisms and triggers.

The word "trash" is colloquially imprecise — the material in worthless gifts is typically biological waste from prey rather than environmental garbage — but this is a quibble about word choice rather than factual accuracy. The core claim is correct, and it is supported by documented scientific research including Albo et al. (2011, *BMC Evolutionary Biology*), Martínez-Villar et al. (2020, *Journal of Arachnology*), Martínez Villar et al. (2023, *Evolutionary Biology*), Albo et al. (2023, *BMC Biology*), and a 2017 study in *Behavioral Ecology* on gift-wrapping dynamics.

## The Wider Picture

The spider nuptial gift literature sits at the productive intersection of behavioral ecology, evolutionary biology, and the study of sexual conflict — a research area that has generated considerable insight into the co-evolutionary dynamics of mate choice, honest signaling, and deception.

**The evolutionary logic of nuptial gifts.** Nuptial gift-giving as a mating strategy poses interesting puzzles for evolutionary theory. The standard sexual selection framework (Bateman, A.J., "Intra-Sexual Selection in Drosophila," *Heredity*, vol. 2, 1948, pp. 349-368) predicts that male mating success is limited by access to females while female success is limited by resource quality. Nuptial gifts in this framework function as honest signals of male quality and as direct material benefits to the female — a male who can provide substantial prey gifts signals foraging ability and territory quality, and the female benefits nutritionally. The existence of worthless gifts is an anomaly in this framework: it predicts selection against deceptive strategies once females have the capability to detect them.

The resolution lies in the co-evolutionary dynamics documented by Albo et al. (2011) and subsequent work. Females systematically fail to detect worthless gifts because the detection mechanism — evaluating the gift's content — requires unwrapping it, and unwrapping typically occurs during or after mating. By the time the female discovers the gift is worthless, the male has already mated. The evolutionary game is therefore between males evolving better deceptions (more convincing wrapping, more believable garbage) and females evolving better pre-copulatory detection mechanisms. The 2023 study by Martínez Villar et al. found that females lack strong preferences for nutritional gifts over worthless ones in most observed cases, suggesting that the detection arms race has not progressed far — females haven't evolved reliable detection, and males don't need to invest in particularly sophisticated deceptions.

**Sexual conflict and the "cheater strategy."** Sebastian Echeverri's explanation of the male's potential motivation — "to get the benefit [of mating] without needing to give up a food item" — situates the behavior within the broader framework of sexual conflict in animal behavior. Sexual conflict theory (Parker, G.A., "Sexual Conflict," *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B: Biological Sciences*, vol. 341, 1993, pp. 221-229) predicts that in species where male and female reproductive interests diverge, selection will favor traits in each sex that serve their respective interests at the expense of the other sex's interests. Male nuptial gift fraud is a text-book example: the male extracts mating access (his interest) while denying the female the nutritional benefit she would receive from a genuine gift (her interest, since more nutrients enable more eggs).

The Albo et al. (2023) finding — that stressful environments favoring food scarcity make the deceptive strategy dominant — adds an important empirical dimension. The cheater strategy is not genetically fixed but environmentally contingent. When food is plentiful, males may find it advantageous to provide genuine gifts (signaling quality, perhaps increasing their offspring's survival through their mother's improved nutrition). When food is scarce, the conservation of personal resources outweighs the value of the honest signal, and deception becomes adaptive. This environmental contingency makes the system dynamically stable rather than vulnerable to evolutionary takeover by one strategy or the other.

**Honest signaling and its subversion.** The spider nuptial gift system relates to the larger literature on honest signaling in animal communication (Zahavi, Amotz, "Mate Selection — A Selection for a Handicap," *Journal of Theoretical Biology*, vol. 53, 1975, pp. 205-214, Academic Press). Zahavi's handicap principle proposes that honest signals are costly to produce, making them reliable indicators of quality — a male who can produce a generous prey gift while remaining healthy and reproductively viable is genuinely demonstrating quality. The worthless gift subverts this logic by decoupling the signal (a gift-wrapped package) from the underlying quality indicator (captured prey), suggesting that the signal has not remained honest across evolutionary time. Systems of honest signaling are vulnerable to invasion by cheaters wherever the cost of producing the false signal is lower than the benefit received — which is precisely what the deceptive nuptial gift system demonstrates.

**Arachnology and public science communication.** The spider nuptial gift story is an unusually successful piece of public science communication — it transmits accurate scientific content to a general audience in a form they find memorable and repeatable. Sebastian Echeverri, described in the Snopes article as a "spider scientist" and identified as an arachnology science communicator at spiderdaynightlive.com, represents a generation of researchers who engage actively with public understanding of their field. The Snopes investigation benefited from access to knowledgeable sources willing to explain complex evolutionary concepts for general audiences. The resulting article does what the best public science fact-checking should do: it confirms the claim's truth, explains the underlying mechanisms, and adds precision to the vernacular framing without dismissing it.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes treatment of the spider nuptial gift claim is a model for fact-checking claims that are true but require scientific context to be properly understood.

**Confirming truth with expert grounding.** The "True" verdict is appropriate and is grounded appropriately in both expert consultation (Bond; Echeverri) and the primary scientific literature (the five papers cited explicitly). This dual grounding — expert opinion plus documented research — is appropriate for a biological behavior claim where the relevant knowledge base is specialized.

**The precision calibration challenge.** The colloquial phrase "random trash" is technically imprecise — it is not garbage but typically prey remnants or plant material. Dapcevich's treatment of this imprecision is a good model: the article acknowledges the imprecision ("Calling such material 'trash' may be misleading in the sense it's not garbage but more so unusable waste") without overweighting it as a deficiency in the original claim. The core content is true; the word choice is loose. Marking this correctly — as a contextual note rather than a verdict-altering factor — preserves analytical proportionality.

**The environmental contingency omission.** The Snopes article does not discuss the 2023 *BMC Biology* finding that food scarcity drives increased deceptive gift-giving, beyond a brief mention. This is a minor gap in the contextualisation. The finding is important because it complicates the simple picture of deceptive gift-giving as a uniform male strategy — it is an environmentally modulated alternative tactic. Including this dimension would strengthen the article's scientific depth without changing the verdict.

**The value of the "True" verdict for societal trust.** Not all viral claims are false. The spider gift claim demonstrates this clearly. One function of fact-checking is to confirm true claims as well as debunk false ones — a fact-checker who only ever issues "False" verdicts trains audiences to expect that viral content is always wrong, which is itself a form of misinformation. The "True" verdict here performs a genuine service: it confirms a scientific finding that is accurately represented in popular form, validates the sharing behavior of people who circulated the claim in good faith, and gives audiences a data point that reinforces trust in their own ability to recognise genuinely interesting science when they encounter it.
