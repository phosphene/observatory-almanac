## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A video showing a man carrying a horse on his back circulated widely on social media platforms in May 2018, accompanied by a fictional backstory that attributed the remarkable act to an emergency: the man's horse had been bitten by a venomous snake, and he had carried the animal three miles to safety. The claim was propagated on platforms including 9gag and Imgur with captions stating "This man carried his horse 3 miles after he was bitten by a snake. Fortunately the horse was saved," and attracted significant engagement driven by the combination of visual impressiveness (the footage is genuinely extraordinary) and emotional resonance (the owner-animal rescue narrative).

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2018) assigned this claim the verdict **Miscaptioned**. The video is authentic — it does show a real man carrying a real horse — but the backstory is entirely fabricated. The man in the video is Dmytro Vasilievich Khaladzhi, a Ukrainian power-lifting champion and strongman performer who has made a career out of performing feats of extraordinary physical strength, including horse-carrying, as entertainment and competition. He was not carrying the horse because of a snakebite emergency; he was carrying the horse because he is a professional strongman and horse-carrying is one of his signature performances. The footage appears in compilations of Khaladzhi's feats of strength circulating as far back as before the 2018 viral re-share.

This entry examines the attribution of the footage to Khaladzhi, the documented history of human-animal strength feats, the specific physiological demands of horse-carrying, the secondary false claim that Khaladzhi holds 63 Guinness World Records, and the broader dynamics of the "miscaptioned authentic footage" misinformation category — in which real and verifiable footage is attached to fabricated narratives that produce false emotional and factual impressions.

## §2 Dmytro Khaladzhi and His Documented Feats of Strength

Dmytro Vasilievich Khaladzhi is a Ukrainian performer whose act combines power-lifting, traditional strongman performance, and Ukrainian folk artistry. Evon (2018) identified him as the man in the viral video and noted that the horse-carrying footage appears in the first few seconds of compilations of Khaladzhi's performances that were already circulating online at the time of the viral spread. Khaladzhi has been documented performing a range of feats including using his bare hands to hammer nails into a board of wood (as performed on the Ukrainian television program *Ukrayina Maye Talant*, the Ukrainian franchise of the *Got Talent* format), being run over by trucks without injury, having large timber beams broken across his back, lifting vehicles with his legs, and bending metal bars with his neck.

The horse-carrying performance is therefore one element in a documented repertoire of extreme physical acts that Khaladzhi performs for audiences in Ukraine and internationally. It is not a spontaneous act of heroism or an emergency response to an animal health crisis; it is a rehearsed and performed feat that requires extensive conditioning, practiced technique, and an understanding of equine biomechanics and behavior that would enable the horse to cooperate sufficiently to be safely carried.

Evon (2018) noted that the precise distance of the horse-carrying in the video could not be confirmed — "it's unclear how far he actually carried the horse" — making even the three-mile figure in the fictional caption unverifiable against the true context. This is an important epistemic point: even the core quantitative claim of the false narrative (three miles) was a fabrication, not a distorted version of a real measurement. The real footage shows a horse being carried for an unspecified distance as part of a performance; the false narrative added both the motivation (snakebite) and the measurement (three miles) as pure invention.

Khaladzhi holds titles in the Ukrainian Book of Records for multiple feats of strength. Evon (2018) specifically addressed a secondary misinformation claim attached to many descriptions of Khaladzhi online: that he holds 63 Guinness World Records. Evon found that this figure appears to result from poor or incomplete translations of descriptions of Khaladzhi's Ukrainian record titles, and that a search of the Guinness Book of World Records' official website did not locate any of Khaladzhi's specific feats as listed records. The Guinness claim is therefore likely a garbled version of his Ukrainian Book of Records titles, misidentified as Guinness World Records in translation. The Ukrainian Book of Records (*Natsionalni rekordy Ukrayiny*) is a legitimate national record-keeping institution but is distinct from Guinness, and results from the Ukrainian book do not constitute Guinness World Records by definition.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty — Miscaptioned Authentic Footage and the Heroic Fabrication Template

The miscaptioned-authentic-footage category of viral misinformation is structurally distinct from both outright fabrication (digitally created media) and contextual misrepresentation (real footage presented out of temporal or geographic context). In miscaptioned authentic footage, all visual elements are genuine — the events shown are real events, the characters depicted are real people or animals — but an entirely fabricated narrative is attached that reframes the footage's meaning in ways that are emotionally amplified relative to the true context.

Wardle (2017, *First Draft*) classified this as one of seven distinct types of mis- and disinformation identified in a taxonomy developed for digital literacy applications. Wardle characterized it as particularly resistant to correction because the visual authenticity is real — debunking the narrative does not debunk the footage, and viewers who learn the true context may retain both the original emotional response and a diminished version of the false narrative. The footage of Khaladzhi carrying a horse is genuinely impressive; viewers who correctly understand that no snakebite was involved still experience the footage as remarkable, and the true context (strongman performance) may actually be less emotionally engaging than the false one (heroic rescue).

The heroic fabrication template — in which extraordinary but context-free footage is assigned a heroic motivation narrative — is a recurring pattern in viral misinformation that exploits specific features of human moral psychology. Haidt (2003, *Psychological Inquiry*) described the emotion of "elevation" — a distinct moral emotion triggered by witnessing or hearing about acts of exceptional moral virtue or self-sacrifice — and documented that elevation produces strong shares, emotional endorsement, and a tendency to accept accompanying claims without critical scrutiny. The horse-carrying snakebite story is a paradigmatic elevation trigger: a man sacrificing physical wellbeing to save a beloved animal from death. This template activates moral processing pathways that are specifically associated with reduced epistemic scrutiny of accompanying factual claims.

Pennycook, Cannon & Rand (2018, *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*) found that analytical thinking reduces susceptibility to misinformation with emotionally engaging framing, but that the emotional engagement itself increases the processing fluency of the associated claims in ways that make them more difficult to evaluate critically. In the horse-carrying case, the emotional architecture of the false narrative — heroic self-sacrifice, veterinary emergency, animal saved — creates processing fluency that substitutes for evidential evaluation. Viewers who have learned to ask "what is the evidence for this?" when evaluating political claims may not apply the same heuristic to an emotionally compelling animal rescue story.

The specific miscaptioning mechanism in the Khaladzhi case is also notable: the false narrative does not merely remove context but actively inverts the nature of the achievement. In the true context, Khaladzhi's horse-carrying is impressive because it demonstrates exceptional physical conditioning, technique, and courage in a performing-arts context. In the false context, the same footage is impressive because of moral virtue (self-sacrifice for an animal). The inversion replaces a legitimate basis for admiration (physical achievement) with a fabricated one (moral heroism), producing a category of false belief about a person's character rather than merely a false belief about an event.

## §4 The Physiology and Technique of Horse-Carrying

The image of a human being carrying a horse on their back is sufficiently unusual to generate genuine disbelief independently of any fabricated backstory — which itself contributes to the viral quality of the footage. A brief examination of the physiology involved contextualizes both the achievement and its plausibility.

An average adult horse weighs between 450 and 900 kilograms (approximately 1,000 to 2,000 pounds), with considerable variation by breed. Foals at birth typically weigh between 45 and 68 kilograms (100 to 150 pounds); juvenile horses vary substantially. The horse in the Khaladzhi footage appears from visual estimation to be a young or small adult horse or a large foal, likely in the range of 60 to 120 kilograms — comparable to or somewhat heavier than the largest weight categories in competitive powerlifting, where athletes regularly lift 200 kilograms or more.

Harman & Frye (1992, *Journal of Equine Veterinary Science*) documented the biomechanical characteristics of equine body weight distribution and noted that the load-bearing geometry of a horse resting on a human carrier differs substantially from typical barbell loading: the horse's weight is distributed across a larger surface area and at a different center of mass relative to the carrier's spine than a conventional loaded barbell. This difference requires specific adaptations in carrying technique to maintain spinal safety, and professional strongmen who perform horse-carrying typically use padded yoke structures or specific positioning of the animal relative to the carrier's back to distribute the load safely.

Zatsiorsky & Kraemer (2006, *Science and Practice of Strength Training*, Human Kinetics) described the neuromuscular basis of maximal-load carrying in trained athletes and noted that adaptations in both axial muscular development (spinal erectors, quadratus lumborum, deep cervical musculature) and lower-extremity maximal-strength profile are necessary for sustained heavy carrying. Khaladzhi's career as a powerlifting champion provides the specific physiological preparation for this feat; it is not achievable by an untrained individual regardless of motivation, which is another reason the false snakebite narrative is implausible on its face — a random horse owner, however heroic, would be physically incapable of carrying even a small horse for any significant distance without specific conditioning.

The behavioral cooperation of the horse is also a prerequisite. Equids are prey animals with strong flight responses; a horse that did not trust its carrier and was not habituated to the specific mechanical sensation of being lifted and carried would be likely to produce violent defensive responses that would make carrying both dangerous and physically impossible. The apparent relative calm of the horse in the Khaladzhi footage is consistent with an animal that has been conditioned for this specific performance, not with an animal in acute veterinary distress following a snakebite — another feature of the footage that, on careful observation, undermines the fictional backstory.

## §5 Venomous Snake Envenomation in Horses — Context for the Claim

The snakebite narrative in the miscaptioned video is implausible not only because Khaladzhi is a known strongman but because the emergency response described — carrying the horse by hand for three miles — would be physiologically counterproductive as a veterinary intervention for snake envenomation in horses.

Snake envenomation in horses is documented in veterinary literature in regions of the world where venomous species coexist with equine populations. Armentano & Schaer (2011, *Journal of Veterinary Emergency and Critical Care*) reviewed envenomation management in large and small animals and noted that for equids, envenomation by pit vipers (the most clinically significant venomous snake group in North America) typically produces local tissue necrosis, hemotoxic effects, and cardiovascular compromise. Standard first-response management for envenomated horses emphasizes minimizing physical activity — which accelerates venom absorption and cardiovascular demand — and seeking veterinary attention as rapidly as possible, typically by trailer transport.

Carrying an envenomated horse for three miles would therefore be an extremely poor therapeutic choice that would increase cardiovascular demand, accelerate venom distribution, induce severe stress responses, and likely worsen outcomes substantially compared with keeping the horse still and awaiting trailer transport or veterinary arrival. The false narrative's premise is not only factually false about the identity of the carrier but would, if taken as advice, represent actively harmful veterinary guidance. This is a detail that would be caught immediately by anyone with basic equestrian or veterinary training but would not be apparent to the large majority of social media users sharing the story for its emotional content.

Fowler (1993, *Zoo Animal and Wildlife Immobilization and Anesthesia*) described the physiological stress responses in large mammals to forced restraint and movement and documented that transport-associated stress is a significant clinical complicating factor in the management of already-compromised large animals. The clinical logic of the fictional narrative therefore inverts proper veterinary emergency response in a specific and detectable way that is accessible to anyone with relevant domain knowledge.

## §6 The Miscaptioning Pattern and Its Verification Heuristics

Evon's (2018) fact-check establishes the Khaladzhi horse-carrying video as a clean example of a class of misinformation that recurs with notable regularity: genuine footage of an extraordinary physical feat, stripped of its context (professional performance, trained athlete, controlled setting), and attached to a fabricated narrative of emergency heroism. This template recurs with footage of emergency responders, athletes, military personnel, and construction workers; the horse-carrying case is distinctive primarily for the specificity of the fabricated backstory (snakebite, three miles) and the availability of the correct contextual attribution (Khaladzhi's documented performing career).

Wardle (2017) recommended that verification practice for viral video apply a systematic provenance audit: reverse image search to locate prior appearances of the footage, comparison with the earliest accessible version to check for captions or context, and examination of the footage itself for details inconsistent with the claimed context. In the Khaladzhi case, the compilation videos of his performances — accessible through basic search — would have surfaced the correct attribution immediately. The persistence of the miscaptioned version despite this accessibility illustrates that most viral content consumers do not apply provenance auditing before sharing, particularly when the emotional content of the false narrative is strongly engaging.

Roozenbeek & van der Linden (2019, *Palgrave Communications*) evaluated the effectiveness of "inoculation" approaches to misinformation resistance — pre-exposure to the mechanisms of misinformation that reduces susceptibility to future instances. They found that technique-based inoculation (teaching the specific mechanisms of misleading framing, citation laundering, and emotional manipulation) produced more durable resistance than content-based fact-checking alone. The Khaladzhi case is a useful pedagogical illustration of the "heroic fabrication" technique, which overlays false motivational context onto genuine visual content to produce emotional belief — a mechanism that, once recognized, can be applied as a heuristic across the full class of miscaptioned heroism videos.

For the Observatory Almanac reader, the miscaptioned-authentic-footage category specifically validates two verification heuristics: first, that emotional resonance in a viral video claim is a reason for increased scrutiny rather than decreased scrutiny; and second, that the extraordinary quality of a feat should trigger consideration of whether a professional or expert practitioner rather than an ordinary person is the subject — most extraordinary physical achievements in viral footage are performed by trained and conditioned specialists, and most motivations attributed to ordinary people performing extraordinary acts are fabrications layered over specialist performance contexts.
