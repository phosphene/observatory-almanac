# A Man Was Living Normally with 90% of His Brain Missing


## 1. The Claim

Since 2007, a set of striking medical images has circulated online showing what appears to be a human skull filled mostly with fluid, with only a thin rim of brain tissue pressed against the inner surface. The claim that accompanies these images, in various formations, asserts that a 44-year-old French civil servant was discovered to be "missing 90% of his brain" and yet was living a "normal life" — married, a father of two, employed, and functioning in society.

The images are invariably accompanied by some version of the sentiment: "You don't need most of your brain to lead a normal life." On Reddit, posts about the case have accumulated tens of thousands of upvotes. On TikTok, YouTube, and Facebook, the story has been retold millions of times, typically framed as a mind-blowing science fact that overturns everything we thought we knew about the brain.

The claim operates at several levels. At its most straightforward, it presents a dramatic medical image and a startling assertion about the amount of brain tissue present. At a slightly deeper level, it implies that the brain is mostly "unused" — resonating with the persistent folk myth that humans only use 10% of their brains. At its deepest, it is often leveraged in discussions of consciousness, free will, and the nature of mind, used to argue that consciousness is not reducible to brain tissue.

The medical images are authentic. The underlying case is real and genuinely remarkable. But nearly everything the viral version claims about what the case means is wrong, starting with the assertion that 90% of the brain was "missing."


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Case Report**

The case was first published on July 21, 2007, in *The Lancet*, one of the world's most prestigious medical journals, in a brief case report titled "Brain of a white-collar worker" by Feuillet, Dufour, and Pelletier. The text is worth reading in full because it is remarkably concise and precise:

The patient was a 44-year-old married civil servant and father of two who presented with a two-week history of mild left leg weakness. His medical history revealed that at six months of age, he had been diagnosed with hydrocephalus of unknown cause and had received a ventriculoatrial shunt — a device that drains excess cerebrospinal fluid from the brain's ventricles into the right atrium of the heart. At age 14, he developed ataxia (poor muscle coordination) and left leg weakness, which resolved after his shunt was revised.

His neurological development and medical history were otherwise unremarkable. CT and MRI imaging revealed severe dilation of the lateral, third, and fourth ventricles, a "very thin cortical mantle," and a posterior fossa cyst. The diagnosis was non-communicating hydrocephalus, probably caused by stenosis (narrowing) of Magendie's foramen — one of the openings through which cerebrospinal fluid normally drains from the ventricular system.

The images are genuinely striking. On CT and MRI, the vast majority of the intracranial space appears to be occupied by fluid-filled ventricles, with brain tissue compressed into a thin layer along the inner surface of the skull. To an untrained eye, it looks as though the brain is largely "missing."

On neuropsychological testing, the man had an overall IQ of 75 (verbal IQ: 84, performance IQ: 70). He was treated with a ventriculocisternostomy (an endoscopic procedure creating a new drainage pathway for cerebrospinal fluid) followed by a ventriculoperitoneal shunt. His leg weakness resolved within weeks, though his neuropsychological profile and imaging appearance did not change.

**What "Missing" Actually Means**

The single most important fact about this case — the one that the viral versions consistently omit or misrepresent — is that the brain tissue was not "missing." It was compressed.

Hydrocephalus involves the accumulation of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) within the ventricular system of the brain. In this patient, the condition had been present since infancy, meaning the fluid accumulation occurred gradually over decades as the brain was developing. This slow, chronic pressure caused the ventricles to expand progressively, pushing brain tissue outward against the skull.

When you look at the scans, what you see is not empty space where brain used to be. You see fluid-filled ventricles that have expanded to occupy most of the intracranial cavity, with brain tissue compressed into a thin but continuous layer around the periphery. The tissue is still there. It has been compressed, reorganized, and in some areas likely damaged — but it has not disappeared.

Axel Cleeremans, the cognitive psychologist who brought renewed attention to the case in 2016, confirmed this directly to Snopes: "It is not true to say this person is missing 90% of his brain." He explained that while "nobody knows exactly how many neurons have been destroyed by hydrocephalus," most of the neurons are "probably compressed into the very fine residual layer." He added that "the patient has lost many neurons, and those neurons that are left are compressed against the skull."

The distinction between "compressed" and "missing" is not semantic. It is the difference between a brain that has been structurally reorganized under chronic pressure and a brain that has lost the vast majority of its cellular constituents. These are profoundly different neuroscientific scenarios with profoundly different implications.

**What the IQ Tells Us**

The man's IQ of 75 is consistently glossed over or minimized in viral versions of the story, which describe him as having "normal social functioning" or living a "completely normal life." An IQ of 75 falls in the borderline intellectual functioning range — below approximately 95% of the population. It is above the threshold for intellectual disability (typically defined as IQ below 70) but significantly below average (IQ 100).

This matters because the viral narrative depends on the contrast between the dramatic imaging and supposedly normal cognition. If the patient had perfectly average intelligence despite his imaging findings, the case would indeed suggest remarkable neural compensation. But with an IQ of 75, the case actually shows exactly what a neuroscientist familiar with hydrocephalus might predict: significant cognitive impact from severe chronic hydrocephalus, partially mitigated by the brain's compensatory mechanisms.

The Lancet case report notes that the patient had "normal social functioning" — meaning he maintained relationships, worked, and participated in community life. This is remarkable given his imaging findings, but it is not the same as "normal" cognitive function. The distinction between social functioning and cognitive function is crucial and is routinely collapsed in popular retellings.

**How Chronic Hydrocephalus Differs from Acute Brain Injury**

The viral claim implicitly invites comparison to acute brain injury: "Imagine losing 90% of your brain!" But chronic hydrocephalus that develops from infancy is a categorically different condition.

When a healthy adult suffers acute brain injury — from trauma, stroke, or surgical removal — the brain has no time to compensate. Functions localized in the damaged tissue are immediately and often permanently lost. The severity of the deficit corresponds roughly to the amount and location of tissue destroyed.

In congenital or early-onset hydrocephalus, the brain develops under gradually increasing pressure from birth. This fundamentally alters the developmental calculus. Neural circuits that normally form in particular anatomical configurations can, under very slow chronic pressure, potentially reorganize into compressed configurations while retaining at least partial function. Neurons that would normally occupy a thick cortical layer can be compressed into a thin layer while maintaining some degree of connectivity.

This is not the brain shrugging off the loss of 90% of its tissue. It is the developing brain adapting in real time to abnormal physical constraints — forming whatever circuits it can within the space and conditions available. The adaptation is remarkable. It is not evidence that most of the brain is dispensable.

The analogy would be to a tree growing in a narrow crack between rocks. The tree's shape is dramatically altered by the constraints, and it may be smaller and less robust than a tree growing in open ground. But it has adapted its growth to its environment, not demonstrated that trees don't need open space.

**What the Imaging Does and Doesn't Show**

CT and MRI provide macroscopic views of brain anatomy. They show the overall shape, the size of fluid-filled spaces, and the thickness of tissue layers. They do not directly visualize individual neurons, synaptic connections, or the microscopic organization of neural circuits.

When the viral narrative claims the man is "missing 90% of his brain," it is interpreting radiological images as though the volume occupied by fluid represents the volume of brain tissue that should be there but isn't. This interpretation has several problems:

First, it assumes that the fluid completely displaced tissue rather than compressing it. Compressed tissue occupies less volume but is not gone.

Second, it does not account for the density of neural tissue. A thin layer of compressed cortex may contain a substantial number of neurons packed into a small volume — potentially many more than the volumetric appearance would suggest.

Third, as Cleeremans noted, the standard imaging did not clearly show certain deep brain structures — the hippocampus, thalamus — that are likely present but difficult to resolve in the context of massive ventricular dilation. The absence of these structures from the images does not mean they are absent from the brain.

**The Broader Context: Hydrocephalus Outcomes**

This case, while dramatic in its imaging appearance, is not unique. The neurosurgical literature contains numerous cases of severe chronic hydrocephalus with varying degrees of preserved function. A 1980 report by John Lorber in *Science* described a series of hydrocephalic patients, including several with "virtually no brain" on imaging who had IQs above 100. Lorber's findings were controversial and his interpretations have been criticized, but the underlying observation — that some individuals with severe hydrocephalus preserve remarkable functional capacity — has been confirmed repeatedly.

A 2014 review by de Oliveira and colleagues in *Child's Nervous System* examined cognitive outcomes in treated childhood hydrocephalus and found wide variability, with some patients achieving normal-range intelligence and others showing significant impairment. The key predictors included etiology (cause) of the hydrocephalus, timing of treatment, number of shunt revisions, and presence of associated brain malformations.

The French civil servant's case sits within this spectrum — toward the severely affected end in terms of imaging, but with functional outcomes that, while below average, represent meaningful adaptation.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The "We Only Use 10% of Our Brains" Resonance**

The viral success of this case is inseparable from one of neuroscience's most persistent folk myths: that humans only use 10% of their brains. This myth, which has been thoroughly debunked — functional neuroimaging consistently shows activity across virtually all brain regions during the course of a day — remains widely believed. A 2013 survey found that 65% of Americans endorsed the "10% myth."

The French civil servant's case appears to provide dramatic visual confirmation of this myth. If a man can live with "only 10% of his brain," then obviously we only need 10%. The two claims reinforce each other in a closed loop of mutual confirmation, despite both being incorrect.

The 10% myth persists because it is psychologically appealing. It implies untapped potential — a vast reserve of cognitive power waiting to be unlocked. It resonates with aspirational narratives about human possibility and feeds into the marketing of "brain-boosting" products and techniques. The hydrocephalus case, when misinterpreted as brain dispensability, slots perfectly into this aspirational framework.

**The Power of the Visual**

The CT and MRI images in this case are genuinely arresting. They show what appears to be a skull filled mostly with fluid, with only a thin rim of brain tissue. The visual impact is immediate and visceral. You do not need medical training to look at these images and think "there's almost nothing there."

This visual immediacy is precisely what makes the case so viral. A textual description of "severe ventricular dilation with thin cortical mantle" is medically precise but visually inert. The images themselves tell a story that bypasses critical analysis: "Look at this. Almost no brain. And yet he lives normally." The gap between the dramatic appearance and the functional outcome creates a sense of wonder that drives sharing.

But visual interpretation of medical imaging by non-specialists is notoriously unreliable. Radiological images require training to interpret correctly. The dark areas on CT that look like "empty space" are filled with cerebrospinal fluid. The thin bright rim that looks like "almost nothing" may contain billions of compressed neurons. Without the training to make these distinctions, the images tell a misleading story.

**The Consciousness Hook**

The case gained its second major wave of viral attention in 2016 when Axel Cleeremans discussed it at the Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness conference in Buenos Aires. Cleeremans' interest in the case was scientific and specific: he was using it to illustrate his "radical plasticity thesis" — the theory that consciousness is learned rather than innate, and that the brain continuously "re-describes" its own activity to itself.

Media coverage of Cleeremans' presentation, however, collapsed this nuanced theoretical argument into the headline-friendly claim that "a man missing 90% of his brain challenges our understanding of consciousness." This framing — a single case overturning established science — is irresistible to media outlets and social media sharers alike. It triggers the "paradigm-busting discovery" narrative template that generates enormous engagement.

In reality, the case does not challenge the necessity of brain tissue for consciousness. It illustrates the brain's capacity for developmental adaptation under chronic pathological pressure — a remarkable phenomenon, but one that operates within, not against, neuroscientific understanding.

**The Narrative Simplicity**

The viral version of this story works because it has an extraordinarily simple narrative structure: man has almost no brain → man lives normal life → therefore brain is mostly unnecessary. Each element is wrong or misleading, but together they form a story so clean and surprising that it practically demands sharing.

The accurate version — man has brain tissue compressed by chronic hydrocephalus → man has below-average but partially functional cognition → developmental neuroscience explains this through well-understood adaptation mechanisms — is more complicated, less surprising, and therefore less viral. As with many medical myths, the false version wins the attention economy because it is simpler and more dramatic than the true version.


## 4. Verdict

**The claim is distorted-but-grounded.** The medical images are authentic. The case is real and genuinely remarkable. But the viral narrative distorts the case through three systematic errors:

1. **"Missing" vs. compressed**: The brain tissue was not gone. It was compressed by decades of gradually expanding fluid. Compressed neurons in a thin cortical layer are profoundly different from absent neurons.

2. **"Normal life" vs. reduced function**: The man had an IQ of 75, in the borderline range. He maintained social relationships and employment, which is remarkable given his imaging, but this is not the "completely normal life" the viral versions describe.

3. **Brain dispensability vs. brain plasticity**: The case demonstrates that a developing brain can adapt to chronic pathological constraints by reorganizing its structure — not that most of the brain is unnecessary. The distinction between plasticity (remarkable adaptation within constraints) and dispensability (the tissue isn't needed) is the central thing the viral narrative gets wrong.

The case is genuinely interesting and scientifically valuable. It illustrates the profound capacity of the developing nervous system to compensate for structural disruption when that disruption occurs gradually from early life. This is important neuroscience that deserves to be widely known. But it does not support the claim that you can live normally without most of your brain, and it does not vindicate the "10% myth."

The irony is that the true story is more interesting than the false one. The brain's ability to compress, reorganize, and maintain partial function under decades of pathological pressure is far more remarkable than the simplistic narrative that people don't need their brains. Brain plasticity is a richer, more nuanced, and more genuinely awe-inspiring phenomenon than brain dispensability. The viral version sacrifices the genuine wonder for a cheap surprise.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The Neuroscience of Plasticity**

This case's real significance lies not in what it tells us about how little brain we need, but in what it tells us about how brains develop under adverse conditions. Developmental neuroplasticity — the brain's ability to organize and reorganize its structure during growth — is one of the most robust phenomena in neuroscience.

The developing brain operates under what neuroscientists call "experience-dependent plasticity" — neural circuits form and strengthen based on the sensory inputs, motor demands, and cognitive challenges the organism encounters. In normal development, this plasticity operates within the physical framework of normally shaped brain tissue. In cases of early-onset hydrocephalus, the same plasticity mechanisms operate under radically different physical constraints.

The result is not a normal brain. It is a functionally compromised but partially viable neural system that has organized itself within the space available — a solution that is architecturally abnormal but functionally sufficient for some degree of cognition, motor control, language, and social interaction. The compromises are visible in the below-average IQ, the episodic motor problems, and the dependency on shunt function. The achievements are visible in the maintained employment, family life, and social participation.

This phenomenon has parallels across neuroscience. Hemispherectomy patients — individuals who have had an entire hemisphere of the brain surgically removed, typically for intractable epilepsy — can achieve remarkable functional recovery when the procedure is performed in childhood, before critical developmental periods close. The remaining hemisphere reorganizes to take over functions normally handled by the removed hemisphere. This works much better in children than adults, precisely because the developing brain's plasticity is greatest when neural circuits are still forming.

The French civil servant's case is an extreme example of the same principle: early-onset, gradual disruption allows developmental plasticity to partially compensate for structural abnormality. It is not evidence against the importance of brain tissue. It is evidence for the importance of developmental timing in determining outcomes.

**The Media Translation Problem (Again)**

The distortion of this case follows a pattern we encounter repeatedly in science communication: a nuanced finding is translated through successive media layers until it becomes a simple, dramatic, and wrong claim.

The Lancet case report (2007) is precise and measured. It describes the imaging findings, the clinical history, the IQ results, and the treatment. It does not claim the brain is "missing." It does not describe the man as living a "normal life." It describes him as a below-average-IQ civil servant with chronic hydrocephalus who presented with leg weakness.

Cleeremans' 2016 conference presentation used the case to illustrate a specific theoretical argument about consciousness and plasticity. It was not a claim about brain dispensability.

Media coverage (2016) transformed both sources into "scientist discovers man living without 90% of his brain." Business Insider's headline — "A man who lives without 90% of his brain is challenging our understanding of consciousness" — exemplifies the translation. "Without" replaces "with severely compressed." "90%" replaces "unknown but significant compression." "Challenging our understanding" replaces "illustrating known plasticity mechanisms."

Social media (2007–present) further simplified: "You don't need most of your brain." With each successive layer of mediation, specificity was lost, drama was gained, and the distance from the original finding increased.

This pattern — specificity in → drama out — is not a failure of individual journalists or social media users. It is a structural feature of how complex scientific findings interact with communication systems optimized for engagement. Engagement rewards surprise, simplicity, and emotional impact. Scientific accuracy rewards precision, qualification, and nuance. These incentive structures are in fundamental tension, and the hydrocephalus case is a near-perfect illustration of what happens when that tension resolves in favor of engagement.

**The "We Only Use 10%" Myth and Its Shadows**

The hydrocephalus case has become entangled with the "10% of the brain" myth in ways that illuminate how folk beliefs and misinterpreted evidence reinforce each other.

The 10% myth has no single origin. It has been attributed (probably falsely) to everyone from William James to Albert Einstein. Its persistence reflects a deep human desire to believe in untapped potential — that there is cognitive capacity lying dormant, waiting to be activated by the right drug, technique, or spiritual practice. Neuroimaging studies have thoroughly disproven the myth: PET and fMRI scans show activity across virtually all brain regions during a typical day, and even "resting state" brain activity involves coordinated patterns across widespread networks.

The French civil servant's case appears — to those already inclined toward the 10% myth — to provide tangible proof. "If this man lives with 10% of his brain, obviously the other 90% is unnecessary." This reasoning fails on every level (the man has more than 10% of his neurons, his function is below average, and the case shows plasticity rather than dispensability), but it feels compelling because it confirms a prior belief through dramatic visual evidence.

The entanglement of the hydrocephalus case with the 10% myth is bidirectional. The myth makes people receptive to the misinterpreted case. The misinterpreted case makes the myth seem more credible. Each reinforces the other in a cycle that resists correction because challenging either claim triggers defense of both.

**What This Case Actually Teaches**

Stripped of viral distortion, the French civil servant's case offers several genuine lessons worth understanding:

*The developing brain is not a fixed machine.* It is a self-organizing system that constructs its circuits based on the conditions available. Given dramatically abnormal conditions, it produces dramatically abnormal but partially functional architectures.

*Gradual pathology is different from acute pathology.* A brain that develops under slowly increasing pressure for 44 years produces very different outcomes than a brain that suffers sudden equivalent levels of tissue compression. Time and developmental timing matter enormously.

*Functional outcomes and imaging appearance do not always correspond.* A brain that looks devastated on imaging may support meaningful function, while a brain that looks normal may harbor severe functional deficits. Imaging is a macroscopic tool; function operates at the cellular and circuit level.

*Consciousness is remarkably resilient.* Cleeremans was right to find the case interesting for theories of consciousness. The fact that meaningful subjective experience and social engagement are maintained despite such extreme structural disruption is worth serious scientific attention — not because it shows brains are dispensable, but because it constrains theories of what neural substrate is necessary for conscious experience.

These are genuinely interesting scientific observations. They deserve wide public understanding. They do not require or benefit from the false claim that a man was living normally without most of his brain.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated this claim "Mixture" in September 2024 — the images are authentic but the "missing 90%" characterization is inaccurate. The article directly quotes Axel Cleeremans confirming that it is "not true to say this person is missing 90% of his brain" and explains the distinction between fluid displacement and tissue absence. The "Mixture" rating includes separate "What's True," "What's False," and "What's Undetermined" sections.

**What Snopes does well:** The article contacts and quotes the primary relevant expert (Cleeremans), who provides the critical distinction between compression and absence. The direct quotes add significant post-Lancet context that was not available from the primary literature alone. The article also correctly identifies the origin of the "90% missing" framing in 2016 media coverage of Cleeremans' conference presentation, rather than in the original 2007 case report.

**Where the Truth Vault adds value:** Several dimensions merit deeper epistemological treatment:

*The IQ question.* The man's IQ of 75 is mentioned in the Snopes article but not foregrounded as strongly as it should be. The viral narrative depends on a contrast between dramatic imaging and normal cognition. But the cognition was not normal — it was borderline. This single data point substantially weakens the viral claim, and it deserves more prominent positioning in any corrections.

*Developmental vs. acute brain injury.* The distinction between chronic developmental pathology and acute brain injury is the key to understanding why this case does not mean what the viral versions claim. The Snopes article does not elaborate on this distinction, which is the central neuroscientific context for interpreting the case correctly. Without understanding that the developing brain adapts to gradual constraints in ways that the adult brain cannot, the case remains mysterious — and mystery drives sharing of the false version.

*The 10% myth connection.* The viral success of this case is inseparable from the pre-existing "we only use 10% of our brains" folk belief. This connection is not addressed in the Snopes article. Understanding that the case is being assimilated into an existing myth framework explains why it generates such enormous engagement and why corrections are so difficult to land.

*The compression mechanics.* Cleeremans' explanation that neurons are "probably compressed into the very fine residual layer" deserves technical elaboration. The viral audience sees "thin layer = almost no brain." The neuroscientific reality is that cortical tissue contains enormous numbers of neurons per unit volume, and compression may redistribute rather than destroy a significant fraction of the cellular population. This is not a subtle distinction — it is the difference between "barely any brain" and "a structurally reorganized brain," and it speaks directly to the viral claim's central error.

*The imaging literacy problem.* This case exposes a broader media literacy challenge: the interpretation of medical imaging by non-specialists. Radiological images are designed to be read by trained professionals in clinical context. When they are extracted from that context and shared on social media, they are inevitably misinterpreted, because the visual language of medical imaging does not correspond to intuitive visual perception. The dark fluid on CT looks like "nothing there." The thin bright rim looks like "almost no brain." Both interpretations are wrong, but they are the natural reading for anyone without radiological training. This imaging literacy gap is an underappreciated vector for medical misinformation.

The broadest fact-checking lesson from this case is about the relationship between wonder and accuracy. The true story of the French civil servant — a developing brain adapting with remarkable plasticity to severe chronic pathology, achieving partial but meaningful function despite profound structural disruption — is genuinely wonderful. But the true version is complex, qualified, and demands intellectual engagement. The false version — man lives without brain — is simple, shocking, and virally optimized. The challenge for science communicators is to make the true wonder as compelling as the false surprise. On this case, we have largely failed.
