## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

The claim under investigation is disarmingly simple and sounds, at first hearing, like a schoolyard joke: manatees — the large, herbivorous aquatic mammals sometimes called "sea cows" — regulate their buoyancy by holding in or releasing intestinal gas. When they retain flatulence, they become lighter relative to the surrounding water and float toward the surface; when they expel it, they sink. The verdict, as assessed by Snopes fact-checker Madison Dapcevich and published on February 9, 2021, is **True**.

What makes this claim epistemically interesting rather than merely amusing is the convergence of multiple independent lines of evidence. The supporting observations do not rest on a single anecdote or a single researcher's testimony. Instead, the case draws on peer-reviewed anatomical research published in *The Anatomical Record* (Rommel and Reynolds, 2000), direct observational reports from zookeepers and veterinary staff at institutions including the Columbus Zoo and the Lowry Park Zoo in Tampa, Florida, and the broader comparative physiology of marine mammals. The claim is further situated within a well-understood ecological context: manatees are obligate herbivores consuming up to 15 percent of their body weight daily in algae and seagrass, and the microbial fermentation of this plant matter in their elongated digestive tracts produces substantial volumes of methane and other gases.

Crucially, the claim gains its mechanical plausibility from a notable absence. Unlike bony fish, manatees lack a swim bladder — the gas-filled organ that most teleost fish use to regulate their position in the water column. In the absence of this standard buoyancy-regulation organ, manatees appear to have co-opted their digestive gas production for an analogous functional purpose. This represents a case of what comparative physiologists would recognize as functional convergence through an entirely different anatomical pathway.

What the Snopes investigation establishes with confidence is the following: the phenomenon is real, it is observable under both normal and pathological conditions, it has been documented in peer-reviewed literature, and it is consistent with what is known about sirenian anatomy and physiology. What remains less fully explored — and what we will address in later sections — is the precise degree of voluntary control manatees exercise over this process, the quantitative contribution of gas regulation versus other buoyancy mechanisms (such as lung volume control), and whether this adaptation varies across the four extant sirenian populations.

## §2 The Evidence Base

The empirical foundation for the claim that manatees use flatulence as a buoyancy-control mechanism rests on several distinct categories of evidence: anatomical studies, behavioral observations in managed-care settings, and pathological case reports.

**Anatomical research.** The key peer-reviewed study was published in *The Anatomical Record* in April 2000 by Sentiel Rommel and John Reynolds. Their anatomical investigations of the Florida manatee (*Trichechus manatus latirostris*) documented several structural features that collectively make gas-based buoyancy regulation both possible and plausible. Among the most significant findings: the manatee's lungs and diaphragm extend along nearly the entire length of the dorsal body cavity, with each lung housed in a separate pleural cavity. This elongated pulmonary anatomy, combined with the animal's unusually heavy and largely pachyostotic (dense, solid) skeleton, creates a body plan in which the center of buoyancy and the center of gravity can be finely adjusted. Rommel and Reynolds (2000, *The Anatomical Record*) observed that manatees could submerge and adjust their vertical position in the water column with minimal muscular movement, suggesting the involvement of a non-locomotor force — namely, the redistribution or release of gas within the body.

**Behavioral observations at the Columbus Zoo.** Staff at the Columbus Zoo reported that manatees in their care demonstrably held in intestinal gas to achieve a lighter, more buoyant state, allowing them to float near the surface with little effort. Conversely, releasing gas allowed the animals to descend. These observations, while not published in a peer-reviewed journal, are consistent with the anatomical findings and represent the kind of expert natural-history observation that, in marine mammal science, often precedes and motivates formal investigation.

**Pathological case reports from Lowry Park Zoo.** Perhaps the most compelling evidence comes from cases of constipated manatees observed at Lowry Park Zoo (now ZooTampa at Lowry Park) in Tampa, Florida. When manatees became constipated and could not properly process or release intestinal gas, the consequences were dramatic and directly observable. Affected animals exhibited distended caudal (tail) ends, lost normal buoyancy control, and floated in an abnormal tail-up orientation. After veterinary staff administered laxatives and the animals were able to expel the accumulated gas, they regained normal buoyancy and locomotion. This constitutes a natural experiment: when the gas-release mechanism was impaired, buoyancy control failed; when it was restored, buoyancy control returned. The inferential strength of such pathological cases is considerable precisely because they demonstrate the counterfactual — what happens when the mechanism breaks.

**Respiratory physiology.** Additional context comes from what is known about manatee respiratory physiology. Manatees must surface every few minutes to breathe, though they can hold their breath for up to 20 minutes. When they do breathe, they replace approximately 90 percent of the air in their lungs in a single respiratory cycle — a figure confirmed across multiple sources and dramatically higher than the roughly 10 percent tidal volume exchange typical of resting humans (West, 2012, *Respiratory Physiology: The Essentials*). This high gas-exchange efficiency means that lung volume changes during breathing also contribute to buoyancy adjustments, but the intestinal gas mechanism operates independently and on a different timescale.

**Confirmation from Nanyang Technological University.** A blog post from Nanyang Technological University confirmed that manatees produce flatulence "just like humans do," situating the phenomenon within the broader context of mammalian digestive physiology and noting that the process is unremarkable from a gastrointestinal standpoint — what is remarkable is its co-option for hydrostatic purposes.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty

The manatee flatulence-buoyancy story is genuinely epistemically novel for several reasons that extend well beyond its comedic surface appeal. It illuminates principles in comparative physiology, evolutionary biology, and the philosophy of functional explanation that deserve serious attention.

**Functional co-option and exaptation.** The most profound lesson is that the manatee's use of intestinal gas for buoyancy regulation represents a textbook case of what Stephen Jay Gould and Elisabeth Vrba (1982, *Paleobiology*) termed "exaptation" — the co-option of a trait that evolved for one function (or as a byproduct of another process) to serve a novel functional role. Methane production in the herbivorous manatee gut is a straightforward consequence of anaerobic microbial fermentation of cellulose and other plant polysaccharides. This is not an adaptation *for* buoyancy; it is a metabolic byproduct that has been recruited into the service of hydrostatic control. The distinction matters because it illustrates how evolutionary novelty can arise not through the invention of new structures but through the redeployment of existing processes in new functional contexts.

**The swim bladder absence as an evolutionary constraint.** The fact that manatees lack a swim bladder — the organ that performs buoyancy regulation in the vast majority of bony fish — places them in an interesting comparative position. Swim bladders are homologous with lungs in the evolutionary history of vertebrates (Liem, 1988, *American Zoologist*), and the transition from aquatic to terrestrial and back to aquatic life in the sirenian lineage meant that the swim bladder was never available as an option. Sirenians descended from terrestrial ancestors that returned to the water, carrying with them a mammalian body plan that included lungs, a diaphragm, heavy bones, and a herbivorous gut — but not a swim bladder. The flatulence-buoyancy mechanism can thus be understood as an evolutionary solution to a problem created by phylogenetic constraint: given that sirenians could not re-evolve a swim bladder, they "found" another way to manage the same physical challenge.

**Pachyostosis and the buoyancy problem.** The heavy, dense bones of manatees — a condition known as pachyostosis — are themselves an adaptation to aquatic life, providing ballast that helps these animals maintain a submerged position without expending energy to counteract positive buoyancy (Domning and de Buffrénil, 1991, *Marine Mammal Science*). But this skeletal density creates a secondary problem: without active buoyancy regulation, the animal would tend to sink and would need to swim continuously to maintain position. The gas-retention mechanism elegantly solves this problem by providing a variable-buoyancy system that can be adjusted without locomotor effort. The interplay between pachyostosis (which makes the animal heavy) and gas retention (which makes it lighter) constitutes a balanced system of antagonistic adaptations — a concept well recognized in biomechanics but rarely illustrated so clearly.

**Epistemic status of zoo observations.** The case also raises interesting questions about the epistemic status of observational evidence from managed-care settings. The constipated-manatee cases at Lowry Park Zoo function as quasi-experimental evidence: the "treatment" (laxative administration) and the "outcome" (restored buoyancy) follow a clear causal logic. Yet this evidence exists in a gray zone between anecdote and experiment. It was not generated under controlled experimental conditions with randomization and blinding, but it does involve a clear intervention, a predicted outcome, and a confirmed result. In the epistemology of natural history, such cases occupy an important intermediate position, and the manatee story illustrates why dismissing "zoo anecdotes" as epistemically inferior can be premature.

**Cross-species generality.** Finally, the phenomenon invites consideration of whether similar gas-based buoyancy mechanisms exist in other aquatic herbivores or in any marine mammals that face analogous physical constraints. The dugong (*Dugong dugon*), the manatee's closest living relative, shares many anatomical features and presumably faces similar buoyancy challenges, but the specific role of flatulence in dugong buoyancy regulation has received less attention in the published literature — a gap worth noting.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

While the Snopes article by Dapcevich (2021) competently establishes the truth of the core claim, several questions remain either unasked or underexplored.

**Degree of voluntary control.** The claim as stated implies that manatees actively and volitionally "hold in" or "release" gas. But the degree of conscious, voluntary control versus involuntary or reflexive sphincter regulation is not clearly established in the available literature. Smooth muscle sphincters in the gastrointestinal tract can operate autonomously, and it is possible that the buoyancy adjustment is partially or wholly a passive mechanical consequence of body orientation and pressure differentials rather than a deliberate behavioral choice. The Columbus Zoo observations suggest intentionality — staff describe manatees as appearing to choose when to float or sink — but distinguishing genuine volition from conditioned response or reflexive adjustment in a non-verbal animal is methodologically difficult. Rommel and Reynolds (2000, *The Anatomical Record*) documented the anatomical basis but did not fully resolve the question of neural control.

**Quantitative contribution.** How much of a manatee's total buoyancy regulation is attributable to intestinal gas versus lung volume? Manatees can replace 90 percent of their lung air in a single breath, and adjusting lung inflation is another powerful buoyancy lever. The relative contributions of pulmonary and gastrointestinal gas to overall hydrostatic balance have not been precisely quantified in the published literature. A complete biomechanical model would need to account for both systems, along with the contribution of the animal's dense skeleton and the variable density of ingested food.

**Variation across species.** The Snopes article mentions four global sirenian populations — West Indian, Amazonian, West African, and dugong — but does not address whether this buoyancy mechanism is uniform across all four. Different species face different ecological conditions: the Amazonian manatee (*Trichechus inunguis*) inhabits freshwater environments with different density properties than the marine habitats of the West Indian manatee. Whether and how the gas-buoyancy mechanism varies with habitat salinity and water density is an open empirical question.

**Methane production rates.** The article does not quantify methane production in manatees. Given the large volumes of plant matter consumed (up to 15 percent of body weight daily, or roughly 120–180 pounds for a 1,000-pound adult), fermentation-derived gas production must be substantial. Quantifying this output would have implications not only for understanding buoyancy regulation but also for assessing the contribution of sirenians to aquatic methane budgets — a topic of growing interest in biogeochemistry and climate science.

**Health implications.** The constipated-manatee cases at Lowry Park Zoo raise clinical questions. How common is constipation in wild manatees? What dietary or environmental factors predispose managed-care manatees to this condition? Are there chronic subclinical cases where impaired gas regulation subtly affects locomotion or energy expenditure?

## §5 Contextual Framing

Manatees occupy a unique position in both biological taxonomy and human cultural history. They belong to the order Sirenia, named after the sirens of Greek mythology — a name that itself reflects the long history of humans misidentifying these animals. In January 1493, Christopher Columbus reportedly spotted three "mermaids" near the coast of the Dominican Republic and noted in his log that they were "not half as beautiful as they are painted." Those "mermaids" were almost certainly manatees — an identification that has been widely accepted by historians and marine biologists alike (Hendrix, 2003, *The Journal of American Folklore*). The mermaid-manatee connection illustrates how human perception is shaped by expectation: Columbus expected to see mermaids because medieval and Renaissance European cartography placed them in the waters of the New World, and so he saw mermaids where there were manatees.

The intelligence of manatees is another contextual element that is often underappreciated. Research has demonstrated that manatees possess long-term memory and associative learning capabilities comparable in some respects to those documented in dolphins (Reep and Bonde, 2006, *The Florida Manatee: Biology and Conservation*). This cognitive sophistication makes the question of whether gas-based buoyancy regulation involves deliberate decision-making more interesting and more plausible. An animal capable of complex learning might well be capable of learning to modulate a bodily function for mechanical advantage.

The conservation status of all sirenian species adds urgency to understanding their physiology. All four extant sirenian populations are classified as endangered or vulnerable by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN). The West Indian manatee has been the subject of intensive conservation efforts in the southeastern United States, where boat strikes, habitat loss, and cold-stress events remain significant threats (Deutsch et al., 2008, *Endangered Species Research*). Understanding the full range of manatee physiological adaptations — including unusual ones like gas-based buoyancy regulation — contributes to the knowledge base needed for effective conservation management.

The ecological role of manatees as the only completely herbivorous aquatic mammals, as noted by the Save the Manatee nonprofit organization, gives them a distinctive function in aquatic ecosystems. Their grazing on seagrass beds helps maintain these habitats in a state that supports broader biodiversity — a role analogous to that of terrestrial grazing megafauna. The loss of manatees from an ecosystem would therefore have cascading effects beyond the species itself, a consideration that reinforces the importance of comprehensive physiological understanding for conservation planning.

## §6 Verdict Calibration and Takeaways

The verdict of **True** is well-calibrated. The claim that manatees regulate buoyancy via flatulence is supported by peer-reviewed anatomical research (Rommel and Reynolds, 2000, *The Anatomical Record*), consistent behavioral observations across multiple managed-care facilities, and compelling pathological evidence from constipated animals at Lowry Park Zoo. The mechanism is physiologically plausible given the known absence of a swim bladder in sirenians, the high volume of fermentation-derived gas in herbivorous gut systems, and the anatomical features (elongated lungs, pachyostotic skeleton) that make fine buoyancy adjustment both necessary and achievable.

The epistemic posture we should adopt toward this claim is one of confident acceptance of the core phenomenon with acknowledged uncertainty about the details: the degree of voluntary control, the quantitative contribution relative to lung volume regulation, and the variation across species all remain open questions. This is a common epistemic situation in organismal biology — the broad phenomenon is well established while the mechanistic details await further investigation.

The broader takeaway is twofold. First, biological systems frequently solve engineering problems through unexpected mechanisms, and the absence of an obvious solution (a swim bladder) does not mean the problem goes unsolved — it means the solution is likely to come from an unexpected direction. Second, claims that sound absurd on first hearing are not necessarily false. The history of science is filled with findings that initially provoked laughter — continental drift, bacterial causation of ulcers, the role of jumping genes — before being accepted as established fact. The manatee flatulence story is a small but instructive reminder that epistemic humility requires taking unlikely-sounding claims seriously when they are backed by convergent evidence from independent sources. The willingness to investigate rather than dismiss is what separates rigorous inquiry from reflexive skepticism.
