## §1 — Claim and Verdict

In October 2017, Mann Packing Company of Salinas, California — one of the largest packaged vegetable producers in the United States — issued a voluntary recall of specific packaged vegetable products sold under the Mann brand and numerous private label brands at retailers including Walmart, Trader Joe's, Aldi, Safeway, Albertsons, H-E-B, and Target. The recall was triggered by a single positive result for *Listeria monocytogenes* found during random sampling by the Canadian Food Inspection Agency. Mann Packing reported no associated illnesses at the time of the recall, and all affected products carried "best if used by" dates no later than October 24, 2017.

By early 2018, months after the recalled products had cleared store shelves and the recall itself had concluded, multiple websites began republishing versions of the original recall warning shorn of all temporal context. These republications used urgent language and sensationalized headlines suggesting a current, ongoing "nationwide listeria outbreak" affecting a broad range of major retailers. Some versions of the article characterized the event as a developing public health emergency requiring immediate attention.

Snopes rated this claim **Outdated** — a verdict category reserved for claims that were once accurate or partially accurate but have been rendered misleading by the passage of time.

The Snopes verdict performs an important epistemic function: it neither dismisses the original recall (which was legitimate and based on real laboratory findings) nor validates the ongoing circulation of the warning (which was being presented as current when it was not). The Outdated rating is architecturally significant because it names a form of misinformation that differs from fabrication and requires different remediation: the facts were true at a specific historical moment but have become disinformation through decontextualization.

## §2 — Origin and Propagation

The Mann Packing recall story exemplifies what information security researchers and Snopes have termed "zombie recall" content — accurate food safety warnings that continue to circulate online indefinitely past their expiration dates, stripped of the temporal context that would permit readers to assess their current relevance.

The original recall was entirely legitimate. On October 19, 2017, Mann Packing filed a recall notice with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) covering minimally processed vegetable products. The Miami Herald and other news organizations provided detailed coverage of the full list of affected products, which spanned numerous private label brands and store brands across multiple retail chains. The products were identified by their sell-by dates — all on or before October 24, 2017 — and the recall had a natural and clearly defined expiration: once that date passed, all affected products would have been consumed, discarded, or removed from shelves.

The content ecosystem that generates zombie recall warnings typically involves websites that monetize high-traffic food safety content through advertising. Because recall stories generate significant search traffic — consumers actively search for them — accurate recall stories from any date remain search-discoverable years after the event. Websites that publish or republish them without dating prominently benefit from that traffic indefinitely. The incentive structure rewards temporally ambiguous republication over accurate, dated archiving.

The sensationalization in the specific sites Snopes documented — using headlines such as "ALERT: Nationwide Listeria Outbreak Affects Vegetables From Walmart, Safeway, Trader Joe's, and MORE!" — added an urgency layer absent from the original recall notice. Mann Packing's official communications, and the FDA's recall record, were clinically worded and explicitly limited in scope. The amplified websites translated a limited recall into a characterization of a "nationwide listeria outbreak" and claimed that "untold numbers of Americans will be impacted." Neither characterization was accurate even at the time of the original recall.

## §3 — Evidence Assessment and Epistemic Novelty

**EARC Rating: O (Outdated — once accurate, now misleading through temporal decontextualization)**

The epistemic novelty of this entry engages with a structural question about how temporal context functions as a truth-value modifier: can a statement that was accurate become misinformation without any of its original factual claims changing?

The answer, which the zombie recall phenomenon illustrates with particular clarity, is yes. A recall notice stating "These products contain listeria risk and should be discarded" is accurate when the products are on shelves. The same statement presented as current information after the recall has concluded is effectively false in its practical implications, even though every individual factual proposition within it might technically still be accurate. "Mann Packing recalled vegetables sold at Walmart with best-by dates before October 24, 2017" remains a true statement in 2018. But presenting that information as an active warning in 2018 creates a false impression about the current safety status of products, the current public health environment, and the need for immediate consumer action.

This is what philosophers of language call a "misleading implicature" — a statement that is technically accurate but whose communicative implication is false. Grice (1975, in *Syntax and Semantics, Vol. 3*) articulated the conversational principle of relevance: statements are conventionally understood to be provided because they are currently pertinent. A communicator who presents months-old recall information in a news-style format exploits the relevance implicature to create a false sense of currency without technically asserting anything false.

Regarding the underlying listeria risk itself: *Listeria monocytogenes* is a gram-positive bacterium that presents genuine public health risk, particularly for pregnant women, newborns, elderly persons, and immunocompromised individuals (Schlech, 2000, *Clinical Infectious Diseases*). It is distinct among bacterial food pathogens in its ability to grow at refrigeration temperatures, meaning that unlike *Salmonella* or *E. coli*, which are suppressed by cold storage, listeria can multiply even in properly refrigerated food products. The bacterium is not uncommon in agricultural environments, and the detection of a single positive sample in random testing — the trigger for the Mann Packing recall — is the expected function of the FDA and Canadian CFIA testing infrastructure working correctly.

The recall process itself represents the food safety system functioning as designed. A single positive result triggered voluntary recall as a precautionary measure. No illness was reported. Products were removed from distribution. The incident was resolved within the recall window. The zombie recall amplification of this episode transformed an example of successful food safety infrastructure into a panic narrative — inverting the actual epistemic status of the event.

## §4 — Scientific and Expert Context

**Listeria monocytogenes biology**: The clinical epidemiology of listeriosis offers important context for evaluating the severity framing of the zombie recall articles. The CDC estimates approximately 1,600 cases of listeriosis per year in the United States, resulting in about 260 deaths — a serious but not catastrophic burden compared to other foodborne pathogens. Unlike *Salmonella* (estimated 1.35 million cases per year) or *Campylobacter* (1.5 million cases per year), listeria has a low overall incidence but a high case-fatality rate of approximately 16 to 20 percent, concentrated in immunocompromised and elderly populations (Scallan et al., 2011, *Emerging Infectious Diseases*).

The severity gradient of listeriosis is highly population-dependent. For healthy adults, *Listeria monocytogenes* exposure typically causes either no illness or a self-limited febrile gastroenteritis. For pregnant women, the bacterium can cross the placental barrier and cause fetal infection, stillbirth, or neonatal listeriosis. For the immunocompromised, it can cause septicemia and meningoencephalitis. The zombie recall articles' symptom lists — "high fever, muscle aches, nausea, diarrhea, headache, stiff neck, confusion, loss of balance, convulsions" — combined presentations from both the mild gastroenteritis and severe invasive forms without distinguishing who is at risk for each.

**Recall science and precautionary action**: The FDA food recall system operates on a graduated risk-based model. Class I recalls involve "reasonable probability that the use of or exposure to a violative product will cause serious adverse health consequences or death." Class II involves "remote probability of adverse health consequences." Class III involves situations where use of the product is "not likely to cause any adverse health consequences." The Mann Packing recall, triggered by a single positive sample in random testing with no associated illness, represents the precautionary end of Class II activity — an appropriately cautious but not catastrophic response (FDA, *Recalls, Market Withdrawals & Safety Alerts*, 2017).

**Information decay and food safety communication**: Shoaf-Sweeney & Quickel (2009, *Journal of Food Safety*) documented the persistence of outdated food safety information in online environments and found that recall-related content exhibited particularly long search-result lifespans because users actively seek it and websites that archive it continue to accumulate inbound links. The study recommended that food recall communications include explicit expiration language — "this recall applies only to products with best-by dates of [X]" — in high-visibility positions to reduce temporally displaced consumption of that information.

**Listeria in packaged vegetables**: Preformed packaged vegetable products represent a recognized risk category for listeria contamination specifically because they undergo minimal processing that would inactivate the organism, are stored at temperatures that permit listeria growth, and reach consumers without a terminal kill step (cooking). Surveys of packaged vegetable products in multiple markets have found occasional environmental listeria contamination (Lynch et al., 2009, *Journal of Food Protection*). The Mann Packing event was not anomalous in this context; it was a documented instance of a known risk within an established surveillance system.

## §5 — Comparative Misinformation Analysis

The "zombie recall" phenomenon places the Mann Packing case within a broader class of what might be called "temporal misinformation" — accurate information that becomes misleading through decontextualization from its original time window. This class is distinct from fabricated claims, doctored images, and distorted characterizations; it operates purely by removing the temporal indexicality that determines whether information is currently actionable.

Comparable examples documenting the zombie recall phenomenon include: repeated recirculation of Peanut Corporation of America recall information (originally 2008–2009) in subsequent years; periodic resharing of Blue Bell Creameries listeria recall information (originally 2015) on anniversaries and in unrelated food safety discussions; and years-long circulation of a 2013 Foster Farms chicken recall warning. In each case, the original recall was real and serious; the zombie version was rendered misleading by removal of dates and relabeling as current.

The websites that generate zombie recall content often use a specific structural technique: they include the full original product list (which lends an aura of specificity and authority) while omitting or minimizing the date range (which would render the information immediately dismissible as historical). Specificity without temporal anchoring is a powerful misinformation format because human cognitive systems use specificity as a proxy for relevance. A list of twenty-seven specific product SKUs from fourteen retailer brands implies current, actionable knowledge — even when every item on the list expired seven months ago.

Rini (2017, *Philosophy and Public Affairs*) examined the epistemology of "fake news" and observed that the social functions of news-format content include the implicit guarantee of temporal relevance — news is news insofar as it describes current states of the world. Content that mimics news format while violating this temporal norm constitutes a specific form of pragmatic deception, exploiting the format's conventional pragmatics.

The "nationwide listeria outbreak" framing in the zombie versions represents an additional layer of fabrication layered onto temporal decontextualization. The original recall involved no confirmed cases of human illness, a single positive sample found in Canadian testing, and a scope limited to products consumed within a two-week window. The framing of this as an "outbreak" is epidemiologically incorrect even for the original period: an outbreak requires confirmed cases with epidemiological links, not a precautionary recall triggered by environmental testing.

## §6 — Verdict Summary and Guidance

Snopes's **Outdated** rating is precisely calibrated to the nature of this particular misinformation case. The rating acknowledges the original truth of the recall (Mann Packing did recall vegetables due to listeria concerns in October 2017) while correctly flagging the deceptive character of its continued circulation as a current warning months later.

The harm from zombie recall misinformation is diffuse but real. Consumers who encounter an outdated recall warning and believe it to be current may unnecessarily discard products that pose no risk, experience unjustified anxiety about foods in their refrigerator, distrust food safety systems based on the severity of the "outbreak" framing, and lose calibration about what a genuine food safety emergency looks like. Paradoxically, zombie recalls may reduce the effectiveness of real recall notices by contributing to recall warning fatigue.

**For readers encountering this claim:** food safety recall information without a clearly visible date should be treated as suspect. The FDA's official recall database — accessible at fda.gov/safety/recalls-market-withdrawals-safety-alerts — provides the authoritative, dated record of current and archived recalls. Any food safety warning that cannot be confirmed against an official source with a visible date should be treated as potentially outdated. Mann Packing vegetables sold in 2018 and beyond were not subject to the October 2017 recall, and no ongoing listeria risk from those products has been documented.

The broader lesson is about the temporal dimension of truth in consumer information: historical accuracy without temporal context produces a form of functional falsehood. Responsible information sharing about food safety requires, at minimum, the date of the original warning and the explicit scope of affected products. Content that omits these elements — regardless of how detailed the product list — is not providing actionable information; it is manufacturing anxiety.

**Epistemic gap flagged:** Research specifically quantifying the consumer harm from zombie recall content — including the incidence of unnecessary food disposal, anxiety burden, and effects on recall-warning credibility — is limited. Economic models of the food safety communication ecosystem that account for the incentive structures rewarding undated republication of recall information would be a productive area for policy research, particularly given the FDA's increasing reliance on web-based communication for recall notices.
