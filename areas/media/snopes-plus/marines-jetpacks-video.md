## §1 — Claim and Viral Context

In late June 2022, a video began circulating at explosive velocity across major social media platforms, accumulating tens of millions of impressions within mere days. The footage, originally posted to Facebook by comedian Josh Wolf on June 27, 2022, showed a screen recording of a Reddit post bearing the caption: "Marines perform boarding exercises with jetpacks and landing on a high-speed ship." Within four days, the video had been viewed more than seven million times on Facebook alone. Readers and viewers in significant numbers contacted Snopes asking whether the footage was genuine or a fabricated digital artifact.

The claim embedded in that viral packaging was compound in nature. It asserted two related propositions: (1) that the video footage itself was real and not computer-generated or manipulated, and (2) that the person shown flying the jetpack was a Marine performing an official training exercise. These two claims, while superficially unified in the viral caption, are distinct and receive different verdicts under scrutiny. The first is straightforwardly true; the second is materially misleading.

Understanding why this clip went so viral requires situating it within the broader landscape of 2022 social media behavior. The year saw continued acceleration in the speed with which dramatic technological demonstrations spread online, particularly when the imagery touched on military capability. Content that evokes both futuristic technology and national pride tends to cross ideological and demographic lines, achieving the kind of broad organic reach that more partisan content cannot. A jetpack-equipped soldier boarding a ship at sea activates the cultural mythology of technological supremacy that has surrounded military science since at least the early Cold War era. The image resonates beyond news value — it functions as a kind of secular mythology of national capability.

The original Reddit post from which the Facebook screen-recording was derived had been published months earlier, on January 6, 2022, on the r/nextfuckinglevel subreddit. By the time Josh Wolf reposted it, the Reddit thread had accumulated more than 117,000 net upvotes — an extraordinary figure that placed it among the most successful posts for that month. Upvote counts of that magnitude represent genuine cross-platform discovery: the post had already penetrated beyond Reddit's native audience before the Facebook viral wave added another layer of amplification.

The footage itself shows a man in a specialized suit equipped with jet propulsion units attached to both arms, flying laterally across open water to board a moving vessel. The visual grammar of the imagery is unambiguous in its appeal: it looks, to all appearances, exactly like the science-fiction scenario it claims to be. The apparent impossibility of the image — human-powered flight, jet-assisted maritime boarding — is precisely what drives the sharing impulse.

## §2 — Source Verification and Evidentiary Record

The original video from which all downstream viral versions derived was posted to YouTube by Gravity Industries on May 1, 2021, more than a year before the Facebook viral wave of June 2022. The video's YouTube description noted: "Thanks to Royal Marines LLC for setting up a very successful exercise testing the capability of the Gravity Jet Suit to support RM boarding ops." This attribution is crucial: it confirms that the demonstration was genuinely conducted in collaboration with Royal Marines — hence the "Marines" in the viral caption is not entirely fabricated — but the description of the flying operator as a Marine is a misstatement.

Gravity Industries, founded by Richard Browning, is a British technology company that has been developing human-propulsion "Jet Suit" technology since at least 2017. The company's YouTube channel contains numerous videos documenting demonstrations conducted globally, across diverse operational contexts ranging from emergency medical response to military assessment trials. The Jet Suit consists of several micro gas turbines mounted on the arms and, in some configurations, the back, enabling a trained operator to achieve brief sustained flight or rapid horizontal transit at low altitude. Browning himself has performed many of the public demonstrations.

Reporting from Business Insider (Pickrell, 2021) confirmed that the pilots shown in the longer YouTube video were Gravity Industries operators, not active Royal Marines. Business Insider further quoted Trial sponsor Lieutenant Colonel Will Clarke of the Royal Marines, who said of the Jet Suit: "It shows significant promise and we will watch its development with continued interest." This phrasing — "watch its development with continued interest" — is the measured language of exploratory evaluation rather than operational adoption, a distinction that matters enormously for understanding what the demonstration actually represented.

The Reuters news agency also filed a dispatch on May 3, 2021 headlined "Jet Suit Innovation Tested on Royal Marines," which provided additional contextual clarity: the British military was hosting a trial to assess the technology, but had not integrated Jet Suit operators into its force structure. The Marines' role was as evaluators and scenario designers, not as pilots.

This chain of evidence establishes the following verified facts: (a) the filming took place during a genuine military-adjacent exercise; (b) the Jet Suit technology is real, functional, and commercially available through Gravity Industries; (c) the operator shown was a trained civilian professional, not a servicemember; (d) the Royal Marines were present and interested but were assessing rather than deploying the technology.

The Reddit post caption, "Marines perform boarding exercises with jetpacks," is therefore accurate in rough outline but misleading in precise attribution. Marines were present; boarding exercises were performed; jetpacks were in use. But the Marines were not themselves flying the jetpacks. This is the specific epistemic gap that Snopes's "Mostly True" verdict addresses.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: The Credibility of the Unusual

This fact-check offers a particularly instructive case study in what might be called the epistemic dynamics of the genuinely surprising true claim. A recurring challenge in the social media information ecosystem is that false claims are frequently flagged as implausible because they seem "too good to be true," while genuinely extraordinary true claims are sometimes *accepted* more readily than they deserve because they happen to align with existing cultural expectations about military capability.

The novelty at play here is not merely technological but epistemological. When a claim sounds fantastical — humans flying with personal jetpacks — audiences often deploy one of two cognitive shortcuts: either they dismiss it as impossible fabrication, or they embrace it as a confirmation of secretly-known capability ("I always suspected the military had this"). Neither response constitutes good epistemic practice. The study of motivated reasoning in misinformation contexts (Pennycook & Rand, 2019, *Psychological Science*) demonstrates that analytical thinking styles, rather than domain expertise, are the strongest predictors of accurate misinformation detection.

Crucially, the Jet Suit technology is not a rumor or a speculative claim. The engineering underpinning it is well-documented in the aerospace and mechanical engineering literature. Browning and Gravity Industries have given extensively documented public demonstrations; the physics of low-altitude human-powered flight using gas turbines is understood and not theoretically problematic. What makes the viral video unusual is not the technology itself but the operational context: the open-ocean maritime boarding scenario creates a visual grammar that activates military mythology in ways that a ground demonstration would not.

The epistemic lesson here is that the dual question — "Is this real?" and "Who is doing this?" — must always be decomposed before analysis. Answering "yes" to the first question does not entail any particular answer to the second. A real video can still mislead through mislabeling. The Gravity Industries footage is among the purest examples of what fact-checkers sometimes call "authentic image, false context" — a category that is, in some respects, harder to debunk than outright fabrication precisely because the visual evidence confirms part of the claim.

## §4 — Gravity Industries and the Jet Suit: Technical and Contextual Background

Gravity Industries was established in 2017 by Richard Browning, a British entrepreneur and former oil trader who began developing personal flight technology as a personal project. Browning's approach centers on micro gas turbines — essentially very small jet engines — distributed across the body to achieve controlled vectored thrust. The Jet Suit requires significant physical conditioning to operate; the pilot's core stability and limb positioning are critical to maintaining stable flight. Browning has described training for Jet Suit operation as similar to intensive athletic preparation.

The company entered commercial production of the Jet Suit and has made the technology available for purchase, training, and licensed demonstration events. The price point — reportedly in the range of hundreds of thousands of dollars per unit — places the technology firmly in the category of defense and high-value commercial clients for now, though Gravity Industries has publicly articulated ambitions to reduce costs over time.

The maritime boarding exercise demonstrated in the viral video represents one of several operational scenarios that militaries have expressed interest in evaluating. Naval special operations forces, including British Royal Marines and units comparable to the United States Navy SEALs, have historically been early adopters of novel insertion and extraction technologies. The ability to rapidly board a vessel underway — particularly one that cannot be stopped or that is moving to evade — is an operational challenge with significant tactical implications. A Jet Suit operator could, in principle, traverse the distance between a pursuing vessel and a target ship in seconds, at angles and altitudes that would be difficult to intercept.

However, as of 2022, the technology remained in evaluation status. The "Mostly True" verdict accurately captures the current posture: the capability exists, the exercises occurred, but the portrayal of Marines themselves as Jet Suit operators overstates the degree of integration achieved.

## §5 — Broader Implications for Military Technology Misinformation

The marines-jetpacks-video case exemplifies a recurring pattern in military technology misinformation: the confusion between evaluation and deployment, between capability testing and capability fielding. Militaries regularly test technologies that never reach operational use; they also regularly decline to publicize technologies that have reached operational use. This dual opacity creates a fertile environment for viral misrepresentation in both directions.

On one side, videos of genuine tests are sometimes viral-shared as proof of deployed capability, as happened here. On the other side, genuine operational demonstrations are sometimes dismissed as CGI fabrications by audiences who cannot believe what they are seeing. Both failure modes share a common root: the absence of institutional context translates into interpretive freedom, and that freedom is often filled by the most narratively compelling option available.

Research on misinformation propagation across social platforms (Vosoughi, Roy, & Aral, 2018, *Science*) documented that false news spreads faster and further than true news on Twitter, with novelty and emotional content as the strongest drivers. The jetpack video, however, is notable as a case where the genuine novelty of the content drove virality even for a mostly-accurate claim — demonstrating that virality itself is not diagnostic of falsity. High engagement can reflect genuine wonder at authentic events.

The Snopes verdict of "Mostly True" occupies an important and underappreciated epistemic space. It avoids the false binary of true/false for claims that are substantially accurate in their core factual content but materially misleading in their framing. This nuanced verdict is especially important for military technology stories, where the distinction between "the technology exists" and "the military is using it" has significant policy and geopolitical implications.

## §6 — Summary Assessment and Research Gaps

**Verdict confirmation:** Mostly True. The footage is authentic and undoctored, derived directly from Gravity Industries' YouTube channel, shot during a genuine exercise conducted in collaboration with British Royal Marines. The sole material inaccuracy in the viral claim is the attribution of the piloting role to Marines themselves; in fact, Gravity Industries operators flew the Jet Suits during the exercise.

**EARC rating — C (Confirmable):** The core claims of this fact-check are confirmable from primary sources (Gravity Industries YouTube, Business Insider reporting, Reuters dispatch). No expert elicitation was required; the facts are established from documentary evidence.

**Epistemic gap — Moderate:** The gap lies in the disambiguation between "exercise conducted with Marines" and "Marines flew the jetpacks." This distinction is meaningful operationally but not immediately intuitive to general audiences who lack familiarity with military technology trial processes. The gap between the visual claim and the technical truth is genuine but not extreme.

**Research gaps:** The long-term fate of the Gravity Industries / Royal Marines evaluation is not documented in public sources as of mid-2026. Whether the British Ministry of Defence has advanced beyond "watch with interest" to procurement or further testing remains unclear. Documentation of the specific conditions of the 2021 trial — duration, operational parameters, personnel involved — is limited to what was voluntarily published by Gravity Industries; independent verification of trial outcomes would require Freedom of Information requests to the UK MoD. The broader question of whether any military force has reached operational deployment of personal Jet Suit technology is, to the best of available public knowledge, unanswered.
