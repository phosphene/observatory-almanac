# Mark Wahlberg refused to work with Tom Hanks because he's "too woke"


## 1. The Claim

Beginning in approximately 2022 and recurring in intensified waves through 2023–2024 during periods of elevated culture-war media coverage, a story spread across Facebook, Twitter/X, Instagram Reels, and various conservative-leaning news aggregation sites asserting that Mark Wahlberg — the Boston-born actor, producer, and devout Catholic who has been public about his religious convictions and his general preference for avoiding overt political statements — had refused to co-star with or collaborate on a project with Tom Hanks. The specific reason cited varies across versions: in some, Wahlberg is quoted calling Hanks "too woke for Hollywood"; in others, he describes Hanks as having lost touch with "regular Americans"; in others, the story involves an unnamed film project that Wahlberg rejected specifically to avoid working alongside Hanks, citing the latter's public liberal politics.

Tom Hanks, for his part, is presented in the story as the representative liberal icon of mainstream Hollywood — a figure who has spoken publicly about the importance of democratic norms, who has made films associated with liberal-coded historical narratives (including *Philadelphia*, *Saving Private Ryan*, *The Post*, and others), and who has on various occasions expressed views consistent with mainstream American cultural progressivism. His cultural positioning as a sympathetic liberal figure in public life makes him a logical target for stories in which a putatively more conservative celebrity refuses association.

Mark Wahlberg's cultural positioning is more complex. He is a devout Catholic who has spoken extensively about his faith in ways that contrast with standard Hollywood cultural positioning. He moved his family from Los Angeles to Nevada in 2022, citing, among other reasons, a desire to raise his children in a less permissive cultural environment, and he has made various public statements about his faith commitments and his belief in hard work and traditional values. He has not, however, positioned himself as a political conservative in partisan terms: his public interview record does not contain endorsements of Republican politicians, and he has actively avoided discussing party politics. He has made statements about wanting to be a voice for faith in Hollywood without characterising his views in the explicit culture-war vocabulary that the fabricated story imputes to him.

The claim typically circulates with a photograph of Wahlberg, sometimes alongside a photograph of Hanks, accompanied by a short text block attributed to Wahlberg and either hyperlinked to a low-credibility news site or presented without any sourcing at all with the expectation that the visual presentation will carry sufficient credibility to generate shares.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Wahlberg's actual public statements.** A thorough review of Mark Wahlberg's documented public interviews, press appearances, social media posts, and reported statements through entertainment industry publications and mainstream press reveals no instance of Wahlberg commenting on Tom Hanks, refusing to work with Hanks, or using the word "woke" in reference to any specific colleague or individual. This is a significant absence: Wahlberg is a highly public figure with an extensive documented interview record spanning three decades of film and television work. He gives press interviews associated with every major film project, maintains active social media accounts, and has been the subject of substantial long-form journalistic profiles. The claim that he made a statement about Tom Hanks that left no trace in entertainment journalism despite the evident newsworthiness of a public celebrity feud of this character strains credulity.

Wahlberg has discussed his Catholic faith in numerous contexts, including a 2020 interview with *Men's Health* in which he described his daily prayer practice and his belief that his faith kept him grounded in Hollywood (Men's Health, "Mark Wahlberg's Faith Formula," November 2020). He discussed his Nevada move in an interview with *Extra* in late 2022. In none of these or related interviews has he named Tom Hanks or any other specific colleague in a negative context related to political views.

**Tom Hanks's public profile.** Tom Hanks has made various public statements that could broadly be characterised as reflecting liberal cultural values, including statements about the importance of the press, statements expressing distress at political divisiveness, and his general association with films that are historically coded as liberal-adjacent. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom from President Obama in 2016. He has not been involved in overt partisan political campaigning and has generally positioned himself as voicing civic values rather than party political positions. He has also made statements that resist easy partisan categorisation, including various expressions of respect for the military and national values that would not be unusual in conservative discourse.

Despite Hanks's status as a prominent cultural liberal in the broadest sense, his actual public record does not identify him as an aggressive political activist in ways that would obviously generate the strong reaction that the fabricated Wahlberg quote imputes. The claim's internal logic — that Wahlberg would refuse professional collaboration with one of Hollywood's most respected and artistically accomplished actors over general cultural politics — is not internally consistent with Wahlberg's documented professional trajectory, which includes extensive work with directors and co-stars across the political spectrum of Hollywood.

**Snopes and professional fact-checker findings.** Snopes rated this claim FALSE in their 2023 assessment, identifying it as originating in the fake news content ecosystem that generates celebrity-culture-war fabrications (Snopes, "Did Mark Wahlberg Refuse to Work With Tom Hanks Because Hanks Is 'Too Woke'?", 2023). AFP Fact Check has addressed similar Wahlberg-woke fabrications and consistently traced them to the same cluster of politically inflected content production sites. No entertainment industry trade publication — including Variety, The Hollywood Reporter, Entertainment Weekly, Deadline Hollywood, or Billboard — carried any reporting on this claim, which would be extraordinary if a statement of this character had been made by a major star at the level of Wahlberg's career profile.

**The absence of any business record.** Beyond the absence of a public statement, there is no business record supporting the claim. Wahlberg's production company, Unrealistic Ideas, and his broader talent relationships do not show any documented rejection of a specific project involving Hanks. Film production is a documented industry: projects attach, detach, and reattach talent in ways that are generally reported by entertainment trade journalism. No trade report of a Wahlberg-Hanks project collapse attributable to political disagreement has been identified.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The culture-war celebrity feud format.** The claim belongs to a well-established content genre that has produced hundreds of similar fabrications since approximately 2020. The genre's operating template is: take Celebrity A with conservative or conservative-adjacent cultural coding; take Celebrity B with liberal cultural coding; generate a quote or action in which Celebrity A repudiates Celebrity B in explicit culture-war vocabulary; distribute through partisan social media networks. The format is highly effective because it serves political identity expression for the target audience — conservative social media users who enjoy seeing a Hollywood figure articulate their own critique of Hollywood liberalism — and because the template's surface plausibility benefits from real attributes of the named celebrities.

Research on viral content mechanics identifies identity-expressive content as reliably achieving higher sharing rates than purely informational content, regardless of accuracy (Berger & Milkman, 2012, "What Makes Online Content Viral?", *Journal of Marketing Research*, 49(2), 192–205). Stories about celebrities endorsing or enacting the political positions of the intended audience travel through partisan networks not primarily because the audience believes they are accurate but because they are satisfying to share as expressions of political identity. The accuracy of the claim is a secondary consideration to its function as political communication.

**Real attributes exploited for fabricated attribution.** The claim's surface plausibility depends on real attributes that have been misrepresented. Wahlberg's Catholic faith and his Nevada move are real and documented. His faith has made him a figure of interest to conservative Catholic media, which has heavily covered his religious life and positions him as a cultural counterpoint to Hollywood liberalism. This coverage creates a background context in which a Wahlberg-says-something-culturally-conservative story faces reduced scepticism from audiences who have encountered many legitimate stories about his faith.

Hanks's cultural-liberal coding is also real. His association with the Obama White House, his public statements about press freedom, and his general Hollywood liberal positioning are documented. A story about "Wahlberg rejects Hanks for being too woke" requires no completely invented attributes — it assembles real attributes of both men into a fabricated event. This is the technique that media literacy researchers have identified as particularly resistant to debunking: claims built from real elements are harder to reject wholesale because partial debunking ("well, Wahlberg IS Catholic" or "Hanks IS a Hollywood liberal") feels like partial confirmation (Ecker, Lewandowsky, Swire, & Chang, 2011, "Correcting False Information in Memory: Manipulating the Strength of Misinformation Encoding and Its Retraction," *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*, 18(3), 570–578).

**Epistemic novelty: the parasocial attribution problem.** A distinguishing feature of this category of celebrity fabrication — as opposed to political event fabrications about politicians — is that it exploits the specific dynamics of parasocial relationships between celebrities and their audiences. Parasocial relationships are one-sided relationships in which audience members develop a sense of personal familiarity, affection, and knowledge of a public figure through media exposure, without any reciprocal relationship (Horton & Wohl, 1956, "Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction," *Psychiatry*, 19(3), 215–229). These relationships generate strong intuitions about how the celebrity would behave and what they believe, which makes fabricated celebrity statements feel confirmatory rather than surprising.

Wahlberg's audiences who follow his faith-related media output have a well-developed model of his values and character. When they encounter a quote that fits this model, the model itself provides evidence for the quote's authenticity: "that sounds like exactly what he'd say" is experienced as confirmation rather than as the prior probability calculation it actually is. This makes the debunking of celebrity fabrications particularly difficult, because the correction must not only present negative evidence (no such statement was made) but must actively counteract the phenomenological sense of confirmation that the target audience has already experienced.

**The viral velocity asymmetry.** Research on corrections and misinformation has consistently found that corrections spread far less widely and rapidly than the original false claims they address (Vosoughi, Roy & Aral, 2018, "The Spread of True and False News Online," *Science*, 359(6380), 1146–1151). For political identity-expressive content, this asymmetry is even more pronounced: debunking a story that served political identity expression is experienced by the original sharers not as a correction but as a politically motivated attack on a satisfying narrative. The correction is received as contested rather than as authoritative, which substantially reduces its spreading efficacy. The original claim reaches millions; the Snopes rating of FALSE reaches thousands; the net informational effect is strongly weighted toward the false claim.


## 4. Verdict

**FALSE — no factual basis, fabricated celebrity culture-war content.** Mark Wahlberg did not refuse to work with Tom Hanks, has not used the word "woke" in reference to Hanks or any specific colleague in any documented interview or public statement, and has made no statement consistent with the claim. The story originates in a content ecosystem specifically designed to produce politically satisfying celebrity fabrications for conservative social media audiences. It exploits real attributes of both Wahlberg (his publicised Catholic faith and Nevada move) and Hanks (his cultural-liberal positioning) to generate surface plausibility without any evidential basis.

The claim is notable for the near-total absence of the verification infrastructure that would exist if it were true: no entertainment trade reporting, no management statement, no court filing, no project documentation, no social media post by either party, and no denial (itself indicating that the parties' teams have not considered the claim newsworthy enough to address). The totality of this absence is the strongest evidence available.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Wahlberg/Hanks fabrication belongs to a wave of celebrity culture-war content that became a significant presence in the partisan media ecosystem from approximately 2020 onward. Similar fabricated stories in this period involved a wide range of celebrities from both political ends of the cultural spectrum: false stories about conservative celebrities denouncing liberal colleagues, false stories about liberal celebrities denouncing conservative ones, false stories about celebrities making grand political gestures, and false stories about celebrities' political conversions. The common thread is that they serve political identity expression rather than informational goals, and that they exploit the parasocial familiarity that audiences have developed with their subjects.

The actors most frequently targeted as subjects of conservative-coded celebrity culture-war fabrications form a recognisable list that includes, along with Wahlberg, actors Mel Gibson, Clint Eastwood, Adam Sandler, and various sports figures. The actors most frequently targeted as their foils in liberal-coded celebrity fabrications include, alongside Hanks, various Oscar-associated figures, late-night television hosts, and musicians associated with mainstream cultural progressivism. This casting reveals the political function of the content: the targeted celebrities are chosen not because they have actually made the attributed statements but because the political symbolism of their pairing is maximally effective for the intended audience.

The academic literature on political misinformation has increasingly focused on this "cultural politics" dimension of false celebrity news. Unlike political event misinformation (which targets voters' factual beliefs about specific events or candidates), celebrity culture-war fabrication targets cultural identity and group affiliation. Its goal is not to deceive voters about electoral facts but to reinforce the sense that cultural battle lines are real, that Hollywood is divided, and that certain admired figures are on the correct side of those lines. The misinformation is deployed in service of identity consolidation rather than specific political outcomes, which makes it harder to address through standard fact-checking because the audience's motivation is not primarily epistemic.

Media scholars studying the genre have noted that the fabrications tend to cluster around specific periods of heightened culture-war salience: Supreme Court decisions, high-profile award show controversies, and news cycles centred on political correctness in entertainment. The Wahlberg/Hanks story gained traction during periods when "woke Hollywood" was a prominent conservative media theme, using the specific vocabulary of that moment ("too woke") to signal cultural contemporaneity. The use of current culture-war vocabulary increases the story's sense of topicality and relevance, which enhances its sharing appeal while also providing a rough dating mechanism for fact-checkers assessing the wave of transmission (Wardle & Derakhshan, 2017, "Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policy Making," Council of Europe Report DGI(2017)09).


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated this claim FALSE and documented the absence of any verifiable sourcing. The assessment methodology applied to claims of this category involves: (1) direct sourcing — identifying whether any credible news source reported the alleged statement or refusal prior to the social media spread of the claim; (2) trade industry check — confirming whether entertainment trade publications, which would cover major project collapses or celebrity feuds as standard beats, reported any aspect of the claim; (3) public record search — reviewing both celebrities' documented public statements, social media, and official communications for any reference to the alleged event; (4) management/PR confirmation — seeking comment from representatives of both parties; (5) origin tracing — identifying the earliest source publishing the claim and evaluating its credibility designation.

All five steps yield negative results. The earliest publication is consistently a satirical or low-credibility fabrication site; entertainment trades have no coverage; neither celebrity's documented public record contains relevant statements; no management confirmation was possible; and origin tracing consistently leads back to the fake news content ecosystem.

For media literacy educators, this claim provides a useful teaching case for what might be called "confirmationally asymmetric debunking" — the situation in which a claim's falseness is established by absence of evidence (no statement was made, no project exists) rather than by contradicting evidence (a statement was made that contradicts the claim). This asymmetry is important because audiences trained on "check the source" heuristics are often better equipped to evaluate claims with bad sources than claims with no genuine source at all. The instruction to "look it up" should lead to finding nothing — no coverage in reliable trade sources — but audiences often interpret the absence of a confident denial on the celebrity's part as an absence of resolution rather than as strong negative evidence.

The Wahlberg claim also illustrates the "real attributes" vulnerability discussed above. Because Wahlberg's faith and Hanks's politics are both genuinely documented and widely known, audiences have access to mental simulations of how these real attributes *could* generate the claimed conflict, and those mental simulations feel like evidence. Media literacy training that addresses this vulnerability must go beyond source verification to include explicit attention to the difference between "this could be true given what I know about these people" (availability heuristic reasoning) and "this is true given the evidence for this specific claim" (evidential reasoning). The conflation of these two reasoning modes is the primary cognitive mechanism that this category of fabrication exploits.
