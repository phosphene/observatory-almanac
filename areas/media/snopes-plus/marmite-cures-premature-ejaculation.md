# Marmite Cures Premature Ejaculation?

## 1. The Claim

On 7 October 2016, the Daily Mail published an article carrying the kind of headline that made British tabloid health journalism simultaneously a global institution and a global object of ridicule: Marmite, the divisive British yeast extract spread beloved and despised in roughly equal measure, had been identified by scientists as a potential solution to premature ejaculation — a condition affecting, the article informed its readers with authority, one in four men in the United Kingdom.

The article drew on a study published in the September 2016 issue of *Andrologica*, a peer-reviewed journal of male reproductive medicine. The research found a statistically significant correlation between vitamin B12 concentration in blood and premature ejaculation (PE), with B12 levels meaningfully lower in men with PE than in controls without the condition. The Daily Mail's contribution was to leap from this finding — a correlation between B12 and PE in a clinical case-control study — to the headline claim that Marmite, one of the richest vegetarian sources of vitamin B12, "could hold the secret to making men last longer in the bedroom."

The claim spread with the velocity characteristic of sexual health stories in tabloid media: broadly, rapidly, and with each retelling either laundered of its caveats or amplified beyond the original overclaim. The story's social media life was driven partly by absurdity appeal — the image of Marmite as a bedroom performance-enhancer is inherently memorable — and partly by the genuine and widespread anxiety around PE, a condition that affects an estimated 20–30% of men globally at some point in their reproductive lives but is rarely discussed openly due to persistent cultural stigma (Waldinger, 2007, *Journal of Sexual Medicine*).

Marmite itself, for readers outside the United Kingdom, requires a word of context. Produced from yeast extract — the concentrated byproduct of brewing — Marmite has been a British dietary staple since 1902. It is dark brown, intensely savory, and so powerfully flavored that the manufacturer's own marketing slogan positions it as an either-or proposition: "Love it or hate it." A single serving (8g) provides approximately 25% of the recommended daily intake (RDI) of vitamin B12, making it one of the few reliable non-animal B12 sources and a significant resource for vegetarians and vegans who cannot obtain B12 from meat.

## 2. What's Actually True

The *Andrologica* study at the center of the claim was published by a team of researchers examining the possible role of micronutrient deficiencies in premature ejaculation. Their study enrolled 109 men — a mix of PE patients diagnosed according to clinical criteria and matched controls without PE — and measured serum vitamin B12 concentrations alongside other relevant parameters. The key finding, stated in the paper: "Vitamin B12 concentrations in the blood were significantly lower in patients with PE than the subjects without PE." The study found statistical significance (p < 0.05) for this difference.

This is a real and potentially meaningful finding. B12 deficiency and PE co-occurring at rates higher than chance across a clinical population of 109 men is worth reporting and worth investigating further. The researchers themselves were appropriately cautious: "It is not yet known whether this finding could be translated into a solution for PE; however, vitamin B12 supplementation for the treatment of PE merits further evaluation." That sentence — "merits further evaluation" — is the correct scientific response to a correlation study finding. It is an invitation to design a follow-up interventional trial, not a declaration that the treatment works.

The Daily Mail's representation elided this critical distinction. "Merits further evaluation" is methodologically miles from "could hold the secret." The first statement describes an unconfirmed correlation that justifies a research hypothesis. The second describes a near-discovery. The two are not interchangeable, and the transformation of the first into the second is the precise mechanism by which tabloid health journalism routinely miseducates its audience about the state of scientific knowledge.

Additional scientific context that the Daily Mail's framing suppressed: the pathophysiology of premature ejaculation is multifactorial. A 2016 paper in the *International Journal of Urology* summarized the current understanding: "The pathophysiology of premature ejaculation appears to be multifactorial, implicating the need for multimodal therapeutic regimens to successfully treat premature ejaculation." PE involves neurological components (serotonergic and dopaminergic pathways), psychological components (anxiety, relationship dynamics, conditioning), hormonal components, and possibly genetic predisposition. A single micronutrient deficiency as a causal driver of PE would require extraordinary evidence; a correlation in 109 patients, without a proposed mechanism tested or ruled out, does not approach that standard.

Marmite's B12 content is genuine: 25% RDI per 8g serving, as confirmed by the manufacturer's nutritional data. But meat substantially exceeds this: a 100g serving of cooked beef liver contains well over 1,000% of daily B12 requirements (National Institutes of Health, Office of Dietary Supplements, 2023, *Vitamin B12 Fact Sheet for Health Professionals*). Many enriched breakfast cereals also provide roughly 25% RDI per serving. Marmite's B12 advantage is specifically in the vegetarian context, not as a general-population B12 optimization strategy.

Snopes rates the overall claim **Mixture**: the B12-PE correlation study is real and reported correctly; the leap from correlation to "Marmite cures PE" is unwarranted and not supported by the research. This is exactly right.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### The Neurobiology of Why the Connection Is Biologically Plausible

One reason the Marmite-PE story achieved traction beyond mere absurdity interest is that the underlying B12-ejaculatory function connection is, in fact, biologically plausible. Understanding the mechanism explains both why the *Andrologica* study is scientifically interesting and why it cannot yet support a therapeutic recommendation.

**B12 and myelin synthesis.** Vitamin B12 (cobalamin) is an essential cofactor in myelin synthesis. Myelin, the phospholipid sheath surrounding nerve fibers, is critical for saltatory conduction of nerve impulses: it enables the rapid, precise transmission of action potentials along axons. B12 deficiency produces a well-characterized clinical syndrome, subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord, in which demyelination of posterior and lateral columns of the spinal cord produces sensory ataxia, weakness, and autonomic dysfunction (Stabler, 2013, *New England Journal of Medicine*). The ejaculatory reflex is mediated by spinal cord pathways — specifically a spinal ejaculation generator located in the lumbar spinal cord — and is therefore dependent on intact myelination of the relevant neural circuits (Coolen et al., 2004, *Trends in Neurosciences*).

A B12-deficient man would theoretically have some degree of altered conduction velocity in the neural circuits mediating ejaculatory timing. Whether this alteration would produce specifically premature ejaculation or other patterns of dysfunction (delayed, absent, or dysesthetic ejaculation) is not straightforwardly predictable from first principles, because the ejaculatory reflex involves excitatory and inhibitory circuits whose relative balance is what determines timing. B12 deficiency affecting both types of circuits could produce variable outcomes depending on which circuits are more vulnerable. Nevertheless, the biological plausibility of a connection is sufficient to justify the *Andrologica* researchers' hypothesis.

**B12 and serotonin metabolism.** A second and perhaps more direct mechanistic pathway involves serotonin. Serotonergic neurotransmission exerts a powerful inhibitory effect on ejaculation: this is why selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are used off-label — and, in the case of dapoxetine, on-label — as the primary pharmacological treatment for premature ejaculation. SSRIs delay ejaculation precisely by increasing synaptic serotonin, thereby enhancing serotonin receptor activation in the inhibitory pathways that modulate ejaculatory timing (Waldinger et al., 1998, *British Journal of Urology*; Waldinger, 2007, *Journal of Sexual Medicine*).

Vitamin B12 is involved, through its role as a cofactor in methylation reactions, in the synthesis and metabolism of several neurotransmitters including serotonin. B12 deficiency impairs the conversion of homocysteine to methionine, which in turn reduces the availability of S-adenosylmethionine (SAMe), the universal methyl donor required for the methylation of serotonin and other monoamines. Reduced SAMe availability can compromise serotonin metabolism, potentially leading to lower functional serotonergic tone in circuits including the ejaculatory control pathway (Bottiglieri, 2013, *CNS Drugs*, reviewing B12 and neuropsychiatric function).

This mechanistic chain — B12 deficiency → reduced SAMe → impaired serotonin methylation → reduced serotonergic inhibition of ejaculation → premature ejaculation — is biologically coherent and internally consistent. It does not prove that low B12 causes PE, but it provides a mechanism by which such a connection could exist, which is exactly why the *Andrologica* study warranted publication and why the authors correctly recommended further evaluation.

### The Tabloid Science Translation Failure

The transformation of "correlation in 109 men suggests a hypothesis worth testing" into "Marmite cures PE" illustrates a systematic failure in the way health research findings are translated into popular media. This failure has been extensively studied and documented.

Churnalism in health journalism — the practice of lightly rewriting press releases without independent evaluation — produces headlines that routinely overclaim the implications of preliminary research (Davies, 2008, *Flat Earth News*; Schwitzer, 2008, *PLOS Medicine*, reviewing coverage quality of medical news). The overclaiming is not random; it follows predictable patterns. Result-to-intervention translations are common: "X is associated with Y" becomes "consuming X improves Y." Population restrictions are dropped: "among elderly diabetic women, consumption of X correlated with reduced Y" becomes "X reduces Y." Study designations are collapsed: correlation studies are presented as if they demonstrated causation; animal studies are reported as if applicable to humans; pilot studies are presented as definitive.

The PE context additionally benefits from a specific demand asymmetry. Conditions associated with sexual performance and masculine identity are particularly prone to over-optimistic coverage because the readership demand is high, the topic is commercially adjacent to large product markets, and the cultural shame surrounding the condition motivates audiences to seek and share apparently promising information regardless of its evidentiary basis. Ben Goldacre documented this pattern systematically in his analysis of British health reporting, noting that sexual health and dietary supplement claims receive especially uncritical coverage because editors and journalists anticipate (correctly) high engagement with the topic regardless of scientific quality (Goldacre, 2009, *Bad Science*).

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status (Marmite cures PE):** False as stated
**Propositional Status (B12 deficiency associated with PE):** Preliminary evidence supports further investigation; not yet established as causal
**Propositional Status (Marmite is a good B12 source):** True
**Confidence in composite Mixture verdict:** High
**Epistemic Basis:** Genuine correlation study in Andrologica; no interventional trial of B12 supplementation for PE; no study testing Marmite specifically; multifactorial PE pathophysiology makes single-factor cure implausible but single-factor contributor possible
**Mechanistic Plausibility:** Moderate-to-high (B12-myelin-ejaculatory nerve and B12-SAMe-serotonin pathways are biologically coherent)
**Therapeutic Claim Status:** Unsupported — correlation demonstrates association, not efficacy
**Snopes Verdict:** Mixture — correctly assigned
**Gap Analysis (EARC Mode C):** Snopes correctly decomposes the claim but does not explain the neurobiological plausibility of the B12-PE connection, does not analyze the tabloid overclaim mechanism structurally, and does not characterize what research design would be required to move from "merits further evaluation" to "demonstrated therapeutic effect."

## 5. The Wider Picture

### The Landscape of PE Treatment

Premature ejaculation is the most commonly reported male sexual dysfunction globally, with prevalence estimates typically ranging from 20–30% when broadly defined using patient self-report, though narrower clinical definitions yield lower figures (Waldinger, 2007, *Journal of Sexual Medicine*). Despite its prevalence, PE receives relatively little primary care attention, carries substantial shame and stigma, and is frequently undertreated or self-managed.

The established pharmacological treatments for PE are well-characterized. SSRIs — including paroxetine, sertraline, fluoxetine, and the short-acting dapoxetine specifically developed for on-demand PE treatment — are the most evidence-supported interventions. Their mechanism of action (enhanced serotonergic inhibition of the spinal ejaculation generator, as described above) is understood, and multiple placebo-controlled trials have demonstrated their efficacy (Waldinger et al., 1998, *British Journal of Urology*; McMahon et al., 2016, *Journal of Sexual Medicine*). Topical anesthetics — lidocaine/prilocaine preparations applied to the glans penis — are also effective and approved in several markets. Behavioral techniques, including the "squeeze technique" and "stop-start" methods developed by Masters and Johnson and subsequently refined, have empirical support for effectiveness in some populations.

Vitamin B12 supplementation does not feature in any current guideline-recommended treatment protocol for PE. No interventional trial has tested it. The *Andrologica* correlation study is the primary published evidence for any B12-PE connection. Whether B12 supplementation in B12-deficient men with PE would reduce PE severity remains genuinely unknown.

### Marmite's Nutritional Profile and Health Claim History

Marmite has a substantial, if mixed, history as the subject of health claims. Its B12 content makes it genuinely valuable for vegetarians and vegans, who cannot obtain B12 from meat and may struggle to meet requirements from plant sources alone. B12, a water-soluble vitamin whose dietary deficiency causes macrocytic megaloblastic anemia and neurodegenerative effects, is one of the few nutrients for which supplementation or fortified foods are actively recommended to vegans (Watanabe et al., 2013, *Nutrients*).

Marmite has also been the subject of claims about GABA (gamma-aminobutyric acid) content and anxiety reduction (Masood et al., 2012, reported in popular media but with very limited primary research), claims about folic acid and neural tube defect prevention (appropriately established given its folate content), and the current B12-PE claim. Its glutamate content — the source of its distinctive umami flavor — has made it adjacent to discussions of dietary glutamate and neurological function, though the scientific basis for dietary glutamate effects on brain function remains contested. In general, Marmite is a nutritionally rich condiment with genuine micronutrient benefits, but it has also served repeatedly as a vehicle for tabloid food-medicine claims that substantially outpace the evidence.

### The Shame Economy of Sexual Health Claims

A specific vulnerability makes PE an unusually attractive topic for tabloid overclaiming. Because PE is experienced as deeply shameful by many affected men — and because it is associated with social and relational consequences that motivate urgent self-treatment — men seeking solutions are more likely than average to engage with, share, and act on apparent cures regardless of evidentiary quality. The demand signal is high and largely hidden from public view, emerging into visibility primarily through search behavior and social sharing of "useful" content.

This pattern means that food-as-cure claims for sexual dysfunction function partially as informational cover: by sharing an article about Marmite and PE, a man can signal interest in the topic without directly disclosing personal experience with the condition. The social function of sharing the content — as curious, humorous, or informative — is separable from its potential use as personal guidance. This dual-use function amplifies sharing rates above what the evidential quality of the underlying claim would otherwise justify.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Alex Kasprak's Snopes analysis of the Marmite-PE claim deploys the publication's "What's True / What's False" format with particular effectiveness. The binary of the headline claim — that Marmite cures PE — is decomposed into its factual components: the *Andrologica* study is real and correctly described, the B12-PE correlation it found is genuine, Marmite is indeed a B12 source, and the specific therapeutic claim — that Marmite addresses PE — is unsupported. The Mixture verdict captures this composite accurately: not everything in the story is wrong, but the central claim as expressed by the Daily Mail is not supported by the research cited.

This is more epistemically sophisticated than a binary True/False verdict would be, and deserves recognition as such. Many of the most problematic health claims in circulation are not outright fabrications but overclaims from legitimate research — claims where something is genuinely true and something is genuinely unsupported, and where the error lies in the distance between the two. The Mixture category is the right tool for this class of claim, and Kasprak uses it correctly.

What the Snopes analysis does not provide is the neurobiological explanation for why the *Andrologica* finding is scientifically interesting in the first place. The B12-myelin pathway, the B12-SAMe-serotonin pathway, and their collective relevance to ejaculatory timing control are the mechanisms that give the correlation study its scientific value and that also explain why the therapeutic leap is premature: the plausibility of the connection is high enough to justify research, but research has not been done. By omitting this mechanistic discussion, the Snopes analysis risks leaving readers with the impression that the *Andrologica* study was simply a random fishing expedition, rather than a theoretically motivated investigation of a biologically coherent hypothesis.

Additionally, the structural critique — why this kind of tabloid overclaim recurs, what design decisions would actually test the claim properly, and what "further evaluation" would specifically require — is absent from the Snopes treatment but central to equipping readers to evaluate the next round of tabloid health coverage. What would a proper B12-PE trial look like? A double-blind, placebo-controlled trial randomizing B12-deficient men with PE to B12 supplementation or matched placebo, with validated intravaginal ejaculation latency time (IELT) as the primary outcome measure, powered to detect a clinically meaningful difference, with stratification by baseline B12 level and PE severity. Describing this hypothetical study converts "merits further evaluation" from a deferral into a specific research agenda — and enables readers to see exactly how far the current evidence is from supporting the Daily Mail's headline.
