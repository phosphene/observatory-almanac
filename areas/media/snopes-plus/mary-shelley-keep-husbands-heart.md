# Mary Shelley Kept Her Husband Percy's Heart After His Death

## The Claim

The claim is a Gothic gift to popular history: Mary Shelley, author of *Frankenstein*, the most famous horror novel ever written, kept her dead husband's heart. After the poet Percy Bysshe Shelley drowned off the Italian coast in 1822, his body was cremated on the beach. Something — an organ of some kind — refused to burn. It was removed from the fire, and after some dispute over ownership, eventually came into Mary Shelley's possession. She kept it for the remaining twenty-nine years of her life, wrapped in silk, inside a box-desk she kept near her bed, alongside a page torn from one of Percy's poems.

The story circulates enthusiastically in October, when its Gothic credentials make it seasonally appropriate. It is one of those pieces of history that seems almost too literary to be real — darkly perfect, almost designed. A horror novelist who kept the physical remains of the man she loved. A heart that survived fire. A Romantic poet elevated to near-mythical status by a death that came before his time. The story fits every available narrative template simultaneously: devotion, tragedy, the Gothic, the morbid Victorian relationship with death, the literary couple as legend.

But is it true?

## What's Actually True

Snopes rates this claim **True**, and the available historical evidence supports that verdict with some important qualifications about the nature of the sources and the nature of the relic.

Percy Bysshe Shelley drowned on 8 July 1822, when his sailing boat, the *Don Juan*, sank during a sudden storm on the Gulf of Spezia in northern Italy. He was twenty-nine years old. His body was not recovered immediately — it washed ashore several days later. Because of Italian quarantine regulations pertaining to bodies recovered from water, Percy's remains could not be transported and had to be cremated on the beach at Viareggio. The cremation took place on 16 August 1822, with Percy's friends present: the novelist and adventurer Edward John Trelawny, the poet Leigh Hunt, and Lord Byron.

Trelawny provided the principal eyewitness account of the cremation and the survival of the heart. According to his testimony — recorded in multiple versions over the following decades — Percy's body burned completely except for one organ, which remained intact through hours of intense heat. Trelawny identified this organ as the heart and reached into the fire to retrieve it. (His arm was apparently burned in the process; he wore the scar with pride for years afterward.) There followed a dispute over possession of the relic. Leigh Hunt initially claimed it; eventually, after some unpleasantness, it was conceded to Mary Shelley.

The material evidence for Mary Shelley's possession of the relic comes from an inventory made after her death in 1851. Her son, Percy Florence Shelley, found among his mother's effects a box-desk she had kept near her bed. Inside was a paper parcel containing a piece of silk wrapped around a small dried organic object, together with some ashes and a torn leaf from Percy Bysshe Shelley's poem *Adonais* — his elegy for the poet Keats. The biographer Emily Sunstein, writing in her 1989 biography *Mary Shelley: Romance and Reality*, provides the most detailed modern account of this discovery, drawing on letters and family testimony.

This physical evidence combined with Trelawny's eyewitness account constitutes a genuinely strong basis for the claim. Mary Shelley did keep some physical remnant of her husband, wrapped in silk, for nearly three decades.

Two significant complications merit acknowledgement, however. The first concerns what the organ actually was. Trelawny identified it as the heart, and this identification passed into the widely accepted account. But in 1885, the London *Athenaeum* published a challenge to this identification, suggesting that the organ that survived the cremation was more likely the liver — specifically, a liver saturated with sea water from the drowning, which would have made it far more resistant to burning than a normal liver. (A waterlogged, dense organ would be much harder to incinerate than a desiccated heart muscle.) The anatomist's case for liver rather than heart is scientifically plausible. Others have proposed that Percy's heart may have been calcified due to a prior bout of tuberculosis, which would also explain its resistance to flames. Trelawny himself was not a physician and his identification was not expert. The possibility that it was a liver has never been definitively settled.

The second complication concerns Trelawny as a source. He was among the most unreliable narrators in the literary-historical archive of the Romantic period. Trelawny rewrote his account of Percy's death and cremation nearly a dozen times over the following fifty years, and each revision added detail, grew more dramatic, and placed Trelawny at increasingly central positions in the story. Biographer Rosalie Glynn Grylls, in her study of Trelawny, described him as someone who "put poetry into what began as a practical proceeding" and whose accounts accumulated baroque embellishment like "sinister biographical coral-reef." His story of reaching into the fire is documented in his own accounts and corroborated in outline by the fact that something did end up in Mary Shelley's possession — but the precise phenomenological details, including the dramatic image of the heart glowing in the flames while everything else was ash, carry the fingerprints of a sensationalist memoirist.

None of this substantially undermines the core claim. Something that came from Percy Shelley's body survived his cremation and ended up wrapped in silk in Mary Shelley's desk-box. Whether it was a heart or a liver, whether Trelawny embellished the scene or accurately reported it, the physical fact of the relic is supported by evidence. Snopes's verdict of True stands.

## Why People Believe This

The claim has a strange epistemological position: it is unusual among popular "Gothic history" claims precisely because it appears to be genuinely, robustly true. Most stories that seem this perfectly designed to be morbidly interesting and literarily appropriate turn out to be embellished, misattributed, or wholly invented upon close examination. This story holds up. That robustness creates its own circulation dynamic — people share it not just for its strangeness but because verifying it becomes itself pleasurable. The fact-check confirms and dignifies rather than punctures.

The story is also carried by the extraordinary power of the Mary Shelley mythology. She wrote *Frankenstein* as a teenager on a bet with Byron and Polidori at Lake Geneva during the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816. She lost her first child at seventeen. She eloped with a married man who subsequently fathered children by others. She witnessed most of the central figures of English Romanticism die young and badly. And then she outlived them all, editing her husband's works, raising their surviving son alone, and apparently carrying a piece of him in a box for the rest of her life. The heart story is not merely a single Gothic anecdote — it is the culminating image of a biography that reads like a novelist's invention.

Medical and social historians also note that the Victorian relationship with death and physical remains was genuinely different from contemporary Western norms. Mourning hair, death photography, post-mortem portraiture, and relics of the deceased were all practised across social classes in the nineteenth century. The BBC has reported on how common it was in Victorian England to take photographs with deceased relatives posed as if living, to craft jewellery from their hair, and to maintain physical connections to the dead that contemporary culture tends to find disturbing. In this context, Mary Shelley's relic-keeping was not evidence of madness or exceptional morbidity — it was broadly consistent with the mourning culture of her time. The fact-checker who applies contemporary emotional standards to evaluate whether Mary Shelley's behaviour was "plausible" is using the wrong frame.

## Verdict

**True.** The physical and testimonial evidence for this claim is robust by historical standards. Mary Shelley did keep a preserved organ from her husband's body for approximately twenty-nine years. The principal uncertainties are about what the organ actually was (heart vs. liver) and about the reliability of the primary narrator (the systematically embellishing Trelawny). Neither uncertainty is sufficient to overthrow the core claim. Snopes's True rating is well-supported. A maximally precise rendering would be: *Mary Shelley kept a preserved organic relic believed to be Percy's heart for the rest of her life, with some uncertainty about whether it was the heart or the liver and with the usual cautions about source reliability in early nineteenth-century literary-historical documentation.*

## The Wider Picture

The Percy Shelley drowning and its aftermath has generated a remarkable density of historical and literary material, making it one of the most thoroughly documented private tragedies in English literary history — and yet the documentary richness comes almost entirely from the accounts of the same group of highly literary, highly self-dramatising individuals who were present. Byron, Trelawny, Leigh Hunt, and the circle of expatriate writers in Italy in 1822 were constitutionally incapable of narrating events without aesthetic and narrative transformation. The historian of the event must work through layers of self-conscious literary performance to reach anything like the factual record.

This is not unique to Percy Shelley's death. The entire Romantic movement invested heavily in the idea that the artist's life and death were themselves art — that biography was a form of poetry, that the deaths of young geniuses (Keats, Shelley, Byron followed shortly) proved something about the incompatibility of artistic vision and vulgar existence. Every account produced within this ideological framework must be read with awareness that the narrators were making meaning rather than simply recording facts. Trelawny's increasingly spectacular retellings of the beach cremation are not simple lies — they are literary constructions that embed historical events within a narrative of Romantic martyrdom.

The survival of the heart (or liver) in the fire was itself immediately interpreted within the symbolic vocabulary of Romanticism. Edward Bulwer-Lytton, among others, wrote about it as proof of the extraordinary nature of Percy's genius — as if the physical body confirmed what the poetry had always suggested. This is pure myth-making, disconnected from anatomy. But it illustrates how quickly raw events were converted into symbols within the Romantic network.

Mary Shelley's own relationship to her husband's memory was complex and somewhat contradictory. She spent years editing his unpublished works and writing notes for his collected poems, an editorial project that involved determining what Percy "really meant" on topics he had addressed ambivalently during his life. Her editorial choices — which poems to include, how to frame his radicalism in introductions — were shaped by her own evolving views and by the need to present Percy in a form acceptable to his conservative father, Sir Timothy Shelley, upon whose financial support she depended. The heart in the box-desk is perhaps best understood not as proof of obsessive devotion but as one element in a multidimensional relationship with a legacy she was simultaneously preserving, managing, and shaping.

The discovery of the relic after Mary Shelley's death also illuminates how the Victorian conception of the private self differed from the public persona. Mary Shelley had never publicly discussed keeping the relic. It was found by her son only after she died. The silk wrapping, the torn page of *Adonais*, the ashes — these were not displayed or exhibited. They were kept near her bed, invisible to visitors. Whatever the relic meant to her, it was profoundly private, a relationship conducted in the interior rather than performed for the literary world that already found her sufficiently fascinating. This discretion is perhaps the most humanising aspect of the whole story.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addressed this claim in an article published on 31 October 2021, written by Dan Evon. Its verdict is True, and the article's handling is exemplary in several respects. Evon confirms the core claim, acknowledges the dispute about whether the organ was the heart or the liver, notes the question of Trelawny's reliability as a primary source, provides the biographical context of Percy's death and cremation, and cites Emily Sunstein's 1989 biography as the most rigorous modern account. He also contextualises the claim within Victorian mourning culture, noting that keeping physical remains of the deceased was not considered pathological in the period.

The article correctly notes that the claim sometimes travels with a version asserting the organ was "definitely" Percy's heart, and gently introduces the liver hypothesis and the anatomical uncertainty without overweighting it. This is a model of calibrated fact-checking: affirming what is known, characterising what is uncertain, and avoiding both overclaiming (it was definitely the heart) and underclaiming (we can't know anything for sure).

One small gap in the Snopes treatment is the limited attention given to Trelawny's source reliability problem — the article notes that he "re-wrote his account nearly a dozen times," but does not fully develop the implications of this for the evidential status of the specific details (the glowing heart, the burned hand, the dramatic retrieval). A reader could come away from the article still believing Trelawny's version of events is more or less exactly what happened, rather than understanding that it is a well-documented literary performance whose factual core needs to be distinguished from its narrative embellishments.

The epistemological takeaway from this case is somewhat unusual for the Truth Vault: it is a reminder that the most Gothic-seeming, most improbable, most "this sounds invented" claims sometimes turn out to be true, and that the relationship between evidence quality and emotional plausibility is not always inverse. Our intuition that "something this perfect must be false" is a cognitive heuristic that sometimes serves us well but sometimes causes us to resist well-supported claims for purely aesthetic reasons. Mary Shelley keeping her husband's heart in a silk-wrapped parcel for twenty-nine years is exactly the kind of thing that would happen in a novel about Mary Shelley — which is precisely why some people reflexively assume it was invented. It wasn't.
