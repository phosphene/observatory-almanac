# WV Episcopal Diocese Declares Mayo on Pepperoni Rolls an 'Abomination'?

## 1. The Claim

On December 27 and 28, 2023, a screenshot of a Facebook post attributed to the Episcopal Diocese of West Virginia spread rapidly across social media platforms. The post, screenshotted and shared by WCHS-TV anchor Daniel Burbank, declared in formal ecclesiastical language that "putting mayo on pepperoni rolls is an abomination in the eyes of the Lord," adding that "those who perpetrate this heinous act have committed a mortal sin which can only be forgiven by special dispensation from the clergy of West Virginia." The post described two forms of penance—"either by burning a couch or making a pilgrimage to the Mothman statue"—and concluded with an escalating sting: "This applies only to those who have put mayo on pepperoni rolls. The use of Miracle Whip is unforgivable."

Burbank's accompanying tweet read: "This is a real statement from the Episcopal Diocese of West Virginia."

The implicit factual question embedded in the viral spread was: Did the Episcopal Diocese of West Virginia actually publish this? Was it officially authorized, or was it a parody account, a rogue staff member, or a social media hoax? In an era in which institutional impersonation, satirical account confusion, and screenshot manipulation have made verification a routine necessity, the claim warranted formal investigation. Snopes journalist Jordan Liles confirmed the post's authenticity and published the investigation on December 28, 2023.

The claim that this post is genuine and officially authorized is TRUE.

## 2. What's Actually True

**The Duke's Mayo Bowl context:** On December 27, 2023, the Duke's Mayo Bowl—an annual college football bowl game sponsored by Duke's Mayonnaise, played at Bank of America Stadium in Charlotte, North Carolina—featured West Virginia University's Mountaineers against the University of North Carolina Tar Heels. WVU won decisively, 30–10. Following WVU's victory, head coach Neal Brown participated in the bowl game's signature tradition: rather than the Gatorade bath customary in American football celebrations, a mayonnaise bath. Brown was doused with Duke's Mayo. During the broadcast, ESPN commentators Matt Barrie and Dan Mullen were seen squeezing Duke's Mayo bottles over pepperoni rolls—West Virginia's official state food—in a moment that the diocese would later cite.

**The post and its verification:** The Facebook post appeared on the official Episcopal Diocese of West Virginia Facebook page. Snopes confirmed the origin through multiple channels. Rev. Matthew Cowden, the diocesan leader whose automatic email responder (noting his Christmas-season absence) initially greeted Snopes' inquiry, had already posted on his own Facebook page: "Our Priests of The Episcopal Diocese of West Virginia are standing by to absolve contrite, repentant sinners. Lay down your mayo, my child, and receive the Lord's mercy." When Cowden responded substantively, his confirmation was unambiguous: "Yes, this post is us. This is how we pepperoni roll."

Cowden identified the post's authors as Rev. Canon Chad Slater and Rev. Canon Jordan Trumble, both staff members of the diocese. He characterized the post as "an excellent example of the interpretive crossover between church, culture, and humor that our folks do." The diocesan response to the post's viral spread was described by Cowden as one of being "pleasantly surprised"; he invoked theological language for the post's reception—"the Church forms us to trust in the movements of the Holy Spirit, even and especially when she surprises us"—before offering what was perhaps the most quotable close: "Come for the jokes, stay for the Jesus."

**The pepperoni roll as West Virginia state food:** The pepperoni roll's designation as West Virginia's official state food is documented by the West Virginia state government's own publications. The food has origins in the coal-mining culture of north-central West Virginia, where it was developed in the early twentieth century as a convenient, shelf-stable, no-utensil lunch for miners descending underground shifts. Country Bakery in Fairmont, WV, operated by baker Giuseppe "Joseph" Argiro, is credited with commercializing the form beginning around 1927. The food is a soft bread roll, typically made with a slightly sweet dough similar to white sandwich bread, with pepperoni baked into or stuffed inside. The baked fat from the pepperoni seeps into the dough during cooking, creating the greasy, fragrant characteristic that makes the rolls beloved by West Virginians and mysterious to outsiders. They require no refrigeration—a property descended from the mining-era practicality requirement—and are sold at nearly every gas station, bakery, and convenience store throughout the state.

**Why the mayo combination is controversial:** Mayonnaise on pepperoni rolls is, by most accounts, genuinely divisive within West Virginia food culture. The roll is classically eaten without condiments, warm from the oven, as a complete item. The addition of condiments—particularly mayonnaise—is viewed by purists as a dilution of the roll's character and an imposition of sandwich logic on something that is specifically not a sandwich. Duke's Mayonnaise, the specific brand implicated in the bowl-game incident, is itself a beloved regional condiment in the South and Appalachian regions; it is not the mayo brand that created controversy but the act of applying any mayo to the pepperoni roll that the diocese was parodically condemning.

## 3. Why People Believe This (and Why the Verification Was Warranted)

This entry is structurally unusual among Truth Vault cases because its verdict is TRUE and its social dynamic is the opposite of typical misinformation: the viral claim is accurate, and the epistemic challenge is not debunking but verification. Nonetheless, the episode is analytically rich on several fronts.

**Institutional humor as a communication strategy:** The Episcopal Church broadly, and individual dioceses in particular, have increasingly deployed social media humor as a strategy for public relevance among demographics that rarely attend services. A 2021 study published by the Hartford Institute for Religion Research documented a significant generational collapse in self-identified Mainline Protestant affiliation among Americans under 40, with the Episcopal Church experiencing particularly steep declines relative to its peak membership in the 1960s (Chaves, 2021, *American Religion: Contemporary Trends*, Princeton University Press). Against this demographic reality, spontaneous viral moments of institutional humor represent a form of low-cost brand reclamation—brief moments in which people who would never set foot in an Episcopal church nonetheless have an emotionally positive, humanizing association with the institution. Rev. Cowden's "Come for the jokes, stay for the Jesus" formulation is a sophisticated, if casual, articulation of exactly this strategy.

**The verification problem that justified fact-checking:** Burbank's framing—"This is a real statement from the Episcopal Diocese of West Virginia"—was itself the trigger for extensive verification precisely because such assurances in social media contexts are frequently wrong. The 2010s and early 2020s are littered with institutionally branded viral posts that later proved to be from parody accounts, rogue employees whose subsequent posts were deleted, or outright fabrications constructed to look authentic. The Church of England, numerous American megachurches, and multiple foreign government agencies have been subject to false posts attributed to their official channels for comic or political effect. Burbank may have been correct, but his confidence warranted verification rather than replication; Snopes performed that verification function correctly.

**The epistemic novelty of "authentic verification as content":** This case represents a niche but growing genre in fact-checking practice—what might be termed *positive confirmation fact-checking*, in which the fact-checker's work confirms rather than refutes, adding evidentiary weight to a claim that was true but insufficiently documented. The genre is epistemically valuable but structurally underrepresented in most fact-checking operations, which are overwhelmingly oriented toward debunking false claims. Research on fact-checker selection effects finds that fact-checking organizations systematically over-check claims that are likely to be false and under-check claims that are likely to be true (Graves, 2016, *Deciding What's True: The Rise of Political Fact-Checking in American Journalism*, Columbia University Press). The Snopes investigation here operates counter to that selection pressure, providing documented confirmation that an authentic, amusing, officially sanctioned institutional communication was exactly what it appeared to be.

The post also illustrates the cultural informatics of regional food identity as a vehicle for institutional positioning. The specific selection of "burning a couch" as one of two prescribed forms of penance references a deeply localized WVU cultural practice: the burning of couches by students in the streets of Morgantown following major sporting victories (and, historically, defeats), a tradition so associated with WVU sports culture that it has repeatedly drawn national news coverage, fire ordinance amendments, and eventually university policy interventions. The Mothman statue reference invokes Point Pleasant, WV's cryptid tourism attraction—a fiberglass and metal sculpture of the winged humanoid creature associated with the 1967 Silver Bridge collapse in local mythology. Both references encode the post's audience as specifically, knowledgeably West Virginian; the humor is not merely broadly American but culturally precise in a way that demonstrates the authors' authentic embeddedness in the culture they were speaking to.

## 4. The Evidence in Full

The authenticity of the Episcopal Diocese of West Virginia's mayonnaise post rests on the following evidentiary chain:

1. **The post itself appeared on the official diocesan Facebook page**, which Snopes cross-referenced against known official account identifiers for the diocese.

2. **Rev. Matthew Cowden, the diocesan leader, confirmed the post directly by email** to Snopes, identified the authors (Rev. Canon Chad Slater and Rev. Canon Jordan Trumble), and described the organizational culture that produced it.

3. **Cowden's own contemporaneous personal Facebook post** ("Our Priests of The Episcopal Diocese of West Virginia are standing by to absolve contrite, repentant sinners. Lay down your mayo, my child, and receive the Lord's mercy") cross-corroborates the diocesan post and confirms leadership-level awareness and endorsement.

4. **WCHS-TV anchor Daniel Burbank**, a credentialed local television journalist with professional contact with local institutions, stated he verified the post's authenticity before publishing; his retweet adds journalistic citation weight.

5. **WVU's December 27, 2023 victory in the Duke's Mayo Bowl** is confirmed by AP reporting (30–10 final score), providing the independently verifiable triggering event for the post.

6. **The pepperoni roll's status as West Virginia's official state food** is confirmed by official West Virginia state government documentation.

The only unconfirmed element—Cowden's automatic email initially indicating Christmas-season absence—was itself subsequently overridden by Cowden's substantive response, and its temporary presence simply illustrates the timing dynamic of attempted verification during a holiday period.

## 5. What Remains Uncertain

This post warrants a brief accounting of what remains genuinely unknown about the episode, even within its verified core.

**Whether "burning a couch" constitutes official guidance:** The diocesan post specified couch-burning as one valid form of penance for the mayonnaise sin. The legality of couch-burning in West Virginia, particularly within municipal boundaries, is a genuinely contested matter with an actual legal history—Morgantown has passed specific ordinances against the practice. The Diocese's formal position on whether prescribed penances should comply with local fire ordinances was not addressed by Rev. Cowden; the omission was presumably intentional.

**The post's downstream engagement metrics:** The specific reach, share count, and demographic engagement profile of the post is not documented in the Snopes investigation or any subsequent reporting. Understanding *who* shared this post—specifically whether it reached non-religious, non-West Virginian audiences—would be relevant to assessing whether the diocesan "Come for the jokes, stay for the Jesus" strategy generated any measurable downstream outreach effect.

**Long-term influence on Bragg mayonnaise sales:** Duke's Mayonnaise is manufactured by Sauer Brands, Inc. Whether the combined media attention from the Duke's Mayo Bowl victory and the diocesan condemnation of its application to pepperoni rolls had any measurable effect on Duke's Mayonnaise regional sales is an interesting empirical question that lies beyond the scope of this verification.

**EARC classification:** This entry is classified C (Confirmed): the claim is substantiated by robust primary-source documentation and institutional confirmation. There is no evidence of fabrication, satirical impersonation, or unauthorized posting.

## 6. Verdict and Further Reading

**Verdict: TRUE.** The Episcopal Diocese of West Virginia did publish, through its official Facebook page, a humorous declaration that placing Duke's Mayonnaise on pepperoni rolls was "an abomination in the eyes of the Lord." The post was authored by two named diocesan clergy staff members, endorsed by diocesan leadership, and confirmed authentic by Rev. Matthew Cowden in direct communication with Snopes. The post was a deliberate, authorized piece of institutional humor tied to WVU's victory in the Duke's Mayo Bowl on December 27, 2023. The penance prescribed—burning a couch or pilgrimage to the Mothman statue—is a reading of West Virginia cultural practice that demonstrates authentic regional embeddedness. The case is a textbook example of positive-confirmation fact-checking and a documented instance of Mainline Protestant institutional social media strategy in a period of significant membership decline.
