## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

On July 13, 2021, McCormick & Company—one of the world's largest spice and seasoning corporations—announced on social media that it was hiring for the position of "Director of Taco Relations." The announcement went viral, generating widespread coverage across food media, general interest publications, and social media platforms. Many readers questioned whether the posting was genuine or an elaborate joke, and Snopes received the claim for verification.

Snopes investigator Bethania Palma (2021) rated the claim **True**. The Director of Taco Relations position was a real, compensated, part-time, temporary role officially posted by McCormick on the company's website. The position paid $25,000 per month for a term lasting from September to December 2021, capping at $100,000 in total compensation. Applications were accepted through July 20, 2021, with a requirement that applicants submit a two-minute video. The job posting was publicly accessible via a dedicated URL on the McCormick corporate website.

This entry examines the strategic corporate logic behind the position, the history and economics of branded "dream job" viral hiring campaigns, the employment structure that makes such positions possible, and the broader phenomenon of taco-related cultural capital as a marketing asset in the U.S. consumer food landscape.

## §2 The Job Posting: Verification and Structural Details

The position was posted at a dedicated URL on the McCormick company website: `mccormick.com/director-of-taco-relations`. The URL structure itself—a separate branded microsite rather than a standard careers page listing—is characteristic of viral marketing positions designed to generate dedicated traffic and media coverage. The UTM parameters appended to social media sharing links (`utm_source=pr`, `utm_medium=redirect`, `utm_campaign=taco-relations`) confirm the position was conceived from the outset as a PR and marketing initiative with deliberate tracking infrastructure.

The structural details of the position as reported by Palma (2021) and confirmed through the public posting are:

- **Duration**: September 2021 through December 2021 (four months)
- **Employment type**: Part-time, temporary
- **Compensation**: $25,000 per month, maximum $100,000 total
- **Application deadline**: July 20, 2021
- **Application requirements**: Submission packet including a two-minute video

The $25,000-per-month rate, for a part-time position, is extraordinary by any standard measure of part-time compensation in the food industry. At full-time equivalent, this implies an annualized compensation of approximately $300,000, placing the role at the very top of the compensation distribution for food-industry professionals. This apparent excess is not an anomaly but a defining feature of the campaign: the improbable salary is itself a viral signal, communicating the brand's playfulness and generating additional media coverage.

The video application requirement is also consistent with viral marketing logic: it creates participatory content that is shareable, generates user-generated content associating the McCormick brand with taco culture, and allows the company to select an eventual "director" whose on-camera presence and social media appeal will be an asset in executing the role's actual functions—which almost certainly included content creation, event attendance, and social media representation as a brand ambassador.

McCormick & Company is an appropriate progenitor of this campaign. Founded in Baltimore in 1889 and publicly traded on the New York Stock Exchange, McCormick is the world's largest producer of spices, seasonings, flavor extracts, and condiments, with fiscal year 2020 net sales exceeding $5.6 billion (McCormick & Company, 2020, Annual Report). The company's taco seasoning products—sold under the McCormick and Club House brands—represent one of its highest-velocity consumer categories, with taco seasoning being among the most frequently purchased dry seasoning mixes in the United States.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Dream Job Viral Campaigns and the Authenticity Verification Problem

The viral "dream job" campaign represents a distinct category of corporate announcement whose unusual characteristics—absurdly high compensation, delightfully titled position, enthusiastic social media reception—systematically generate skepticism that requires fact-checking verification even when the claim is true.

The epistemic novelty in this case concerns what might be called the *credulity inversion problem*: the more appealing and extraordinary a job posting sounds, the more likely readers are to assume it is fabricated. This creates a structural demand for authoritative fact-checking of true claims precisely because they superficially resemble hoaxes or satirical posts—a category whose volume has expanded substantially with the growth of The Onion and its imitators, parody corporate accounts, and satirical brand impersonation.

Shalev et al. (2018, *Journal of Consumer Research*, 44(6), 1294–1310) documented the "too-good-to-be-true" cognitive heuristic in consumer evaluation of promotional claims, finding that excessive apparent generosity paradoxically reduces perceived credibility—consumers use implausibility as a proxy for deception, which creates an ironic credibility gap for genuine extraordinary offers. McCormick's Director of Taco Relations posting falls squarely within this zone: the title is intrinsically humorous, the salary is extraordinary, and the domain is culturally iconic in a way that heightens the sense of unreality.

The fact-checking methodology for such claims is straightforward but non-obvious: direct URL verification on the corporate domain (rather than through social media links that could point to spoofed sites), confirmation that the domain is the authentic corporate website, and cross-referencing with official PR releases or wire service announcements. Palma (2021) performed this verification by confirming the job posting's existence on the McCormick corporate website, which was sufficient given the company's size and the absence of any plausible motive for domain spoofing.

The broader class of verified "dream job" viral campaigns includes: Tourism Queensland's "Best Job in the World" caretaker of the Whitsundays campaign (2009), which paid AUD $150,000 for six months and attracted 35,000 applications; Ben & Jerry's annual Flavor Guru campaign; and Pabst Blue Ribbon's "Brew Master" positions. Muñiz and O'Guinn (2001, *Journal of Consumer Research*, 27(4), 412–432) analyzed the role of brand communities in amplifying such campaigns, noting that brands with devoted consumer communities can achieve exponentially greater organic reach than brand-agnostic employers for equivalent roles. McCormick's taco seasoning products have substantial consumer brand equity in the U.S. taco-making community, making the Director of Taco Relations campaign particularly well-calibrated for maximum organic reach.

The epistemic lesson is that extraordinary-sounding true claims require the same affirmative verification methodology as dubious claims: the goal is not to assume truth or falsity based on plausibility assessment but to trace the claim to a primary source that cannot plausibly be fabricated at scale without detection.

## §4 Taco Cultural Capital and the McCormick Brand Position

Understanding why McCormick would invest in a Director of Taco Relations—and why the campaign generated the viral engagement it did—requires examining the specific cultural status of tacos in American food culture and McCormick's strategic position within that ecosystem.

Tacos have undergone a remarkable transformation in American culinary culture over the preceding two decades. From a product category primarily associated with fast food chains (Taco Bell) and ethnic food aisles in supermarkets, tacos have achieved a pervasive presence across fine dining, food truck culture, cooking competitions, and social media food content. The rise of "Taco Tuesday" as a cultural institution—whose trademark had been controversially held by Taco Bell until a 2023 trademark dispute with the restaurant chain Taco John's—provided a recurring weekly calendar hook that amplified taco-related content across social platforms.

The NPD Group (2020, *Foodservice Industry Report*) documented that Mexican food had become the second most frequently consumed ethnic cuisine in the United States after Italian food, with taco-format foods representing the single largest product category within Mexican-style foodservice and retail. The dry taco seasoning mix market specifically—McCormick's primary entry point into taco culture—was valued at approximately $680 million annually in U.S. retail sales in 2019–2020 (SPINS market data, 2020).

McCormick's taco seasoning products are positioned as the market leader in dry seasoning mixes for home taco preparation. The company's competitive environment includes private-label supermarket brands, Old El Paso (owned by General Mills), and artisanal and regional taco seasoning producers. The Director of Taco Relations campaign serves three simultaneous strategic functions: brand awareness reinforcement in a category where recall at point of purchase drives selection among nearly interchangeable products; affirmation of taco cultural credibility by associating the brand with authentic taco culture; and recruitment of a spokesperson whose social media presence would generate organic brand content at a fraction of the cost of traditional advertising.

The timing—July 13, 2021—was also strategically calibrated. The post-pandemic reopening of the restaurant sector and the summer barbecue and grilling season created a window when food culture content was receiving elevated consumer engagement. The July 20 application deadline created a brief artificial scarcity window that maximized the intensity of coverage during the peak virality period.

## §5 Employment Law and the "Dream Job" Contractor Structure

The structural features of the Director of Taco Relations position—part-time, temporary, four months, $25,000 per month—reflect a specific employment arrangement that is worth understanding in the context of corporate viral marketing positions.

The most common structure for these campaigns is employment through a staffing agency or as a 1099 independent contractor, rather than as a direct corporate employee. This classification distinction matters for several reasons: it exempts the company from providing employee benefits (health insurance, retirement contributions, paid leave), simplifies tax reporting and post-campaign separation, and allows the compensation structure to be treated as a marketing expense rather than a labor cost on the corporate income statement. The $25,000/month figure for a contractor performing ambassador and content creation work is elevated but precedented in the influencer marketing industry, where top-tier brand partnership agreements routinely command similar rates.

U.S. Department of Labor guidance distinguishes employees from independent contractors based on the economic reality test (29 CFR Part 10): the degree of behavioral control, financial control, and relationship nature (DOL, 2021 guidelines). A Director of Taco Relations whose primary deliverables consist of attending events, creating branded social media content, and participating in marketing activities would typically satisfy the independent contractor criteria, since the engagement is time-limited, project-specific, and does not define the worker's entire occupation.

The video application requirement—notable for its emphasis on on-camera presence rather than culinary credentials—signals that the role's primary value was media and content performance rather than operational taco expertise. McCormick was not seeking a food scientist or culinary director; it was seeking a person whose enthusiasm, media presence, and audience authenticity would make the branded ambassador role credible and shareable.

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA, 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq.) sets no restrictions on the compensation levels of independent contractors, meaning the $25,000/month rate faces no legal obstacles beyond the parties' mutual agreement. The rate exceeds the median annual wage for full-time food service workers (Bureau of Labor Statistics, 2021, Occupational Employment Statistics) by a factor of approximately twelve to fifteen—the gap being a feature rather than a bug, as the improbable compensation is integral to the campaign's virality architecture.

## §6 Verdict Confirmation and the Broader Viral Job Campaign Phenomenon

The Snopes verdict of True is fully substantiated. McCormick & Company posted a genuine, compensated, temporary position for a Director of Taco Relations on July 13, 2021, with the terms confirmed through the official corporate website. The position was not satirical, not a sweepstakes, and not a clickable-ad misdirection; it was a real paid engagement that would have resulted in a real hiring decision following a genuine application process.

The residual epistemic caveat is that "real" in this context means real as a marketing initiative rather than real as a traditional corporate hire. The Director of Taco Relations is structurally more analogous to a brand ambassador or promotional spokesperson than to a traditional C-suite adjacent leadership role, despite the "Director" title. The title itself—which carries executive-level connotations in standard corporate taxonomy—is applied here to create ironic humor rather than to accurately describe organizational hierarchy. This is a common feature of viral job campaigns, where the combination of impressive-sounding titles and improbable job descriptions creates the dual novelty signal necessary for viral spread.

For readers evaluating similar claims about unusual job postings in the future, the key verification steps are: (1) does the job exist at the employer's official domain, not a third-party jobs aggregator or social media profile? (2) are the key terms—compensation, duration, application requirements—consistently stated across multiple official sources? (3) is the employer a genuine entity with a plausible strategic motive for the position? All three conditions are satisfied here, making the McCormick Director of Taco Relations one of the cleaner True verdicts in the viral-marketing-job-posting category.

Whether the enthusiastic reception to the posting reflects genuine enthusiasm for tacos, genuine enthusiasm for a $25,000-per-month part-time job, or some inseparable combination of both is left as an exercise for the behavioral economist.
