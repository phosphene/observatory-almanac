## §1 Claim Overview

The claim under review concerns a formal voluntary recall initiated by McCormick & Company, Inc. in July 2021, affecting four of its consumer spice and seasoning products—specifically, products in the Italian Seasoning line and one Frank's RedHot branded seasoning. The recall was issued due to possible contamination with *Salmonella* bacteria, detected during routine FDA testing of the affected products.

This is a public health recall claim with documented regulatory standing: it was initiated by the manufacturer, announced through official corporate investor relations channels, registered with the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA), and verified by multiple independent sources. Unlike many Snopes fact-checks involving contested interpretations or viral misinformation, this claim is a regulatory record that is either factually accurate or it is not. Snopes fact-checker Madison Dapcevich assessed this claim on July 28, 2021, and rated it **Recall**—a special Snopes rating category used for verified government or manufacturer recall notifications rather than the standard True/False/Mixture spectrum.

The four affected products are:
1. McCormick Perfect Pinch Italian Seasoning, 1.31 oz bottle
2. McCormick Perfect Pinch Italian Seasoning, 2.25 oz bottle
3. McCormick Culinary Italian Seasoning, 1.75 lb bottle
4. Frank's RedHot Buffalo Ranch Seasoning, 153g bottle

The recall covers products shipped between June 20 and July 21, 2021, to grocery retailers in 32 states and internationally to Bermuda and Canada. No illness had been reported at the time of the recall announcement, a fact McCormick noted explicitly in its announcement.

## §2 Evidence Review

**Official manufacturer announcement.** McCormick & Company, Inc. published a formal recall notice on July 26, 2021, through its investor relations communications infrastructure (ir.mccormick.com). This announcement satisfies the primary evidentiary requirement for a recall claim: it constitutes the manufacturer's own acknowledgment of the contamination risk, the specific products involved, and the corrective action being taken. The announcement was released to the public record through a wire service, making it independently verifiable.

**FDA registration.** The FDA published a corresponding voluntary recall notice on its official recalls, market withdrawals, and safety alerts portal. FDA registration of a voluntary recall represents a secondary official verification of the manufacturer's announcement and is the regulatory mechanism through which consumers, retailers, and distributors are formally notified of recall status. The FDA's posting is independently accessible and provides a cross-check against the manufacturer's own account.

**Scope of the recall.** The affected products were distributed to grocery retailers in Alabama, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Delaware, Florida, Georgia, Illinois, Indiana, Kansas, Kentucky, Louisiana, Massachusetts, Maryland, Maine, Michigan, Minnesota, Missouri, Mississippi, North Carolina, Nebraska, New Hampshire, New Jersey, New York, Ohio, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania, South Carolina, Tennessee, Texas, Virginia, and Wisconsin. International distribution reached Bermuda and Canada. This geographic scope—involving 32 US states and two international territories—reflects the scale of McCormick's national distribution infrastructure and the systemic nature of the contamination event, which was not isolated to a single retail channel or regional market.

**No reported illnesses.** McCormick explicitly stated in its recall notice that no illnesses had been reported in connection with the possible contamination at the time of announcement. This is an important contextual detail: the recall was precautionary rather than reactive, initiated on the basis of testing findings rather than illness reports. This distinction is relevant both to risk communication (consumers need not panic about already-consumed product if they haven't experienced symptoms within the relevant time frame) and to regulatory practice (proactive recalls based on testing findings represent best-practice behavior by manufacturers, not evidence of unusual danger).

**Consumer guidance.** McCormick instructed affected consumers not to return products to stores, but to dispose of the recalled product and its container, and to contact McCormick Consumer Affairs at 1-800-635-2867 for a replacement or full refund. This guidance reflects standard FDA-recommended recall response protocol for shelf-stable food products where return logistics do not serve consumer safety goals.

**Bacterial health data.** The CDC salmonella data cited in Dapcevich's article is primary public health source material: approximately 1.35 million salmonella infections occur annually in the United States; approximately 26,500 require hospitalization; and approximately 420 result in death. Symptom onset typically occurs six hours to six days after exposure to contaminated food, with illness typically lasting four to seven days. The large majority of Salmonella infections resolve without antibiotic treatment; however, severe cases, particularly in immunocompromised individuals, the elderly, and young children, can progress to life-threatening bacteremia and meningitis.

## §3 Epistemic Analysis

**Epistemic novelty.** The epistemically distinctive feature of this recall claim is its involvement of a product category—dried herbs and spices—that most consumers do not associate with microbial contamination risk. This perceptual mismatch between actual microbiological risk and consumer risk perception is itself a significant and underexamined public health communication problem.

The intuition that shelf-stable, low-moisture spice products cannot harbor viable *Salmonella* is understandable but incorrect. Research in food microbiology has established that *Salmonella* spp. are highly desiccation-tolerant: dried cells can maintain viability in low-water-activity substrates for extended periods—potentially years—particularly when protected by the fat and oil matrices present in many spice mixtures (Podolak et al., 2010, *Journal of Food Protection*). The thermal inactivation of *Salmonella* in low-water-activity environments also requires higher temperatures and longer exposure times than in high-moisture substrates (Gruzdev et al., 2011, *International Journal of Food Microbiology*), meaning that brief cooking of seasoned foods does not reliably eliminate viable bacterial cells in blended spice products.

This food science reality has significant implications for salmonellosis epidemiology that are poorly reflected in public perception. The FDA's initial detection of the contamination through routine testing—rather than through illness surveillance traceback—is itself evidence of the surveillance infrastructural improvement needed for spice-borne Salmonella: because illness attribution to specific spice products is difficult (spices are consumed in combination with multiple other foods, often cooked, and rarely considered as the illness vehicle), outbreak detection for this product category has historically lagged behind infection occurrence (Van Doren et al., 2013, *Food Microbiology*).

**The epistemics of voluntary recalls.** The fact that McCormick initiated this recall voluntarily—on the basis of FDA routine testing—rather than being compelled by an enforcement action or an illness cluster reported to the CDC presents an epistemically important signal about the regulatory relationship between the FDA and large food companies. Voluntary recalls allow companies to control the narrative and timing of their public safety communications to a greater degree than mandatory recalls, and they impose lower reputational costs than enforcement-driven removals. Research on the political economy of food safety regulation has found that voluntary recall frameworks, while effective in many contexts, may create systematic incentives for under-reporting of contamination events that do not appear destined to generate illness-based regulatory pressure (Coglianese et al., 2013, *Harvard Law Review*).

The absence of reported illnesses at recall time is consistent with the explanation that the recall preempted illness—but it is also consistent with the possibility that contamination levels in the affected products were below the threshold of clinical significance for most consumers, with infection risk concentrated in the subset of consumers who are immunocompromised, elderly, or very young. These populations bear disproportionate risk from Salmonella exposures that produce no illness in healthy adults.

**Information gap: spice contamination and consumer mental models.** The recall represents a case where epistemic work is needed not only to verify the claim (which is straightforward—the recall is real and documented) but to update consumer mental models about product categories they have never associated with contamination risk. A consumer who knows that this recall is real but does not update their general beliefs about spice product safety has gained only narrow factual knowledge. The broader educational significance of the recall—that dried spices can and do harbor viable Salmonella, that contamination can persist through the supply chain, and that routine FDA testing rather than illness clusters is the mechanism that catches this category of contamination—is absent from most media coverage of the event, including the Snopes fact-check.

## §4 Contextual Background

**McCormick & Company: scale and significance.** McCormick & Company, Inc. is the world's largest producer of spices, herbs, seasonings, and extracts, with annual revenues exceeding $5 billion and distribution across more than 150 countries. Its consumer-facing brand (McCormick) and professional foodservice brand (McCormick Culinary) are among the most recognized in the global spice market. The company's scale means that a recall affecting even a subset of its product portfolio can have significant national distribution implications, as the 32-state distribution scope of this recall demonstrates.

As a Fortune 500 company, McCormick operates under intense regulatory scrutiny from the FDA and is subject to both the Food Safety Modernization Act (FSMA) of 2011 and the older Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act requirements. The voluntary recall represents compliance behavior consistent with FSMA's preventive controls framework, which requires manufacturers to implement and monitor hazard analysis and preventive controls programs for their production facilities.

**Salmonella in spices: the broader context.** The 2021 McCormick recall was not an isolated event in the context of spice industry safety. The FDA has documented and investigated multiple Salmonella contamination events in the spice supply chain over the preceding decade. A comprehensive FDA study published in 2013 found that approximately 7% of domestic and imported spice samples tested positive for Salmonella contamination, representing a significantly higher contamination prevalence than most other food categories (Van Doren et al., 2013, *Food Microbiology*). The study's authors noted that spice-borne salmonellosis is likely substantially underreported because illness is rarely attributed to spice consumption in clinical settings.

The sources of Salmonella contamination in the spice supply chain are diverse and include: raw agricultural materials contaminated in the field through soil, water, wildlife, or worker contact; processing facility cross-contamination; packaging environment contamination; and transit and storage conditions that allow survival of contaminated cells. The global nature of spice supply chains—with raw materials sourced from multiple countries across Asia, South America, and Africa before processing and blending in the United States—means that contamination can enter at any of multiple upstream points before reaching the consumer.

**Frank's RedHot Buffalo Ranch: the secondary product.** One of the four recalled products—Frank's RedHot Buffalo Ranch Seasoning—warrants specific discussion because it represents a brand extension product that combines the Frank's RedHot hot sauce brand identity with a dry seasoning format, rather than a core McCormick branded product. Frank's RedHot is owned by McCormick & Company following McCormick's 2017 acquisition of a suite of French's and Frank's brand products from Reckitt Benckiser for approximately $4.2 billion. The inclusion of a Frank's RedHot product in the recall illustrates the operational integration of these acquired brands into McCormick's production and distribution infrastructure—and the consequent scope of McCormick's quality control obligation.

**The Conservation Reserve Program juxtaposition.** By coincidence of timing, this recall appeared in the same summer 2021 news cycle as the agricultural conspiracy theory claims addressed in the companion entry (government-farmers-destroy-crops). The simultaneous occurrence of a real food safety recall (verifiable, documented, involving major brand) and false food-destruction conspiracy claims (unverifiable, satirically sourced, lacking documentation) provides an illuminating contrast for thinking about how the food safety information environment functions. Legitimate food safety concerns, announced through formal regulatory channels, exist alongside and compete for public attention with entirely fabricated food safety panics propagated through social media. This dual-channel information environment creates navigation challenges for consumers who must distinguish between legitimate recalls and false alarms.

**Consumer response and product disposal.** McCormick's instruction to dispose of products rather than return them reflects a calculation about risk reduction efficiency: for a widespread distribution of many small packages of dried spice, the logistical friction of store returns significantly reduces the fraction of contaminated product that would be acted upon, while simple disposal of the specific date-code products achieves near-equivalent risk mitigation. Studies of consumer recall response behavior have found that compliance rates with detailed product-specific recalls (specific date codes, specific SKUs) are substantially lower than compliance with broad product category recalls, suggesting that the complexity of the recall identification conditions itself represents a public health communication challenge (Taylor, 2010, *Food Control*).

**The broader FSMA framework.** The FSMA's preventive controls requirements, implemented through the FSMA Final Rule on Current Good Manufacturing Practice issued in 2015, represent the most significant expansion of FDA authority over the domestic food supply since the original 1938 Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act. Under FSMA, food facilities are required to identify hazards in their operations, implement preventive controls to address those hazards, monitor those controls, take corrective actions when they fail, and verify that the controls are working as intended. The McCormick recall, which was triggered by FDA routine testing and responded to with a voluntary recall, is consistent with FSMA's intended regulatory mechanism. Whether the specific contamination event reflected a FSMA compliance failure at the production facility, a contamination event at a supplier, or a testing detection of chronic low-level contamination that had persisted below the detection threshold of routine facility monitoring are questions not addressed by the public record.

## §5 Verdict Assessment

The Snopes verdict of **Recall** is fully supported by primary documentation from McCormick & Company, Inc. and the FDA. This is a verified voluntary recall affecting four specific consumer products due to possible *Salmonella* contamination detected by FDA routine testing. The recall was announced July 26, 2021, covers products shipped June 20–July 21, 2021, to retailers in 32 US states plus Bermuda and Canada, and applies to lot-code-specific production runs of the affected SKUs. No illnesses were reported at the time of the recall.

This entry is assigned **EARC: E** (Established) based on complete and corroborated primary documentation. The recall is verified through both manufacturer and FDA official channels.

The safety-relevant distinctions for consumers are: (a) the recall is lot-code-specific, not a categorical recall of all McCormick Italian Seasoning products; (b) no illness had been reported at the time of announcement; (c) the relevant time window for Salmonella symptom onset is six hours to six days post-consumption; (d) consumers with questions or seeking replacement should contact McCormick Consumer Affairs at 1-800-635-2867.

The broader public health significance extends beyond the immediate recall: dried spice products represent a systematically underrecognized Salmonella contamination risk in consumer perception, reflecting a knowledge gap between food science understanding of desiccation-tolerant Salmonella survival and consumer mental models of shelf-stable product safety. Salmonella surveillance in spice products, while maintained by the FDA, generates insufficient public health communication to update the consumer risk perception model that treats dried herbs and spices as categorically safe from microbial hazard.
