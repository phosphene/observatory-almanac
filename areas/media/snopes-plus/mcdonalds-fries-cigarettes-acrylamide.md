# Do McDonald's French Fries Contain an 'Ingredient' Found in Cigarettes?

## 1. The Claim

Since at least August 2023, a warning circulated on Facebook in both copied-and-pasted text and image form claiming three distinct things about McDonald's french fries:

1. "One large McDonald's french fry from McDonald's has the same amount of Acrilane as a pack of cigarettes."
2. "Acrilane is the most carcinogenic ingredient in cigarettes."
3. "McDonald's french fries are harvested from potatoes that are sprayed with a pesticide that is so harmful to humans that the farmers must let it sit for four days before they can safely handle it."

The post concluded with the invitation: "Enjoy your fries!"

The framing of the claim is that McDonald's french fries pose a singular and dramatic carcinogenic risk comparable to heavy tobacco use, derived partly from cigarette-like chemical contamination and partly from pesticide residue in the raw potato supply. This framing positions McDonald's french fries not merely as an unhealthy food choice (which is a defensible, evidence-grounded consumer-health position) but as a product that is categorically comparable to a tobacco product in its cancer-causing chemical composition.

Snopes journalist Jordan Liles investigated the claim and published findings on December 18, 2023. McDonald's did not respond to multiple media relations inquiries about the specific allegations.

The Snopes treatment categorizes this as misleading rather than simply false: acrylamide (the likely intended substance) does form in french fries under high-heat cooking conditions, is documented in cigarette smoke, and is considered a probable human carcinogen by regulatory agencies. The rumor thus has a real chemical substrate. But it distorts that substrate with a misspelling, at least one invented "fact" (the quantitative equivalence claim), and a mischaracterization of what acrylamide is and how it relates to cigarettes.

## 2. What's Actually True

**Acrylamide is real; "acrilane" is not:** The chemical named in the rumor—"Acrilane," capitalized—does not correspond to any recognized substance in the scientific literature, food-safety databases, or chemical registries. A Google search for "acrilane" in quotation marks returns primarily results for street names, commercial solvents, and occasional use as a trade name for unrelated products. As at least one Facebook commenter noted in response to the viral post, "there is no such thing as 'acrilane.'" The likely intended substance is **acrylamide** (chemical formula C₃H₅NO; CAS number 79-06-1), a small organic molecule with well-documented formation pathways in heated food and a robust toxicology literature.

**What acrylamide actually is:** Acrylamide forms through a well-characterized non-enzymatic chemical pathway known as the Maillard reaction—specifically, through the reaction between the amino acid asparagine and reducing sugars (primarily glucose and fructose) at temperatures above approximately 120°C. Potatoes, which have high native starch content that hydrolyzes to glucose, and contain meaningful levels of free asparagine, are one of the major acrylamide-forming foods in the human diet when prepared by frying, roasting, or baking at high temperatures. Other commonly cited acrylamide-forming foods include coffee (the roasting process), bread and crackers (baking), and breakfast cereals (extrusion-heating). Acrylamide formation in food was first identified and publicly reported by Swedish researchers at the National Food Administration in 2002 (Tareke et al., 2002, *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*), a finding that prompted immediate regulatory concern and an extensive international research effort.

The FDA's public-facing guidance describes acrylamide formation as "a natural chemical reaction between sugars and asparagine, an amino acid, in plant-based foods" that occurs during common cooking methods. It is not an additive; it is not applied to food or potatoes; it is generated endogenously during heating. This distinguishes acrylamide from a "pesticide" or "ingredient" in any conventional sense.

**The cigarette connection, accurately stated:** Acrylamide is found in cigarette smoke. It is one of several hundred known chemical compounds generated by the combustion of tobacco; others in this category include acetaldehyde, formaldehyde, benzene, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, and nitrosamines. Acrylamide is definitively *not*, however, an "ingredient in cigarettes." It is a combustion byproduct—a compound that forms through pyrolytic chemistry when tobacco and its additives are burned, analogous to the way acrylamide forms in food through high-temperature Maillard chemistry. Describing acrylamide as "the most carcinogenic ingredient in cigarettes" is doubly wrong: it is not an ingredient, and cigarette smoke contains numerous other chemicals with stronger established carcinogenic profiles than acrylamide, including benzo[a]pyrene (a PAH classified as Group 1 carcinogen by the IARC) and tobacco-specific nitrosamines such as NNK and NNN (also Group 1) (IARC Working Group, 2012, *IARC Monographs on the Evaluation of Carcinogenic Risks to Humans*, vol. 100E).

Acrylamide is classified by the IARC as Group 2A: "probably carcinogenic to humans." This classification is based on sufficient evidence of carcinogenicity in animal models exposed to high doses and limited—specifically described as "insufficient"—epidemiological evidence of carcinogenicity in human food-consumption contexts. The FDA's own position is carefully calibrated: "Acrylamide has been shown to cause cancer in animals exposed to very high doses, and although there is no consistent epidemiological evidence on the effect of acrylamide from food consumption on cancer in humans, both the U.S. National Toxicology Program and the Joint Food and Agriculture Organization/World Health Organization Expert Committee on Food Additives consider acrylamide to be a human health concern." The European Food Safety Authority's 2015 risk assessment reached the analogous position: acrylamide "potentially increases the risk of developing cancer for consumers in all age groups," but "studies on human subjects have provided limited and inconsistent evidence of increased risk of developing cancer" (EFSA, 2015, *EFSA Journal*).

**The quantitative equivalence claim:** The rumor states that "one large McDonald's french fry"—evidently meaning a large order of fries—contains "the same amount of Acrilane as a pack of cigarettes." No authoritative public source supports this equivalence. The FDA tested acrylamide levels in various food products in its 2002–2004 surveys; fried potatoes were among the highest-acrylamide food items measured, with average levels in the range of hundreds of micrograms per kilogram of food (FDA, 2004, *Exploratory Data on Acrylamide in Food*). Cigarettes have been measured generating acrylamide in smoke at levels that vary substantially by cigarette type, temperature, and smoking behavior, typically in the range of single-digit to tens of micrograms per cigarette. Direct pack-for-order equivalence claims require specific numerical assumptions that are not found in the regulatory literature and would depend heavily on methodological choices (consumed weight of fries, cigarettes smoked in a "pack," acrylamide availability from food versus smoke inhalation). No study appears to support the specific equivalence asserted.

**The pesticide sub-claim:** Potatoes grown for major fast-food chains, including McDonald's, have historically been treated with chlorpropham (CIPC), a sprout inhibitor applied during storage to prevent premature sprouting of harvested potatoes while they are held in cold storage for processing. Chlorpropham is regulated as a pesticide in both the United States (EPA) and the European Union. It has documented human toxicity at high doses and is classified by the EPA as a possible human carcinogen (Group C). Re-entry intervals—the period that must elapse after pesticide application before workers can safely enter a treated area—are a standard feature of pesticide regulation; CIPC has stated re-entry intervals depending on application method and ventilation conditions, but characterizing these as "so harmful to humans that farmers must stay away for four days" significantly overstates the regulatory severity and conflates standard regulatory precautionary intervals with evidence of extraordinary acute danger. McDonald's has previously disclosed its use of CIPC in potato handling; the chemical is approved for use under existing EPA and EU regulatory frameworks when properly applied.

**The California Proposition 65 disclosure:** McDonald's restaurants in California are required under California's Proposition 65 (Safe Drinking Water and Toxic Enforcement Act of 1986) to post a notice that their fried potato products contain acrylamide, a chemical listed under Proposition 65 as potentially causing cancer. This regulatory requirement has been documented by social media users as evidence that acrylamide in McDonald's fries is a confirmed danger. Proposition 65 listings reflect a broad precautionary standard—chemicals are listed based on regulatory agency carcinogen classifications rather than demonstrated harm at consumption levels. The presence of a Prop 65 warning is an accurate disclosure of acrylamide's status on the regulatory list; it is not, by itself, evidence of cancer causation in humans at the doses consumed.

The CDC documentation cited in the Snopes investigation adds additional context: acrylamide exposure markers can be found in the blood of approximately 99.9% of the U.S. population—a figure reflecting the compound's ubiquity across dietary and environmental exposure routes, not specifically attributable to McDonald's fries.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The McDonald's-fries-as-cigarettes claim navigates several cognitive mechanisms that have been well studied in food health communication research.

**The carcinogen = cancer certainty fallacy:** Regulatory classifications of substances as carcinogens operate within a probability framework that most lay audiences do not have the training to interpret. IARC Group 2A ("probably carcinogenic to humans") means something specific: sufficient animal evidence, limited and inconsistent human epidemiological evidence. It does not mean "this substance causes cancer in humans at normal dietary exposure levels." The same Group 2A list that includes acrylamide in food includes red meat, working as a hairdresser, and drinking very hot beverages. The Group 1 list (definite human carcinogens) includes alcohol and processed meats at consumed doses. Lay audiences, encountering the word "carcinogen" without calibrating context, tend to apply binary logic: substance is carcinogenic; substance causes cancer; therefore food containing this substance causes cancer. This binary logic is epistemologically closer to pre-scientific folk toxicology than to the dose-response and probabilistic risk models that characterize modern toxicology (Paracelsus' aphorism "the dose makes the poison" remains foundational; Trevan, 1927, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, for early dose-response formalization).

**The cigarette comparison as rhetorical weapon:** Cigarettes occupy a specific cultural-symbolic register as the paradigm case of a consumer product that is definitively, catastrophically harmful to human health, with corporate concealment of that harm constituting a historical moral failure of major proportions. Comparing any food product to cigarettes is therefore an extraordinarily effective rhetorical move: it invokes the entire cultural memory of the tobacco litigation era, the surgeon general's warnings, the concealed research, and the corporate bad-faith discourse that surrounded tobacco exposure for decades. Even a technically inaccurate cigarette comparison activates these cultural associations. Research on analogy and risk communication finds that analogical framing with high-affect anchor cases (like cigarettes) disproportionately elevates perceived risk relative to the quantitative evidence (Böhm & Pfister, 2005, *Journal of Risk Research*).

**The epistemic novelty of verified-but-distorted fact clusters:** The acrylamide rumor represents a category of misinformation that is particularly resistant to simple correction because it contains multiple independently verifiable true components. Acrylamide is real. It forms in french fries. It forms in cigarette smoke. It is classified as a probable carcinogen. McDonald's operates in an atmosphere of legitimate consumer skepticism about its product ingredients. A California Proposition 65 warning genuinely appears in McDonald's California restaurants. Each of these individual true claims forms a frame within which the false claims (the quantitative equivalence, the "ingredient in cigarettes" characterization, the invented word "acrilane") can shelter. When a fact-checker demonstrates that "acrilane" doesn't exist, a skeptical reader can reasonably say: "Fine, but they clearly meant acrylamide, and that part is real." The debunking of the false elements does not automatically dismantle the inferential structure built on the true elements.

This phenomenon—the strategic shelter of false claims within clusters of true claims—is identified in cognitive science literature on belief perseverance: specific false components of a compound belief can be corrected without altering the overall evaluative conclusion drawn from the compound belief, because the true components remain as anchors (Ross, Lepper, & Hubbard, 1975, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*). Applied to the acrylamide case: even after a thorough debunking, the audience may reasonably retain an elevated concern about McDonald's fries and acrylamide, which is—in a carefully calibrated way—appropriate. The concern is legitimate; the specific quantifications are wrong.

**The regulatory communication gap:** The FDA's own position on acrylamide is deliberately hedged and calibrated to uncertainty. Regulatory agencies are institutionally structured to avoid overstatement; their communications are designed to be accurate to the state of the evidence, which includes genuine uncertainty. This institutional caution, however, reads to many audiences as corporate-political suppression of alarming truths. When an agency says "we consider this a concern; the evidence in humans is inconsistent," and a viral Facebook post says "this will give you cancer," the viral post is communicatively more powerful—even though the agency is being more scientifically honest. The scientific communication norms that produce hedged regulatory language create systematic vulnerabilities to more assertive but less accurate claims.

## 4. The Evidence in Full

The evidentiary components of the Snopes investigation and relevant supplementary literature:

1. **"Acrilane" does not exist** as a recognized chemical substance in any scientific, regulatory, or chemical registry database. The substance referred to is almost certainly acrylamide.

2. **Acrylamide forms in starchy foods at high heat** through the Maillard reaction (asparagine + reducing sugars + heat), a mechanism first reported in food context by Tareke et al. (2002, *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*).

3. **Acrylamide is a combustion byproduct in cigarette smoke**, not an ingredient. Its presence in cigarette smoke is among hundreds of known pyrolytic byproducts; calling it "the most carcinogenic ingredient in cigarettes" is both taxonomically and comparatively inaccurate relative to known Group 1 carcinogens in tobacco smoke.

4. **IARC classification is Group 2A** ("probably carcinogenic to humans"), based on animal evidence and insufficient human evidence (IARC, 2012).

5. **FDA and EFSA** both published position statements acknowledging acrylamide as a health concern while noting inconsistent human epidemiological evidence for cancer causation at dietary exposure levels.

6. **No authoritative source supports the pack-of-cigarettes quantitative equivalence** for a large order of McDonald's fries. The claim is unsupported and likely invented or extrapolated from undisclosed sources.

7. **Chlorpropham (CIPC)** is a real pesticide used in McDonald's potato supply chain for sprout inhibition in storage; it carries EPA re-entry intervals and is classified as a possible human carcinogen, but its use is regulated under standard agricultural frameworks. The "four days" framing overstates the severity.

8. **CDC data**: Acrylamide biomarkers are detectable in approximately 99.9% of the U.S. population, reflecting ubiquitous environmental and dietary exposure across multiple sources—not a McDonald's-specific exposure profile.

9. **Proposition 65 warnings** at California McDonald's locations are a real and accurately observed phenomenon reflecting California's broad chemical disclosure framework.

## 5. What Remains Uncertain

**Actual McDonald's-specific acrylamide levels:** While regulatory surveys have measured acrylamide in various commercial french fry products, published peer-reviewed measurements specific to McDonald's large-size fries at point of sale in recent years are not readily accessible in the open literature. Product acrylamide levels vary by frying temperature, oil type, potato variety, and holding time; whether McDonald's operations are optimized for acrylamide minimization versus other quality or efficiency parameters is not publicly documented.

**Acrylamide dietary risk at realistic consumption levels:** The scientific question of whether acrylamide exposure at realistic dietary levels (including frequent french fry consumption) meaningfully increases human cancer risk remains genuinely open. Multiple large prospective cohort studies have found no significant association; others have found associations for specific cancer subgroups (particularly endometrial and ovarian cancer in women) in some analyses but not others. The EFSA's 2015 review characterized the epidemiological picture as "limited and inconsistent." This is a legitimate area of ongoing scientific inquiry, not a settled question.

**Pesticide residue levels in McDonald's fries at point of consumption:** CIPC and its metabolites have been detected in processed potato products in regulatory monitoring studies; the levels at which they occur at point of consumption in a McDonald's order, and whether those levels approach any adverse-effect threshold, is not documented in the sources cited here.

**EARC classification:** Classified A (Assessed/Ambiguous). The claim is misleading—built on real chemical facts distorted by false characterizations, non-existent terminology, and unsupported quantitative claims. The underlying concern (acrylamide as a potential carcinogen in starchy foods) is legitimate; the specific claims are not.

## 6. Verdict and Further Reading

**Verdict: MISLEADING.** The claim that McDonald's french fries contain a cigarette "ingredient" called "Acrilane" equal in carcinogenic load to a pack of cigarettes is misleading on every specific point: "acrilane" is not a recognized substance; the presumably intended substance, acrylamide, is not an ingredient in cigarettes but a combustion byproduct found in both cigarette smoke and high-heat-cooked starchy foods; no authoritative source supports the quantitative equivalence between a large fry and a pack of cigarettes; and acrylamide, while a documented regulatory concern with clear animal carcinogenicity, has not been shown to cause cancer in humans at realistic dietary exposure levels in consistent epidemiological evidence. The underlying concern—that acrylamide forms in french fries under high-heat frying and is a documented concern of food-safety regulators—is accurate, making this a case of legitimate worry expressed through illegitimate distortion.
