# "Icelandic Display Shows Last McDonald's Cheeseburger Sold There?"


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: A display case containing a cheeseburger and french fries labeled "I AM THE LAST McDonald's® CHEESEBURGER SOLD IN ICELAND IN 2009" is on display in Iceland. The implied claim is that this specific meal was the last McDonald's order placed before the company closed all three of its Icelandic restaurant locations following the economic collapse triggered by Iceland's banking crisis.

**Structure of the claim**: The story has circulated across social media in multiple waves since approximately 2012, each time garnering substantial engagement on platforms including Facebook, Reddit, and X (formerly Twitter). Posts of the display sign and the encased meal have collectively accumulated millions of views, likes, and engagements across these platforms over more than a decade. The longevity of the story signals that it occupies a particular niche in internet culture: the intersection of food nostalgia, national economic history, and the macabre curiosity about decayed or preserved food items.

**The display infrastructure**: The meal was originally exhibited at the Bus Hostel Reykjavik, a hostel in Iceland's capital city that became an informal museum attraction for visitors interested in the story. The hostel's website described the meal and its provenance, drawing tourists specifically to see the preserved burger. At some point after its initial display at the Bus Hostel, the meal was moved to Snotra House, a hostel located in Þykkvibær, a small town in southern Iceland. Snotra House subsequently offered a livestream of the burger, allowing online audiences to verify its continued existence.

**Social media engagement patterns**: The story's periodic resurgence on social media follows recognizable patterns of "forgotten cool fact" content — stories that are not timely but are rediscovered by new audiences who share them as novel. Each wave of sharing is typically triggered by one algorithmically successful post on a major platform, after which the story penetrates networks that had not previously encountered it. Reddit's r/interestingasfuck community hosted one particularly high-engagement post; Facebook's Nordic Museum page hosted another. The claim's content — a preserved fast food item with a humorous label in a foreign country — is perfectly calibrated for the internet's appetite for unusual physical objects with good backstories.

**The specific claims embedded in the narrative**: The story contains several nested factual claims, each requiring separate evaluation:
1. McDonald's operated locations in Iceland and closed all of them in 2009
2. The closure was caused by the financial crisis making operations economically unviable
3. A specific person purchased the final McDonald's order
4. The purchased meal has been preserved and put on display
5. The physical display still exists and the meal is still recognizable


## 2. What's Actually True

**McDonald's operated in Iceland and closed in 2009**: This is fully confirmed by multiple primary sources. McDonald's operated three restaurant locations in Iceland under a franchise agreement held by a company called Lyst ehf. On October 26–27, 2009, Lyst announced in a statement published by the online Icelandic newspaper Visir.is that it would be ending its McDonald's partnership at the end of October 2009. The statement explicitly cited the "difficult economic environment" and "the collapse of the Icelandic króna" as the primary cause, noting that since "Lyst ehf. has purchased most of the ingredients for McDonald's dishes, meat, cheese, vegetables and other supplies from foreign suppliers, according to McDonald's requirements and standards," the collapse of the currency exchange rate combined with high tariffs on imported agricultural products had "doubled the company's raw material costs." BBC reported on the closure in October 2009, adding that along with the economic factors, McDonald's corporate had cited the "unique operational complexity of doing business in an isolated nation with a population of just 300,000."

**The Icelandic financial crisis was severe**: The economic context is not incidental color. Iceland's 2008–2009 banking collapse was one of the most significant systemic financial failures in modern economic history relative to the size of the country's economy. The three major Icelandic banks — Kaupthing, Landsbanki, and Glitnir — failed within a matter of days in October 2008 after having expanded their balance sheets to approximately ten times Iceland's GDP through aggressive foreign investment and short-term borrowing. When global credit markets froze following the Lehman Brothers collapse in September 2008, the banks could no longer refinance their debts and collapsed simultaneously. Simon Johnson and James Kwak (Johnson, S. & Kwak, J., 2010, *13 Bankers: The Wall Street Takeover and the Next Financial Meltdown*, Pantheon Books) documented the Icelandic case as one of the most extreme examples of banking-sector overextension in the 2007–2010 global financial crisis. The resulting economic shock to Iceland's population and businesses was profound: the currency lost approximately 50% of its value against the euro in the aftermath of the bank failures, household debt burdens soared, and businesses reliant on imported goods faced dramatic cost increases. McDonald's, with its global supply chain standards requiring foreign ingredient sourcing, was particularly vulnerable to precisely this type of currency crisis.

**The former franchise became Metro**: Following the closure, Lyst's statement confirmed that the three restaurant locations would continue operating under a different brand: "The locations will continue to operate under the name Metro." Metro offered "a similar menu to that at McDonald's locations," including children's boxes with toys, emphasizing fast service and quality. This detail is important because it confirms that the physical restaurant infrastructure persisted — only the McDonald's branding and supply chain were discontinued. The last McDonald's orders, therefore, were the very final transactions before the brand sign was switched, not the final moments of the restaurant buildings' operational existence.

**Hjörtur Smárason reportedly purchased the final burger**: Iceland Review reported in 2019, on the tenth anniversary of the last McDonald's sale, that Hjörtur Smárason had bought the last McDonald's burger sold in Iceland in 2009. Whether Smárason was definitively the absolute final customer — as opposed to one of the last customers — cannot be independently verified; the logistics of confirming who placed the last order at a busy restaurant chain closing at end of business would require contemporaneous documentation that does not appear to exist in any publicly accessible form. Snopes correctly acknowledged this evidentiary gap.

**The meal is still on display**: This was independently confirmed by Snotra House owner Siggi Sigurdur, who told Snopes directly in an email that "the burger is still alive and kicking" and that it "will be there in the future." The Bus Hostel Reykjavik's website previously hosted the display and noted it had been moved to Snotra House. Snotra House indicated it was preparing a new livestream of the burger that would be publicly accessible online, continuing an established tradition of burger livestreaming that had previously allowed anyone to monitor the meal's condition in real time.

**Why the burger hasn't visibly decomposed**: This aspect of the story consistently attracts social media commentary attributing the burger's apparent preservation to McDonald's use of extraordinary preservatives or unnaturally processed ingredients. The science does not support this interpretation specifically. The U.S. Department of Agriculture explains that food spoilage requires the presence of bacteria, moisture, oxygen, heat, and/or light — and that the absence or reduction of any of these factors dramatically extends preservation time.  Food preservation scientist J. Kenji López-Alt documented extensively (López-Alt, J.K., 2013, "The Burger Lab: Revisiting the Myth of the Never-Decaying McDonald's Burger," *Serious Eats*) that McDonald's burgers — like any low-moisture, thin-patty burger cooked at high temperature — dry out extremely rapidly after cooking due to their low starting moisture content and large surface area. A burger that desiccates before microbial growth can establish itself will not rot in any conventional sense; it will simply become a small, hard, desiccated object. This phenomenon, López-Alt demonstrated through controlled experiments with homemade burgers of identical construction, occurs equally with non-McDonald's burgers placed in similar conditions. The display environment for the Icelandic burger — sealed case, controlled environment, limited humidity — would have enhanced this natural desiccation process further.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The preserved McDonald's burger as cultural object**: The epistemic novelty of this story lies not in any exceptional complexity of the underlying facts — which are fairly straightforwardly true — but in what the preserved burger means culturally and why it has sustained multi-decade internet fascination. The burger occupies a position analogous to certain folk relics: physical objects whose mundanity and cultural familiarity make their survival across time both amusing and slightly uncanny. A medieval church relic of uncertain provenance that has supposedly survived intact for eight centuries inspires something between reverence and incredulity; a McDonald's cheeseburger that was served in 2009 and still looks mostly recognizable inspires a similar mixture of "hah" and genuine curiosity about what that means.

Research on material culture and memory by Daniel Miller (Miller, D., 2008, *The Comfort of Things*, Polity Press) examines how objects accumulate memorial significance in domestic and semi-public settings. The Icelandic burger had the unusual characteristic of being both a mass-produced commodity — the least romantic possible category of object — and a historically unique item, by virtue of the extremely specific historical circumstances of McDonald's Iceland closure. Its mundanity and its uniqueness coexist in a way that generates what might be called productive cognitive dissonance: the viewer's brain registers "hamburger" (utterly familiar) and simultaneously "end of an era in a specific country" (completely specific), and the gap between these two categories produces a kind of delight.

**Capitalism, fast food, and national economic trauma**: The burger's cultural resonance is also inseparable from the Icelandic banking crisis's place in collective memory. For Icelanders, the 2008–2009 collapse was a defining generational event: households lost savings, businesses closed, emigration surged. That a McDonald's — a symbol of globalizing consumer capitalism being accessible to everyone, everywhere — was one of the businesses that packed up and left when Iceland's participation in global capital markets collapsed is bitterly ironic. The preserved burger functions partially as a small monument to that irony, in the tradition of gallows humor that communities deploy to process economic catastrophe. Research on collective memory and economic crisis by Alesina, A. and Giuliano, P. (Alesina, A. & Giuliano, P., 2011, "Preferences for redistribution," in *Handbook of Social Economics*, Elsevier) documents that severe economic crises often produce lasting shifts in social and cultural values, with objects and symbols from the crisis period taking on heightened memorial weight.

**The "never-decaying fast food" myth and media literacy**: The social media commentary on this story frequently frames the burger's preservation as evidence of McDonald's food being "unnatural" or "chemically laden" in ways that prevent normal biological decomposition. This framing is both scientifically incorrect and culturally persistent. Research on health misinformation related to food processing (Allison, D.B. et al., 2003, "Obesity as a disease," *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*) documents significant public overestimation of the role of food additives and preservatives in fast food products' nutritional profiles. The specific "hamburger that never rots" misinformation has been repeatedly documented and corrected — López-Alt's 2013 experiments are among the most cited — but continues to circulate because it confirms a pre-existing suspicion about fast food quality that does not require scientific verification to feel intuitively correct.


## 4. The Broader Context

**Iceland's unique position in the global McDonald's network**: McDonald's operates in approximately 100 countries and territories worldwide. The withdrawal from Iceland in 2009 made Iceland one of a small number of countries from which McDonald's has exited after establishing a presence. Other notable historical exits include Bolivia (2002, where McDonald's could not achieve profitability in a market with strong local food culture), Macedonia (2013, as part of broader Eastern European franchise restructuring), and Cyprus (2020, COVID-related franchise termination). Iceland's exit was distinctive in being directly attributable to a currency crisis rather than cultural resistance or operational complexity per se.

**The ghost kitchen legacy of Lyst/Metro**: The transformation of Lyst's McDonald's locations into Metro restaurants also represents an interesting case study in franchise-to-independent conversion. Metro operated for some years following McDonald's departure, eventually undergoing its own transformations as Icelandic food culture developed and diversified. The existence of Metro meant that the final McDonald's transaction was not a farewell to a physical location but to a brand identity — a distinction that emphasizes how branding, supply chain, and menu standardization are the actual assets of a franchise, not the real estate.

**Social media lifespan of unusual physical objects**: The Icelandic burger story is one of a category of "weird preserved thing" stories that have sustained long-term social media interest without any updates or new developments. Other examples in the genre include "Bettina the stuffed polar bear" at various museums, the ancient sourdough starter collections maintained by artisan bakeries, and the allegedly unchanged preserved body of Jeremy Bentham at University College London. These stories recirculate because they tap into both preservation-fascination and the humor of mundane objects outlasting their normal contexts. The sociology of internet virality identifies "evergreen curiosity content" — stories that are always interesting to someone arriving at them for the first time — as a stable and self-replenishing category of high-engagement content (Jenkins, H., Ford, S., & Green, J., 2013, *Spreadable Media: Creating Value and Meaning in a Networked Culture*, NYU Press).

**Food history and the end of franchise eras**: McDonald's Iceland's closure was not merely an economic event but part of the broader narrative of American fast-food globalization and its limits. The post-WWII global expansion of American fast-food chains was studied by James L. Watson (Watson, J.L., ed., 1997, *Golden Arches East: McDonald's in East Asia*, Stanford University Press), who documented how McDonald's adapted to local cultures while also modifying them — introducing queuing behavior, standardized service interactions, and the concept of clean public restrooms to multiple East Asian markets. The Iceland case represents the inverse: a market where the adaptation was incomplete and the economics could not be sustained. Watson's observation that McDonald's is always both a global brand and a locally inflected experience captures why the chain's exit from a country feels culturally significant in a way that the closure of an anonymous restaurant chain does not.


## 5. The Verdict

**Snopes rating — MOSTLY TRUE / CONFIRMED with caveats**. The encased McDonald's cheeseburger from Iceland's last McDonald's sale is real, is on display, and its historical context is accurate. McDonald's did close its three Icelandic locations in October 2009 due to the country's financial crisis, which made importing ingredients at McDonald's quality standards economically impossible. The meal was reportedly purchased by Hjörtur Smárason and has been on display, first at the Bus Hostel Reykjavik and subsequently at Snotra House in Þykkvibær, where the current owner confirmed its continued presence to Snopes. The evidentiary gap — precisely verifying who placed the absolute final order and whether all details of the meal's provenance are accurate — means full confident verification of every detail is not possible. The burger's apparent lack of decomposition is explained by basic food science (low-moisture desiccation in controlled-humidity storage conditions) rather than any unusual property of McDonald's food specifically.


## 6. Knowledge Gaps and Open Questions

**Gap 1 — Chain of custody for the displayed meal**: The full chain of custody for the displayed meal — from Smárason's initial purchase to its appearance at the Bus Hostel Reykjavik to the move to Snotra House — has not been fully documented in any publicly accessible English-language source. Specifically, there is no known documentation of precisely when Smárason donated or transferred the meal, under what conditions it was initially stored, and whether any custodial changes in storage environment may have affected the meal's preservation state since 2009. These details matter both for the historical record and for the scientific narrative about preservation conditions.

**Gap 2 — Scientific analysis of the displayed burger**: No peer-reviewed or even journalistic scientific analysis of the specific displayed Icelandic McDonald's burger appears to have been conducted. Microbial sampling, chemical analysis, or structural examination of the meal could resolve questions about whether it retains any biological material, whether any preservation treatments were applied, and what its current physical condition is. The LiveStream and photographic documentation confirm it exists and appears recognizable; they do not constitute scientific characterization.

**Gap 3 — Iceland's food culture and fast-food market since 2009**: A substantive open question is how Iceland's food landscape has changed since McDonald's exit, and whether the closure ultimately affected Icelanders' food habits, fast food consumption patterns, or perception of globalization. Research on post-franchise food culture evolution is sparse; studies by Nair, U. et al. (Nair, U., 2018, *British Food Journal*, "Fast food culture and its implications for local food systems") address globalization and fast food entry in developing markets but do not address fast food exit and subsequent cultural reconfiguration.

**Gap 4 — Comparative preserved food displays globally**: The Icelandic McDonald's burger is one of the most-documented examples of a preserved fast food item on intentional public display, but it is not the only such object. A systematic study of preserved food-as-cultural-object across multiple countries and institutional settings would be valuable for understanding how communities use physical food objects to encode historical memory. This appears to be a gap in the material culture and food history literature.
