## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

On February 3, 2024, Reddit user u/Riflemen45 posted what appeared to be a photograph of a McDonald's menu board in Ukraine, showing an item that looked like a cheese sandwich made with two buns positioned with their flat sides facing outward—essentially a grilled-cheese-style sandwich assembled from standard hamburger bun components in an upside-down configuration. When translated from Ukrainian, the menu item read "Toast with cheese." Shortly after the Reddit post, the claim spread to X and TikTok, generating widespread discussion about what appeared to be an unexpectedly simple yet novel McDonald's menu item.

Snopes investigator Jordan Liles (2024) assigned this claim the rating **True**. The product—known as the McToast—was confirmed to be a real menu item at McDonald's Ukraine through the official McDonald's Ukraine website, which described the basic version as a "hot bun with two slices of cheddar cheese." Additionally, Liles (2024) confirmed McToast versions at McDonald's locations in Malta, Italy, France, and the Czech Republic, each with locally adapted variations including ham, bacon, fried mushrooms, and other additions.

This entry examines the details of the McDonald's McToast product, the broader pattern of market-specific McDonald's menu items, the relationship between McDonald's Ukraine and the dramatic disruption caused by Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 and subsequent restaurant closures and reopenings, the commercial logic behind simple cheese-based fast-food items in cost-sensitive markets, and the epistemics of why "surprising McDonald's menu item" claims generate persistent social media engagement.

## §2 The McToast: Product Details and Multi-Market Presence

The McToast in Ukraine is, in its basic form, an exceptionally simple product: a regular McDonald's bun, heated and pressed with two slices of cheddar cheese inside, with the bun halves positioned so that their flat (cut) sides face outward and the rounded (crust) sides face inward toward the cheese—which creates the visual impression described in the original post as "two upside-down buns." This apparent inversion is not merely aesthetic; it likely serves a functional purpose: the flat cut sides of the bun are denser and more structurally intact than the rounded tops, meaning that when used as the outer surface and toasted or pressed against a hot surface, they create a more uniform toasting and a better-bonded package that holds the cheese securely.

According to the official McDonald's Ukraine website (mcdonalds.com/ua), the standard McToast is described as "a hot bun with two slices of cheddar cheese." The simplicity of the product—no protein, no vegetable additions in the base version—positions it as an affordable, cheese-forward comfort food item rather than a full meal component. In the Ukrainian market context, this price-point simplicity is commercially significant; McDonald's Ukraine has historically operated in a market where average consumer purchasing power is substantially lower than in Western Europe, making low-cost hot snack items particularly competitive.

The McToast is not, in fact, exclusively a Ukrainian product. Liles (2024) documented identical or closely related products in several other McDonald's national markets:

- **Malta**: Offers the standard McToast (cheese), McToast with ham and cheese, and McToast with bacon and cheese as distinct menu items.
- **Italy**: Lists McToast products on its official menu (mcdonalds.it).
- **France**: Offers the "Croque McDo," a French localization of the same concept with direct callout to the classic French croque-monsieur sandwich.
- **Czech Republic**: Offers a McToast with fried mushrooms and cheese—the most distinctive regional variant.

The France variant is particularly notable as an example of double localization: France has a deeply entrenched culinary tradition of the croque-monsieur (a grilled ham-and-cheese sandwich made on white bread), and naming the McDonald's equivalent a "Croque McDo" applies the brand's standard prefix-with-"Mc" localization strategy while explicitly referencing a locally beloved food form. This is the same logic deployed with "Cool American" Doritos in European markets: rather than translating an American reference, the brand substitutes a locally resonant reference.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: McDonald's as a Localization Observatory and the Epistemics of "Secret Menu" Claims

The epistemic novelty in this case operates along two distinct axes: the McDonald's localization system as a real-world natural experiment in consumer preference across markets, and the specific social media dynamic that produces "secret McDonald's menu item" claims at high frequency.

McDonald's Corporation operates approximately 40,000 restaurants in more than 100 countries as of 2024 (McDonald's Corporation Annual Report, 2023). The company's global strategy combines core menu consistency (Big Mac, Chicken McNuggets, Filet-O-Fish, french fries) with deliberate local menu differentiation designed to capture culturally or economically specific consumer segments. The McToast is an example of market-responsive product design: in markets where consumers want a hot, inexpensive, cheese-based snack that is not a full hamburger, the McToast fills a segment gap that the standard full-protein burgers do not address.

Watson (2006), in *Golden Arches East: McDonald's in East Asia* (Stanford University Press), described McDonald's localization strategy as "glocalization"—a term originally coined by sociologist Roland Robertson—arguing that McDonald's does not simply export American food culture but actively adapts to local food practices, social rituals around eating, and price-sensitivity patterns. The McToast represents this process at the product-design level: it is not an American product being localized but a locally originated product that happens to exist within the McDonald's framework.

The epistemic gap this creates for American consumers is significant. The default American mental model of McDonald's is a menu largely fixed around burger-and-fries templates, with the possibility of regional variations (the McRib, regional chicken sandwiches, etc.) as exceptions. The discovery that McDonald's menus differ substantially across national boundaries—and that some national variants are genuinely novel food concepts rather than variations on American originals—violates this mental model and produces the kind of surprise that drives social media engagement.

This brings us to the "secret menu" framing mentioned by Liles (2024): one TikTok video claimed the McToast was available on a "secret menu" in the United States, implying that consumers could replicate it by ordering off-menu. This is an example of a specific misinformation pattern in fast-food culture: national or regional menu items that are genuinely available overseas are sometimes reframed as "secret" domestic options in order to generate engagement and to give viewers the sense that they are accessing insider information. The secret menu claim for the McToast was not supported by any evidence of U.S. availability; it appears to have been either a deliberate content strategy or a confusion between "not available here" and "secretly available everywhere if you know what to ask."

Ritzer (2015, *The McDonaldization of Society*, SAGE) discussed the tension between McDonald's global standardization drive and local market pressures, arguing that standardization is ultimately a tendency rather than a total system: local product variants represent permanent pressure against full homogenization, and the result is a global fast-food landscape that is simultaneously more standardized than a generation ago and more diverse than McDonald's original domestic template would suggest. The McToast is a minor but telling example of this dynamic: its existence is neither a brand anomaly nor a corporate experiment but a routine output of the localization machinery that McDonald's operates across its international footprint.

## §4 McDonald's Ukraine: Context of Operation Under War Conditions

The specific Ukrainian context of the viral McToast post carries additional weight given the circumstances of McDonald's operations in Ukraine following Russia's invasion on February 24, 2022. McDonald's Corporation made the decision in mid-2022 to temporarily close all its Ukrainian restaurants—which numbered approximately 109 locations at the time—in response to the safety and operational challenges of operating in an active war zone (Simonetti, Isabella, *The New York Times*, 19 Sept. 2022). After several months, beginning in September 2022, McDonald's undertook a phased reopening of its Ukrainian restaurants, starting with Kyiv locations and subsequently expanding to other cities as operational security assessments permitted.

The decision to reopen—and the fact that McDonald's sustained its commitment to the Ukrainian market throughout the conflict—became symbolically significant for some Ukrainian observers as a marker of corporate confidence in Ukraine's continued existence and future. Brands choosing to remain in Ukraine rather than fully exiting sent a message to both Ukrainian consumers and international audiences about risk assessments of Ukraine's long-term prospects. McDonald's reopening preceded or aligned with reopenings by several other international retail and food-service brands.

The McToast, as a low-cost cheese-based item on the Ukrainian McDonald's menu, exists within an economic context shaped by the ongoing war: household income disruption, supply chain constraints, and the prioritization of affordable calories over premium food experiences that characterizes wartime consumer behavior in affected populations. The commercial persistence of an item as simple as a cheese toasted bun suggests it meets a genuine demand function in that specific market context—a hot, inexpensive, readily available comfort food that does not require the protein integration of a full burger.

## §5 Fast-Food Localization and the Commercial Logic of Cheese-Forward Items

Cheese-based items without protein occupy an underappreciated category in international fast-food menus. In markets where protein components (beef, chicken) represent a significant portion of menu item cost and therefore price, a cheese-only warm item can provide margin flexibility that protein items cannot: cheese in bulk supply is significantly cheaper than meat per gram of edible weight across most markets, and processing requirements are minimal.

The croque-monsieur tradition in France and the cheese-on-toast tradition in the UK both demonstrate that cheese-forward hot sandwiches represent a legitimate culinary category with deep cultural roots in European contexts—one that has no direct American equivalent at the mass-market fast-food level. The McToast, in both its Ukrainian basic form and its various European variants, occupies this space: it is the McDonald's brand's entry into a product niche that exists in European food culture but does not translate directly to the American market, where the closest analogues (grilled cheese sandwiches) remain largely absent from major fast-food chains at national scale.

Anderson et al. (2011, *American Journal of Agricultural Economics*, Oxford University Press) analyzed cross-national fast-food menu composition across 25 McDonald's markets and found a consistent pattern: markets with lower average GDP per capita tended to have higher proportions of low-cost, carbohydrate- and cheese-forward menu items relative to protein-heavy premium items, while high-GDP markets showed the inverse. Ukraine, as a relatively lower-income market, follows this pattern precisely with the McToast as its representative low-cost cheese item.

## §6 Assessment, Epistemic Status, and Cultural Implications

The McToast available at McDonald's Ukraine is a real product, documented through the official McDonald's Ukraine corporate website and confirmed through consistent prior reportage. It is not a secret menu item in the United States; it is a market-specific product offered in Ukraine and several other European nations. Snopes' Liles (2024) correctly applied the "True" verdict with appropriate contextual framing.

The cultural implications are twofold. First, the McToast case illustrates the extent to which the standard American mental model of McDonald's as a near-universal, near-identical fast-food template underestimates the genuine diversity of McDonald's national menus. The degree to which McDonald's adapts to local markets—not just localizing individual items but constructing menus with fundamentally different item compositions—is consistently surprising to American consumers who encounter it, partly because American McDonald's menus have converged on a set of core offerings that American consumers encounter repeatedly across decades of experience with the brand.

Second, the viral response to the McToast post—which treated the product as simultaneously novel, slightly absurd (the "upside-down bun" framing), and vaguely charming—reflects a broader cultural dynamic in which simplicity in fast-food items, when discovered in a foreign context, becomes a form of culinary spectacle. The McToast is, objectively, a very simple product. In the United States, its simplicity might read as disappointing; in the social media framing of a Ukrainian McDonald's offering, it reads as appealingly rustic and unexpectedly direct. This context-dependence of food evaluation is a consistent finding in cross-cultural food psychology.

The EARC gap (C) here reflects source completeness: Snopes confirmed the claim for Ukraine and several European markets but did not provide a full accounting of all markets where the McToast or equivalent is available. McDonald's Corporation did not respond to Liles's inquiry requesting a comprehensive list by the time of publication. The full geographic distribution of the McToast—and whether it exists in any Latin American, Asian, or African McDonald's markets—remains incompletely documented.
