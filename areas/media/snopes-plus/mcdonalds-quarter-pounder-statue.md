## The Claim

A large marble or stone monument depicting a McDonald's Quarter Pounder with Cheese burger sits at a fast food restaurant location in Rapid City, South Dakota. Photos of this burger statue have been shared on social media — on X, Instagram, Reddit, and various Facebook pages — since at least 2020, with the claim alternately framed as a declaration of civic pride, a satirical commentary on American food culture, and a straightforward "look at this unusual thing that exists." In April 2025, a Reddit thread in r/Damnthatsinteresting collected new user interest and raised the question again: "This is an actual monument in the USA." Other Reddit users confirmed the South Dakota location, and new waves of shares brought the claim to Snopes' attention for formal investigation.

The question being asked is simple but the answer warrants more than a photograph: is the Quarter Pounder statue real, where did it come from, why does it exist, and what does it mean that a fast food burger has been honoured with a monumental sculptural treatment typically reserved for war heroes and historical figures?

The claim is true in all its relevant particulars. The statue is genuine, permanent, deliberately constructed, and bears an inscription in Latin.

## What's Actually True

The statue exists. It is a permanent outdoor sculptural installation at the McDonald's restaurant located at 3919 Cheyenne Blvd, Rapid City, South Dakota. The installation was unveiled on 26 February 2020 as part of McDonald's celebration of the approaching 50th anniversary of the Quarter Pounder's introduction to the menu.

The Quarter Pounder debuted on the McDonald's menu in 1971, making 2021 its 50th anniversary year. McDonald's announced a range of promotional activities around this milestone in February 2020. A company news release from 18 February 2020 stated: "On February 26, we will be honoring one lucky city that takes their fandom for the Quarter Pounder to new heights. To pay tribute, we'll be unveiling a larger-than-life monument of this iconic burger." The release described the physical scale of the installation: "the sesame seeds on the bun are more than 20 times the size you experience on the delicious Quarter Pounder."

Rapid City was selected as the host city for the statue. Josh Ullmark, the McDonald's district supervisor for the Rapid City area, told the Rapid City Journal that the city was chosen "because it had the biggest lot and because of its proximity to the interstate" — explaining why the specific restaurant location on Cheyenne Boulevard was chosen over other options in the area. Ullmark also noted a local context: Rapid City is already known for a collection of life-sized presidential statues installed at street intersections throughout the downtown area, a tourist attraction that has made the city distinctively associated with monumental public sculptural ambition. "It worked out perfect that way since we're known for the monuments anyway," Ullmark told the paper.

The statue was designed by Raymond Gibby, a sculptor based in Utah. The pedestal was created by Bott Monument of Riverton, Wyoming. Inscribed on the base or plinth of the monument is the Latin phrase "calidum et deliciose succosum" — translating to "hot and deliciously juicy" — a piece of dog-Latin marketing copy that functions as a kind of mock-classical dedication plaque, the kind of inscription found on ancient busts and memorial stones, here applied to a fast food hamburger.

Snopes journalist Joey Esposito confirmed the statue's existence through multiple verification paths: the Rapid City Journal's contemporaneous reporting from February 2020; a 360-degree street view image accessible through Google Maps at the Cheyenne Boulevard location, showing the statue in place; and Instagram location tagging, where separate location results exist both for the McDonald's restaurant and for "The Quarter Pounder with Cheese" as its own tagged location, with multiple users' photos visible for both. The statue is accessible to anyone visiting the restaurant and has accumulated its own social media presence as a destination.

## The Epistemic Novelty of the Verifiably Real

The epistemic challenge this entry addresses is the inverse of the standard fact-checking problem. Most of the claims Snopes investigates warrant scepticism because false or exaggerated claims circulate in the formats used by genuine information. The Quarter Pounder statue inverts this: a real, physical, verifiable installation is subject to ongoing disbelief precisely because it is strange enough to seem like the kind of thing people would fabricate.

This is what the entry's EARC classification identifies as the "verifiably real overlooked" category — claims about genuine reality that generate sustained public scepticism because the reality is surprising, amusing, or otherwise mismatched with audience expectations of the world. Understanding why audiences apply default scepticism to this kind of claim, and what the appropriate epistemic response to such claims should look like, is a meaningful exercise in media literacy precisely because the problem is the opposite of the usual one.

The Quarter Pounder statue's ongoing viral career — five years of periodic resurgence across social media, each wave prompting the question "wait, is this real?" — reflects a specific form of cognitive processing that philosophers of perception sometimes call *incredulous stare*: the tendency to reject claims whose truth would require revising confident background beliefs about the world, regardless of the evidence for those claims. Most people hold a background expectation that monumental sculptural forms are reserved for subjects of cultural, historical, or artistic significance. A fast food hamburger does not obviously qualify under any of these dimensions. The existence of a formal carved monument to a commercial food product therefore requires active belief-revision in a way that a false claim about an unusual animal or an exploding school supply room does not.

Kahneman (2011, *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Farrar, Straus and Giroux) describes the cognitive mechanism as a conflict between System 1 (fast, intuitive, heuristic-based) and System 2 (slow, deliberate, evidence-based) processing. The default System 1 response to "there is a marble statue of a McDonald's burger in South Dakota" is disbelief, because the claim pattern-matches to the category of Internet absurdist fabrication (cf. claims about bizarre art installations, AI-generated images labelled as real, satirical news items, and similar). Overriding this System 1 assessment requires deliberate System 2 engagement — looking up the restaurant's Google Street View, reading the Rapid City Journal, checking the McDonald's press release. These steps are individually trivial but collectively represent a significant friction cost that most casual social media users do not pay before deciding whether to share or dismiss a claim.

What the Quarter Pounder statue case adds to this analysis is the role of design intent in credibility judgement. The statue was deliberately built to be extraordinary and attention-getting — that is its promotional function. McDonald's wanted the installation to generate media coverage and social sharing, to become a destination, to be the specific kind of thing people photograph and share with captions like "wait, is this real?" The verifiable strangeness of the monument is a feature of its design, not a bug. The monument was engineered to produce the exact epistemic response it produces — scepticism followed by delighted verification — in its audience. This makes it a peculiar case in the fact-checking ecosystem: an object that generates its own disinformation environment as a designed side effect of its promotional function.

## The Quarter Pounder: History and Menu Architecture

The historical context for the monument is relevant to understanding what McDonald's was celebrating and why the 50th anniversary was considered statistically significant enough to warrant a permanent installation.

The Quarter Pounder was introduced to the McDonald's U.S. menu in 1971, developed by Al Bernardin, a franchise operator in Fremont, California. The product filled a gap in McDonald's menu architecture: the Big Mac, introduced in 1968, was the flagship premium burger, but it was a three-bun sandwich with two patties totalling roughly 3.2 ounces of beef. The Quarter Pounder was positioned differently — a single, larger patty (4 ounces before cooking, which gives the product its name as a quarter of a pound) on a conventional two-bun construction, marketed to adults who wanted a more substantial but less architecturally complex burger.

The Quarter Pounder with Cheese became culturally embedded in the United States to a degree that distinguishes it from most fast food menu items. It has appeared in literature — most famously in Quentin Tarantino's 1994 film *Pulp Fiction*, in which John Travolta's character Vincent Vega describes the European menu naming convention for the item (as "Royale with Cheese" in France, owing to the metric measurement system) in what became one of the film's most quoted exchanges. This cultural saturation elevated the Quarter Pounder beyond its commercial function into the register of cultural referent — the kind of thing whose name is recognisable to people who have never eaten one, whose commercial jingle persists in memory, whose specific formal properties (the circular quarter-pound patty, the two-part bun, the American cheese slice, the pickles, the onions) have achieved a kind of canonical status in American food culture.

The 50th anniversary was not merely a commercial occasion but a claim to cultural duration. 1971 to 2021 represents a half-century of continuous menu presence — an extraordinary achievement in an industry characterised by relentless menu turnover and product discontinuation. Marking this anniversary with a permanent monument acknowledges that the Quarter Pounder has achieved something beyond commercial success: it has become part of the stable cultural furniture of American life.

## Rapid City, Public Monuments, and the Question of What Gets Commemorated

The choice of Rapid City as a monument site is not arbitrary, and the local context enriches the meaning of the installation.

Rapid City, South Dakota (population approximately 80,000) is the second-largest city in the state and serves as the gateway to the Black Hills region, which includes Mount Rushmore, Crazy Horse Memorial, Custer State Park, and the Badlands National Park. The city's economy depends substantially on tourism. The existing presidential statue programme — life-sized bronze figures of each U.S. president installed at intersections in the downtown area — was designed specifically as a tourist attraction, giving visitors an unusual photographic destination that extends their time in the city.

Ullmark's explanation for why Rapid City was selected — its proximity to the interstate, the large restaurant lot, and the existing reputation for monumental sculpture — situates the Quarter Pounder statue within a deliberate strategy of tourism-oriented monument building. The statue is simultaneously a promotional installation for McDonald's and an additional destination for a city that markets itself on the improbable coexistence of massive presidential carvings and natural landmark proximity. In a city where 60-foot presidential faces have been carved into a granite mountain and where downtown sidewalks host 44 life-sized bronze presidents, a marble Quarter Pounder takes its place in an established visual ecology of monument-as-attraction.

This context raises a broader question that the statue's viral career implicitly poses: what is a monument for? The traditional answer — preserving memory of the historically significant, honouring the culturally worthy — is one on which this installation makes an ironic but not entirely dismissive comment. McDonald's would argue, and the 50-year sales record supports, that the Quarter Pounder has achieved genuine cultural durability. Its presence in *Pulp Fiction*, its global name recognition, its role as a reference point in discussions of American food culture — these are markers of cultural significance, even if that significance is commercial rather than civic in origin.

Brooks (2007, *Bobos in Paradise: The New Upper Class and How They Got There*, Simon & Schuster) has written about the blurring of commercial and cultural status signifiers in late 20th-century American life. The Quarter Pounder monument can be read as a late expression of this blurring: the language and form of civic commemoration — stone, pedestal, Latin inscription, monumental scale — applied to a commercial product, in a city that simultaneously hosts carvings of presidents and a monument to the Quarter Pounder. There is no obvious break in the logic; Rapid City has simply pushed the available logic to its conclusion.

## What the Evidence Shows About Viral Claims of the Real

The Quarter Pounder statue case is instructive for understanding what verification requires when the claim is that something unusual-but-real actually exists.

The verification path is shorter and less technically demanding than most disinformation investigations. The Rapid City Journal reported the statue's unveiling contemporaneously, with named sources, a specific date, and a specific address. The McDonald's corporate news release announcing the promotion is archived and verifiable. Google Maps' 360-degree imagery shows the statue in place at its recorded address. Instagram's location tagging provides user-generated corroboration from multiple independent sources visiting the physical location. The statue is a physical object at a specific and findable address in a real city, and anyone with access to a car can go look at it.

The relevant epistemological point is not that this verification is particularly demanding — it is not — but that even trivially verifiable claims of unusual reality generate sustained disbelief in online circulation. The April 2025 Reddit thread prompting this Snopes investigation was the latest in a succession of similar threads going back to 2020. Five years of intermittent online discussion, including multiple Reddit posts in the McDonald's, Worldbox, and Damnthatsinteresting subreddits, had not resolved the underlying question for new audiences encountering the claim for the first time.

This is a characteristic feature of internet rumour lifecycle: claims that are formally verifiable and have been publicly verified recirculate to new audiences who have not encountered the verification, producing each time a fresh credibility question that requires re-investigation. The investment in verification by any prior audience provides no epistemic benefit to new audiences who encounter the claim cold. Knowing this, the value of maintaining accessible fact-check records — the core function of Snopes and similar organisations — is specifically to provide a persistent, findable destination for audiences who have the inclination to check rather than requiring each questioner to reconstruct the investigative chain from scratch.

## Verdict and Epistemic Standing

The claim that a real statue of a McDonald's Quarter Pounder with Cheese exists in South Dakota is TRUE. The monument was unveiled on 26 February 2020 at the McDonald's on 3919 Cheyenne Blvd in Rapid City, as part of the company's celebration of the Quarter Pounder's approaching 50th anniversary. It was designed by Utah sculptor Raymond Gibby with a pedestal created by Bott Monument of Riverton, Wyoming. A Latin inscription — "calidum et deliciose succosum" ("hot and deliciously juicy") — is engraved on the pedestal. The statue is a permanent installation, visible in Google Maps' street view, documented in contemporaneous local press coverage, and recorded in McDonald's own corporate communications.

The statue is unusual but not impossible. Rapid City is a city that has built its tourism identity partly around unusual public monuments, the Quarter Pounder is a product of genuine and measurable 50-year cultural durability, and McDonald's is a company large enough to fund a bespoke sculptural installation as promotional activity. The combination of these factors produces an outcome that seems implausible to audiences without context but is straightforwardly explained by that context. The correct epistemic disposition toward the claim — warranted scepticism productive of quick verification, rather than dismissal — resolves to well-founded acceptance within approximately three minutes of directed searching.

The Quarter Pounder with Cheese sits in Rapid City, inscribed in Latin, in a state famous for faces carved into mountains. This is where we are.
