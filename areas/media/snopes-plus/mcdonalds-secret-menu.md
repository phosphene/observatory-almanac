## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In July 2015, media outlets including Eater and Complex Magazine reported widely on a Reddit AMA (Ask Me Anything) thread in which a user claiming to be a McDonald's manager in Scotland appeared to confirm the existence of a "secret menu" at McDonald's, including the long-rumored "McGangbang" sandwich. Headlines declared the secret menu "confirmed," and social media circulated the claim as settled fact. The underlying question—does McDonald's have an officially recognized secret menu including the McGangbang—was not new; the McGangbang had circulated as a fast-food folk artifact since at least 2006, and questions about its existence had appeared on Snopes forums as early as 2009.

Snopes investigator Kim LaCapria (2015) assigned this claim the rating **Mixture**: a blend of what is true and what is false. What is true: a self-described McDonald's manager on Reddit did make statements acknowledging that customers who describe off-menu sandwich configurations can, in principle, have those assembled from existing ingredients, and that individual employees at some locations might be familiar with the McGangbang by name. What is false: McDonald's has a corporate, officially recognized "secret menu." McDonald's itself explicitly denied the existence of a secret menu, both through a now-deleted statement on its website and through the McDonald's UK Twitter account on 21 July 2015. The Reddit commenter's own language undercut the "secret menu confirmed" interpretation: they noted that workers "might not know [secret menu items] by name" and characterized the items as "a random person's guide to burgers you could potentially 'hack' at McDonald's."

This entry examines the origins and social transmission of the McGangbang myth, the distinction between corporate secret menus and customer-initiated hack customizations, the media dynamic that produced overclaiming in the July 2015 coverage, and what this case reveals about how folk food knowledge circulates, stabilizes, and intersects with brand marketing.

## §2 The McGangbang: Origins, Structure, and Spread

The McGangbang is a documented example of what might be called **folk fast-food engineering**: a customer-invented sandwich combination assembled from multiple standard menu items, typically by purchasing two items and physically combining them into a single eating unit. In the canonical version—which achieved stable form by at least the mid-2000s—the customer orders both a McDouble (or double cheeseburger) and a McChicken from the dollar menu, then inserts the entire McChicken sandwich (bun and all, or chicken patty only, depending on variant preference) between the two beef patties of the McDouble. The result is a three-protein sandwich costing, in the original dollar-menu era, $2.00–$2.19 before tax.

The name itself—deliberately provocative and designed to confound counter staff when ordered aloud—was part of the appeal. A 2006 Urban Dictionary entry cited by LaCapria (2015) defined the McGangbang and noted its origin as Daytona Beach McDonald's restaurants, though this provenance claim is unverified and may be apocryphal. By March 2009, the sandwich had acquired enough cultural traction that food blogger Eatmedaily compiled a loose history of its progression "from web rumor to occasional real life," noting that ordering it by name at a drive-through had become a performative act documented on YouTube.

The structure of the claim belongs to a well-defined category of fast-food folk artifacts. Similar items include the "Mc10:35," available only during the transitional ten-minute window between McDonald's breakfast and lunch service, when Egg McMuffins and McDoubles could theoretically be combined—a sandwich that existed only for chronologically advantaged customers and did not require staff compliance. The temporal constraint made the Mc10:35 categorically different from the McGangbang: it was less a "secret" than an emergent opportunity.

Other chains have developed parallel folk artifacts: the "Animal Style" burger at In-N-Out Burger represents an interesting counter-case because In-N-Out actually does maintain an official unlisted menu, printed internally and acknowledged as existing. The In-N-Out "secret menu" case is what made consumers plausibly expect similar structures at other chains—but In-N-Out's model is distinctive precisely because the chain deliberately maintains and trains staff on the unlisted items, something that McDonald's does not do. Starbucks's various off-menu drinks similarly exist in a different institutional relationship with the chain than the McDonald's hack customizations, as Starbucks baristas are trained to fulfill any recipe any customer can articulate using available ingredients.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Difference Between Institutional Endorsement and Technical Feasibility

The central epistemic confusion in the July 2015 media coverage was a failure to distinguish between two entirely different claims: (a) "an item is technically producible at McDonald's using available ingredients" and (b) "McDonald's has an officially recognized, institutionally endorsed 'secret menu' that staff are trained to execute by name." The Reddit manager's comment, accurately read, supported only claim (a). The media coverage treated it as confirming claim (b).

This distinction is not merely semantic. Claim (a) is trivially true of any menu customization system with modular ingredients: a customer at Subway can theoretically order the sandwich components in any food-safe combination a staff member is willing to assemble, but this does not mean Subway has an "official secret menu." The relevant question is whether the chain's institutional structure—training, standardized ordering systems, point-of-sale infrastructure—encodes and supports the off-menu items as named entities with stable configurations. The answer for McDonald's is clearly no, and was explicitly confirmed so by corporate communications.

The epistemic novelty here lies in a form of **anonymous authority laundering** that the Reddit AMA format enables. The AMA participant was, by their own representation, a single manager at a single Scottish McDonald's location. Their statement said, in essence, "if you explain what you want and are willing to pay for all the ingredients, it's just another grill order that we can make up." This is a statement of general modular customization policy, not a confirmation of a corporate secret menu. Yet the statement was attributed—by Eater, Complex, and subsequent coverage—to "a McDonald's manager," stripping the locational single-case context and implicitly elevating an individual's statement to corporate-level authority.

Miller and colleagues (2012, *Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication*) documented that anonymous claims of expertise in online forums receive credibility ratings roughly 30–40 percent lower than attributed expert claims when readers are prompted to reflect on source quality—but receive ratings comparable to attributed claims under normal rapid-reading conditions, where source scrutiny is not prompted. The July 2015 coverage did not prompt scrutiny; it performed the laundering itself by converting "Reddit user claiming to be a manager" into the shorthand "a McDonald's manager confirms."

The broader epistemological observation is that corporate denial—even explicit, public corporate denial—receives less credibility weight in viral claim adjudication than positive confirmation from any individual source. McDonald's UK Twitter and the now-deleted McDonald's website statement both denied the secret menu; these denials were noted in the Snopes analysis but were not treated as authoritative by the outlets that amplified the claim. This asymmetry—where denials are discounted and confirmations (even anonymous, unverified ones) are credited—is a systematic feature of how rumors spread and resist correction.

Lewandowsky and colleagues (2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) examined the "continued influence effect" of misinformation: corrections reduce-but-do-not-eliminate the influence of false claims on subsequent reasoning, partly because corrections require people to actively suppress a belief they have already encoded, while the original claim merely required passive encoding. The McGangbang-confirmed story was structurally positioned to exploit this asymmetry: the confirming story was simple, shareable, and exciting; the correction (it's just a grill order, there's no official menu) was ambiguous, required comprehending a nuanced distinction, and was less emotionally engaging.

## §4 Corporate Secret Menus as a Brand Phenomenon

The concept of the "corporate secret menu" exists on a spectrum from entirely fictitious (McDonald's) to explicitly real (In-N-Out) with many intermediate cases. Understanding where McDonald's falls on this spectrum requires understanding what corporate interests a secret menu would and would not serve.

For a chain like In-N-Out, the secret menu serves identifiable brand functions: it cultivates insider loyalty, creates a reason for repeat visits to unlock new information, and functions as organic word-of-mouth marketing at zero cost to the company. It works because In-N-Out is a regional chain with a devoted following and limited national advertising budget, where the secret menu has become a cultural asset.

For McDonald's, the calculus is different. As the world's largest hamburger chain, with standardized operations across tens of thousands of locations and a supply chain optimized for menu-item uniformity and speed of execution, an officially recognized secret menu introduces operational complexity, training burden, and risk of inconsistent customer experience across locations. The standard McDonald's POS (point of sale) system is not designed to accommodate ad hoc combinations outside the preset button assignments; a "grill order" for a non-standard item requires staff knowledge and judgment rather than system-guided assembly.

This is why the manager's Reddit statement, properly understood, was accurate: any sufficiently patient and articulate customer can obtain the components needed to assemble a McGangbang, and a cooperative staff member will put it together on request—but this is true because of the modularity of McDonald's ingredient system, not because McDonald's has institutionally endorsed the item. The difference matters for the consumer experience, because the probability of successful execution varies by location, time of day, and individual staff knowledge in a way that an officially trained item would not.

## §5 The Social Life of Folk Food Artifacts

The McGangbang and items like it belong to a category of cultural objects with distinctive transmission properties. They circulate outside mainstream media channels (primarily word of mouth, Reddit, YouTube, and eventually mainstream media), derive part of their value from perceived exclusivity or in-group knowledge, involve an element of transgression (the provocative name, the circumvention of the standard ordering process), and generate social capital for those who perpetuate them.

Sutton (2010, in *Annual Review of Anthropology*) discusses the social functions of food as a medium of cultural transmission. Folk food knowledge—passed down, shared among communities, adapted to local conditions—serves identity functions beyond mere nutritional value. The McGangbang, despite its profoundly unserious appearance, fits this frame: knowing about it, being able to successfully order it, and initiating others into the knowledge all constitute small acts of social bonding and cultural transmission within a community of people who share the reference.

The geographic origin claim (Daytona Beach) is itself characteristic of this kind of folk artifact: disputed, difficult to verify, and ultimately less important than the artifact's present social function. Comparable provenance disputes attach to virtually every major foodstuff folk claim—the origin of the hot dog, the lobster roll, the origins of various cocktails—and scholars of food history (such as Smith, 2010, *Food and Drug Law Journal*) have documented how contested origins function as identity stakes rather than factual disagreements.

## §6 Research Gaps and Epistemic Assessment

The EARC gap assignment for this entry is **A (Archival)**: the factual record is reasonably complete, but documentation of how the claim spread and mutated through media channels is incompletely preserved. The Reddit AMA thread in question was deleted—the original poster's account no longer exists—meaning that the primary source relied upon by Eater, Complex, and Snopes is no longer directly verifiable. This is a characteristic vulnerability of web-native primary sources in fact-checking contexts: the evidence that generated a claim may not persist.

There is a genuine research gap in empirical documentation of how McDonald's grill-order customization is actually executed across locations. A systematic report on actual staff response rates to McGangbang orders—by name versus by ingredient description—would reveal whether the Reddit manager's characterization (staff may not know the name but will make the sandwich if explained) holds universally, in some regions only, or not at all. No published academic study appears to address this question directly.

The broader epistemological question—how media outlets came to treat a single anonymous Reddit comment as corporate confirmation—is better documented in the media criticism literature. Pariser (2011, *The Filter Bubble*, Penguin Press) and subsequent researchers have documented how shared-content incentive structures on social platforms systematically reward shareable surprise-and-vindication content over cautious, qualified reporting.

**Verdict reaffirmed: Mixture.** Some McDonald's employees at some locations may be able to assemble sandwich combinations known as the McGangbang or similar hack creations when a customer explains what they want and can pay for the individual components. McDonald's does not have an officially recognized "secret menu" in the institutional sense—corporate denials are explicit and the Reddit AMA comment, properly read, does not support that stronger claim. The Mixture verdict accurately captures the partial truth and partial falsity of the viral framing.
