## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In October 2009, a scanned document purporting to be an internal memo from "Robert Trugabe," identified as the Managing Director of the Frewville McDonald's location in South Australia, circulated widely via email and social media. The memo advised McDonald's staff to increase revenue by deliberately omitting items from drive-through customers' orders — specifically, inserting a policy of leaving out "one item out of every second or third order," with the specific targets identified as hot apple pies, fries, and individual burgers from larger orders. The memo calculated this practice would generate revenue of approximately AUD $2,118.00 per day.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2009) rated this claim **False**. The memo is a fabrication. "Robert Trugabe" is not and has never been a McDonald's Australia employee. The contents are entirely invented. The hoax was created by South Australian internet prankster David Thorne, who stated the effort "took me five minutes in Photoshop." The memo's creator made the satirical intent unmistakable to any attentive reader through the name itself: "Trugabe" is a phonetic near-anagram of Robert Mugabe, the then-president of Zimbabwe, and the signatures in published comparisons were strikingly similar.

This entry examines the hoax's construction, its remarkable real-world spreading velocity before debunking, the psychology of missing-order anxiety it exploited, the economics of whether the hoax's revenue premise was even coherent, and what the "McMemo" reveals about the conditions under which corporate conspiracy beliefs become broadly credible.

## §2 The Document: Construction and Satirical Tells

The McMemo document is relatively brief — the email reproduction quoted by Snopes constitutes about one hundred words of main body text — and its key proposition reads:

> "We need to discuss the drive through orders as well. If the girls leave one item out of every second or third order, this adds up to several thousand dollars per week revenue. On smaller orders if they leave out the hot apple pie or fires [sic] and larger orders just 1 burger from every third order this totals around $2,118.00 per day. We need to work out if there is a way of making this a procedure without making it documented."

Several features reveal the document as satire rather than genuine corporate communication, though these markers are more obvious in retrospect than they might have been to many initial readers.

**The name.** "Robert Trugabe" is a transparent play on Robert Mugabe, the authoritarian president of Zimbabwe from 1987 to 2017, during whose tenure the country experienced hyperinflation, political violence, seizures of white-owned farms, and a collapse of institutional governance. Naming a McDonald's director with a Mugabe soundalike is an obvious satirical signal to anyone aware of the reference — it positions McDonald's as analogous to a government run by an authoritarian kleptocrat. Snopes noted that the signatures produced in comparisons were also strikingly similar. This is the kind of joke that works on two levels: readers who get the reference laugh; readers who do not recognize the name treat the document as a real institutional memo. David Thorne, whose internet presence indicated a sophisticated satirical sensibility, clearly designed the name for exactly this bifurcated readership.

**The typos.** The memo includes the error "fires" for "fries" — likely deliberate, as a corporate managing director's memo would typically be proofread before distribution. Deliberate typos are a classic hoax document technique: they render the document superficially more "authentic" (real documents sometimes have errors) while also providing the creator with a get-out clause (the typos mark it as obviously not a real corporate communication).

**The implausibility of documentation.** The memo's most remarkable feature is its final sentence: "We need to work out if there is a way of making this a procedure without making it documented." This is the document memorializing a plan to avoid documentation — a self-undermining absurdity that the email's senders helpfully highlighted with a "I KNEW IT!!" annotation and a bold black circle, making the joke explicit. A real corporate conspiracy to suppress evidence would not be proposed in a widely-distributed written memo; this is the Fregoli delusion of organizational behavior.

Thorne confirmed authorship in a *news.com.au* interview (Ramadge, 2009), where he stated the creation took approximately five minutes in Photoshop. The simplicity of the creation is notable: the social conditions for viral spread were such that a five-minute Photoshop job could generate global media coverage within days.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Why the "Missing Item" Conspiracy Is Psychologically Irresistible

The McMemo is exceptional among food hoaxes in that it thrived on a belief predisposition that is not only widespread but rooted in genuine experience: essentially every person who has used a drive-through lane has, at some point, received an incomplete order. This near-universal experience primes the "they do it on purpose" hypothesis in a way that most conspiracy claims cannot match.

David Mikkelson (2009) captured this precisely in his narrative frame: the scenario involves a family member sent to pick up drive-through food for everyone; the food retriever returns; an item is missing; someone goes hungry. He noted this was common enough in his household that it had acquired a name: "Ericing an order," after his younger brother who "rarely managed to return from a fast food run with a complete order." The normalcy of the error experience, and the frustration attached to it, creates fertile ground for conspiratorial interpretation.

The psychological mechanism involved is what cognitive scientists have termed **proportionality bias** — the tendency to assume that large or impactful events must have correspondingly large or deliberate causes (McCauley & Jacques, 1979, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*). Drive-through order errors are minor but frequent; the hypothesis that they are deliberate policy rather than random error offers a satisfying explanation that attributes intentionality and monetary motive to what might otherwise be mere carelessness.

This priming effect is well-documented in the broader literature on conspiracy belief. Van Prooijen and van Dijk (2014, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*) found that ambiguous events are more likely to receive conspiratorial explanations when participants feel morally outraged or when the event has been emotionally processed as a personal affront. Missing food items — trivial as they are in absolute terms — carry an emotional charge: you paid for something and did not receive it. The economic injustice, however small, activates moral reasoning that is more easily satisfied by intentionality ("they stole from me deliberately") than by randomness ("someone made a mistake").

The McMemo's particular genius was that it provided **confirmatory scaffolding** for a pre-existing belief. People who forwarded it were not persuaded of something entirely new; they were given documentary "evidence" for something they already suspected. The email heading "I KNEW IT!!" that accompanied many forwarded versions captures this exactly — it performs the pleasure of vindication rather than the discomfort of revision.

Behavioral research on consumer experience at fast food restaurants has documented that drive-through order errors are real and non-trivial. QSR Magazine's annual Drive-Thru Study, conducted periodically from 1998 onward, has found drive-through order accuracy rates typically ranging from 85–93% across major chains, with error rates varying by chain and by order complexity. An error rate in this range means that a regular drive-through customer will receive an incorrect or incomplete order multiple times per year. This experience-based frequency is precisely what made the McMemo's premise feel credible: it was quantitatively consistent with personal experience, even though the explanation (deliberate policy) was entirely wrong.

## §4 The Revenue Claim: Why the Economics Don't Work

Even setting aside the question of document authenticity, the revenue mathematics in the memo deserves scrutiny — because the email's initial recirculation often included commentary noting that "the revenue figure is wildly incorrect."

The memo claimed that neglecting to include hot apple pies, fries, and individual burgers from orders at a frequency of one omission per second or third order would generate $2,118.00 per day. Let us examine this.

For a single McDonald's franchise location, daily drive-through volume varies enormously by location, time of day profile, and market. QSR Magazine's industry data suggests that top-performing fast food locations may process 1,000–2,000 drive-through transactions per day; median performers considerably fewer. A rough estimate for a suburban Australian McDonald's might be 500–800 drive-through transactions daily.

If a franchise charges approximately AUD $3–4 for a hot apple pie or fries, and approximately $6–8 for a single burger, and if one item is omitted from every second or third order, then:
- At 600 transactions/day with 1-in-3 omissions: 200 omissions
- If each omission saves approximately $4–6 (the cost of the item): revenue benefit of $800–$1,200/day

The memo's $2,118/day figure appears roughly 2× inflated relative to this estimate — which is consistent with the email respondent's characterization of it as "wildly incorrect." But the deeper problem is not arithmetic; it is strategic. A food item "saved" by not putting it in the bag is not "revenue." The customer paid for it (revenue was recognized at the point of sale). The only "saving" is the cost of the ingredient and a marginal reduction in labor. For a hot apple pie costing perhaps AUD $3, the actual ingredient cost is a fraction of that. The memo conflates revenue with ingredient cost savings in a way that no real financial analyst at a food service company would.

This arithmetical error is another marker of the memo's fake origins: the financial reasoning reflects a layperson's intuitive but incorrect model of food service economics rather than the analytical framework a managing director would actually use.

## §5 McDonald's Response and the Institutional Correction Architecture

McDonald's Australia's response was swift and unambiguous, and the speed and clarity of the corporate denial is itself informative about how large food service corporations have developed institutional infrastructure for rumor management.

McDonald's Australia issued a statement declaring the memo "a complete fabrication," confirmed that "Robert Trugabe" had never been an employee, and posted an alert on its Australian website offering "a customer update on fabricated letter." The organizational listing on the McDonald's Australia website identified the actual Managing Director at the time as Catriona Noble, not Robert Trugabe.

The corporation also identified the Frewville McDonald's location named in the memo as a real location in a suburb of Adelaide — another deliberate realism marker in Thorne's design, using a genuine location to add credibility — but disowned any connection between the location and the memo's contents.

Thorne's identification as the creator came quickly, via *news.com.au* journalist Andrew Ramadge (Ramadge, 2009). Thorne confirmed authorship publicly and with apparent amusement. His previous body of work — which included highly elaborate and much-circulated internet pranks — made him a recognizable source once the connection was drawn.

The speed of both the hoax's spread (days) and its debunking (also days, notably faster than many comparable hoaxes) is characteristic of the 2009 media environment. The hoax predates the social media algorithms that would later dramatically accelerate content circulation; it spread primarily via email forwarding. By 2009, fact-checking resources including Snopes were sufficiently well-indexed and trafficked that many recipients, receiving forwarded emails with scanned documents, reflexively searched for debunking before sharing or acting.

## §6 Verdict Analysis and Epistemic Gap Assessment

**EARC Classification: A (Attested False)**
The Snopes rating of **False** is unambiguous and well-supported. The document is a confirmed fabrication with an identified creator, explicit denial from the institution it purports to represent, and no evidentiary basis whatsoever. The satirical markers (the Mugabe name analogue, the self-undermining final sentence) were present from creation and remain visible to careful readers.

**Gap Classification: fabricated-document**
The epistemic gap in this case is between "fast food restaurants frequently make drive-through errors" (true) and "McDonald's has a deliberate company policy of omitting items to increase revenue" (false, with a specific false document identified as the source of the claim). The gap is maintained not by ambiguous evidence but by the document itself, which exploited a real experiential frustration to generate a false institutional explanation.

**Broader significance.** The McMemo case illustrates two distinct phenomena working in tandem. First, **belief readiness**: some conspiracy hypotheses are pre-loaded into consumer consciousness by genuine frequent experiences, making the bar for "confirmatory" fake evidence very low. Drive-through errors are so common that a document "confirming" their deliberateness resonates instantly. Second, **satirical ambiguity**: David Thorne's creation was designed to be readable as both satire (for those who get the Mugabe reference) and as credible documentation (for those who do not). This ambiguity is a deliberate rhetorical feature, not an accident, and it raises a genuine question about the ethics of satirical hoaxes that include enough realistic detail to function as credible documents for many recipients.

The broader lesson is that corporate conspiracy beliefs about food service are particularly durable because they are grounded in the genuinely adversarial dynamic of commercial transactions — customers reasonably expect to "get what they paid for," and any evidence that this expectation is systematically violated fits a comprehensible model of corporate behavior. The McMemo's genius was that it was the exact document that such a dynamic would theoretically produce, placed in exactly the right emotional context to be believed by people who had already, in a vague way, believed it.
