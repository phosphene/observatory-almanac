# Did TikTok Videos 'Expose' How McDonald's McRib is Cooked?

## 1. The Claim

In December 2020, the McRib sandwich returned to McDonald's nationwide—for the first time in eight years according to the Associated Press—and almost immediately TikTok began filling with videos supposedly "exposing" its preparation. The core claim was simple: TikTok videos showed a McDonald's employee cooking and storing the McRib menu item, and this constituted some kind of revelatory or alarming disclosure.

The scare-quote framing around "expose" is appropriate and does important work in parsing what was actually at stake. Nothing in the videos showed improper food handling, contamination, fraudulent ingredients, or any preparation practice outside normal fast-food standards. What the videos showed was this: frozen McRib patties—shaped, pre-formed, molded pork products—being removed from packaging and placed on a grill, and cooked patties being held in a warming unit before assembly into sandwiches. The "expose" framing derived almost entirely from the visual appearance of the frozen patties (which resemble a solid white brick with ridges, bearing little resemblance to the finished, sauced product shown in McDonald's marketing photography) and from the general fast-food kitchen "reveal" genre that TikTok had already established through prior employee videos.

The December 2020 videos were not the first time this kind of content existed. TikTok user @thatonedepressedginger posted on December 7, 2020, and a second video from the same account reached nearly 3 million views. Snopes journalist Jordan Liles downloaded and reviewed the videos in the days before they were removed from TikTok. Two additional videos from separate TikTok accounts showed the same McRib preparation. All were subsequently taken down. The pattern of employee videos about fast-food preparation appearing and then being removed—likely under pressure from the chains—was already well established in the context of TikTok's fast-food employee content ecosystem.

## 2. What's Actually True

Liles confirmed that the TikTok videos were genuine. They showed McDonald's McRib patties in an authentic McDonald's kitchen environment: large bags containing frozen patties labeled with McDonald's branding, the patties themselves resembling a solid, ribbed-form white or off-white frozen meat object, a standard kitchen grill of McDonald's specification, and a warming/holding apparatus in which cooked patties awaited sandwich assembly.

The McRib itself has a long and well-documented product history. It debuted in 1981, making it one of McDonald's older specialty items. Ken Hoffman, writing in a syndicated column reproduced in the *Florida Today* on February 13, 1998, described it as "4 ounces of slapped-together pork chunks molded in the shape of a miniature rack of ribs, smothered in tangy barbecue sauce and topped with slivered onions and pickles on a hoagie roll." This description, published more than two decades before the TikTok videos, provides context for what the videos actually showed: the "slapped-together pork chunks molded in the shape of a miniature rack of ribs" is the frozen patty that viewers found alarming. Its appearance was not a secret; it was a structural feature of a product whose whole premise involves reshaping pork shoulder meat (McRib's primary protein source is boneless pork) into a rib-rack form for aesthetic reasons.

The 2013 precursor event is significant because it demonstrates that the visual reveal of a frozen McRib patty had already achieved viral circulation roughly seven years before the TikTok videos. In November 2013, a photograph of a frozen McRib patty appeared on Reddit (posted by user DJDanaK), accompanied by the caption "My buddy works at McDonald's and sent me this." PennLive.com described the photograph's virality: "It looks like a solid white brick with ridges. But it's supposed to be a succulent pork patty that serves as the basis for one of McDonald's most popular items: the McRib sandwich." The photograph generated significant online commentary about the disparity between the frozen patty's industrial appearance and McDonald's marketing imagery. This suggests that the visual "shock" of the McRib patty is a recurring phenomenon, not a discovery—roughly a decade separates its first notable viral circulation and its TikTok iteration.

The TikTok videos about McRib preparation were themselves situated within a broader genre. Liles's investigation cited prior Snopes fact-checks of TikTok videos showing Taco Bell's refried bean preparation, Chick-fil-A's chicken nugget preparation, and Panera Bread's soup and macaroni and cheese preparation. Each of these had followed a similar trajectory: employee video shows standard preparation process, viewers respond as if seeing something shocking, fact-checkers confirm the video's authenticity while contextualizing that the preparation shown is normal.

## 3. Epistemic Novelty: The Appearance-Expectation Gap and the "Expose" Manufactured Frame

The McRib TikTok episode illustrates a highly specific epistemological phenomenon that deserves formal name and conceptualization: the **appearance-expectation gap exploit**. This is a persuasion frame in which the speaker presents ordinary, normal, and publicly documented information as if it were a scandalous revelation, relying on the audience's prior mis-expectation rather than any actual irregularity in the information presented.

In the McRib case, the "expose" frame worked because McDonald's marketing consistently presents the McRib in its finished, sauced, assembled form—never as a frozen industrial product. The gap between marketing imagery and kitchen reality is real and visually striking. But the gap does not reveal anything ethically, legally, or food-safety-problematically wrong. McDonald's McRib patties are manufactured pork products made from boneless pork shoulder (or similar cuts), shaped under industrial forming equipment, frozen, and shipped. This is normal manufacturing and supply-chain practice for a mass-market fast-food sandwich. Nothing in the videos suggested the product contained unlisted ingredients, violated food safety protocols, or differed from what McDonald's had disclosed in its ingredients list.

The appearance-expectation gap exploit is distinct from genuine whistle-blowing, in which an insider reveals actual wrongdoing, and from informational revelation, in which genuinely unknown information becomes known. It is closer to what might be called **manufactured disgust by prior ignorance**: viewers' negative reactions reflect the surprise of seeing behind a deliberately cultivated marketing image rather than evidence of any actual problem with the product.

This mechanism has been studied indirectly in the context of food neophobia and disgust psychology. Paul Rozin, who developed much of the foundational empirical work on human disgust responses (Rozin & Fallon, 1987, *Psychological Review*, 94(1), 23–41), documented that disgust responses to food are often triggered by contact with unfamiliar, industrial, or visually challenging preparation processes even when the resulting product is objectively safe. The McRib's frozen patty form—white, structured, lacking visual resemblance to conventional pork cuts—activates precisely these disgust triggers. The TikTok "expose" format amplifies this by framing the visual as revelatory, prompting viewers to interpret their visceral response as informational rather than psychological.

This framing has consequences for how fact-checks of this type should be evaluated. Liles correctly assesses the videos as "True"—they are genuine, they show what they claim to show—but the larger manufactured claim (that seeing this preparation constitutes an "expose" of something problematic) is not directly addressed by a True/False rating on the factual question of whether the videos are real. A complete epistemic assessment of the episode requires disaggregating the factual question (did these TikTok videos show McDonald's McRib being cooked? Yes.) from the implied normative claim (does seeing this preparation justify alarm or behavior change? This is much more contested).

## 4. Context: The McRib's Cultural Life and Limited-Time Availability Strategy

The McRib is one of the most documented examples of fast-food scarcity marketing, the practice of making a product available only periodically to sustain demand through anticipation and prevent the normalizing of access that leads to declining sales. The McRib has been introduced, withdrawn, and reintroduced numerous times since its 1981 debut. Each return generates media coverage, consumer anticipation, and a brief renewal of cultural visibility that a permanently available item would not achieve.

The scarcity strategy's effectiveness for the McRib has been discussed in business journalism, though the academic literature on fast-food limited-time offer (LTO) effectiveness provides the relevant empirical framework. Research on temporal scarcity effects in consumer choice has found that time-limited availability increases perceived value and purchase intent through urgency mechanisms, though the effect is moderated by product familiarity and brand attachment (Aggarwal, Jun, & Huh, 2011, *Journal of Consumer Research*, 37(5), 937–952). The McRib case is unusual because its scarcity cycle is multi-decade—consumers have grown up within multiple McRib return cycles and the anticipation structure is deeply socialized, making the product not merely scarce but **ritually scarce**: its return signals a cultural moment rather than merely a menu update.

The December 2020 McRib return was distinctive because the Associated Press reported it as a nationwide return for the first time in eight years. This scale of return amplified the cultural event significance and may have contributed to the intensity of TikTok employee documentation: an item whose nationwide availability was genuinely newsworthy was likely to generate more kitchen-camera attention than routine items. McDonald's advertising and marketing history with the McRib is extensive; the 1989 TV advertisement reviewed by Liles is one of many in a decades-long campaign.

The prior virality of the 2013 Reddit photograph suggests a recurring consumer need to reconcile marketing imagery with industrial food reality that is not unique to TikTok. The digital medium changes the velocity and reach of this reconciliation but not its fundamental structure: consumers who grew up seeing appealing marketing images are periodically confronted with the industrial origins of familiar products, and the emotional processing of this confrontation consistently generates high-engagement content.

## 5. The TikTok Fast-Food Employee Video Ecosystem

By December 2020, TikTok had already established a significant and culturally recognized genre of employee-generated fast-food kitchen content. These videos were not generated by investigative journalists or corporate whistleblowers but by ordinary workers who, for reasons ranging from boredom to pride in their work to deliberate sensation-seeking, filmed aspects of their work environment and shared them publicly.

The existence of this content ecosystem raises questions about first-amendment-adjacent rights, employment contracts and non-disclosure expectations, corporate power to suppress employee speech, and the relationship between consumer information rights and employer information control. McDonald's and other chains have historically sought to remove such videos, typically by citing employee policy violations (filming in food-service environments may violate health codes or internal policy, depending on jurisdiction and context). The rapid removal of the McRib TikTok videos—Liles was able to download and review them before removal—illustrates the effective response chain: employee posts, video gains traction, corporate notices, content is removed, but fact-checkers may have already captured the content.

The rapidity of this cycle creates asymmetric information access: news organizations and fact-checkers with active social media monitoring and download tools can verify and archive content before removal; ordinary consumers searching for the videos after they've been removed cannot. This forms a de facto public record that is accessible only to researchers and archivists rather than to the general public. The Wayback Machine and Internet Archive have partially addressed this gap for text-based content, but ephemeral video content on platforms that actively enforce removal is more challenging.

The question of what employees knew when they posted these videos is also relevant. @thatonedepressedginger appeared to know the content would generate attention—the caption and framing of the video were clearly designed to be compelling—but may not have anticipated the removal pressure that followed. The TikTok user from the Taco Bell refried bean episode cited by Liles in the same article had already left the company he filmed ("I haven't worked there in 6 months"), suggesting some employees post these videos with awareness that they carry employment risk.

## 6. Verdict, Gaps, and Ongoing Questions

Snopes rates the claim **True**. TikTok videos did depict McDonald's McRib being cooked and stored in a McDonald's kitchen. Journalist Jordan Liles downloaded, reviewed, and documented those videos. They were genuine. The McRib's preparation process as shown—frozen pork patties on a grill, cooked patties in a holding unit—is consistent with standard fast-food food safety protocols and with what has been publicly documented about the McRib product since its 1981 introduction and certainly since the 2013 Reddit photograph.

**EARC classification: E (Empirical)**. The claim is factual and verifiable through direct documentation of the videos themselves, prior documentation of the McRib product, and corroboration from McDonald's product history.

**Gap analysis:** The central epistemological gap remaining is the **meaningful distinction between "the videos existed and were authentic" (what the True rating establishes) and "the preparation shown reflects anything unusual, alarming, or ethically relevant" (what the manufactured "expose" frame implies but what the fact-check does not directly address)**. A fully adequate treatment of this episode would include at minimum a consumer food science contextualization of the McRib patty's composition, the food safety status of the holding temperature visible in the videos, and an explicit assessment of whether the preparation shown falls within or outside of FDA food safety guidelines for fast-food operations.

A secondary gap is the absence of documentation about the downstream effects of these viral kitchen videos on McDonald's McRib sales, either in December 2020 or over the lifetime of the scarcity cycle. Whether TikTok "expose" content for menu items leads to sales increases (curiosity-driven), sales decreases (disgust-driven), or no measurable effect is not established in the available literature for this specific intervention type, though adjacent research on food disgust and purchase intent suggests that the relationship is not linear and varies significantly by prior product familiarity and brand attachment.

A tertiary gap involves the complete chain of employee content: the two verified McRib videos and two corroborating videos were removed, but Liles's documentation (pre-removal downloads) is sufficient for verification. Whether other, less widely noticed employee videos of McRib preparation have circulated at other times, and what their content showed, is not documented.
