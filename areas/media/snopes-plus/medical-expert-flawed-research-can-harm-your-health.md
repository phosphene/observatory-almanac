# Medical Expert: Flawed Research Can Harm Your Health


## 1. The Claim

The Snopes article under examination is not a conventional fact-check of a specific false claim; it is a republication of an educational piece from The Conversation, authored by Lisa Bero (Professor of Health Policy and Clinical Pharmacy, University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus), focused on how readers — both professionals and general audiences — can identify medical research that should not be trusted. The article's orienting premise is itself a factual claim subject to empirical evaluation: that flawed and fraudulent medical research exists in sufficient quantities to constitute a practical public health risk, and that specific identifiable warning signs allow such research to be screened out before it influences medical decision-making.

The piece's headline claim — "Flawed Research Can Harm Your Health" — is not controversial among researchers in health sciences methodology. The more specific claims embedded in the article merit examination: (1) that a meaningful proportion of published clinical research contains flawed or fabricated data; (2) that peer review does not reliably detect fraudulent or deeply flawed studies; (3) that systematic reviews can provide a corrective filter by aggregating evidence and applying exclusion criteria; and (4) that specific warning signs — ethics approval status, clinical trial registration, plausibility of results, funding source independence — provide useful heuristics for identifying studies warranting extra scrutiny.

Bero's article appeared on Snopes in August 2022 as part of an occasional series presenting independent science commentary on topics relevant to public health literacy. The article draws directly on a study Bero and colleagues published in the *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology* in 2022, which developed a checklist of warning signs for unreliable medical research based on interviews with thirty international experts from twelve countries engaged in systematic review work. This grounding in primary research distinguishes the piece from opinion commentary and gives its claims specific empirical referents.

The wider claim — that scientific research, including peer-reviewed published research, is a more heterogeneous and reliability-variable enterprise than its institutional framing regularly implies — intersects with a large body of methodological literature on research bias, research misconduct, replication failures, selective reporting, and the systemic distortion introduced by publication and funding pressures. Bero's article represents a condensed introduction to this territory for general audiences.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Prevalence of Flawed Research**: Bero's article cites a study finding that 8% of patient trials in anesthesiology had fake data and 26% were critically flawed (Carlisle, 2021, *Anaesthesia*). This finding emerged from a systematic analysis by John Carlisle of 526 published randomized controlled trials in the anesthesiology literature, using statistical techniques to identify distributions of baseline characteristics that were implausibly smooth — a pattern inconsistent with genuine random sampling from patient populations and consistent with data fabrication or selective adjustment. The finding is consistent with a larger literature on research misconduct prevalence estimates, though prevalence estimates vary substantially across fields, study populations, and detection methods.

Broader estimates of research misconduct are genuinely difficult to establish because misconduct by definition involves concealment, and most unreliable research is difficult to distinguish from merely underpowered or poorly designed research without specialized investigation. Survey-based estimates of self-reported misconduct from researchers across disciplines suggest that outright data fabrication is relatively rare (less than 2% self-report) while questionable research practices (selective reporting, p-hacking, inappropriate data exclusion) are common (John et al., 2012, *Psychological Science*). The distinction between fraud and poor methodology is practically important: fraud requires intent and is categorically different from research that produces unreliable findings through genuine but unrecognized error or incentive-distorted design choices. Both produce unreliable findings that, if incorporated into clinical guidelines, can harm patients.

**Peer Review's Limitations**: The claim that peer review does not reliably detect fraudulent or deeply flawed studies is well-supported by empirical evidence. Peer review is designed to assess the logical coherence, methodological appropriateness, and contribution to a field of submitted manuscripts; it is not designed primarily as a fraud detection mechanism and typically operates without access to raw data. Smith's analysis of peer review's function and limitations established that reviewers do not systematically detect even egregious errors deliberately introduced into manuscripts submitted for review (Smith, 2006, *Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine*). This finding has been replicated in experimental studies across multiple fields and is now a standard acknowledgment in discussions of peer review reform.

The limitations of peer review as a quality guarantor are structural, not merely a reflection of inadequate reviewer diligence. Reviewers evaluate manuscripts against described methods, cited literature, and presented statistical analyses; they cannot access the laboratory notebooks, raw data, or experimental conditions that would allow genuine verification of the reported work. Pre-registration of studies, data sharing requirements, and registered reports — in which the hypothesis, methods, and analysis plan are evaluated before data are collected — are reform mechanisms designed to address these structural limitations, with evidence that they reduce the rates of positive findings in fields where they have been implemented, consistent with their intended effect of reducing selective reporting (Chambers et al., 2014, *Cortex*).

**Systematic Reviews as Evidence Filter**: The claim that systematic reviews provide a higher-quality evidence base by aggregating multiple studies and applying inclusion/exclusion criteria is foundational to evidence-based medicine as a movement. The Cochrane Collaboration, established in 1993, was built on the premise that systematic reviews of randomized controlled trials provide more reliable estimates of treatment effects than individual studies, because aggregation reduces noise and explicit methodological assessment removes low-quality evidence from the synthesis (Sackett et al., 1996, *BMJ*). This claim is empirically supported: systematic reviews based on rigorous inclusion criteria have repeatedly identified that the initial promising results of individual studies fail to replicate when aggregated, saving patients from interventions that individual published studies incorrectly suggested were beneficial.

However, systematic reviews are themselves subject to quality variation. Publication bias — the greater likelihood that positive results will be published than negative or null results — affects the literature available to be synthesized, meaning that even a perfect systematic review of the available published literature may produce biased estimates if the underlying literature is skewed toward positive findings through selective publication. Funnel plot analysis, Egger's test, and similar methods provide statistical tools for detecting publication bias, but their power is limited when the number of studies is small or when suppression is severe (Egger et al., 1997, *BMJ*). The systematic review does not fully solve the research reliability problem; it changes its structure.

**The Warning Signs Framework**: Bero and colleagues' 2022 study identified a set of warning signs derived from interviews with systematic review experts, published in the *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*. The key warning signs discussed in the article — ethics approval absence, lack of clinical trial registration, implausibly clean results, industry funding without transparency — are consistent with a broader literature on research integrity indicators. Clinical trial registration, required by the Declaration of Helsinki (amended in 2008 to add mandatory registration) and enforced as a condition of publication by the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, enables comparison of pre-registered outcomes with published outcomes, which reveals selective reporting when registered primary endpoints are changed or omitted in publications (Mathieu et al., 2009, *JAMA*). Studies without registration may predate mandatory requirements or may represent a circumvention of reporting norms.

Funding source independence is associated with outcome direction: industry-funded studies are substantially more likely to reach conclusions favorable to the sponsor's product than independently funded studies of the same interventions, across multiple therapeutic areas (Lundh et al., 2017, *BMJ* — a systematic review of the relationship between funding and findings). The mechanism is not necessarily outright fraud; it includes study design choices (comparators, patient populations, outcome selection), publication timing and emphasis, and access to research platforms that systematically advantage sponsor-preferred results.


## 3. Why People Believe Flawed Research — Or Don't Know to Question It

The epistemic situation of a clinical patient or health-interested lay reader attempting to evaluate medical research claims is structurally challenging in ways that the DHMO and diamond-battery cases, discussed elsewhere in this series, illuminate from complementary angles. Unlike those cases, where a single falsifiable claim can be evaluated in isolation, medical research reliability involves meta-epistemics: the challenge is not whether a specific claim is true but whether the process by which the claim was generated is trustworthy, and whether the communication of that process to the public accurately represents its confidence level.

**Institutional Trust Transfer**: The dominant heuristic applied by lay audiences to medical research claims is institutional trust transfer: if the source of the claim is a prestigious university, a major journal, or a recognized medical authority, it is assumed to be more reliable than if it comes from an unknown or disreputable source. This heuristic is not irrational — institutional credentialing does correlate with average research quality — but it systematically underestimates the within-institutional variance in research reliability. High-impact journals in medicine, including *JAMA*, *The Lancet*, and *The New England Journal of Medicine*, have published research that was subsequently retracted for fraud or that failed to replicate, in some cases with significant clinical consequences.

The Vioxx (rofecoxib) case represents one of the most consequential examples. Research published in high-prestige journals and conducted by a major pharmaceutical company supported the cardiovascular safety of Vioxx; the drug was widely prescribed and estimated to have caused tens of thousands of excess cardiovascular deaths before its market withdrawal in 2004. Subsequent analysis revealed selective reporting of cardiovascular event data in clinical trial publications (Curfman et al., 2005, *New England Journal of Medicine*). The institutional prestige of the publications and the corporate infrastructure behind the research did not protect patients; it may have amplified harm by providing false reassurance of reliability.

**The Positive Results Bias in Public Science Communication**: A structural feature of how science is communicated to the public exacerbates the reliability problem. Science journalism predominantly covers positive results — new treatments that work, new associations confirmed, new mechanisms discovered — because negative results (treatments that don't work, associations not confirmed, mechanisms disproven) are typically less newsworthy in the commercial sense. This asymmetric coverage means that the general public is systematically exposed to a non-representative sample of scientific findings: those that were published (which are already skewed toward positive results by publication bias) and among those, those that were newsworthy (which are further skewed toward positive, unexpected, or dramatic results).

The cumulative effect of this filter is described in Ioannidis's influential 2005 analysis "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False" (*PLOS Medicine*): under conditions of low prior probability, modest statistical power, and common analytical flexibility, the majority of statistically significant findings in fields with small average effect sizes will be false positives. Ioannidis's analysis is mathematical rather than empirical and has been contested on its assumptions; but its directional conclusion — that the rate of false positives in the published medical literature is substantially higher than the nominal p-value threshold implies — is consistent with subsequent replication data across multiple fields.

**Epistemic Novelty — The Accountability Gap Between Evidence Generation and Clinical Guidance**: The most significant epistemic gap in public understanding of medical research reliability concerns the translation pathway from individual study findings to clinical guidelines and treatment recommendations. An individual patient or clinician who encounters a research finding in media coverage or even in a primary journal article is observing one data point in an evidence stream that requires methodological synthesis to be interpretable. The claim that a treatment "works" based on a single study, or that a risk association "exists" based on one epidemiological finding, is almost always an overstatement of the individual study's epistemic contribution.

Clinical guidelines — the documents that translate evidence into specific clinical recommendations — are explicitly designed to address this by grading evidence quality (distinguishing randomized controlled trial evidence from observational study evidence, and accounting for consistency across studies) and incorporating this grading into the strength of recommendations. The GRADE framework (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations), developed by a working group beginning in 2000, has become the dominant international standard for evidence-to-recommendation translation in clinical medicine (Guyatt et al., 2008, *BMJ*). Under GRADE, a recommendation can only be "strong" when the underlying evidence is "high quality" — meaning based on multiple consistent, well-designed studies with minimal bias risk.

The accountability gap is this: clinical guidelines exist and are of increasing methodological rigor, but the public communication of medical science rarely invokes their conclusions or their evidence grading. Media coverage of a new study typically describes the study as if its finding were a clinical recommendation, when in fact a single study, however well-conducted, rarely supports a specific treatment recommendation without corroboration. The patient or reader has no structural way to know from news coverage whether a reported finding has equivalent status to the foundational evidence behind aspirin prophylaxis (thousands of studies across decades) or to a preliminary phase 2 trial whose translation to clinical practice is years and several confirmatory studies away.

Bero's framework for identifying warning signs in individual studies is a partial response to this gap — it helps users identify clearly problematic studies — but does not fully address the meta-level problem of understanding where an individual study sits in the evidence hierarchy. What it does contribute is a set of practical process-based signals that are accessible without disciplinary training: does the study have ethical oversight? Was it pre-registered? Do the results look too good to reflect real experimental variation? These questions are answerable without statistical sophistication and help users identify the most clearly problematic segment of the published research space.


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: Not rated (informational/news article)**

The Snopes article is a republication of a commentary from The Conversation and is categorized as a "News" piece rather than a traditional fact-check, making a single verdict rating inapplicable in the usual sense. The central claim of the article — that flawed and fraudulent medical research can harm patients, and that specific warning signs can help identify it — is empirically well-supported and consistent with a large primary literature on research integrity, publication bias, and evidence-based medicine methodology.

**Gap Category: Research Integrity**

The piece addresses a research-integrity gap: the systematic risk to public health from the incorporation of flawed evidence into clinical practice, and the tools available to both specialist reviewers and educated lay readers for identifying the most clearly problematic studies.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Lisa Bero is one of the field's most productive researchers in research integrity and research bias. Her work spans tobacco industry influence on research (a subject where her analyses revealed systematic distortion of published evidence by industry-funded research programs), pharmaceutical industry funding effects, systematic review methodology, and the detection of data anomalies indicative of research misconduct. Her institutional position at the University of Colorado Cochrane Center places her at the intersection of evidence synthesis and research quality assessment.

The broader context of research integrity in medicine has become more publicly prominent since approximately 2010, driven by several factors: high-profile retractions in major journals (particularly the fraudulent Wakefield paper claiming autism-MMR vaccine links, retracted by *The Lancet* in 2010 after investigation confirmed data fabrication); the replication crisis identified across psychology and biomedical fields; the growth of retraction tracking infrastructure (Retraction Watch, launched 2010); and increased awareness of publication bias through systematic review meta-analyses. These developments have substantially increased the sophistication of mainstream science journalism's treatment of research reliability, though the progress is uneven and the structural incentives that produce unreliable research remain largely unchanged.

The publication of Bero and colleagues' 2022 screening tool in the *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology* represents one contribution to a broader infrastructure of quality assessment tools that includes the Cochrane Risk of Bias tools, the CONSORT reporting standards for clinical trials, the PRISMA standards for systematic reviews, and GRADE's evidence quality assessment framework. These tools are designed for use by methodologists and systematic reviewers, but educational adaptations — including the The Conversation piece Snopes republished — represent efforts to make the underlying insights accessible to broader audiences.

Industry influence on medical research remains a particularly significant concern. Systematic reviews of treatment comparisons across therapeutic areas consistently show a "sponsorship effect": industry-funded studies are two to four times more likely to produce findings favorable to the sponsor's product than independently funded studies of identical comparisons (Lundh et al., 2017, *BMJ*). This effect is not explained by differences in study size or design quality alone; it reflects a combination of selection bias (industry funds studies more likely to succeed based on prior data), design bias (comparators, doses, and patient populations that favor the test product), and publication bias (suppression of unfavorable studies). The mechanism has been directly documented in released internal industry documents in the tobacco, pharmaceutical, and food industries (Kearns et al., 2016, *JAMA Internal Medicine* — on sugar industry funding of nutrition research).

The practical implication for patients is that treatment recommendations based primarily on industry-funded research, without independent replication, carry elevated uncertainty even when published in high-prestige journals. The expansion of independent clinical trial infrastructure — through public funding bodies like the National Institutes of Health and the UK's National Institute for Health Research — provides a counterweight, but adequately powered independent studies of established (and therefore not patent-valuable) treatments are chronically underfunded, creating a systematic gap in publicly available evidence for the most common clinical decisions.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The handling of this particular Snopes article is distinctive because the piece is not a fact-check but a republication of science commentary. Snopes publishes a curated selection of science and health content from The Conversation and similar outlets as part of its science literacy mission. The decision to route readers to this content carries an implicit editorial endorsement of the piece's claims as accurate and its framing as responsible. For pieces making empirical claims about medical research reliability, this editorial review function is itself informative.

The meta-question of how fact-checkers verify science journalism pieces that themselves address verification — that is, how to fact-check an article about fact-checking research quality — requires the same tools the article describes: checking whether the cited studies exist and say what the article claims they say, whether the empirical findings are accurately characterized, and whether alternative perspectives on contested claims are adequately represented. For Bero's piece, the core empirical claims (prevalence estimates, specific warning signs, characteristics of research fraud) are consistent with the primary literature and with the published study directly cited.

The article does not claim that medical research is generally fraudulent or unreliable; it makes the more precise claim that flawed research exists in sufficient quantity to cause patient harm and that practical warning signs are available to identify it. This is a calibrated, evidence-consistent position that avoids both the naive view that peer-reviewed publication guarantees reliability and the nihilistic view that medical research cannot be trusted at all. For audiences encountering medical claims in everyday health decision-making, the framework it provides — ask whether the study had ethics approval, trial registration, plausible results, and independent funding — offers a practical starting point for appropriate epistemic calibration.
