# Medical Expert: Flawed Research Can Harm Your Health


## 1. The Claim

Unlike the majority of Snopes entries, this item is not a fact-check of a specific circulating claim. It is an academic commentary article, authored by Lisa Bero (Research Professor in Public Health and Medicine, University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus) and originally published by The Conversation, that was republished on Snopes in August 2022 because its topic—how to identify flawed and fabricated medical research—was considered directly relevant to Snopes' readers.

The article's central thesis constitutes the claim being examined here: **that flawed or fraudulent medical research is a real problem in the published scientific literature, that systematic reviews provide the best available filter against such research, that early identification tools for suspect studies can help reviewers and the public evaluate research quality, and that critical questions about study design, funding, transparency, and plausibility can help detect unreliable findings before they inform clinical decisions or public health guidance.**

This represents a second-order claim about how to evaluate first-order claims—a form of research about how to do research. Its relevance to a Truth Vault entry lies in its foundational character: any serious engagement with empirical claims, whether in fact-checking, science communication, or personal health decision-making, rests on assumptions about how research quality can be assessed and how systematic evidence can be distinguished from unreliable evidence. The Bero article examines those assumptions explicitly.

The context is the rapid proliferation of COVID-19 research from 2020 onward, during which the peer review system was visibly stressed by the volume and pace of submission, producing a documented series of high-profile retractions and corrections that made research credibility a front-page concern rather than a specialist methodological debate.


## 2. What's Actually True

Bero's article summarizes findings from an original research study published in the *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology* (Bero, L., et al., 2022, *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*, 147), which conducted structured interviews with 30 international experts from 12 countries who specialize in systematic review and research integrity. The study developed a practical screening tool for identifying suspect studies—those that may be fabricated, substantially flawed, or manipulated—based on patterns identified by expert reviewers across multiple disciplines.

The empirical foundation of the article's concern about fraudulent and flawed research is documented. One study of 526 patient trials in anesthesiology found that 8% contained fake data and 26% were critically flawed (Carlisle, J.B., 2017, *Anaesthesia*, 72(7)). While this proportion may not generalize to all medical subdisciplines, it establishes that the problem is not marginal. The mechanism by which such studies enter the peer-reviewed literature is well understood: peer review is designed to evaluate scientific plausibility, methodological clarity, and logical coherence, not to detect sophisticated data fabrication. A study whose fraud is executed at the level of data generation rather than logical structure can pass peer review intact.

The characteristics that the Bero study identifies as warning signs for suspect research are: missing information about ethical approval, missing institutional affiliation details, missing or vague descriptions of study site and population, data that seems "too good" (e.g., patient enrollment numbers exceeding entire national disease prevalence), implausibly consistent results across replications, funding from sources with direct financial interests in outcomes, and absence of clinical trial registration. None of these characteristics individually prove fraud, but their presence warrants additional scrutiny.

The article's examples from COVID-19 research are well-documented. Ivermectin, an antiparasitic promoted by some as a COVID-19 treatment without adequate evidence, became the subject of extensive systematic review after multiple studies supporting its use were identified as fabricated or severely flawed. The Cochrane review of ivermectin (Hill, A., et al., 2022, Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, updated analysis) found that after excluding suspect trials, the evidence for ivermectin's benefit disappeared, confirming the screening tool's value in practice. By contrast, corticosteroids like dexamethasone showed sustained benefit in rigorous systematic reviews (Horby, P., et al., 2021, the RECOVERY Collaborative Group, published in the *New England Journal of Medicine*), demonstrating that systematic review applied consistently can both filter out bad evidence and confirm good evidence.

The Bero article accurately characterizes the role of systematic reviews as the gold standard of evidence synthesis: they identify, evaluate, and combine studies according to pre-specified criteria, apply inclusion and exclusion filters, weight studies by methodological quality, and provide a coherent summary of the best available evidence on a specific question. The methodology was articulated by Chalmers, I. and Altman, D.G. (1995, *Systematic Reviews*, BMJ Books) and has become the evidence backbone of clinical guideline development worldwide.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemic novelty in the Bero article is not a specific false belief requiring correction—it is the articulation of a positive epistemological framework for evaluating research quality under conditions where the conventional signaling system (peer review, journal prestige, author affiliation) is insufficient. The article addresses what might be termed **the meta-credibility problem**: the conditions under which normal markers of credibility cease to be reliable.

This problem has become increasingly acute in the networked information environment. Pre-internet, the friction involved in publishing medical research meant that genuinely fraudulent studies were relatively rare encounters for most clinicians, because the bottlenecks of institutional affiliation, journal access, and visible track record provided informal filters. The internet has reduced these frictions in multiple ways: preprint servers allow pre-peer-review findings to reach massive audiences; predatory journals with nominal peer review publish for fees; the speed of social media spread can make a single dubious study internationally known before any replication or review has occurred.

The epistemological implication, articulated by Goldacre, B. (2012, *Bad Science*, Fourth Estate) but given technical form by the Bero research, is that the transition from a low-friction to a high-friction publication environment requires a corresponding transition in how audiences evaluate medical claims. What worked as a credibility heuristic in a low-friction environment (publication in a named journal implies peer review implies methodological scrutiny) has become unreliable in a high-friction environment where the publication quantity has drastically outpaced the capacity of expert reviewers and where fraudulent publications have learned to mimic the surface features of legitimate research.

The Bero study's contribution is to move this from a general observation to a specific, operationalizable tool. By interviewing systematic review experts about the patterns they identify as suspicious in their professional practice, the study converts tacit expert knowledge—the kind of pattern recognition that experienced reviewers apply intuitively—into explicit, teachable criteria. This conversion from tacit to explicit knowledge is itself a significant epistemological contribution: it enables the criteria to be applied by less expert reviewers, to be communicated to non-specialist audiences, and to be tested and refined through further empirical work.

The COVID-19 pandemic provided an adversarial test for existing research integrity systems that had real-time public health consequences. The speed of the pandemic's progression created pressure to publish and act on research faster than normal quality control processes could operate. The resulting mixture of high-quality rapid research, more cautious preliminary findings, and outright fraud required audiences from policy-makers to journalists to the general public to evaluate research quality in real time without the benefit of the synthesis processes that normally precede guideline formation. Bastian, H. (2020), writing in *PLOS Medicine*, documented the extraordinary scale of COVID-19 publication and the ways normal quality filters were strained, providing empirical context for why the Bero research was timely.

There is also a deeper epistemological dynamic at work in the attention paid to flawed medical research: the **asymmetry of harm from Type I and Type II errors in health domains**. In medical research, a false positive (believing an ineffective treatment works) does not symmetrically cancel a false negative (failing to recognize an effective treatment). Depending on the treatment in question, false positives can lead to widespread adoption of harmful interventions, while false negatives result in failure to adopt beneficial ones. The stakes are not equal in all cases, and a research integrity screening tool that is better at detecting false positives in dangerous treatments than false negatives in beneficial ones has different public health consequences than one that applies uniformly. The Bero study does not explicitly address this asymmetry, which represents an area for future research.

The core claims in the Bero article are well-supported by evidence. Flawed and fraudulent medical research does enter the published literature. Systematic review processes provide the most reliable available filter against such research. The specific warning signs identified through expert interview are consistent with documented patterns in known cases of fraud and are operationalizable as a screening tool. COVID-19 research provides a well-documented case study in which systematic review successfully identified and excluded suspect trials—particularly for ivermectin—while confirming the evidence for treatments with genuine effect, such as dexamethasone.

The article appropriately hedges its conclusions: it does not claim that all research is unreliable, that peer review has failed comprehensively, or that the screening tool it proposes is definitive. The claim is more limited and therefore more defensible: that suspect patterns exist, can be identified, and that doing so improves the quality of the evidence base used for clinical decisions.

The item is not a fact-check and does not have a conventional Snopes verdict. Its republication on Snopes as a news opinion piece reflects the platform's recognition that meta-research on research quality is directly relevant to its core mission of evaluating claims.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Bero article represents one facet of a substantially larger research ecosystem focused on research integrity, reproducibility, and the sociology of scientific knowledge production. This ecosystem has grown substantially since the early 2010s and has produced findings that are both technically important and deeply relevant to public understanding of science.

**The Replication Crisis and Its Predecessors**: Modern concern about research integrity is often dated to the "replication crisis" of the 2010s, triggered in part by the Open Science Collaboration's 2015 study (Nosek, B.A., et al., 2015, *Science*, 349(6251)) which attempted to replicate 100 psychology studies and achieved results consistent with the originals in only 36-39% of cases. This finding, and parallel results in economics, cancer biology, and clinical medicine, generated widespread recognition that the published literature in several fields contained a substantial proportion of results that would not survive replication.

The causes of the replication crisis are multiple and interacting. Simmons, J.P., Nelson, L.D., and Simonsohn, U. (2011), writing in *Psychological Science*, coined the term "p-hacking" to describe the practice of analyzing data in multiple ways and selectively reporting the subset of analyses that achieve statistical significance—a practice that inflates false positive rates while technically adhering to standard statistical norms. This form of research misconduct occupies an uncertain ethical space: it is not fabrication, but it systematically overstates the reliability of findings. Estimating its prevalence is difficult precisely because it is rarely disclosed.

John P.A. Ioannidis' landmark 2005 paper "Why Most Published Research Findings Are False" (*PLOS Medicine*, 2(8)) demonstrated mathematically that under realistic assumptions about statistical power, effect sizes, and research practices, the majority of significant findings in low-powered studies of small effects (a description applicable to much of clinical and social science research) are likely false positives. This paper has been widely cited and also widely challenged; its mathematical framework is sound, though the empirical assumptions are debated. The Bero work on fabricated data is a distinct but related concern: even setting aside statistical practices, deliberate fabrication represents an irreducible fraud component.

**Publication Bias and the Evidence Base**: A structural problem underlying individual research integrity issues is publication bias: the systematic over-representation in the published literature of statistically significant, positive results compared to null or negative findings. Easterbrook, P.J., et al. (1991), in one of the early systematic analyses of this phenomenon published in *The Lancet*, showed that studies with significant results were substantially more likely to be published than those with null findings—a pattern that has been repeatedly confirmed in subsequent meta-analyses. Publication bias distorts systematic reviews: reviewers synthesizing the published literature will overestimate effect sizes when the underlying distribution of completed studies includes many unpublished null findings.

The pre-registration movement—requiring trial registration with a specified primary outcome before data collection begins—was developed specifically to address this problem by making it possible to identify unpublished studies and to verify whether published outcomes match pre-specified ones. Kaplan, R.M. and Irvin, V.L. (2015), in *PLOS ONE*, compared outcomes of NIH-funded clinical trials before and after a 2000 policy shift requiring registration, and found that the proportion of trials with significant primary outcome results dropped substantially after registration was required, strongly suggesting that pre-registration had reduced selective outcome reporting. This is one of the clearest demonstrations in the literature that structural incentives, not individual ethics alone, drive research quality.

**Industry Funding and Conflicted Research**: The Bero article briefly mentions funding source as a warning sign for suspect research—and the evidence base for this concern is extensive. Bero herself has a long research history on tobacco and pharmaceutical industry influence on research, and the patterns documented in that work are directly relevant to the screening tool she advocates. Lexchin, J., et al. (2003), in a widely-cited meta-analysis published in the *British Medical Journal*, found that industry-funded clinical trials were four times as likely to report results favorable to sponsors as independently funded trials of the same interventions. This finding has been replicated across multiple therapeutic areas and methodological refinements.

The mechanism is not necessarily direct fabrication—though that has been documented in specific cases. More commonly, industry funding is associated with favorable framing of ambiguous results, selective reporting of secondary endpoints, selective publication of positive trials and non-publication of negative ones, and subtle methodological choices (comparator selection, outcome timing, population restriction) that favor the sponsor's product. These practices exist on a continuum from clearly fraudulent to borderline, and the screening tool that Bero advocates would catch only the most extreme cases; the more subtle forms of industry influence require different detection methods, including systematic registration and results reporting requirements.

**The Role of Systematic Review in Epistemic Governance**: Systematic review has emerged over the past three decades as a central institution of what might be called medical epistemic governance—the processes by which evidence is evaluated, synthesized, and translated into clinical recommendations. The Cochrane Collaboration, established in 1993, created a network of researchers committed to conducting and updating systematic reviews according to explicit, pre-specified, non-conflicted methods. Cochrane reviews carry substantial weight in clinical guideline development because they represent the best available synthesis of evidence.

However, systematic review is not immune to the problems it is designed to detect. Review authors have biases, inclusion criteria can be set in ways that favor particular conclusions, and the quality of the underlying literature limits the quality of any synthesis. Oxman, A.D. and Guyatt, G.H. (1993), writing in the *Journal of Clinical Epidemiology*, identified ways in which review quality itself can vary and proposed evaluation frameworks. The GRADE system (Grading Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations), developed by Guyatt, G.H., et al. (2008, *BMJ*, 336(7649)), provides a standardized framework for rating the quality of evidence and strength of recommendations, addressing some of these concerns at the synthesis level.

**Implications for Science Communication and Public Trust**: The Bero article's core audience is systematic reviewers rather than the general public, but its republication on Snopes reflects recognition that public audiences need accessible frameworks for evaluating medical claims, particularly in high-information-load environments like pandemics. The tension between expert and lay epistemologies is particularly acute in health domains: clinical recommendations require high-quality evidence synthesis to be reliable, but communicating the uncertainty that is pervasive in medical research honestly may reduce public trust in evidence-based guidance more than it promotes well-calibrated health behavior.

Fischhoff, B. (2013), writing in *PNAS*, examined the challenge of communicating scientific uncertainty in ways that preserve public confidence in the scientific enterprise while accurately representing what is and isn't known. He distinguished between communicating uncertainty (appropriate, necessary, honest) and performing uncertainty (strategic emphasis on unknowns to evade accountability or manufacture doubt). The research integrity literature documents real cases of both, and the lay audience faces the difficulty of distinguishing them without access to the underlying methodological details.

The practical question raised by the Bero work is whether public audiences can realistically apply research quality screening criteria, or whether the appropriate role for such criteria is exclusively in expert systematic review processes. The article suggests that some criteria are accessible to non-specialists—particularly the questions about ethics approval, trial registration, conflict of interest disclosure, and plausibility. If so, public provision of simplified versions of these criteria could meaningfully improve population-level health literacy when evaluating medical claims. The broader evidence on health literacy interventions (Berkman, N.D., et al., 2011, a systematic review for the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality) suggests that structured decision aids and evaluation tools can improve the quality of lay health decision-making when they are well-designed and appropriately targeted.

**Predatory Publishing and the Democratization of Fake Credibility**: One mechanism by which flawed and fabricated research enters the literature at accelerating rates is the growth of predatory publishing: journals that charge open-access publication fees while conducting little or no genuine peer review. Beall, J. (2012), in his influential analysis in *Nature*, estimated that thousands of predatory journals existed by the early 2010s, publishing hundreds of thousands of articles annually. The number has grown substantially since then. Predatory journals allow researchers to claim peer-reviewed publication for work that has not been genuinely evaluated, creating a type of false credibility that is indistinguishable from legitimate peer review to non-specialist audiences.

The implication for the Bero framework is that journal name recognition, which has been a useful (if imperfect) proxy for peer review quality, is increasingly unreliable. The screening criteria she advocates—which focus on study-level features rather than venue-level reputation—are better adapted to this environment because they are less dependent on consistent standards across publishing venues.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes treatment of this article is formally unusual: it is a republication of a third-party commentary piece rather than an original fact-check. The piece appears under the "News" category rather than "Fact Check," and it carries a note that "this content is shared here because the topic may interest Snopes readers; it does not, however, represent the work of Snopes fact-checkers or editors." The article was authored by Lisa Bero under her own name as an academic at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus, and was originally published on The Conversation under a Creative Commons license that permits republication.

This treatment reflects a legitimate editorial strategy: Snopes regularly publishes expert-authored content that contextualizes or extends its fact-checking work without itself making the specific false/true determination that a fact-check does. The Bero piece is relevant to Snopes' mission because it discusses mechanisms by which unreliable medical information enters and propagates through the evidence base—a problem directly relevant to medical fact-checking.

The absence of a traditional verdict creates an interesting formal case: the article is on Snopes but the platform has not certified its claims. Users arriving at the page through a search for fact-checked information may not immediately recognize the different editorial status of the piece. The "News" categorization is accurate but may not be sufficiently visible to prevent misclassification.

For Truth Vault purposes, the Bero article's epistemological content is more important than its editorial classification. Its core contribution—a practical framework for identifying suspect medical research through screening questions about transparency, registration, plausibility, and funding—is directly relevant to the meta-level work of calibrating how much confidence to place in any empirical claim. Every claim in the Truth Vault ultimately rests on some assessment of evidence quality; the Bero framework makes the implicit criteria for that assessment explicit and teachable.

The main analytical gap in both the Bero piece and its Snopes republication is the absence of engagement with the political economy of evidence production: why incentives in academic publishing, pharmaceutical development, and grant funding create systematic pressures toward the behaviors the screening tool is designed to detect. Understanding the structural drivers of research misconduct is as important as developing tools to detect its outputs—without addressing root causes, screening tools will remain in a permanent catch-up position as fraud adapts to new detection criteria.
