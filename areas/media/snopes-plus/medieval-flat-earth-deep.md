# "People in the Middle Ages Thought the Earth Was Flat"


## 1. The Claim

**The Claim**: During the Middle Ages (roughly 500-1500 CE), Europeans believed the Earth was flat and that Christopher Columbus's voyage was controversial because people thought he would sail off the edge of the world.

This narrative appears in textbooks, popular histories, museum exhibits, and cultural references as evidence of medieval scientific ignorance and religious obscurantism. The story typically positions the medieval period as a "Dark Ages" when Christian superstition suppressed classical learning, with flat-Earth belief serving as emblematic evidence of how religious dogma corrupted rational thought. Columbus emerges as an enlightened figure battling medieval ignorance, with his voyage representing the triumph of scientific reason over religious mythology.

The flat-Earth medieval narrative serves modern ideological purposes by establishing a framework where scientific progress requires overcoming religious authority, positioning contemporary science-religion conflicts as continuation of ancient battles between reason and superstition. But this entire narrative is a 19th-century fabrication created by Protestant historians to discredit Catholic intellectual history, making it one of the most successful lies in the history of ideas.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim inverts historical reality: medieval Europeans not only knew the Earth was spherical but inherited this knowledge directly from ancient sources and never questioned it:

**Medieval scholarly consensus**: Every major medieval intellectual—from the Venerable Bede in the 8th century through Thomas Aquinas in the 13th century to Dante in the 14th—assumed Earth's sphericity as established fact. Medieval encyclopedias, astronomical treatises, geographical works, and theological commentaries consistently describe a spherical Earth. No medieval text advocates flat-Earth cosmology, and no evidence suggests any educated European ever believed the Earth was flat.

**Classical knowledge transmission**: Medieval scholars inherited spherical Earth knowledge directly from Roman sources, particularly Pliny's *Natural History* and Macrobius's *Commentary on the Dream of Scipio*, which transmitted Greek astronomical learning. These texts were continuously available in medieval libraries and formed standard components of education in the seven liberal arts. The transmission was unbroken—there was no period when spherical Earth knowledge was "lost" and later "rediscovered."

**Columbus controversy fabrication**: The actual Columbus controversy involved distance calculations, not Earth's shape. Columbus believed the Earth was smaller than it actually is and that the Atlantic crossing to Asia would be shorter than competing estimates suggested. His critics were correct—the voyage would have failed if America hadn't been there to break the journey. The debate involved sophisticated geographical calculations by people who universally agreed the Earth was spherical.

**Iconographic evidence**: Medieval art consistently depicts the Earth as a sphere. The *orbis terrarum* (orb of the Earth) appears in countless medieval manuscripts, paintings, and sculptures showing rulers holding spherical globes representing their earthly dominion. Church architecture incorporates spherical Earth symbolism. These visual sources provide overwhelming evidence that medieval Europeans conceptualized the Earth as round.


Comprehensive examination of medieval sources demonstrates universal acceptance of Earth's sphericity among educated Europeans, while historiographical research reveals the flat-Earth myth's deliberate 19th-century construction and ideological motivations.

**Jeffrey Burton Russell's definitive debunking**: Russell's 1991 *Inventing the Flat Earth: Columbus and Modern Historians* provided comprehensive documentation that no medieval European source advocates flat-Earth cosmology. Russell examined hundreds of medieval texts—astronomical treatises, geographical works, encyclopedias, theological commentaries, travel narratives—and found universal acceptance of spherical Earth theory. Russell traced the myth's fabrication through Irving, White, and Draper, demonstrating how academic authority transformed romantic fiction into historical "fact."

**Primary source evidence**: Medieval sources consistently describe spherical Earth cosmology with sophisticated understanding of astronomical implications. Bede's 8th-century *De temporum ratione* explains how Earth's sphericity creates seasonal variations and climate zones. Aquinas's 13th-century *Summa Theologica* treats Earth's roundness as established fact requiring no defense. Dante's *Divine Comedy* assumes spherical Earth geometry throughout its cosmic architecture. The *Livre de l'Espere* and other medieval astronomical texts provide detailed mathematical treatments of spherical Earth theory.

**Manuscript and library evidence**: Medieval library catalogs show continuous availability of classical texts containing spherical Earth theory. Macrobius's *Commentary*, Pliny's *Natural History*, and Martianus Capella's *Marriage of Philology and Mercury* appear in monastery libraries throughout the medieval period. These texts formed core components of the quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, music) in medieval education, ensuring that spherical Earth knowledge was transmitted to every educated person.

**Archaeological and artistic confirmation**: Medieval maps, globes, and astronomical instruments assume Earth's sphericity. The Hereford Mappamundi (c. 1300) depicts the world as a circular disk representing the habitable portion of a spherical Earth, not a flat world. Medieval astrolabes and armillary spheres model spherical cosmic geometry. Church architecture incorporates spherical symbolism, with rulers depicted holding *orbis terrarum* globes representing spherical earthly dominion.

**Islamic and Jewish scholarship integration**: Medieval European scholars integrated Islamic astronomical works (particularly Al-Battani and Ptolemy translations) and Jewish scholarship (especially Maimonides) that universally assumed Earth's sphericity. The translation movement of the 12th century brought additional classical and Arabic astronomical texts to Europe, all confirming spherical Earth theory through multiple intellectual traditions.

**Navigation and practical knowledge**: Medieval European navigation and cartography assumed Earth's sphericity for practical calculations. Medieval sea charts account for curved distances, and navigation manuals explain how Earth's curvature affects compass readings and star positions. The practical requirements of long-distance trade and pilgrimage would have quickly revealed any fundamental misconceptions about Earth's shape.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The medieval flat-Earth myth was deliberately created by 19th-century historians to support Protestant narratives about Catholic intellectual corruption, making it a case study in how academic fabrication becomes cultural orthodoxy.

**Washington Irving's foundational lie**: The myth's primary source is Washington Irving's 1828 *A History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus*, which fabricated the flat-Earth controversy for dramatic effect. Irving presented his invented Columbus-versus-flat-Earthers conflict as historical fact, complete with fictional speeches and debates that never occurred. Irving explicitly acknowledged in letters that he prioritized "poetic" over literal truth, but readers treated his romantic biography as historical scholarship.

**Andrew Dickson White's academic weaponization**: Cornell University's first president, Andrew Dickson White, systematized Irving's fabrication in his 1896 *A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom*. White used the flat-Earth myth as foundational evidence for his "conflict thesis"—the idea that science and religion are inherently antagonistic, with religious authority systematically opposing scientific progress. White's academic credentials and institutional position gave the fabrication scholarly legitimacy.

**John William Draper's amplification**: Draper's 1874 *History of the Conflict between Religion and Science* similarly promoted the flat-Earth medieval myth as evidence of Christianity's anti-scientific influence. Draper and White were writing during the post-Civil War period when American Protestant intellectuals were establishing secular higher education and needed historical precedent for limiting religious authority in academic contexts. The medieval flat-Earth myth provided perfect historical justification for contemporary institutional conflicts.

**Textbook institutionalization**: By the early 20th century, the flat-Earth medieval myth had entered American and European textbooks through the influence of White's and Draper's works. Educational authorities repeated the claim without checking primary sources, assuming that university presidents and established historians had verified their assertions. The myth became self-perpetuating as each generation of teachers transmitted it to students who later became teachers themselves.

**Cultural resonance factors**: The myth succeeded because it confirmed multiple modern prejudices: Protestant assumptions about Catholic intellectual corruption, Enlightenment narratives about progress from superstition to reason, nationalist claims about modern scientific superiority, and anti-religious sentiment among secular intellectuals. The story was too useful to various constituencies to be critically examined, creating cultural investment in maintaining the fabrication regardless of evidence.


## 4. Verdict

**Classification**: **Reconstructed Reality**

The medieval flat-Earth claim represents successful academic fabrication where 19th-century historians created fictional historical narratives that became foundational cultural knowledge despite lacking any evidence whatsoever.

**What actually happened in medieval Europe**:
- Every educated medieval European knew the Earth was spherical based on classical Greek and Roman sources that were continuously available in monastery libraries
- Medieval scholars like Bede, Aquinas, and Dante assumed Earth's sphericity as established scientific fact requiring no defense
- No medieval text advocates flat-Earth cosmology, and no evidence suggests any educated European ever believed the Earth was flat
- Medieval navigation, cartography, and astronomy all assumed spherical Earth geometry for practical calculations
- The Columbus controversy involved distance calculations and the feasibility of Atlantic crossing, not debates about Earth's shape

**How the fabrication was created**:
- Washington Irving's 1828 romantic biography of Columbus invented the flat-Earth controversy for dramatic effect, explicitly prioritizing "poetic" over literal truth
- Andrew Dickson White and John William Draper transformed Irving's fiction into academic "history" to support the "conflict thesis" positioning science and religion as inherently antagonistic
- These Protestant historians needed historical examples of religious authority opposing scientific truth to justify contemporary institutional conflicts over secular higher education
- The fabrication entered textbooks through academic authority rather than evidence, becoming self-perpetuating as each generation transmitted it without verification

**Why the lie succeeded**:
The medieval flat-Earth myth serves multiple modern ideological functions: it supports Protestant narratives about Catholic intellectual corruption, Enlightenment progress stories about advancement from superstition to reason, nationalist claims about modern scientific superiority, and anti-religious sentiment in academic culture. The fabrication was too useful to various constituencies to be critically examined, creating cultural investment in maintaining the lie regardless of overwhelming contrary evidence.

**What this reveals about knowledge transmission**:
Academic authority can manufacture historical myths that become culturally foundational when fabrications align with institutional ideology and contemporary political needs. The medieval flat-Earth case demonstrates how distinguished scholars can deliberately create false histories that persist for generations, revealing the importance of primary source verification and the dangers of accepting academic credentials as sufficient evidence for historical claims.

The success of this fabrication represents one of the most dramatic examples in modern scholarship of how ideological commitment can override empirical evidence in creating historical knowledge that serves contemporary political purposes rather than understanding the past.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The medieval flat-Earth myth exemplifies how academic fabrication can become foundational cultural knowledge when it serves powerful ideological and institutional interests.

**The "conflict thesis" ecosystem**: The flat-Earth myth functions as crucial evidence for the broader "conflict thesis"—the idea that science and religion are inherently antagonistic. This framework shapes public understanding of contemporary debates about evolution, climate change, and medical interventions by positioning religious perspectives as inherently anti-scientific. The thesis requires historical examples of religious authority opposing scientific truth, making the fabricated medieval flat-Earth controversy essential supporting evidence for a broader ideological narrative.

**Protestant anti-Catholic historiography**: The myth emerged during 19th-century American Protestant establishment of secular higher education, when academic leaders needed historical justification for limiting Catholic intellectual influence. Portraying medieval Catholicism as systematically anti-scientific supported contemporary institutional conflicts while establishing Protestant intellectual superiority. The fabrication served specific sectarian purposes that became generalized into broader anti-religious academic culture.

**Enlightenment progress narratives**: The flat-Earth medieval myth supports linear progress narratives where human history moves from superstition toward reason, with scientific method as the engine of advancement. This framework positions contemporary scientific institutions as inheritors of a heroic tradition battling persistent religious obscurantism. The myth provides a foundational example of how religious authority corrupts human reason, making contemporary science-religion conflicts appear as continuation of ancient patterns.

**Educational institutional capture**: Once the myth entered textbooks, institutional momentum made correction nearly impossible. Publishers repeated claims from previous textbooks without verification. Teachers transmitted received wisdom without checking sources. Students learned fabricated history as established fact, then became teachers and administrators who perpetuated the cycle. Educational institutions developed investment in maintaining the myth rather than acknowledging their role in transmitting fabricated history.

**Popular culture amplification**: The flat-Earth medieval myth appears in novels, films, television shows, and other media as shorthand for medieval ignorance and religious superstition. Cultural references reinforce the fabrication through repetition, making correction appear pedantic or apologetic. Popular culture creates its own evidence for historical claims through narrative embedding that becomes independent of scholarly verification.

**Modern flat-Earth conspiracy theories**: Contemporary flat-Earth movements often reference medieval flat-Earth belief as evidence that spherical Earth theory is modern propaganda. The fabricated medieval precedent provides historical legitimacy for contemporary conspiracy theories, creating ironic situations where modern conspiracy theorists defend medieval intellectual positions that medieval scholars never actually held. The myth's persistence enables contemporary pseudoscientific movements that claim to restore "traditional" cosmologies that are actually modern fabrications.

**Historiographical methodology lessons**: The medieval flat-Earth case demonstrates how distinguished academics can manufacture historical myths that persist for generations when they serve institutional interests. White held a university presidency, published through respected presses, and received scholarly honors while promoting fabricated history. Academic credentials provide insufficient protection against deliberate misinformation when fabrications align with institutional ideology. The case reveals the importance of primary source verification and the dangers of accepting academic authority without independent evidence.
