# "People in the Middle Ages Thought the Earth Was Flat"


## 1. The Claim

A persistent historical narrative asserts that people during the European Middle Ages (roughly 500-1500 CE) believed the Earth was flat, and that this belief was supported or enforced by the Catholic Church. According to this claim, medieval Europeans imagined the world as a flat disk, possibly surrounded by water or ending in dangerous edges where ships might fall off. The narrative often frames Christopher Columbus's 1492 voyage as particularly heroic because he supposedly challenged this flat-earth orthodoxy and risked sailing off the edge of the world.

This claim typically extends to suggest that medieval "ignorance" about Earth's shape represented a broader pattern of scientific regression during the supposed "Dark Ages," contrasting medieval Christian belief with classical Greek knowledge and Renaissance enlightenment. Some versions specifically assert that the Church actively suppressed knowledge of Earth's sphericity as part of a broader campaign against scientific thinking.

The claim appears in countless educational materials, popular histories, and cultural references, often serving as a convenient symbol for the supposed conflict between religious faith and scientific reason, or between medieval "darkness" and Renaissance "light."


## 2. What's Actually True

**Primary Medieval Sources**: Medieval texts consistently describe a spherical Earth. The Venerable Bede's "De temporum ratione" (725 CE) discusses Earth's spherical shape and calculates its circumference. Thomas Aquinas's "Summa Theologica" (13th century) treats Earth's sphericity as established fact. Medieval encyclopedists like Bartholomeus Anglicus and Vincent of Beauvais described spherical Earth models in their widely circulated works.

**Educational Curriculum**: Medieval universities taught spherical Earth models as part of the quadrivium (astronomy, arithmetic, geometry, music). Students at Paris, Oxford, Cambridge, and other medieval institutions studied Ptolemy's "Geography," Aristotle's "De Caelo," and other classical works that assumed Earth's sphericity.

**Ecclesiastical Sources**: Church authorities consistently accepted spherical Earth models. Pope Sylvester II (Gerbert of Aurillac, 999-1003 CE) was a mathematician and astronomer who taught spherical geometry. Medieval cathedrals often featured spherical Earth imagery in their architectural and artistic programs.

**Navigation Practices**: Medieval navigators and cartographers operated under spherical Earth assumptions. Portolan charts from the 13th century onward reflect understanding of curved Earth surfaces. Medieval pilgrimage routes and trade networks demonstrate practical application of spherical geography knowledge.

**The Columbus Documentation**: Historical records of Columbus's proposals to reach Asia by sailing west show that opposition came from concerns about distance and feasibility, not Earth's shape. The Spanish crown's advisors correctly calculated that Columbus had underestimated Earth's size and the distance to Asia. Their skepticism proved accurate—Columbus would have died without encountering the Americas.


The historical evidence overwhelmingly demonstrates that educated medieval Europeans accepted Earth's spherical shape based on classical authorities, observational evidence, and mathematical reasoning. This knowledge was not confined to elite scholars but appeared in educational curricula, popular encyclopedias, and practical applications like navigation and timekeeping.

**Documentary Evidence Chain**: The transmission of spherical Earth knowledge from classical antiquity through medieval period to Renaissance can be traced through specific texts and institutional contexts. Eratosthenes's calculation of Earth's circumference (c. 240 BCE) was known to medieval scholars through multiple transmission routes, including Macrobius's "Commentary on the Dream of Scipio" and various Islamic sources.

**Institutional Analysis**: Medieval universities, monasteries, and cathedral schools maintained libraries containing classical geographical and astronomical texts. The preservation and copying of these materials demonstrates active engagement with spherical Earth concepts rather than suppression or ignorance.

**Cross-Cultural Verification**: Medieval European spherical Earth knowledge aligns with contemporary understanding in Islamic and Byzantine contexts. The translation movement in medieval Islamic civilization preserved and expanded upon classical geographical knowledge, and this material was increasingly available to medieval European scholars through translation projects in Spain, Sicily, and other contact zones.

**Practical Applications**: Medieval achievements in navigation, architecture, and timekeeping required spherical Earth knowledge. The construction of accurate sundials, the calculation of Easter dates, and the planning of long-distance voyages all depended on understanding Earth's curved surface and its relationship to celestial motion.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The flat-earth myth about medieval beliefs reflects several historiographical problems that compromise its epistemological foundation. First, it exemplifies "Whig history"—interpreting past events through the lens of present assumptions about progress and enlightenment. This approach treats medieval period as necessarily inferior to later periods rather than examining it on its own terms.

Second, the myth employs selective evidence, highlighting obscure or misinterpreted sources while ignoring the substantial corpus of medieval texts that demonstrate spherical Earth knowledge. When medieval authors used flat-earth imagery, it typically appeared in allegorical, poetic, or theological contexts rather than literal geographical descriptions.

Third, the claim reflects anachronistic assumptions about the relationship between religious faith and scientific knowledge in medieval contexts. Modern conflicts between certain religious groups and scientific consensus are projected backward onto medieval Christianity, despite substantial evidence that medieval churchmen were often leading advocates for mathematical and astronomical knowledge.

**Source Evaluation Problems**: Many supposedly "medieval" flat-earth references come from much earlier periods (ancient Near Eastern cosmologies) or much later periods (post-Renaissance artistic allegories). Proper chronological analysis reveals that medieval European sources consistently support spherical Earth models.

**Translation and Interpretation Issues**: Some apparent flat-earth references in medieval texts result from translation errors or misunderstanding of allegorical language. Medieval writers often used cosmological imagery symbolically rather than literally, and modern readers sometimes mistake metaphorical descriptions for scientific claims.

**Archaeological Evidence**: Medieval material culture supports spherical Earth knowledge. Armillary spheres, astrolabes, and other astronomical instruments from medieval contexts assume spherical Earth models. Medieval world maps (mappae mundi) often appear "flat" but represent attempts to project spherical surfaces onto flat media, similar to modern map projections.


## 4. Verdict

**REJECTED**: The claim that medieval Europeans believed in a flat Earth is historically false and represents a modern myth about medieval knowledge rather than accurate historical description. Educated medieval Europeans consistently understood and accepted Earth's spherical shape based on classical sources, observational evidence, and mathematical reasoning.

**Historical Reality**: Medieval European scholars, clergy, navigators, and educated laypeople operated under spherical Earth models inherited from classical antiquity and refined through contemporary observation and calculation. This knowledge appeared in university curricula, popular encyclopedias, practical navigation, architectural projects, and theological works throughout the medieval period.

**Myth Origins**: The flat-earth myth about medieval beliefs was largely fabricated by 19th-century American authors, particularly Washington Irving, and was subsequently popularized by writers promoting "conflict thesis" narratives about science and religion. These authors used invented medieval flat-earth beliefs to support contemporary arguments about progress, enlightenment, and American cultural superiority.

**Educational Implications**: Correcting this myth requires understanding medieval intellectual culture on its own terms rather than through later stereotypes about "Dark Ages" ignorance. Medieval European civilization maintained and developed sophisticated geographical, mathematical, and astronomical knowledge that formed essential foundations for Renaissance and early modern scientific developments.

**Methodological Lessons**: The persistence of this myth demonstrates the importance of primary source research in historical studies and the dangers of repeating claims without verification. It also illustrates how historical myths can serve contemporary cultural and political functions that make them resistant to scholarly correction.

The medieval flat-earth claim represents a "distorted-but-grounded" myth because it contains elements that appear plausible (religious authorities opposing scientific knowledge) but completely misrepresents the actual historical relationship between medieval Christianity and geographical knowledge. Such myths require careful historiographical analysis to separate legitimate historical patterns from anachronistic projections and deliberate fabrications.

**Research Recommendation**: Future educational materials about medieval science should emphasize positive medieval achievements in mathematics, astronomy, optics, and natural philosophy while accurately contextualizing the transmission and development of classical geographical knowledge through medieval Islamic and Christian scholarly networks.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The flat-earth myth about medieval beliefs intersects with broader patterns in historical interpretation, science communication, and cultural memory. Understanding this myth requires examining several related phenomena:

**The "Dark Ages" Narrative**: The flat-earth myth forms part of a larger historiographical pattern that characterizes the medieval period as intellectually stagnant or regressive. This "Dark Ages" concept, largely developed by Renaissance humanists and later Enlightenment thinkers, served polemical purposes in their contemporary debates rather than reflecting accurate historical analysis. Modern medieval studies has thoroughly revised this picture, revealing the period's substantial intellectual achievements in philosophy, science, technology, and mathematics.

**Conflict Thesis Origins**: The flat-earth myth supports the "conflict thesis"—the idea that science and religion are inherently antagonistic—popularized by John William Draper's "History of the Conflict Between Religion and Science" (1874) and Andrew Dickson White's "A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom" (1896). Both authors used the supposed medieval flat-earth belief as evidence for religious suppression of scientific knowledge, despite lacking credible medieval sources for this claim.

**Washington Irving's Fabrication**: The most influential source for the Columbus flat-earth story was Washington Irving's "A History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus" (1828). Irving deliberately fictionalized Columbus's story for dramatic effect, inventing the flat-earth opposition to make Columbus appear more heroic. Irving's book was enormously popular and shaped American education about Columbus for generations, despite its fictional elements.

**Educational Persistence**: The flat-earth myth became embedded in American educational materials through the 19th and early 20th centuries, often serving nationalist purposes by making Columbus's achievement seem more significant and by contrast making European medieval culture appear inferior to American progress and rationality. Textbook authors repeated Irving's fabrications without checking medieval sources.

**Modern Flat-Earth Movements**: Ironically, the historical myth about medieval flat-earth beliefs has been appropriated by actual modern flat-earth believers who use it to argue that spherical Earth knowledge is a recent conspiracy. This demonstrates how historical myths can acquire unexpected contemporary relevance and political significance.

**Historiographical Methodology**: The flat-earth myth illustrates the importance of primary source research in historical studies. The myth persisted because historians repeated earlier claims without returning to medieval sources. Only systematic examination of medieval texts, educational curricula, and material culture revealed the extent to which spherical Earth knowledge was widespread and accepted.

**Science Communication Lessons**: The persistence of the flat-earth myth despite scholarly correction demonstrates challenges in science and history communication. Correcting deeply embedded cultural myths requires sustained educational effort and attention to the social and psychological functions these myths serve in contemporary culture.

**Medieval Scientific Achievement**: Accurate understanding of medieval geographical knowledge reveals a period of substantial scientific achievement including advances in optics (Roger Bacon, Robert Grosseteste), mathematics (Fibonacci, Jordanus Nemorarius), astronomy (Sacrobosco, Campanus), and natural philosophy (Albertus Magnus, Thomas Aquinas). Medieval scholars developed sophisticated methods for combining observational evidence with mathematical reasoning and classical authorities.

**Islamic-Christian Knowledge Exchange**: Medieval European spherical Earth knowledge benefited substantially from translation and adaptation of Islamic geographical and astronomical works. Scholars like Gerard of Cremona, Adelard of Bath, and Robert of Chester translated major works by Al-Battani, Al-Farghani, and other Islamic astronomers who had refined and corrected classical geographical knowledge.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates claims about medieval flat-earth beliefs as **False**, emphasizing that educated Europeans throughout the Middle Ages understood and accepted Earth's spherical shape. The fact-checking organization notes that this misconception represents a modern myth about medieval knowledge rather than an accurate historical account.

Snopes traces the flat-earth myth to 19th-century sources, particularly Washington Irving's romanticized biography of Columbus and later works that promoted narratives of medieval scientific ignorance. The assessment emphasizes that medieval scholars, clergy, and navigators possessed sophisticated geographical knowledge based on classical sources and contemporary observation.
