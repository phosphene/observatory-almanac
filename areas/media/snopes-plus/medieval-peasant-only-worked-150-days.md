# Medieval Peasants Had More Holidays and Leisure Time Than Modern Workers


## 1. The Claim

The meme is everywhere. Set above or below a miniature from the *Très Riches Heures du Duc de Berry*—a luminous 15th-century French illuminated manuscript depicting peasants toiling at harvest—the text reads:

> *Medieval peasants worked only about 150 days in a year. The Church believed it was important to keep them happy with frequent, mandatory holidays. You have less holidays than a Medieval peasant.*

The meme appears to have originated on May 15, 2022, from a historical humor Instagram account called Classical Damn. As of its 2024 circulation peak, a single Instagram version had accumulated more than 388,000 likes—approximately twenty-five times the engagement of the original post. It circulates widely on Reddit, X, Facebook, and TikTok, often in anti-work and labor rights communities, and functions less as a historical claim than as a critique of contemporary working life.

The claim has several moving parts. First, a quantitative assertion: medieval peasants worked approximately 150 days per year. Second, a causal mechanism: the Church scheduled numerous mandatory feast days and imposed Sunday rest, ensuring the labor force was kept sufficiently content. Third, an implied comparison: modern workers—especially in the United States, which provides fewer statutory holidays than most wealthy nations—have materially less leisure time than a thirteenth-century English serf.

All three elements have been seriously contested by economic historians, though the controversy is more nuanced than the confident social media debunkers or the equally confident meme sharers typically acknowledge.


## 2. What's Actually True

The 150-day figure did not emerge from a meme fabricator. It originates in unpublished work from 1986 by Gregory Clark, an economic historian then working from English manorial records, who estimated that a thirteenth-century peasant family worked approximately 150 days annually on their land—or roughly 135 twelve-hour days for an adult male, yielding around 1,620 hours per year. Clark's estimate was subsequently popularized by economist Juliet Schor in her 1991 book *The Overworked American: The Unexpected Decline of Leisure*, which argued that capitalist industrialization systematically eroded the leisure time that pre-industrial workers had previously enjoyed.

The estimate gained genuine scholarly traction. Manorial records from fourteenth-century England—particularly from the decades following the Black Death of 1348–1351—show working years that did hover around 175 days for servile laborers. Farmer-miners with greater autonomy over their schedules reportedly worked only 180 days between 1400 and 1600. After the Black Death, the catastrophic loss of roughly one-third of England's population created acute labor shortages, wages rose sharply, and—consistent with what economists call a "backward-bending labor supply curve"—workers chose to work fewer days once they could meet their subsistence needs with less effort. In this specific context, the 150-day working year is not pure invention: it reflects a real, documented phenomenon in post-plague England.

This matters enormously, because the meme does not say "some English wage laborers in the late fourteenth century, during an extraordinary demographic crisis, worked fewer days than modern Americans." It implies this was the typical condition of medieval peasant life across centuries and regions—and that is where the claim goes seriously wrong.

Economic historians are divided into two camps on what the typical medieval working year actually looked like. One camp, including Humphries and Weisdorf in their influential "Unreal Wages?" study, argues that the conventional assumption of 250 days per year is badly miscalibrated upward, and that medieval workers—at least in England—genuinely worked fewer days than the Industrial Revolution would impose on their descendants. Their revised income series, using annual contract wages rather than daily wages multiplied by assumed working days, suggests a medieval working year closer to 150 days, with a dramatic increase to 300+ days during the Industrial Revolution.

The opposing camp, including Clark's own later revised position, argues that workers in medieval England likely worked close to modern levels—roughly 250 days per year—and that the apparent low working year in some estimates reflects methodological artifacts. Clark came to this view by examining the relationship between food expenditure on workers and their daily wages across several centuries, finding remarkable consistency that implies a stable working year rather than the sharp leisure-to-labor transition implied by the Industrious Revolution thesis.

The honest answer is that we do not know with confidence what the typical medieval working year looked like, and estimates range from below 150 days in post-plague England to well over 250 days in other periods and regions.

The Church causation element of the meme stands on still weaker ground. Sundays were indeed rest days across Christendom, and the liturgical calendar included major feasts—Christmas (traditionally twelve days), Easter (a week), Pentecost, and dozens of saints' days. Historians of the period have counted anywhere from forty to over a hundred official feast days per year in various locales during the medieval period. These were genuinely observed: field labor for lords typically ceased, markets closed, and communal celebrations occurred.

However, economic historians studying the question—including Clark and Schor themselves, when interviewed by Snopes—generally reject the claim that religious authorities exerted significant control over the *total number of days* peasants worked. What actually drove the length of the working year were mundane economic factors: the amount of wage-based work available, the going wage rate, the gap between subsistence needs and earnings, and individual and household decisions about how much labor to sell. The Church provided days that were culturally protected from certain forms of labor, but it did not set the overall labor supply—that was determined by agrarian economies and, after 1350, by a catastrophic reduction in the workforce.

There is also a critical definitional problem that the meme and most popular discussions ignore entirely: what counts as "work"? A peasant household—man, woman, and children—operated largely outside the wage economy. The 150-day estimate and most manorial record analyses measure *wage labor* performed for a lord or in the market. They do not typically count the constant subsistence labor that kept a peasant household alive: tending animals (which required attention every day, including feast days), collecting firewood, spinning yarn, weaving cloth, cooking, maintaining equipment, managing vegetable plots, and child-rearing. These activities were not neatly bounded by "working days" that could be contrasted with days off. They were simply the ongoing texture of life, often performed by women and children outside the formal labor arrangements that left records. A medieval peasant woman may have appeared to be "not working" on a feast day while simultaneously spinning thread, tending a fire, managing an infant, and feeding livestock.

This omission is not a minor caveat. It fundamentally distorts the comparison to modern workers, whose leisure time—however inadequate—is genuinely leisure in a way that medieval feast days mostly were not.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The meme's appeal is not primarily historical. It is political and psychological.

Since the emergence of the internet anti-work movement, and accelerating during and after the COVID-19 pandemic, there has been enormous appetite for narratives that frame modern working conditions as historically aberrant—not the natural order of things but a specific, contingent imposition of industrial capitalism. The medieval peasant leisure claim serves this narrative perfectly. It locates an Edenic past before the destruction of traditional leisure, and it indicts the contemporary employer-employee relationship by implicit comparison.

Juliet Schor's *The Overworked American* gave the claim a degree of academic respectability it might not otherwise have had. When a Harvard economist with genuine scholarly credentials argues that medieval peasants had more leisure than modern Americans, the claim gains a veneer of authority that travels well across citation chains growing progressively less careful. By the time the claim reached viral Instagram memes in 2022, the careful hedging in Clark's original estimate and the contested nature of Schor's interpretation had evaporated entirely.

There is also the phenomenon of context collapse: the Black Death years in England, which genuinely produced anomalously short working years, get generalized to "medieval" as a unified era. The medieval period spans roughly a thousand years across dozens of regions with radically different labor regimes. Post-plague fourteenth-century England and thirteenth-century France were not the same place or time, and neither was the same as medieval Spain (where some workers did reportedly enjoy close to five months of holidays in certain regions during certain periods) or pre-plague England or Viking-era Scandinavia. The flattening of this tremendous variation into a single meme comparison is a kind of historical category error.

Finally, the comparison systematically edits out everything that made medieval peasant life genuinely terrible: the Black Death itself and endemic disease, regular famine, serfdom that bound millions legally to the land, infant mortality rates that killed roughly a third of children before age five, the complete absence of painkillers or anesthetic for injury and illness, and the total lack of legal protection against violent lords. "Medieval peasants had more holidays than you" sounds rather different when those holidays were enjoyed by people whose average life expectancy was forty years, who had no recourse against arbitrary dispossession, and who were genuinely at risk of starvation in bad harvest years.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** Mixture — the 150-day figure reflects genuine scholarship for specific periods; the Church causation claim is weak; the broader comparison is distorted
**Confidence:** Medium
**Epistemic Basis:** Read-together
**Historical Scope:** Post-Black Death England, not medieval Europe generally
**Omission Status:** Subsistence labor substantially underrepresents total work burden
**Comparison Validity:** Low — ignores quality-of-life conditions that make formal leisure comparisons misleading

The meme is not wholesale fabricated. It draws on genuine economic history scholarship that identified anomalously short working years in late medieval England, and the general finding that Industrial Revolution workers labored dramatically more days per year than their pre-industrial predecessors is well-supported by the Humphries-Weisdorf research program. The "Industrious Revolution" is a real concept in economic history with serious scholarly backing.

But the meme presents this scholarship in a form that is misleading in every major element. The 150-day figure, if defensible at all, describes exceptional circumstances (post-plague labor scarcity) rather than typical medieval conditions. The Church causation story reverses the actual economic mechanisms that determined working days. And the comparison to modern workers ignores the vast unrecorded subsistence labor that filled "days off," as well as the material conditions—disease, famine, legal bondage, lack of medicine—that made medieval leisure a quite different thing from modern leisure.

Snopes correctly rates this "Mixture" and correctly identifies the tensions in the economic history literature. The meme is not pure misinformation. It is credentialed, selectively quoted historical scholarship, stripped of context and cherry-picked to support a comparison that the scholars themselves would largely disavow.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The medieval peasant leisure meme is a case study in how legitimate economic history gets transformed through successive cycles of simplification into something its original authors might not recognize.

Gregory Clark's 1986 estimate was never intended as a claim about the pleasures of medieval life. It was a technical exercise in reconstructing annual income from daily wage data, and the 150-day working year was a derived figure, not an independently measured one. When Schor picked it up for *The Overworked American*, she deployed it as part of a broader argument about the specific historical contingency of American overwork—a claim she has always acknowledged rests on contested evidence. Schor herself, when interviewed by Snopes, noted that her goal was never to idealize medieval life but to challenge the assumption that long working hours are natural or inevitable.

The scholarly landscape behind the meme is actually one of productive, ongoing debate about how to measure economic welfare in pre-industrial societies. The central methodological challenge is that medieval peasants were not primarily wage earners. They produced much of what they consumed on land to which they had customary access rights. Measuring their "working year" through records of days worked for wages systematically underestimates labor intensity, but measuring it through total subsistence activity makes comparison to wage-economy modernity equally difficult. Different methodological choices yield radically different pictures.

The post-Black Death period that supplies the best evidence for short working years was itself a demographic catastrophe: the 150-day working year in 1370s England was partly a consequence of the fact that one in three people had recently died. Labor scarcity producing worker autonomy and higher real wages is well-documented in this period—and it was temporary. By the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, as population recovered, wages fell, and the working year lengthened. The "Golden Age of the English Worker" that some historians identify in the decades after the Black Death was bought at an appalling price that no one celebrating the meme acknowledges.

The class dimension is also entirely absent from popular versions of the claim. Medieval agricultural society was not homogeneous. Serfs—legally bound to their lord's land, unable to leave, marry, or inherit without permission—labored under obligations that have no modern equivalent. The most famous studies of "leisured" medieval labor often describe free wage workers, the hired day laborers who had genuine flexibility in how many days they offered to the market. Serfs experienced the calendar differently: their labor obligations were largely fixed duties to the manor, punctuated by religious holidays they could not refuse to observe. Treating a thirteenth-century English villein as having "enjoyed" more holidays than a modern American worker would be an unusual use of the word "enjoyed."

The meme also participates in a long Western tradition of pastoral nostalgia—a romanticized image of pre-industrial life that has recurred in various forms since the Industrial Revolution itself. This tradition has genuine political functions: it challenges naturalized assumptions about work, disrupts the idea that long working hours are simply the price of material prosperity, and provides historical grounding for labor activism. These are not illegitimate uses. But pastoral nostalgia also tends to project modern values backwards in ways that distort historical understanding—treating medieval feast days as equivalent to modern paid vacation, and treating agrarian subsistence labor as equivalent to modern industrial work, are errors that benefit the narrative at the expense of accuracy.

The broader labor history question the meme gestures toward—whether modern workers in wealthy countries work more than their pre-industrial counterparts—is genuinely contested and depends heavily on how you count. If you count only formal wage labor hours, the comparison may favor medieval workers in some periods and regions. If you count total hours of economically necessary activity (including subsistence labor), the comparison likely reverses. If you factor in what those hours were purchasing—material security, health, legal rights, life expectancy—the comparison between medieval and modern labor regimes becomes difficult to reduce to any simple verdict.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published a fact-check on this claim in August 2024, rating it "Mixture" and conducting notably thorough research that included direct interviews with Gregory Clark (the economist whose estimate underlies the claim), Juliet Schor (who popularized it), and Humphries and Weisdorf (whose recent work provides the most sophisticated reconstruction of medieval working years).

The Snopes analysis correctly establishes that the 150-day figure has genuine scholarly origins, that it has some evidentiary support for specific medieval periods (particularly post-Black Death England), and that it coexists with competing scholarly estimates of 250 or more days. Snopes also correctly identifies that economic historians do not support the meme's Church causation story—the number of working days was determined by economic factors, not religious calendar management.

This is a more sophisticated treatment than Snopes typically provides, and the "Mixture" verdict is defensible. Where Snopes' analysis is thinner is on the subsistence labor problem—the fact that "working days" in the meme's sense excludes the unrecorded household labor that filled the gaps in wage work records. Snopes acknowledges this issue briefly but does not develop its significance for the modern comparison, which is arguably the meme's central claim. A fact-checker more focused on the methodological assumptions behind the comparison—rather than the evidentiary dispute within economic history—would have given more weight to this structural problem.

Snopes also does not substantially engage with the class stratification issue: the conditions of free wage workers (whose records supply most of the short-working-year data) versus bound serfs were radically different, and the meme's generalization across all "medieval peasants" is a significant misrepresentation that the "Mixture" verdict somewhat obscures.

The fact-check format constrains these issues in a familiar way. Snopes' task is to render a verdict on whether the claim is true or false; it is less equipped to examine the deeper question of whether a comparison between medieval and modern leisure is even coherent given the structural differences in labor regimes, economic systems, and living conditions. That question requires the kind of sustained epistemological analysis that journalism with a deadline cannot provide—which is precisely what independent review of fact-checking exists to supply.

The medieval peasant meme is a reminder that the most epistemologically difficult claims are not fabrications. They are legitimate scholarly findings, stripped of their methodological caveats, deployed in comparisons their authors never intended, in service of political arguments the evidence does not cleanly support. Debunking these claims as "false" misses the real scholarly dispute. Endorsing them as "true" misrepresents it. The honest answer—limited evidence for specific periods, contested methodology, structurally incommensurable comparison, genuine question about industrial-era labor intensification—is not the answer a meme can carry.
