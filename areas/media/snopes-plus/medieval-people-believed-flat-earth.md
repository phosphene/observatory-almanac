# Medieval Europeans believed the Earth was flat until Columbus

## 1. The Claim

One of the most durable stories in popular history runs as follows: for centuries, ordinary Europeans—and even educated ones—believed that the Earth was flat. Christopher Columbus, in 1492, bravely challenged this superstition by sailing westward into the unknown, risking sailing off the edge of the world to prove that the Earth was round. His voyage overturned centuries of ignorance and opened the modern era of exploration and rational inquiry.

This story appears in textbooks, is repeated by teachers, features in popular films and documentaries, and serves as a foundational metaphor for scientific courage against religious dogma. It is also, in its central historical claim, almost entirely false. Educated medieval Europeans knew perfectly well that the Earth was spherical. The idea that they believed otherwise is a historical fabrication—one with a traceable origin, an identifiable set of 19th-century propagandists, and an ideological function that historians have thoroughly documented.

## 2. What's Actually True

### Ancient Greek Knowledge and Its Medieval Inheritance

The spherical shape of the Earth was established as scientific consensus in ancient Greece by no later than the 4th century BCE. Aristotle, in *On the Heavens* (350 BCE), offered multiple empirical arguments for a spherical Earth: the circular shadow cast on the Moon during lunar eclipses, the appearance of new constellations as one travels south, and the way ships disappear hull-first over the horizon. These arguments were not fringe positions—they were taught in Greek schools and became foundational to the educated Hellenistic world.

Eratosthenes of Cyrene, working around 240 BCE, not only accepted spherical Earth as a given but calculated its circumference with remarkable accuracy using the angles of shadows cast in two Egyptian cities at the same time on the summer solstice. His estimate of approximately 252,000 stades (roughly 39,375 km) compares favorably with the actual polar circumference of 40,008 km—an error of less than 2 percent by some calculations, depending on the length of his stade unit.

This knowledge was transmitted to medieval Europe through multiple channels. Roman authors including Pliny the Elder, Ptolemy, and Strabo all assumed and discussed a spherical Earth. Early Christian scholars inherited and preserved this knowledge. The Venerable Bede (c. 672–735 CE), writing in Northumbria, discussed the spherical Earth matter-of-factly in *The Reckoning of Time*. Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) referenced Earth's sphericity as established fact in his theological and philosophical writings. Dante Alighieri's *Divine Comedy* (c. 1308–1320) is organized around a spherical Earth with Hell descending to its center, Purgatory rising from the opposite surface, and the heavens arranged in concentric spheres—a thoroughly spherical cosmological model.

Medieval European universities, established from the 12th century onward, taught natural philosophy based on Aristotle and incorporated spherical Earth as standard curriculum. Roger Bacon, John of Sacrobosco, and Albertus Magnus all wrote about the Earth's spherical shape as established knowledge. Sacrobosco's *Tractatus de Sphaera* (c. 1230), a widely used university textbook for centuries, begins with the spherical shape of the Earth as its first demonstrated fact.

### What Columbus's Voyage Was Actually About

If educated Europeans already knew the Earth was round, what was Columbus actually arguing with his patrons at the Spanish court? The historical record is clear: the debate was not about whether the Earth was spherical, but about its *circumference*. Columbus argued that the circumference was small enough that a westward route to Asia was practically navigable. The Spanish court's advisors—who had access to better estimates of Earth's circumference—argued that Columbus was wrong and that the ocean between Europe and Asia was far larger than he claimed. The experts were correct. Columbus reached the Americas partly by luck (an unexpected continent happened to be in the way) and partly through errors in his calculations that made him underestimate the circumference by roughly a quarter.

This is not the story of rational courage against superstition. It is the story of an explorer with overconfident calculations convincing skeptical but incorrect-for-different-reasons patrons to fund a voyage that succeeded for reasons he did not anticipate. The flat-earth narrative erases this complexity entirely.

### The Myth's Creation: Washington Irving and the 19th Century

The myth of medieval flat-earth belief can be traced with unusual precision. Jeffrey Burton Russell, a historian of medieval Christianity at the University of California, Santa Barbara, documented the myth's origins in his 1991 book *Inventing the Flat Earth: Columbus and Modern Historians*. Russell identified Washington Irving's 1828 fictionalized biography *A History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus* as the primary origin point.

Irving's book was presented with the trappings of historical research but was largely fiction. In one pivotal scene, Columbus faces a council of clerics and scholars who insist the Earth is flat and warn him he will sail off the edge. This scene has no historical basis—no such council meeting is recorded in any primary source, and no one at the actual Spanish court argued for a flat Earth. Irving invented it for dramatic effect, but readers took it as historical fact.

The myth was subsequently amplified by two 19th-century writers with explicit ideological agendas. John William Draper's *History of the Conflict Between Religion and Science* (1874) and Andrew Dickson White's *A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom* (1896) presented the flat-earth belief as a characteristic medieval Christian position, using it as evidence for their "conflict thesis"—the idea that religion and science are inherently in opposition and that religious dogma has consistently impeded scientific progress. Both books were enormously influential and are now recognized by historians of science as ideologically driven distortions.

The irony is sharp: the conflict thesis itself—the idea that medieval Christianity suppressed spherical-Earth knowledge—was a piece of 19th-century Protestant anti-Catholic propaganda that required inventing the very ignorance it claimed to document.

### The Actual Historical Debate: Circumference, Not Shape

The genuine medieval scholarly debates about Earth's geometry concerned its size and the habitability of various zones, not its basic shape. Some scholars debated whether the antipodal regions could be inhabited by humans (which had theological implications for the universality of Adam's lineage and Christ's redemption). Others worked to refine estimates of Earth's circumference. None of this involved uncertainty about the basic spherical shape.

Flat-earth ideas were held by a small minority of early Christian writers—most notably Lactantius and Cosmas Indicopleustes—but these were recognized as eccentric positions even in their own time, not mainstream views. Medieval scholars who encountered these texts generally disagreed with them. The flat-earth minority was peripheral, not central, to medieval natural philosophy.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### The Power of Origin Myths

The flat-earth myth functions as an origin story for the modern world: Columbus as rational hero overcomes medieval superstition and opens the age of exploration and science. Origin myths serve important cultural functions—they provide clear narratives about where we came from and who we are. A culture that sees itself as defined by scientific rationalism and the overcoming of religious ignorance will tend to produce and preserve myths that dramatize exactly this transition. The Columbus story serves this function beautifully, which is why it has been so resistant to correction.

### Textbook Inertia

Once a myth is embedded in educational materials, it becomes extremely difficult to dislodge. Teachers teach what they were taught; textbooks draw from earlier textbooks; curricula are slow to update. The flat-earth myth entered American textbooks in the late 19th century, when Draper and White's conflict-thesis arguments were at their most influential, and remained there for generations despite historians' efforts to correct it. The myth has been criticized in the scholarly literature since at least the 1940s, but correction travels much more slowly than misconception.

### Temporal Flattening of History

The flat-earth myth also reflects a broader tendency to compress and simplify the past into a single undifferentiated "before modernity." People often project a general sense of historical ignorance backward onto any period before the Renaissance or Scientific Revolution, populating that period with beliefs that seem suitably primitive. The idea that people in 1200 CE would believe in a flat earth feels plausible to someone who holds a vague picture of medieval Europe as uniformly superstitious, illiterate, and scientifically naive. The actual complexity of medieval scholarship—the universities, the natural philosophy, the careful engagement with Greek and Arabic scientific texts—does not fit this caricature.

### 19th-Century Propaganda Still in Circulation

The Draper-White conflict thesis has been thoroughly rejected by contemporary historians of science, including figures like David Lindberg, Ronald Numbers, and Peter Harrison. But the books themselves remain in print and in circulation, and their core claims continue to be repeated in popular discourse. The specific claim that medieval Christians believed in a flat Earth was used so effectively as anti-religious propaganda that it became embedded in secular popular culture as a matter of course, where it persists independently of any current ideological agenda.

### Columbus as National Hero

In the United States, Columbus Day was established as a federal holiday in 1937, and Columbus's story has been central to Italian-American cultural identity and American national mythology. The dramatized version of Columbus as rational hero requires the foil of medieval superstition. Revising that story requires revising a story that carries significant cultural and emotional investment, which creates resistance beyond simple factual correction.

## 4. Verdict

**False.**

The claim that medieval Europeans believed the Earth was flat until Columbus is false as a historical statement. Educated medieval Europeans knew the Earth was spherical; this was mainstream natural philosophy inherited from ancient Greece, transmitted through Roman and early Christian scholarly traditions, and taught in medieval universities. The Venerable Bede, Thomas Aquinas, Dante, and virtually every significant medieval intellectual figure assumed a spherical Earth as a matter of course.

The myth of medieval flat-earth belief was primarily created by Washington Irving in an 1828 work of fiction and amplified by Draper and White in the 1870s–1890s as ideological ammunition for the conflict thesis—the claim that religion and science are inherently opposed. Historians of science have rejected the conflict thesis and documented the flat-earth myth's origins in detail, but popular culture has been slow to update.

Columbus's actual disagreement with Spanish court advisors was about the *circumference* of the Earth, not its shape. His advisors—who were closer to correct—argued that the ocean to Asia was too large to cross. Columbus underestimated the circumference significantly; he reached the Americas because the continent happened to be there, not because he proved the Earth was round.

This claim earns a **Refuted** classification (EARC: R): the historical consensus is clear, the myth's origins are documented, and the primary sources for what medieval scholars actually believed are directly accessible.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### The Conflict Thesis and Its Legacy

The conflict thesis—the narrative framework that generated the flat-earth myth—was enormously influential throughout the 20th century and continues to shape popular understanding of the relationship between science and religion. The thesis holds that these two domains are fundamentally and inevitably in conflict, and that throughout history, religious authorities have suppressed scientific inquiry. The flat-earth myth was one of several historical examples used to illustrate this narrative.

Historians of science have spent decades documenting the empirical failures of this thesis. The Galileo affair—often cited as the paradigmatic science-religion conflict—was considerably more complicated than the simple "church suppresses heliocentric theory" narrative, involving court politics, personal rivalries, and Galileo's own combative personality in addition to theological objections. The relationship between medieval Christianity and natural philosophy was substantially more collaborative and institutionally supportive than the conflict thesis implies; it was, after all, Christian monasteries and cathedral schools that preserved classical texts and medieval universities—explicitly Christian institutions—that taught Aristotelian natural philosophy including spherical Earth.

None of this is to deny that science and religion have sometimes conflicted, or that religious authorities have sometimes impeded specific scientific advances. But the blanket conflict thesis, and the specific historical examples used to support it, have not survived scholarly scrutiny.

### Epistemological Loading

The flat-earth myth is classified under **epistemologically-loaded** gap category because its persistence and political function are inseparable from its factual content. Correcting the myth is not merely a matter of updating a historical fact—it requires engaging with a narrative framework (secular rationalism vs. religious superstition) that many people hold as a core component of their worldview. Pushback against the correction ("are you defending medieval Christianity?") is predictable and reflects the myth's ideological loading.

This loading also makes the myth particularly resistant to casual correction. Someone who learns that medieval scholars actually knew the Earth was spherical may unconsciously reframe this as an exception—"well, the educated ones knew, but ordinary people believed in a flat Earth"—without any evidence for this new claim. The correction generates new myths to defend the old function.

### Medieval Intellectual Culture

The flat-earth myth causes collateral damage to popular understanding of medieval intellectual culture. Medieval Europe produced the university system that generated modern academia, sophisticated theological and philosophical systems that engaged directly with Greek and Arabic scientific knowledge, practical mathematics and engineering that built the Gothic cathedrals, and astronomical observations that contributed to later scientific revolutions. Roger Bacon in the 13th century wrote about experimental method; Hildegard of Bingen produced systematic natural history; Islamic scholars preserved and extended Greek mathematics and astronomy in ways that reached Europe through Spain and Sicily. Reducing this to a period of flat-earth-believing ignorance is not just wrong about the flat-earth question—it distorts an entire millennium of intellectual history.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

### What Major Outlets Have Done

Unlike many misconceptions in the Truth Vault, the medieval flat-earth myth has received some direct attention from popular science writers and historians, though not typically from formal political fact-checkers like Snopes or PolitiFact (whose focus is primarily on contemporary claims). The myth has been addressed in pieces at *Scientific American*, *History Today*, and various academic public-facing publications.

The consistent finding across these accounts is that the myth is false and its origins traceable. Jeffrey Burton Russell's *Inventing the Flat Earth* (1991) remains the definitive scholarly treatment and is the primary source any rigorous fact-check should cite.

### Why Political Fact-Checkers Rarely Address It

The myth's absence from major fact-checking databases reflects the gap between political fact-checking and historical misconception correction. Snopes, PolitiFact, and similar outlets are primarily organized around contemporary political claims—statements by politicians, viral social media posts, and topical misinformation. Historical misconceptions that are not currently being used in a specific political argument tend not to trigger formal fact-check entries, even when they are widely held and demonstrably false.

### How the Claim Would Be Rated

If rated formally, the claim would receive a **False** verdict without qualification. The evidence that educated medieval Europeans knew the Earth was spherical is overwhelming and available in primary sources; the myth's origins are documented by Russell and subsequent historians; and there is no credible scholarly defense of the flat-earth belief as a medieval mainstream position. The most that could be said for any qualifier is that *some* very early Christian writers held flat-earth views—but this is a weak minority position from the patristic era, not characteristic of medieval scholarship.

### A Note on Primary Sources and Verification

Any journalist or researcher verifying this claim can check primary sources directly. Bede's *De Temporum Ratione* is available in English translation. Sacrobosco's *Tractatus de Sphaera* is available in translation and begins with spherical Earth as its opening premise. Aquinas references spherical Earth multiple times in the *Summa Theologica*. The Dante *Commedia* provides a vivid literary illustration of the medieval spherical cosmology. These sources are not obscure; they are the canonical texts of medieval intellectual culture. Any secondary source claiming medieval flat-earth belief would need to grapple with all of them.
