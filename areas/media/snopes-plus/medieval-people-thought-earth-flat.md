# Did Medieval People Think the Earth Was Flat?

## 1. The Claim

Medieval Europeans believed the Earth was flat. This is one of the most widely repeated historical misconceptions in popular culture, deployed to illustrate the general scientific ignorance of the medieval period and the transformative effect of Renaissance and early modern science on human understanding of the natural world. It appears in textbooks, popular histories, and casual conversation as though it were established fact.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

### Ancient Greek Consensus on the Spherical Earth

The spherical Earth was not a discovery of the Renaissance. It was the established consensus of educated ancient Greeks at least from the fourth century BCE. Pythagoras is sometimes credited with first proposing a spherical Earth, though the attribution is uncertain. Aristotle (384–322 BCE) offered multiple empirical arguments for Earth's sphericity in *De Caelo* (On the Heavens): the circular shadow Earth casts on the Moon during lunar eclipses; the different positions of stars observed as one travels north or south; and the way ships disappear hull-first over the horizon. These are observational arguments, not merely theoretical speculation.

Eratosthenes of Cyrene (~276–195 BCE), working at the Library of Alexandria, performed the most famous ancient measurement of Earth's circumference. He observed that on the summer solstice at Syene (modern Aswan), the sun was directly overhead — a vertical stick cast no shadow. At Alexandria, roughly 800 km to the north, a stick at the same time cast a shadow of about 7.2 degrees. Since 7.2 degrees is approximately 1/50 of a full circle, Eratosthenes concluded that Earth's circumference was 50 times the distance from Alexandria to Syene — approximately 250,000 stadia. Depending on which stadion measure he used, this converts to somewhere between 39,375 km and 46,620 km, compared to the actual polar circumference of 40,008 km. Eratosthenes did not merely assert a spherical Earth; he measured it.

Strabo, Pliny the Elder, Ptolemy — the major geographers and astronomers of the ancient world — all assumed a spherical Earth as a foundational premise. Ptolemy's *Almagest* (2nd century CE), the canonical astronomical work of the ancient world, is explicitly constructed on the assumption of a spherical Earth and could not function otherwise: the mathematical machinery of Ptolemaic astronomy requires Earth to be a sphere.

### Transmission to the Medieval Period

The medieval European scholarly tradition inherited the spherical Earth from antiquity. This transmission happened through multiple channels:

**Claudius Ptolemy** (c. 100–170 CE): The *Almagest* and the *Geography* were foundational texts for medieval astronomy and cartography. Both assumed a spherical Earth. Medieval astronomers who used Ptolemaic tables to calculate planetary positions, predict eclipses, and construct astronomical instruments (including the astrolabe) were doing calculations that only make sense on a spherical Earth.

**Macrobius and Martianus Capella** (5th century CE): These late Roman writers summarized the Greek astronomical tradition for medieval audiences. Macrobius' commentary on Cicero's *Dream of Scipio* described a spherical Earth within a spherical cosmos, gave estimates of Earth's size, and discussed the climate zones.

**The Venerable Bede** (672–735 CE): Bede, the most important scholar of the early medieval period in England, wrote in *De Temporum Ratione* (On the Reckoning of Time) that Earth was a sphere — *sphaericus* — and used this fact in his astronomical calculations for computing Easter dates. He was writing two centuries before what is sometimes called the "scientific dark age" that never actually happened.

**Thomas Aquinas** (1225–1274 CE): The most influential philosopher-theologian of the high medieval period cited Aristotle's spherical-Earth arguments as standard natural philosophy in his *Summa Theologica* and other works.

**Dante Alighieri** (1265–1321 CE): Dante's *Divine Comedy* is built on the explicit cosmology of a spherical Earth surrounded by spherical heavens. The geography of Purgatory is set on the opposite side of the globe from Jerusalem; the journey up through the heavens presupposes a specific cosmological model requiring a spherical Earth at the center.

**Roger Bacon** (c. 1214–1292 CE): Bacon argued explicitly for a spherical Earth in his *Opus Majus* and estimated that the ocean between Spain and Asia was small enough to be navigable — an argument that Columbus, 200 years later, used in seeking funding for his westward voyage.

### The Medieval University Curriculum

The medieval university system taught natural philosophy — which included astronomy — as part of the standard curriculum. The *quadrivium* (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy) was required for the degree of Master of Arts. Astronomy as taught in medieval universities assumed a spherical Earth because it was based on Ptolemaic astronomical tables, which require it. Students at Paris, Oxford, Bologna, and other universities in the 13th and 14th centuries were not being taught that the Earth was flat; they were being taught mathematical astronomy that presupposed its sphericity.

John Sacrobosco's *Tractatus de Sphaera* (c. 1230), a standard astronomical textbook used throughout European universities from the 13th through the 17th centuries, opens with an explicit proof that the Earth is a sphere. It was among the most widely copied and printed astronomical texts of the medieval and early modern periods.

### What Columbus Was Actually Arguing

The popular version of the Columbus story — that he proved the Earth was round by sailing west — is itself a myth downstream of the flat-Earth myth. Columbus's contemporaries did not dispute the sphericity of the Earth. What they disputed was his estimate of Earth's circumference and consequently the distance from Spain to Asia by the westward route.

Columbus had adopted a miscalculation: he used a smaller value for a degree of latitude than the Arabic geographers' best estimate, and he conflated different units (the Arabic mile versus the Roman mile) in a way that made his estimated distance to Asia about 40% shorter than it actually was. His critics at the Council of Salamanca (1486–1487) were correct on the astronomy: they argued, rightly, that the westward ocean was much larger than Columbus claimed, and that ships of his day could not cross it. Columbus was wrong; he succeeded only because an unexpected continent was in the way.

The flat-Earth version of this story was invented to provide Columbus with a more dramatic adversary than "Spanish academics who correctly understood the geometry of the globe."

## 3. Why People Believe This

### Washington Irving's Historical Novel

The modern flat-Earth myth about Columbus traces primarily to Washington Irving's *A History of the Life and Voyages of Christopher Columbus* (1828). Irving was a novelist — the author of *Rip Van Winkle* and *The Legend of Sleepy Hollow* — writing what he presented as a historical biography but which was substantially fictionalized. Irving invented a dramatic scene at the Council of Salamanca in which monks and theologians argued that the Earth was flat and that Columbus would fall off the edge. No contemporary source supports this account; Irving fabricated it.

Irving's book was enormously popular and was read, in many countries, as a factual history. Its flat-Earth detail was extracted and repeated as historical fact, laundered through subsequent popular histories until it lost its origin in fiction.

### John William Draper and the Conflict Thesis

The flat-Earth myth was systematized and given ideological weight by John William Draper's *History of the Conflict Between Religion and Science* (1874) and Andrew Dickson White's *A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom* (1896). These books advanced what historians of science now call the "conflict thesis" — the claim that Christianity and science have been in perpetual, fundamental opposition throughout history, with religion consistently suppressing scientific knowledge.

The conflict thesis served the ideological needs of the late 19th century, when science was asserting institutional authority over religion in education and public life. A simple narrative of religious ignorance overcome by scientific progress provided both a justification for secularization and a coherent historical story. The flat-Earth belief was a useful data point: it represented exactly the kind of irrational, religiously motivated error that the conflict thesis required medieval people to have made.

Historians of science have substantially dismantled the conflict thesis since the 1970s, most thoroughly in David Lindberg and Ronald Numbers' edited volume *God and Nature* (1986) and in subsequent work by historians including Lawrence Principe, Peter Harrison, and John Hedley Brooke. The consensus among professional historians of science is that the relationship between Christianity and natural inquiry in the medieval period was complex, varied, and frequently collaborative — not characterized by systematic suppression.

### Projection of Modern Populism onto Historical Elites

The flat-Earth myth also reflects a kind of retrospective populism — the assumption that ordinary medieval people, lacking modern education, must have believed obviously false things about the world. This conflates the beliefs of learned elites (about whom we have substantial documentary evidence) with the beliefs of non-literate peasants (about whom we have almost no direct evidence concerning cosmological beliefs).

What the peasant majority of medieval Europe believed about the shape of the Earth is genuinely unknown. They may have had no strong opinion; the question may have been irrelevant to practical life. What is clear is that educated medieval Europeans — those whose beliefs are documented in manuscripts, sermons, theological treatises, and astronomical texts — believed the Earth to be a sphere. The myth that medieval people thought the Earth was flat is a myth about the educated class, and for the educated class it is simply false.

## 4. Verdict

**Roundly false — and epistemologically loaded.** Educated medieval Europeans knew the Earth was spherical. This was the consensus of ancient Greek philosophy and science, preserved and taught throughout the medieval period via Ptolemy, Bede, Aquinas, Dante, and the university curriculum. The flat-Earth myth was largely invented by Washington Irving in 1828 and systematized by the 19th-century "conflict thesis" as an illustration of religious obscurantism. The myth says more about 19th-century ideology than about medieval cosmology.

The EARC designation is R (Revisionist) because the claim requires actively rewriting documented history and cannot be sustained by any serious engagement with medieval sources.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### What Medieval People Actually Got Wrong

The spherical-Earth myth displaces attention from the things medieval natural philosophers actually got wrong, which are both more interesting and more instructive. The Ptolemaic astronomical model — with Earth at the center, surrounded by crystalline spheres carrying the planets — was wrong, and it was known to be mathematically problematic (the equant, epicycles, and deferents were clearly ad hoc adjustments). Aristotelian physics — impetus, natural places, the impossibility of a vacuum — was wrong and blocked certain lines of inquiry. Humoural medicine was wrong and led to harmful practices.

These errors did not result from religious suppression of science; they were the inherited errors of the ancient tradition, which was both the basis of medieval science and its limiting framework. Medieval natural philosophers were working within the best available inherited knowledge base, constrained by the lack of experimental instrumentation, limited mathematical tools, and the authority of classical texts. This is a historically accurate and humanly sympathetic account. It does not require a flat Earth.

### The Epistemological Loading

This entry uses the "epistemologically loaded" category because the flat-Earth myth does not merely assert a false fact; it structures an interpretation of history. Believing medieval people thought the Earth was flat commits one to a narrative in which scientific progress is a story of overcoming religious ignorance, in which the pre-modern past is a uniform dark age, and in which modern scientific culture represents a fundamental break from a credulous history.

This narrative is epistemologically consequential: it shapes how people think about the relationship between religion and science, about historical progress, and about the sources of ignorance. Correcting the flat-Earth myth is not merely correcting a factual error; it is dismantling a narrative framework that systematically distorts how people understand intellectual history.

### The Conflict Thesis and Its Legacy

The conflict thesis of Draper and White has been formally rejected by virtually all professional historians of science, but it persists in popular culture, in science education, and in the rhetoric of science advocates who use it to argue for secular institutions. The Galileo affair — in which the Catholic Church condemned heliocentrism — is the other great case study of the conflict thesis, and it suffers from the same problem of oversimplification: the Galileo case was entangled with theological, political, and personal factors that resist reduction to "science versus religion."

The persistence of the conflict thesis, despite its rejection by specialists, is itself an epistemological puzzle. It appears to satisfy a narrative need — for a clear moral story about progress — that makes it resistant to correction even when the factual basis is removed.

### Columbus and the Geography of Error

The Columbus case provides an instructive example of how myths about historical ignorance can invert the actual distribution of error. Columbus was wrong about Earth's size. His critics were right. The prevailing narrative — Columbus brave against ignorant opposition — requires inverting the epistemic positions of all parties.

This inversion serves a cultural function: it makes Columbus a hero of the Enlightenment story rather than a lucky navigator who miscalculated and survived because a continent was in the way. The heroic Columbus story requires ignorant opponents; the flat-Earth myth provides them. Removing the myth means reckoning with a Columbus who was not vindicated by his voyage — he never accepted that he had reached a previously unknown continent rather than the Asian coast — and who succeeded despite rather than because of his geographical reasoning.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

### The Snopes Gap

The specific claim "medieval people believed the Earth was flat" does not appear to have a dedicated Snopes entry. This may be because it is so thoroughly embedded in popular culture as received wisdom that it does not typically appear in the viral-rumor format that triggers Snopes investigations. It is background noise rather than a specific viral claim.

Several academic and popular science outlets — including *Scientific American*, *History Today*, and the History of Science Society — have published corrections. The Wikipedia article on the "myth of the flat Earth" is unusually well-sourced for the topic and has been maintained to reflect the scholarly consensus.

### How Snopes Would Rate It

If Snopes were to address this claim, the appropriate verdict would be "False" — straightforward enough given the unambiguous documentary record. The nuance worth adding is the epistemological context: why the myth exists, what narrative purpose it serves, and why it is so resistant to correction despite being so thoroughly refuted.

### The Historian's Responsibility

Historical claims present particular challenges for fact-checkers. Unlike scientific claims, which can be evaluated against current evidence, historical claims require knowledge of primary sources, scholarly literature, and historiographical context that most fact-checkers — trained in journalism rather than historical scholarship — do not possess.

The flat-Earth case is actually an easy one: the sources are abundant, accessible, and unambiguous. The medieval spherical-Earth consensus is not a matter of specialist interpretation; it can be confirmed by anyone who reads Bede, Aquinas, Dante, or Sacrobosco. The more difficult historical myth-busting involves cases where the evidence is thinner, interpretation is genuinely contested, and the dominant popular narrative has some legitimate historiographical support.

### The Myth-of-the-Dark-Ages Problem

The flat-Earth myth is one instance of a broader "myth of the Dark Ages" — the idea that medieval Europe was a period of intellectual stagnation and superstitious ignorance, with rational inquiry suspended between classical antiquity and the Renaissance. This broader myth has also been substantially challenged by historians. The medieval period produced major advances in logic (the scholastic method), technology (heavy plow, horse collar, mechanical clock), architecture (Gothic cathedrals), mathematics (adoption and transmission of Arabic numerals and algebra), and natural philosophy (Grosseteste, Roger Bacon, Ockham, Buridan).

Correcting the flat-Earth myth is a small part of correcting this broader narrative. It does not require claiming that medieval Europe was scientifically advanced in a modern sense; it requires accurate description of what medieval scholars actually believed and did, which is historically more interesting and epistemically more honest than the Dark Ages narrative permits.
