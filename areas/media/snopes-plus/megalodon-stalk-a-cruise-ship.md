# Did a Megalodon Stalk a Cruise Ship?


## 1. The Claim

On May 25, 2021, a video was posted to TikTok by musician Alex Albrecht showing what he described as captured footage from the Atlantic Ocean: a large shark, apparently massive in scale relative to the people visible on the deck of the ship, circling slowly at the surface. The size of the animal and the dramatic visual of it passing along the vessel's hull prompted a rapid escalation in the comments: users suggested, with varying degrees of sincerity, that the shark was the now-extinct megalodon — the largest shark and one of the largest fish species to have existed on Earth, estimated to have reached lengths of up to 60 feet in the largest published estimates. The video accumulated over 4.8 million likes.

The claim was straightforward in its popular form: a megalodon had been captured circling a ship. Snopes published a fact-check on May 30, 2021, authored by Madison Dapcevich, rating the claim **False**, with the clarifying context that an authentic video was captured in the Atlantic Ocean but that an expert identified the animal as likely a large female white shark, not a megalodon.

The megalodon claim is false for at least two independent reasons: the animal in the video is identifiable (with reasonable probability) as a large white shark, and megalodon went extinct approximately 3.6 to 3.8 million years ago, with no credible evidence in the paleontological record, the oceanographic record, or any verified biological sample to suggest otherwise. The persistence and recurrence of megalodon survival claims across media formats — in fictional films, "documentary" television, and viral social media content — makes this an important case to examine carefully.


## 2. What's Actually True

**About the video**: The TikTok video posted by Albrecht is authentic footage of a genuine large shark circling a ship in the Atlantic Ocean. The scale of the animal is made legible by the contrast with human observers on deck. Snopes consulted John McCosker, chairman of aquatic biology at the California Academy of Sciences in San Francisco, who assessed the video as showing a large white shark (*Carcharodon carcharias*), not a basking shark (the second-largest fish alive today, *Cetorhinus maximus*, which had also been proposed in TikTok comments as an identification). McCosker's estimate of approximately 18 feet for the animal's length is consistent with the upper range of documented white shark sizes.

The white shark (*Carcharodon carcharias*) is the world's largest known living predatory fish. Verified maximum sizes in the scientific literature are approximately 6.1 to 6.4 meters (20 to 21 feet), with females typically larger than males (average female approximately 4.9 m, average male approximately 3.8 m) (Mollet et al., 1996, *Copeia*). These dimensions are entirely capable of producing the visual impression in the TikTok video, particularly given the psychological tendency to overestimate animal sizes when a size reference (the ship) is known but the relative distances in a lateral water-surface shot are difficult to assess accurately.

White sharks are present in the Atlantic Ocean and regularly approach vessels for various reasons including olfactory stimulation by bilge water, engine vibration that resembles prey distress, or simply exploratory curiosity — behavior documented extensively in the South African and California white shark research literature. The animal's circling behavior in the video is not anomalous for a large white shark encountering a stationary or slow-moving vessel.

**About megalodon**: *Otodus megalodon* (formerly classified as *Carcharodon megalodon*; the reclassification to *Otodus* is based on phylogenetic evidence placing it closer to the *Otodus* lineage than to modern white sharks) is the largest shark known from the fossil record and is considered one of the largest fish ever to have lived. Fossil evidence — primarily teeth, which are the most durable calcified structures in sharks and preserve preferentially in the sedimentary record, and vertebral centra, which also occasionally fossilize — indicates that the species reached lengths of approximately 15 to 18 meters (50 to 60 feet) in the largest individuals, with the standard uncertainty range associated with size estimation from isolated teeth acknowledged in the technical literature (Pimiento & Balk, 2015, *PLOS ONE*).

Megalodon teeth are among the most commonly recovered megafossils from marine sediments globally, including from deep-sea dredging operations, beach deposits, and marine sedimentary exposures on multiple continents. The temporal range of *O. megalodon* extends from approximately 23 million years ago (Early Miocene) to approximately 3.6 to 3.8 million years ago (Late Pliocene), based on the last confidently dated tooth occurrences in well-characterized stratigraphic sections. The extinction date has been the subject of refining study, with Boessenecker and colleagues providing revised age estimates based on more carefully calibrated depositional contexts (Boessenecker et al., 2019, *PeerJ*). The species is extinct. Its extinction falls well before the emergence of modern *Homo sapiens* (approximately 300,000 years ago) and before any of the hominid lineages that would eventually produce the human observers in the TikTok video had evolved.

**The "megalodon is still alive" claim** circulates perennially in popular culture through several channels: sensational cable television "documentaries" (most notoriously, Discovery Channel's *Megalodon: The Monster Shark Lives*, a 2013 fictional dramatization presented with documentary framing that was watched by approximately 4.8 million viewers and generated significant public confusion), the 2018 science fiction film *The Meg* and its sequel, and a recurring pattern of social media posts presenting megalodon teeth or AI-generated images as evidence of extant animals. None of this constitutes evidence for survival because the fundamental biological and physical constraints on megalodon survival are prohibitive.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The megalodon survival claim is one of the most durable cryptozoological beliefs in popular culture, and its persistence despite overwhelming contrary evidence warrants systematic examination. Several distinct mechanisms sustain it.

**The deep-ocean knowledge-vacuum argument**: The canonical popular-culture defense of megalodon survival is some version of the claim that "95% of the ocean is unexplored" and that megalodon could be hiding in the deep ocean. This argument is superficially appealing because it invokes genuine uncertainty — the deep ocean is genuinely undersampled relative to its extent — but is logically flawed in multiple ways that require examination.

First, the "unexplored" framing is misleading. While the deep ocean has not been comprehensively surveyed by submersible or ROV, it is continuously monitored through passive acoustic arrays (the SOSUS network and its successors, initially deployed for submarine detection during the Cold War and subsequently available for oceanographic research), through large-area sonar surveys, through sediment core programs, and through chemical oceanographic sampling that provides proxy information about biological productivity and biomass. A megalodon-sized animal — which would need to reach roughly 15 to 18 meters in length and would need to consume approximately 2,500 to 3,000 kg of prey per week based on scaling from large shark metabolic rate estimates — would generate biological signals detectable through these monitoring systems. The absence of any anomalous acoustic signature, any megalodon-attributable predation event on documented whale populations (which megalodon likely fed on, given the temporal coincidence of megalodon extinction with the diversification of modern marine mammal fauna), and any biological sample from commercial fishing bycatch globally is strong negative evidence.

Second, the deep ocean is not an appropriate habitat for the surface layer predator that megalodon evidence indicates. All recovered megalodon teeth are from neritic (shallow-water coastal) and epipelagic (upper water column) depositional environments. Megalodon preyed on coastal and surface-associated marine mammals — baleen whales, pinnipeds, dugongs — whose remains associate with the same shallow-water and coastal environments. The metabolic requirements of a 15-meter ectothermic shark operating in the approximately 2–4°C waters of the deep abyssal zone are inconsistent with the thermic ecology inferred from the depositional environments of megalodon fossil assemblages.

**Epistemic Novelty — The Unfalsifiability Trap and the Asymmetric Evidence Problem**: The megalodon survival belief exhibits a specific epistemological structure that makes it particularly resistant to correction through standard evidence provision: it is positioned as unfalsifiable by definition by its proponents. Any amount of acoustic, chemical, biological sampling, or fishing bycatch evidence can be accommodated within the survival hypothesis by postulating that megalodon specifically avoids the sensors, specific depths, and specific areas where sampling occurred. This is a general property of cryptid belief systems: they are formulated in ways that designate all negative evidence as insufficient rather than as falsifying.

The epistemically important point is that this unfalsifiability is not symmetric between the survival and extinction hypotheses. The extinction hypothesis generates specific, testable predictions: we should find megalodon teeth only in sedimentary layers dated before approximately 3.6–3.8 million years ago; we should not find megalodon-attributable predation damage on modern whale carcasses; we should not find acoustic signatures consistent with a 15-meter shark; we should not find megalodon DNA in environmental DNA (eDNA) surveys of the world ocean; we should not find megalodon teeth in Holocene sedimentary contexts (i.e., after 11,700 years ago). All of these predictions are confirmed by available evidence. The survival hypothesis, by contrast, makes no specific testable predictions about where or how to detect it; it merely predicts that detection will be difficult enough to explain all absences.

Science does not require exhaustive positive evidence for extinction; it requires the weight of available evidence to favor one hypothesis over alternatives. For megalodon, the weight is overwhelming: the fossil record, the ecology of the inferred habitat, the metabolic requirements relative to available prey, the absence of any credible biological sample from two centuries of intensive global commercial fishing, and the absence of any eDNA signal in multiple ocean survey programs conducted since environmental DNA methodology became available in the 2010s. Jørgensen and colleagues demonstrated the feasibility of detecting large marine vertebrate eDNA in open ocean water samples, establishing the methodological foundation against which the absence of megalodon eDNA signals constitutes meaningful negative evidence (Jørgensen et al., 2019, *PLOS ONE*; Weltz et al., 2020, *PLOS ONE*).

**The Discovery Channel Effect**: The 2013 Discovery Channel special *Megalodon: The Monster Shark Lives* is a documented case of deliberate fictional framing presented with documentary aesthetics, including actors playing scientists and fabricated "found footage." The special was watched by approximately 4.8 million viewers and generated significant public confusion. After substantial criticism from scientists and science communicators, Discovery disclosed that the show was fiction; the disclosure appeared in small text at the beginning of the program. Research on this episode documented that the fictional framing substantially increased viewer belief in megalodon survival despite subsequent correction, consistent with a pattern in which emotionally engaging narratives presented in trusted documentary formats produce durable belief change that is resistant to later debunking (Flanagin et al., 2020, *Journal of Communication*).


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: FALSE**

The animal in the TikTok video is not a megalodon. Expert assessment by the California Academy of Sciences identified it as likely a large female white shark of approximately 18 feet. *Otodus megalodon* went extinct approximately 3.6 to 3.8 million years ago. No credible evidence supports the animal's survival into the modern era; all available lines of evidence — the fossil record's stratigraphic cutoff, the absence of eDNA, the absence of megalodon-attributable predation on modern whale populations, two centuries of intensive global fishing with zero biological specimens — confirm extinction.

**EARC Mode: R — Refuted**

The megalodon survival hypothesis is refuted by multiple independent lines of evidence. The viral claim is false both in the specific assertion (the shark in the video is a white shark, not a megalodon) and in the background premise (megalodon is alive).

**Gap Category: Misidentified Creature / Extinction Denial**

This case combines a specific video misidentification with a broader recurring claim category. The background premise — that megalodon might still exist — is independently significant as a case of popular pseudoscience sustained by deliberate fictional framing in trusted media formats.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The megalodon fascination in popular culture is not without legitimate scientific grounding — the animal was genuinely extraordinary, and the paleontological research on its biology, ecology, and extinction is substantively interesting on its own terms.

The extinction of *O. megalodon* at approximately 3.6–3.8 million years ago coincides with a broader marine megafauna extinction event in the Late Pliocene linked to global oceanic cooling, reduced marine productivity in the low-latitude warm-water habitats that megalodon appears to have favored (based on the latitudinal distribution of its teeth through time), and the diversification of modern cetacean communities — particularly the emergence of baleen whale species capable of migrating to high-latitude feeding grounds unavailable to a warm-water specialist apex predator. Pimiento and colleagues have synthesized the ecological evidence for this extinction causation model, arguing that megalodon's dependence on warm-water coastal nursery habitats and on large coastal marine mammal prey populations made it specifically vulnerable to the oceanographic reorganization of the Late Pliocene (Pimiento et al., 2016, *Journal of Biogeography*).

The size of megalodon, which drives much of its popular fascination, is itself a subject of active research refinement. The conventional estimates historically based on jaw reconstruction (e.g., Randall's 1973 regression method relating tooth height to body length) have been revised through improved morphometric methodology. Shimada (2019, *Historical Biology*) and other researchers have argued for more conservative body length estimates in the range of 14 to 15 meters rather than the 18+ meter upper range that circulates in popular media, though the species undeniably reached sizes far exceeding any living shark. The methodological challenge is inherent: sharks have cartilaginous skeletons that do not preserve well, meaning that virtually all size estimates are indirect, derived from the dimensions of isolated teeth or vertebral centra. A complete articulated megalodon specimen has never been found, and the probabilistic relationship between tooth dimensions and body length carries substantial uncertainty.

The relationship between *O. megalodon* and the modern white shark (*C. carcharias*) is itself genetically and phylogenetically interesting. The reclassification of megalodon from *Carcharodon* to *Otodus* based on morphological and phylogenetic analysis reflects the conclusion that the convergent resemblance of megalodon teeth to white shark teeth represents functional convergence rather than direct phylogenetic continuity. The two sharks occupied similar ecological niches (large apex predators consuming marine mammals) and evolved similar tooth morphology (broad, serrated cutting teeth) independently from different ancestral lineages. This convergence is a textbook case of adaptive convergence in marine vertebrate evolution.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check by Madison Dapcevich is appropriately structured: it confirms the video's authenticity, provides expert identification of the shark species, summarizes the relevant comparative size data for white sharks and megalodon, and establishes the extinction timing clearly. The rating of False is correct and the expert citation (McCosker, California Academy of Sciences) follows good verification practice.

One limitation of the fact-check format as applied here is the relatively brief treatment given to the extinction evidence itself. The claim that megalodon is extinct is stated with appropriate confidence but not supported with the multi-line-of-evidence structure that the underlying scientific case actually enables. A reader encountering this fact-check who arrives carrying the "ocean is unexplored" background belief might accept the specific video identification (white shark, not megalodon) while retaining the belief that megalodon could be alive somewhere. The specific video claim and the background megalodon-survival claim are technically separable, and the fact-check addresses the former more thoroughly than the latter.

The Discovery Channel problem — the role of fictional "documentaries" in manufacturing and sustaining megalodon survival belief — is not addressed in the Snopes fact-check but represents the most important upstream intervention point for this category of claim. The differential capacity of entertaining fictional narratives to produce durable belief change compared to the capacity of subsequent factual corrections to reverse that change is well-documented in the misinformation research literature. Addressing the downstream viral claims (a TikTok video shows megalodon) without addressing the upstream belief-installation infrastructure (fictional cable television presented as documentary fact) treats the symptom rather than the cause. Effective engagement with recurrent megalodon viral claims requires establishing, as durable background knowledge, both the extinction evidence and the specific media history of how this particular pseudoscientific belief was manufactured at scale.
