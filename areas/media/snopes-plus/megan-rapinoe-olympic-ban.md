# Megan Rapinoe was banned from the Olympics


## 1. The Claim

The claim that Megan Rapinoe was banned from the Olympics, or that she faced official suspension from international competition, has circulated in various forms in American political and sports media since 2016. The claim appears as a categorical assertion — "Rapinoe is banned from the Olympics" — and in more granular forms that specify a reason, a source of the ban, or an institutional response. Version one: she was banned or penalised following her decision to kneel during the national anthem before national team games in September 2016, in solidarity with NFL quarterback Colin Kaepernick's protest against racial injustice and police violence. Version two: she was suspended or restricted from participating in the Olympics by US Soccer or the US Olympic and Paralympic Committee (USOPC) as a consequence of political statements. Version three: she was subject to Olympic eligibility restrictions arising from disciplinary actions related to political speech on the national team.

None of these versions is accurate. No ban was imposed. No suspension was enacted. Megan Rapinoe competed for the United States Women's National Team (USWNT) throughout the period in which these claims circulated, including in the 2019 FIFA Women's World Cup — which the United States won, with Rapinoe receiving both the Golden Boot (top scorer) and the Golden Ball (tournament MVP) — and in the 2020 Tokyo Summer Olympic Games, held in July 2021 following the COVID-19 postponement, in which the United States finished third and Rapinoe received a bronze medal. These outcomes are incompatible with any plausible reading of an effective Olympic or international competition ban.

The claim nonetheless persisted across multiple years and generated sufficient circulation to appear in search results, parenting and sports discussion forums, conservative media commentary, and organised social media disinformation campaigns. Its persistence despite easy refutation by reference to Rapinoe's continuing competition record illustrates characteristics of politically motivated sports misinformation: it is primarily propelled by ideological motivation rather than factual credibility, and its primary function is expressive (conveying disapproval of an activist athlete) rather than informational (accurately reporting institutional actions).

The claim also exists in a specific context: Rapinoe is one of the most prominent and politically engaged athletes in US women's sports history, with a public career that has involved sustained advocacy on LGBTQ+ rights, racial justice, equal pay for women athletes, and political commentary including explicitly anti-conservative and anti-Trump statements. She is, in short, a figure who has generated substantial political opposition from portions of the population who both follow sports and share social media. That opposition creates a motivated audience for claims that she has been sanctioned, punished, or restricted from the activities that generate her public platform, even when no such sanctions exist.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The 2016 kneeling protest and actual institutional response.** In September 2016, Rapinoe became the first white professional athlete to kneel during the national anthem in solidarity with Colin Kaepernick, doing so before a National Women's Soccer League game. She subsequently kneeled before a USWNT exhibition match. US Soccer's board of directors responded not by suspending Rapinoe but by adopting a standing national anthem policy, requiring players to "stand respectfully" during the anthem at USWNT games. This policy was adopted in April 2017 (US Soccer Federation, 2017, *US Soccer Federation Board of Directors Meeting, April 6, 2017 — Policy on National Anthem*). Rapinoe complied with the policy for USWNT games thereafter, though she had stated she would not stand when interviewed prior to the policy adoption.

The policy itself became a flashpoint. Rapinoe and other players did not protest the policy publicly during the 2019 World Cup, and no player was sanctioned under the policy. The policy was subsequently suspended by US Soccer in June 2020, following the death of George Floyd and the wave of protest activity that followed, when the federation acknowledged that it had been wrong to require players to stand and issued an apology to Rapinoe and other players it had effectively penalised through the policy (US Soccer Federation, 2020, Statement on the National Anthem policy, June 10, 2020). The reversal received significant coverage and represented an institutional acknowledgment that the organisational response to the original protest had been unjust.

At no point during this sequence was Rapinoe suspended, banned from competition, removed from the national team roster, or subject to any Olympic eligibility restriction. The US Soccer standing-anthem policy was a conduct requirement applicable during USWNT games, not a competition ban.

**Confabulation with actual Olympic protest rules.** In 2021, the International Olympic Committee adopted Rule 50 guidance that had existed in the Olympic Charter in modified form, further clarifying — and ultimately somewhat relaxing — restrictions on athlete demonstrations at Olympic venues and during ceremonies. The rule, which prohibited "demonstrations or political, religious or racial propaganda" in Olympic venues, had been applied to restrict athlete protest activities at previous Games. In 2021, following significant lobbying from athlete groups and human rights organisations, the IOC updated its guidance to permit some forms of athlete expression — including gestures of support for social justice causes — in some contexts (International Olympic Committee, 2021, *IOC Framework on Fairness, Inclusion and Non-Discrimination*, and associated Rule 50 guidance). The USOPC had also established a Safe Sport and Athlete Expression framework that defined permissible and impermissible activities for US Olympic athletes.

Neither the IOC's Rule 50 nor the USOPC framework resulted in any action against Rapinoe. She was not cited, warned, suspended, or restricted under these rules. Conflation of the existence of Olympic speech restrictions with the claim that Rapinoe was specifically subject to such restrictions is a non-sequitur: rules exist, Rapinoe had done things that some commentators believed should trigger rules, therefore Rapinoe must have been restricted. The logic fails at the key empirical link.

**The Tokyo 2020 record.** Rapinoe competed in the Tokyo 2020 Summer Olympic Games from July 21 to August 6, 2021. She was selected to the USWNT Olympic roster, played in group stage matches, scored goals, and received her bronze medal following the United States' third-place finish. Her participation was unrestricted. She was 36 years old at the time of the Games, and she subsequently announced her retirement from professional soccer in October 2023 at the end of her final NWSL season with OL Reign. At no point between 2016 and her retirement in 2023 was she subject to any competition ban from any sanctioning body.

**What facts underlie the claim's construction.** The ban claim, when it has been given specificity in misinformation sources, typically builds from several real but misrepresented facts: (1) US Soccer did adopt the standing anthem policy in 2017; (2) there have been real restrictions on athlete expression under Olympic Rule 50 in some contexts; (3) Rapinoe did make statements that generated significant controversy and official responses; and (4) other athletes in other sports and contexts have been suspended for conduct. The claim reconstructs these facts into a false narrative of punishment by removing the crucial contextual details that distinguish a conduct policy from a competition ban, and by omitting the evidence of Rapinoe's continued, unrestricted international competitive career.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Political motivation and motivated reasoning.** Megan Rapinoe is one of the most politically polarising figures in contemporary American sports. She wore a rainbow-striped jersey, celebrated World Cup victories in explicitly political statements, declined to visit the White House following the 2019 World Cup, made explicitly negative public statements about then-President Donald Trump, and was a prominent national figure who regularly used her sports platform for advocacy on LGBTQ+ rights and racial justice. For portions of the American public who held views opposed to these positions, the emotional and political desire for Rapinoe to face institutional consequences for her activism is strong. The false ban claim fulfils that desired narrative outcome in a form that circulates easily.

Research on politically motivated misinformation acceptance consistently finds that people are more likely to believe and to share false claims that align with their political preferences and dislikes than claims that contradict them, even when the false claims are about easily verifiable factual matters (Pennycook & Rand, 2019, *The Implied Truth Effect: Attaching Warnings to a Subset of Fake News Stories Increases Perceived Accuracy of Stories Without Warnings*, Management Science, 66(11), 4944–4957; Guess, Nagler & Tucker, 2019, *Less Than You Think: Prevalence and Predictors of Fake News Dissemination on Facebook*, Science Advances, 5(1), eaau4586). Rapinoe's case fits cleanly into this pattern: the people who would find it satisfying if she were banned are precisely the people most motivated to share and least motivated to scrutinise a claim reporting that she had been.

**The activist athlete sanction script.** There is a clearly established cultural script around activist-athlete controversy in American sports media: an athlete does something political; officials react; the athlete is disciplined. This script is based on real historical precedents — Tommie Smith and John Carlos were suspended and removed from the 1968 US Olympic team following their Black Power salute on the medal podium; Muhammad Ali was stripped of his boxing licence following his draft refusal. The existence of genuine historical cases of athlete punishment for political expression creates a credible template that makes subsequent false claims of punishment easier to believe. When someone encounters a claim that Rapinoe was banned, the cultural memory of prior athlete sanctions makes it plausible in a way that a ban on a non-activist athlete would not be.

**The epistemic novelty: sports misinformation and plausibilty anchoring.** Most research on misinformation focuses on political claims rather than sports claims. But sports figures who become political figures — as Rapinoe, Colin Kaepernick, LeBron James, and others have — sit at the intersection of two distinct information ecosystems, each with different verification norms. Sports journalism has strong factual verification norms for game results, statistics, and roster composition; it is relatively easy to check whether an athlete actually competed. Political commentary has weaker verification norms for claims about consequences, sanctions, and institutional responses, particularly when those claims fit pre-existing ideological narratives.

The ban claim circulates primarily in the political-commentary ecosystem rather than the sports-journalism ecosystem. In political-commentary spaces, the relevant check — did Rapinoe actually compete in Tokyo? — is less automatic than it would be in a sports coverage context where competition records are immediately accessible. The claim therefore benefits from existing in a context where the most decisive refutation (the competition record) requires a modest cross-ecosystem lookup that not all readers perform. Roy et al.'s research on misinformation correction found that visual and statistical evidence of real-world outcomes is among the most effective correction form for false claims about events — in this case, showing Rapinoe competing, scoring, and receiving a medal in Tokyo (Roy et al., 2021, *Misperceptions about COVID-19 Vaccines across Social Media Platforms and Their Implications for COVID-19 Vaccination Uptake*, medRxiv preprint, later published 2022). But that correction has to be encountered, sought out, or pushed to the believer in the ban claim.

**Disinformation amplification patterns.** The Rapinoe ban claim has been documented in several waves of social media amplification, particularly during the 2019 World Cup — when Rapinoe's public profile was at its highest and her political visibility generated the most controversy — and during the lead-up to and coverage of the Tokyo 2020 Games. Institutional fact-checking by Snopes, PolitiFact, and Reuters Fact Check documented a phase of active amplification in 2021 in which the claim was pushed through networks of politically conservative Facebook pages. These amplification events followed a common pattern for sports-political disinformation: a claim from an obscure or anonymous source is picked up by partisan media aggregators, shared through social network clusters, and amplified in volume to a point where search results return the false claim prominently enough to be encountered by casually searching users.

The network structure of this amplification — conservative audience media → partisan sharing → search result prominence — means the claim's reach does not depend on being believed in detail. It depends on generating enough ambient noise that some users encounter it, decide the core assertion sounds plausible given their political priors, and pass it on. The number of people who believe Rapinoe was banned is probably smaller than the number of people who have encountered the claim, precisely because the competition record provides an accessible corrective. But both the believers and the encountering-without-actively-disbelieving populations are larger than zero.


## 4. Verdict

**False.** Megan Rapinoe was not banned from the Olympics. No ban was imposed. No suspension was enacted by US Soccer, the USOPC, FIFA, or the International Olympic Committee. The claim is directly refuted by her participation in every major international competition during the period the claim circulates about, culminating in her bronze medal performance at the 2020 Tokyo Olympic Games. The foundational factual premise of the claim — that an institutional ban was imposed — did not occur.

US Soccer did adopt a standing anthem policy in 2017, which required players including Rapinoe to stand during the anthem at USWNT games. This policy was a conduct requirement, not a competition ban, and it was subsequently reversed in 2020 with an official apology. The Olympic Rule 50 restrictions on athlete expression are real and have been applied in various contexts, but they were never applied to restrict Rapinoe's competition eligibility. Confabulating the US Soccer anthem policy, the existence of Olympic expression rules, and general controversy about athlete activism into a claim that Rapinoe was banned from Olympic competition requires ignoring her actual competition record and the absence of any documented sanction.

The claim is not a matter of interpretation or legitimate ambiguity. It is straightforwardly false.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Rapinoe ban claim is one instance of a broader and well-documented pattern in which prominent athlete-activists are targeted with false sanction claims. Colin Kaepernick, following his 2016 anthem kneeling protest, was subject to widespread false claims that he had been suspended or banned by the NFL — when in fact he remained a free agent who was not signed by any NFL team through what many observers attributed to an informal blacklisting rather than a formal league action. Caster Semenya, the South African middle-distance runner, was subject to a complex intersection of false claims about bans and the reality of World Athletics hyperandrogenism regulations, making her case a particularly difficult fact-checking challenge where real restrictions and false claims coexisted. LeBron James has been subject to periodic false claims about NBA sanctions related to political speech.

The common structure is: (1) the athlete engages in public political or social expression; (2) controversy follows; (3) false news of official sanction circulates; (4) the false sanction claim is propagated by politically opposed audiences who find the narrative satisfying; (5) the athlete continues to compete, unmolested by the non-existent ban, and the contradiction is acknowledged only by those actively seeking correction.

What makes these cases distinct from simple misinformation about events is that they are misinformation about institutional actions. The relevant factual question is not "did something happen?" but "did an institution do something?" This is harder to check through ordinary experience and requires either direct access to institutional records or reliance on institutional communications. Most people do not check whether the USOPC issued a disciplinary notice; they encounter either the false claim or the correction through their information environment, and which one they encounter depends on which networks they inhabit.

The connection between athlete activism and false sanction claims documents a specific variant of the "silencing narrative" in political discourse. When a figure the audience dislikes continues to speak and act without facing institutional punishment, the false sanction claim provides a satisfying counter-narrative: they were punished; the system did respond; the athlete's public platform was curtailed. The fact that this is false is secondary to its function as narrative closure for the political emotion the athlete generates.

Jonathan Roozenbeek and colleagues have developed the inoculation approach to misinformation — pre-bunking false claims by exposing the logical and rhetorical techniques used to construct them — and the athlete-sanction false claim is amenable to this approach, since the technique (fabricating institutional punishment for a politically unpopular activist) is consistent across many cases (Roozenbeek & van der Linden, 2019, *The Fake News Game: Actively Inoculating Against the Risk of Misinformation*, Journal of Risk Research, 22(5), 570–580). Teaching people to recognise the pattern — "this sounds like a false sanction claim about an athlete I already dislike" and to immediately check the competition record — could reduce the claim's spread more efficiently than individual post-hoc corrections of specific instances.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Rapinoe ban claim has been addressed by multiple professional fact-checking organisations. The general approach has been direct refutation using the competition record: she competed in Tokyo; therefore she was not banned. This is the most accessible and decisive evidence and it is appropriate to lead with it. Supporting evidence includes official USOPC and US Soccer rosters, game logs, official score and medal records from Tokyo 2020, and the absence of any documented disciplinary filing from any relevant sporting authority.

Fact-checkers also typically contextualise what did happen: the US Soccer anthem policy, which was a real event distinct from a competition ban, and the subsequent reversal and apology, which is relevant to accurate understanding of the institutional response to Rapinoe's protest. Including this contextual history prevents the correction from appearing to deny that there was any controversy or institutional response — there was a real anthem policy, it had real effects, and it was ultimately reversed — while clearly maintaining that the controversy did not produce the competition ban that the false claim reports.

The Rapinoe ban case is useful for media literacy curricula because it involves unusually clear falsifiability. Competition results are among the most thoroughly documented facts in professional sports. There is no ambiguity about whether Rapinoe competed in Tokyo 2020 — she is in the official roster, the game logs, the medal records. The claim is not a matter of interpretation or complex empirical inference; it is a direct factual assertion that is contradicted by readily available and irrefutable public record. When students ask why people believe false claims even when refutation is trivial, the Rapinoe ban claim provides a sharp illustration: because the motivation to believe is independent of the evidence, and because the correction requires a lookup (consulting competition records) that is slightly more effortful than receiving the false claim passively through a social feed. The asymmetry between ease of claim reception and ease of evidence-based refutation is at the heart of contemporary misinformation dynamics.
