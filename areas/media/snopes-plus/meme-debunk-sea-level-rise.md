# "A Glass of Melting Ice Proves Sea Level Rise Is a Myth"


## 1. The Claim

A viral meme, recurring across social media platforms in various versions since at least 2008, claims to deliver a definitive physics-based refutation of sea level rise caused by melting ice. The standard form shows a glass filled with water and ice cubes, accompanied by text to the effect of: "Fill a glass with ice and water to the top. Wait for the ice to melt. Did it overflow? No. So explain to me again how melting ice causes sea levels to rise."

Some versions attribute this reasoning to celebrity commentators or television personalities. Others present it as a thought experiment performed by a skeptical science teacher or as the punchline of a casual observation made at a dinner party. The core logical structure is consistent regardless of framing: ice melting in water does not cause the water level to rise (because floating ice already displaces its own meltwater equivalent, per Archimedes' principle), and therefore the scientific claim that melting ice contributes to sea level rise must be flawed or fraudulent.

The implicit syllogism runs as follows: (1) Sea level rise is attributed to melting ice. (2) Melting ice does not raise water levels (as demonstrated by the glass experiment). (3) Therefore, sea level rise from ice melt is physically impossible, and the scientific consensus claiming otherwise is in error. The appeal of this argument lies in its apparent rigor—it invokes a real physical principle, it can be verified by anyone with a glass and access to a freezer, and it positions the person making it as applying basic physics to challenge expert consensus.

Extended versions of the claim argue that climate scientists know about this basic physics and are therefore either incompetent for overlooking it or dishonest for ignoring it. This framing converts a physical question into an ethics question about scientific integrity, which serves double duty: it provides an explanation for why mainstream scientists disagree and it insulates the belief from straightforward correction by framing any counterargument as defensive institutional behavior.

The meme gained particular traction following high-profile statements about sea level rise from scientific bodies including the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, and NASA, which have documented observed sea level increases and projected continued rises. This institutional alignment gave the meme additional resonance in communities already skeptical of those institutions.

It is worth noting what the meme gets right: the underlying physics observation is real. When floating ice melts, the resulting water occupies the same volume as was previously displaced by the ice. The glass does not overflow. Archimedes' principle is not in error. The mistake lies not in the physics but in the analogy—in the assumption that the glass of ice water is a valid model for the Earth's cryosphere and oceans. Understanding why the analogy fails requires understanding the actual sources of observed sea level change, which the meme systematically omits.


## 2. What's Actually True

The "glass of ice water" experiment correctly illustrates Archimedes' principle for floating objects. When ice floats in water, it displaces a volume of water equal to its own mass. When that ice melts, the meltwater exactly fills the displaced volume and the surface level does not change. This is accurate for sea ice—the floating ice found in the Arctic Ocean—and constitutes a real consideration in discussions of sea level change.

However, the glass analogy fails to represent the actual sources of observed sea level rise for three distinct, independently verifiable reasons.

First, the dominant contribution to current sea level rise comes from land-based ice, not floating ice. The Greenland Ice Sheet, the Antarctic Ice Sheet, and mountain glaciers worldwide sit on or above land, not on water. When this ice melts, it flows as liquid water into the ocean as new water mass—water that was not part of the ocean before. This is categorically different from floating sea ice melting: it adds volume to the ocean that was previously stored on land. Church and White (2011, *Surveys in Geophysics*) documented that contributions from glaciers and ice sheets have been consistently positive across the instrumental period, with acceleration in recent decades. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2021) attributed approximately 40% of observed sea level rise to ice sheet and glacier mass loss over 1993–2018.

Second, thermal expansion of ocean water—the physical swelling of water molecules as they gain heat energy—accounts for roughly another 40% of observed sea level rise over the same period, according to the same IPCC report synthesis. Water expands when warmed. The ocean has absorbed approximately 90% of the excess heat energy trapped by increased greenhouse gas concentrations since the mid-20th century (Cheng et al., 2020, *Science*). This absorbed heat causes water molecules to move faster and occupy more volume, raising sea level with no ice involvement whatsoever. No glass-of-ice-water demonstration can model this process because it involves neither ice nor a thermally inert experimental container.

Third, Nerem et al. (2018, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) analyzed satellite altimetry data from 1993 to 2017 and found not only a measured rise of approximately 3.1 mm per year but an acceleration in the rate of rise. An accelerating rate is inconsistent with a dominant mechanism that has been constant (floating ice melt) but is consistent with the actual mechanisms: accelerating ice sheet discharge and ocean warming driven by a progressive increase in atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations. If the glass analogy were correct, the rate of sea level rise would be relatively stable; the observed acceleration is direct evidence that the dominant mechanisms are not the ones the analogy assumes.

Tide gauge records, among the most direct and long-running observational datasets in Earth science, corroborate satellite measurements. Woodworth et al. (2009, *Journal of Geophysical Research*) analyzed tide gauge data spanning multiple centuries from global stations and documented consistent positive trends in sea level, with acceleration beginning in the 20th century. These measurements are independent of climate model assumptions and represent direct physical observation at specific coastal locations.

It is true, and the meme's proponents sometimes discover this independently, that melting Arctic sea ice contributes minimally to sea level rise. The meme is not entirely without physical basis when applied to this specific case. But current scientific accounting does not claim Arctic sea ice is the primary driver of sea level rise—and the fact that the meme's physics is correct in this limited case does not vindicate its conclusion that sea level rise from ice melt is a myth.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemic novelty here is structural: this meme belongs to a category of climate misinformation that achieves persuasive force specifically by being *partially correct*. It does not simply assert a falsehood. It correctly identifies a real physical principle, demonstrates it with a reproducible experiment, and then extends that principle far beyond its valid domain of application. This "valid-principle extrapolation" pattern is distinctive and more resistant to simple debunking than straightforward fabrications, because any correct attempt to challenge it must affirm the physical principle (conceding something to the skeptic) before explaining why the analogy breaks down.

This structure exploits a well-documented feature of human reasoning described in the cognitive science literature as analogical reasoning bias: when a source domain and a target domain share surface features, people tend to assume structural correspondence even when the underlying mechanisms differ (Gentner & Markman, 1997, *American Psychologist*). A glass of water and the global ocean *look* similar enough—water, ice, a container—that the analogy is intuitively plausible without close examination of the relevant differences. The critical differences (land-based ice versus floating ice, thermal expansion, the open non-rigid nature of the ocean) are not visible in the analogy; they require domain knowledge to notice.

The meme also exploits a deficit framing where the apparent simplicity of the physical argument implies that scientists must have overlooked it. This framing is psychologically compelling because it positions the audience as having discovered something that credentialed experts missed, activating what Bromme et al. (2022, *Psychological Review*) have described as "epistemic empowerment" through apparent mastery of a technical issue—a phenomenon commonly observed in science communication research. The feeling of having seen through a deception is cognitively rewarding and difficult to relinquish once established.

Social media distribution mechanics amplify the meme's reach among audiences with pre-existing skepticism about climate science. The format is optimized for sharing: it is brief, visually simple, apparently self-contained, and carries an emotional charge of "aha, got you." These features make it highly shareable within networks where climate skepticism is a social identity marker, because sharing the meme functions as signaling belonging and critical thinking simultaneously. This social function is independent of the truth of the claim and continues operating even after the claim has been debunked within the same network.

A further layer is the implicit accusation carrying the meme: if the glass experiment disproves sea level rise claims, then scientists promoting those claims have either not thought of this obvious test or are ignoring it. This positions acceptance of scientific consensus on sea level rise as credulous or as complicity with institutional deception. For audiences whose trust in scientific institutions has been eroded by other political or cultural vectors, this framing is consistent with their prior model of how science works. Debunking the specific physics is necessary but not sufficient to address this layer of the claim.


## 4. Verdict

**REFUTED** — The "glass of ice water" meme applies a real physical principle (Archimedes' principle for floating objects) to a situation it does not validly describe. Sea level rise as documented by satellite altimetry, tide gauges, and glaciological surveys is driven primarily by: (1) the addition of water mass from melting land-based ice sheets and glaciers, which sit on land and add new water to the ocean when they melt; and (2) thermal expansion of ocean water as it absorbs heat from greenhouse gas forcing. Neither of these mechanisms is modeled by a glass of floating ice cubes. The meme's core physics is accurate in a narrow sense—floating sea ice melt contributes minimally to sea level rise—but this correct partial observation does not refute the broader scientific finding that sea levels are rising, as observed and measured by multiple independent methods. The acceleration in the rate of sea level rise documented by Nerem et al. (2018) is directly inconsistent with the dominant mechanism being one that has been relatively constant, and consistent with the actual documented mechanisms.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Sea level rise is one of the most extensively documented consequences of anthropogenic climate change and one of the most directly relevant to near-future human infrastructure. Coastal cities, island nations, river deltas, and low-lying agricultural regions face concrete planning and investment decisions that depend on accurate understanding of projected sea level changes. Misinformation that suggests the scientific basis for sea level projections is physically incoherent has direct policy implications, as it provides apparent support for inaction on coastal adaptation and greenhouse gas mitigation.

Observed sea level rise since the late 19th century, as documented in the tide gauge record (Church & White, 2011), is approximately 20 cm (about 8 inches), with the rate accelerating through the 20th century and into the 21st. Satellite altimetry, which began in 1993 with TOPEX/Poseidon, has documented a rate of approximately 3.6 mm per year as of the early 2020s (IPCC AR6 WGI Chapter 9, Fox-Kemper et al., 2021). This is not projected rise based on models alone—it is directly measured ocean surface height change corroborated by multiple independent satellite missions and independent tide gauge networks.

The Greenland Ice Sheet has been losing mass at an accelerating rate. Mouginot et al. (2019, *PNAS*) synthesized ice flux and surface mass balance data across 260 outlet glaciers from 1972 to 2018, finding cumulative mass loss of 3,902 ± 342 gigatons over that period, with the rate of loss accelerating from 42 ± 2 Gt/yr in the 1980s to 286 ± 20 Gt/yr in the 2010s. These numbers are not theoretical outputs of climate models—they are derived from direct measurement of ice volume, surface elevation, and flow velocity using aircraft, satellite, and ground-based instrumentation.

Antarctic ice dynamics are more complex. West Antarctica's marine ice sheets are partially grounded below sea level, making them subject to marine ice sheet instability mechanisms that could produce substantially larger sea level contributions under sustained warming. Bamber et al. (2019, *Nature*) analyzed expert elicitation data and found that 5th percentile projections for 21st century sea level rise have increased substantially relative to earlier estimates, largely because of revised understanding of potential Antarctic ice sheet behavior. The "high end" scenarios now extend into ranges relevant to infrastructure planning for major port cities.

Mountain glaciers globally have been retreating at documented rates for over a century, with retreat accelerating in the satellite era. Zemp et al. (2019, *Nature*) reconstructed global glacier mass changes from 1961 to 2016 using in-situ measurements combined with modeled extrapolations, finding cumulative mass loss of 9.6 ± 3.6 mm sea level equivalent over the period, with acceleration in the most recent decades. These glaciers represent significant sources of freshwater for downstream agriculture and urban supply in addition to their contribution to sea level.

The physics the meme gets right—that floating ice melt does not raise sea level—has actually had productive scientific applications. It informs estimates of how much sea level rise would occur from Arctic Ocean sea ice cover loss: the answer is approximately zero, which matters for correctly apportioning contributions from different cryosphere components. Confusion between floating and land-based ice in public discourse—partly perpetuated by media coverage that does not always distinguish clearly between Arctic sea ice and Greenland glaciers—does contribute to some misunderstanding. The meme exploits this confusion while resolving it in the wrong direction.

The attribution of sea level rise to specific causes has itself been an active area of research. The IPCC AR6 synthesizes estimates of the sea level budget—the accounting of how much rise comes from each source—and finds that the individual terms now close within measurement uncertainty, a significant advance from earlier assessments. This means that observed sea level rise can be fully explained by identified contributing processes (thermal expansion, glacier mass loss, ice sheet discharge) with no unexplained residual, which is a strong test of the completeness of the physical understanding.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has addressed variants of this claim and rated them **False**, pointing specifically to the confusion between floating ice and land-based ice as the central error, and noting that thermal expansion is a contributing mechanism entirely absent from the glass analogy. The Snopes treatment correctly identifies that the physical experiment the meme describes is accurate while being inapplicable to the actual mechanisms driving sea level rise.

Climate Feedback, a network of climate scientists who evaluate media coverage of climate claims, has reviewed multiple versions of the sea level meme and reached consistent conclusions: the analogy fails because it excludes the dominant physical mechanisms. Reviewers affiliated with the network have noted that the claim reveals a category of misunderstanding where apparent physical sophistication—invoking Archimedes—masks a failure to account for the actual source geography of the ice under discussion.

The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration maintains a publicly accessible explainer page on sea level rise that specifically addresses the "ice in a glass" confusion, explaining the distinction between floating sea ice and land-based ice and providing accessible quantification of the contribution from thermal expansion. NASA's Climate page similarly includes clear visualization of observed sea level rise from satellite data alongside attribution analysis.

Outreach efforts by scientific institutions have increasingly recognized that correcting the meme requires more than asserting that scientists have accounted for this physics. Effective corrections need to explain why the analogy breaks down at the point of application—which requires communicating the distinction between floating and grounded ice clearly enough that it is memorable and shareable by the people who encounter the meme in their social networks. Research on climate communication by Lewandowsky et al. (2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*) found that misconceptions persist when corrections only negate a claim without providing an alternative causal account, suggesting that effective responses to this meme should lead with the actual mechanisms rather than simply refuting the glass analogy.
