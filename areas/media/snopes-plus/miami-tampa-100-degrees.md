# Miami and Tampa have each had just a single 100-degree day in recorded history?

## 1. The Claim

On July 27, 2025, thermometers in Tampa, Florida reached 100 degrees Fahrenheit for what was immediately claimed to be the first time in recorded meteorological history. Social media posts documenting the milestone spread rapidly across platforms — a single Instagram post drew more than 250,000 likes, a Reddit thread in the Tampa subreddit attracted over 1,000 upvotes, and an X post viewed more than two million times sparked both celebration and disbelief among commenters who found the claim implausible. How could a city in the subtropical American South, notorious for its crushing summer heat, have never crossed the 100°F threshold before 2025?

The viral momentum around Tampa's record amplified a parallel and equally striking claim: that Miami, one of the most famously hot cities in the United States, had itself crossed 100°F only once in more than a century of recorded temperatures. According to meteorologist Alex Libby of Nashville's WKRN, Miami's sole 100-degree day in recorded history occurred on July 21, 1942 — more than eighty years before Tampa finally joined the triple-digit club.

The underlying claim being evaluated is therefore compound: that two major subtropical coastal cities, both internationally synonymous with extreme heat, have each experienced that specific physiological and meteorological threshold of 100°F only once in the full span of their documented weather records. This claim raises immediate intuitive questions. Is this plausible? Is it evidence of climate change? And does the raw temperature record actually tell us what people most want to know about how hot these cities really feel?

The claim became a flashpoint not only for climate discussion but for broader disputes about how weather data is communicated to the public, whether maximum daily temperature is the right metric for understanding urban heat risk, and what the persistence of both cities' near-miss temperature histories reveals about the specific way subtropical maritime climates produce extreme heat through humidity rather than through raw thermometer readings.

## 2. What's Actually True

The core factual claim is straightforwardly correct. According to records maintained by the National Weather Service (2024, "Climatological Records for Miami, FL, 1895–2024"), the maximum daily high temperature in Miami reached 100°F only once in the period from 1896 through 2024: on July 21, 1942. As of the date of publication, Miami had not surpassed 97°F in the 2025 season. For Tampa, records from the National Weather Service (2024, "Tampa Bay Original Climate Page") confirm that the city's highest recorded temperature between 1890 and December 2024 was 99°F, achieved in June 1985 and again in June 2020. Tampa's single crossing of the 100°F threshold occurred on July 27, 2025.

The data source here is the official instrumented record maintained by the National Weather Service, using standardized observation methods at designated official stations. Temperature records of this kind represent the dry-bulb temperature — the direct temperature of the air measured in the shade, away from surfaces, using equipment that is not sensitive to wind or humidity. This is what meteorologists call the official high temperature for a given day.

Why have these cities, both famous for their heat, so rarely crossed 100°F on the official temperature scale? The answer lies in the thermodynamics of coastal subtropical climates. Both Miami and Tampa sit close to large bodies of water — the Atlantic Ocean, the Gulf of Mexico, and the vast expanse of Tampa Bay — which act as thermal moderators. Sea breezes, particularly common in Florida's afternoon hours, regularly push cooler maritime air inland and prevent the inland heat accumulation that drives the extreme dry-bulb temperatures common in cities like Phoenix, Las Vegas, or Dallas. The National Weather Service (2024) notes that these marine influences are the primary explanation for why coastal Florida's maximum temperatures remain suppressed relative to inland subtropical and desert climates even when heat risk is objectively severe.

The claim's accuracy is therefore not in question. The more important story — the one that a simple True verdict obscures — concerns the gap between the official temperature record and the lived experience of heat in these cities.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The surprise people feel upon learning that Tampa had never reached 100°F illustrates a fundamental gap in how official temperature metrics communicate heat risk in humid subtropical environments. The National Weather Service developed the Heat Index — a composite measure that integrates both dry-bulb temperature and relative humidity — specifically to address this gap. First formalized by Robert Steadman in a foundational paper (Steadman, 1979, "The Assessment of Sultriness," *Journal of Applied Meteorology*) and later refined for operational meteorological use, the heat index provides a "feels like" temperature that more accurately reflects physiological heat stress than the dry-bulb temperature alone.

Because high humidity inhibits the evaporation of sweat — the body's primary cooling mechanism — a 90°F day at 80% relative humidity can produce physiological stress equivalent to a dry 106°F day. Coastal Florida, with its ocean proximity and abundant surface moisture, routinely produces heat index values far above the official temperature reading. This creates what might be called the subtropical paradox: cities that feel intensely and dangerously hot, day after day, week after week, without ever officially registering the triple-digit temperature that most people treat as the threshold of extreme heat.

Data compiled by the University of Miami's Atmospheric Sciences department (McNoldy, B., University of Miami, "Miami Heat Index and Dewpoint Climatology") reveals just how systematic this gap is. Miami has experienced 30 or more consecutive days with a heat index of at least 100°F at least three times on record, meaning that for stretches of a month or more, the city felt as hot or hotter than 100°F every single day — while the official thermometer never broke 100°F at all. The National Weather Service (Tampa Bay Weather Office, 2024, "Heat Index Statistics") reports that Tampa has had its heat index surpass 100°F every day of summer at least once historically, and recorded a heat index above 100°F as late as November 6 in 2024 — well into what most of the country would consider autumn.

This epistemic gap — the gap between what the official record says and what human bodies experience — is not merely an academic concern. Heat is the deadliest weather phenomenon in the United States (Donoghue et al., 2003, "Heat-Related Mortality in Philadelphia," *Environmental Health Perspectives*), and public understanding of heat risk is shaped primarily by media coverage that emphasizes the official temperature record. When Tampa finally crossed 100°F and the milestone was widely reported, the coverage implicitly framed 100°F as a threshold that had previously kept heat risk at bay. In fact, Tampa had experienced heat index conditions equivalent to 100°F and above for decades before the thermometer finally confirmed it.

The belief that official temperature accurately captures heat risk in coastal cities reflects a widely shared but systematically misleading intuition. Most people's mental model of temperature is built from experience in drier inland climates or from popular media that routinely conflates temperature with heat danger. When cities like Miami and Tampa are described by national media using the same temperature benchmarks applied to Phoenix or Dallas, the effective heat burden is substantially understated.

The public's surprise at the revelation also reflects a deeper relationship between climate literacy and data interpretation. The claim "Miami has only hit 100°F once" reads as counterintuitive because most people know Miami is hot — but the mechanism producing that heat is invisible to the official temperature record. The heat index, by contrast, is a calculated composite that rarely appears in news headlines, sports weather reports, or the digital displays people check on their phones. It exists as a secondary datum, referenced in heat advisories and emergency alerts, but not part of the ambient cultural measurement system that shapes popular understanding.

The specific claim — that Miami and Tampa have each reached a daily high of 100°F only once in their recorded histories — is true by every standard meteorological measure. The National Weather Service data for both cities is clear, consistent, and authoritative. Miami's sole 100°F day was July 21, 1942; Tampa's was July 27, 2025. These facts are not disputed.

However, the claim carries implicit assumptions that are false in ways that matter significantly for public health and climate communication. The assumption that 100°F represents the meaningful threshold of extreme heat in these cities, that crossing it for the first time in Tampa represents a qualitative shift in risk, or that the rarity of triple-digit temperatures means these cities have been spared the worst of heat stress — all of these assumptions are contradicted by the heat index record.

The deeper truth is that Miami and Tampa have been extraordinary heat cities for decades — they have simply expressed that heat through humidity rather than through raw temperature. The 100°F milestone is real and historically notable. It is not, however, the moment at which Tampa became dangerous. Tampa was already producing heat stress conditions physiologically equivalent to 100°F-plus every summer. What changed on July 27, 2025 was not the severity of the heat burden but its representation on the official instrument.

The Snopes fact-check correctly verifies the factual claim and appropriately contextualizes it by noting the heat index distinction. This context is essential rather than supplementary: without it, a True verdict may inadvertently reinforce the misconception that triple-digit temperatures have been rare events in cities where extreme physiological heat has been routine.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The meteorological history embedded in this claim touches several intersecting domains: the science of humidity and thermal comfort, the sociology of climate communication, the history of heat index methodology, and the evolving relationship between climate change and regional temperature extremes.

**The Science of Subtropical Heat**: The Florida peninsula occupies an unusual position in North American climatology. Unlike the interior Southwest, where extreme temperatures result from low humidity and intense solar loading on dry ground, Florida's heat originates primarily from the moisture dynamics of a narrow peninsula surrounded by warm ocean water. The Gulf of Mexico's surface temperatures routinely exceed 85°F in summer, loading the lower atmosphere with moisture. Florida's afternoon thunderstorm cycle — one of the most reliable on Earth — provides temporary cooling but maintains the high humidity that defines the region's heat character.

This pattern means that Florida's heat is best understood not through the temperature record alone but through measures of total heat burden like wet-bulb temperature or apparent temperature. Research by Sherwood and Huber (2010, "An Adaptability Limit to Climate Change Due to Heat Stress," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) established that wet-bulb temperatures exceeding 35°C (95°F) represent an absolute physiological limit for human survival — a threshold that dry-bulb temperatures dramatically understate in humid climates. Subsequent climate modeling by Im et al. (2017, "Deadly Heat Waves Projected in the Densely Populated Agricultural Regions of South Asia," *Science Advances*) demonstrated that humid heat extremes are increasing globally at rates that exceed dry-bulb temperature changes.

Florida's coastal cities, despite their suppressed dry-bulb extremes, are among the areas expected to experience the most severe physiological heat stress increases under climate change projections, precisely because they combine already-high baseline humidity with rising temperatures. A dry-bulb temperature ceiling imposed by marine breezes offers no protection against heat index increases driven by rising sea surface temperatures and atmospheric moisture content.

**Historical Context of Temperature Record-Keeping**: The fact that Miami's temperature record extends to 1896 and Tampa's to 1890 is itself historically significant. These records were established through the network of Weather Bureau stations created following the Congressional act of 1870 and expanded systematically through the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Early temperature records were taken with liquid-in-glass thermometers housed in Stevenson screens — louvered wooden boxes designed to shade the instrument while allowing free air circulation — that remain the standard housing for official temperature measurements today.

Temperature record continuity across such periods involves substantial methodological complexity. Station relocations, changes in instrument housing, shifts in the time of observation, and urban heat island effects can all introduce systematic biases into long-term records. The National Weather Service follows detailed protocols for data homogenization to maintain comparability across periods, but the resulting official record represents a constructed product of measurement and statistical adjustment rather than a simple reading of unmediated reality (Karl et al., 1988, "Historical Climatic Data Adjustments," *Journal of Climate and Applied Meteorology*). This does not undermine the validity of the Miami and Tampa records, which are among the better-quality long-term records in the national network, but it provides necessary context for treating any single extreme event — including Tampa's first 100°F day — as part of a statistically meaningful trend rather than as an isolated anomaly.

**Climate Change and Extreme Heat Trends**: Tampa's July 2025 milestone arrived against the backdrop of an ongoing trend in global surface temperature increase. Annual mean surface temperatures have risen approximately 1.1°C above the 1850–1900 baseline, with the five warmest years on record all occurring since 2015 (Copernicus Climate Change Service, annual reports, 2024). Regional analyses of the southeastern United States show warming trends in both mean temperatures and the frequency of extreme heat days, though coastal areas like Tampa show smaller increases in dry-bulb extremes than inland areas due to ocean moderating effects.

The probability that Tampa would eventually cross 100°F was increasing with each passing decade, as background warming gradually raised the probability distribution of daily temperature extremes. Research by Diffenbaugh et al. (2017, "Quantifying the Influence of Global Warming on Unprecedented Extreme Climate Events," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*) established that the majority of extreme heat events in recent years have been made significantly more probable by anthropogenic climate change. Tampa's 2025 milestone is consistent with this trend, representing not a random meteorological anomaly but the eventual expression of a decades-long shift in the tail of the temperature distribution.

**Heat Mortality and Public Health Communication**: The public health stakes of heat index literacy are substantial. The Heat Index was introduced into operational meteorological practice precisely because medical research had established that physiological heat strain — the body's inability to maintain core temperature homeostasis — correlates with apparent temperature rather than dry-bulb temperature alone. Research by Bouchama and Knochel (2002, "Heat Stroke," *New England Journal of Medicine*) established the pathophysiology of heat-related illness: when core body temperature exceeds 40°C (104°F), protein denaturation and cell death accelerate rapidly, leading to multi-organ failure within hours without intervention.

The deceptive coolness of official dry-bulb temperatures in humid climates creates a systematic public health hazard. People who hear that the temperature is "only 95°F" may fail to recognize that the heat index is 108°F or 112°F, underestimating the risk of outdoor labor, exercise, or inadequate cooling. Elderly populations, outdoor workers, and residents without air conditioning are disproportionately exposed to this risk (Semenza et al., 1996, "Heat-Related Deaths During the July 1995 Heat Wave in Chicago," *New England Journal of Medicine*). Public health campaigns that communicate risk through dry-bulb temperature alone while cities have experienced heat-index conditions equivalent to 110°F for decades represent a systematic mismatch between measured reality and communicated risk.

**The Jackalope-Like Mythology of Temperature Thresholds**: There is something culturally revealing about the intense public interest in Tampa's first 100°F day. Triple-digit temperatures function in American popular culture as a threshold of genuine climatic extremity — a marker that separates serious heat cities from merely warm ones. This threshold effect explains why the milestone generated so much media attention despite the fact that the additional single degree of temperature (from the previous record of 99°F) carries no special meteorological significance. The cultural weight of the round number 100°F is entirely a function of the decimal system; the Fahrenheit scale's origin in a set of fixed points chosen by Daniel Gabriel Fahrenheit in the early 18th century has no relationship to human physiology or climate risk.

The threshold's cultural weight reveals how deeply our intuitions about climate extremes are shaped by numerical conventions rather than physical reality. In Celsius, Tampa's milestone — approximately 37.8°C — creates no particular cultural moment; in the Celsius countries of Europe, it would not be marked as a round number worth celebrating or fearing. The arbitrariness of the threshold does not diminish its communicative power, but it does underscore the value of moving toward physiologically grounded metrics for public heat risk communication.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim as **True**, with an important contextualizing caveat: although the dry-bulb temperature has reached triple digits only once in each city, the heat index — the temperature it feels like when humidity is factored in — has surpassed 100°F on many occasions in both cities. The Snopes analysis by Emery Winter (published August 26, 2025) draws directly on National Weather Service data for both Miami and Tampa, citing the specific dates of record high temperatures and noting the sources by name.

The fact-check cites the NWS climatological records for Miami (showing the July 21, 1942 record), the Tampa Bay Original Climate Page (showing the June 1985 and June 2020 records of 99°F before the 2025 milestone), and the University of Miami's heat index climatology for context. The sourcing is solid and institutional.

The contextualizing note about the heat index is crucial to the fact-check's intellectual honesty — it prevents the True verdict from functioning as inadvertent reassurance about Florida's heat burden. However, the Snopes treatment understandably does not develop the deeper implications of the heat index distinction for climate communication, public health messaging, or the interpretation of regional climate change trends. These are not criticisms of a fact-check format designed for rapid public verification; they are observations about where the deeper epistemic work remains to be done.

The claim is a useful entry point into one of the more consequential gaps in popular climate literacy: the systematic underrepresentation of humid heat. In a world where climate risk communication increasingly depends on public ability to interpret temperature data, the Miami-Tampa case offers a memorable illustration of why dry-bulb temperature alone is an insufficient guide to lived heat experience in coastal subtropical environments.
