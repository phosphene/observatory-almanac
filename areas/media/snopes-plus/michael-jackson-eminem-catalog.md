# Michael Jackson, Eminem, and the Catalogue That Launched a Thousand Memes

## 1. The Claim

In early 2024, a rumor swept across social media platforms — Instagram, TikTok, Facebook, and Reddit — that wove together two of the most recognizable names in modern music into a single, irresistible narrative of calculated revenge. The story went something like this: In the mid-2000s, Eminem released a song that "dissed" Michael Jackson at a time when Jackson was defending himself against child-molestation accusations in a courtroom. According to the claim, Jackson, rather than responding publicly, stayed silent — biding his time. Then, the story continues, Jackson quietly purchased the rights to Eminem's "whole catalogue," ensuring that every time Eminem performed, a portion of the proceeds would flow to the King of Pop. The implication was unmistakable: Jackson had executed one of the most elegant acts of musical retaliation in history, weaponizing the publishing infrastructure of the music industry against a rapper who had dared to mock him.

The rumor's appeal is immediately obvious. It contains every element of a satisfying cultural narrative: a provocateur (Eminem), an aggrieved icon (Jackson), an apparent silence that masks a deeper strategy, and a comeuppance delivered not through words but through ownership — the ultimate form of power in the music business. It transforms Jackson from a figure under siege into a chess master, and it reframes Eminem's provocation as the opening move in a game he never knew he was playing. The fact that Jackson died in 2009 adds a layer of poignancy: the revenge, if it happened, was set in motion by a man who would not live to see its full fruits.

Snopes, evaluating this claim in February 2024, assigned it a rating of "Mixture" — meaning that elements of the story are true, elements are false, and the overall framing requires significant qualification. As is so often the case with viral music-industry narratives, the truth is not so much a contradiction of the rumor as it is a complication of it. The distance between what happened and what the rumor says happened is not the distance between truth and fabrication; it is the distance between the granular mechanics of music publishing and the narrative shorthand that social media demands.

What follows is an attempt to close that distance — to trace the claim through its constituent parts, to examine what Snopes got right and where the fact-check itself could have gone further, and to situate the entire episode within the larger history of music catalogue ownership, celebrity feuds, and the cultural appetite for stories about powerful people exercising their power in ways that are simultaneously petty and brilliant.

## 2. What's Actually True

The factual core of the rumor rests on two verifiable events: Eminem released a song called "Just Lose It" in late 2004 that referenced Michael Jackson in unflattering terms, and a company in which Jackson held a 50% stake — Sony/ATV Music Publishing — acquired a publishing company called Famous Music in 2007, an acquisition that included rights to Eminem's back catalogue. These are not matters of interpretation. They are documented events in the public record.

Let us take them in order.

**"Just Lose It" and its reception.** The song appeared on Eminem's "Encore" album, released in November 2004. The lyrics are unambiguous in their reference to Jackson, even if they are couched in Eminem's characteristic mode of deflection-through-self-awareness. The opening verse includes the lines: "Come here, little kiddies, on my lap / Guess who's back with a brand new rap? / And I don't mean 'rap' as in a new case / Of child molestation accusations." Later in the track, Eminem offers what he frames as a clarification — or perhaps a provocation dressed as a clarification: "I done touched on everything but little boys / And that's not a stab at Michael / That's just a metaphor, I'm just psycho." The music video amplified the lyrical references considerably, featuring Eminem dressed in Jackson-esque attire, including scenes that parodied the 1984 Pepsi commercial in which Jackson's hair caught fire, as well as other visual references to Jackson's life and public image.

The context in which this song arrived mattered enormously. In 2004, Michael Jackson was in the midst of the legal proceedings that would culminate in the 2005 trial on felony child molestation charges involving Gavin Arvizo. Jackson had previously faced child sexual abuse accusations in 1993, a case that was settled for $22 million without criminal charges being filed. The 2003–2005 proceedings were of a different magnitude: they involved formal felony charges, a trial, and ultimately an acquittal on all counts in June 2005. "Just Lose It" was released into this volatile atmosphere, and regardless of Eminem's artistic intent, the song was received by many — including, as we shall see, Jackson himself — as a mockery of a man under extraordinary legal and personal pressure.

Whether the song constitutes a "diss" in the hip-hop tradition is, as Snopes correctly notes, a subjective judgment. In hip-hop, a "diss track" typically refers to a song whose primary purpose is to attack or disparage a specific individual. "Just Lose It" is more broadly a satirical pop-culture romp — Eminem's stock-in-trade — but Jackson is clearly the primary target among the public figures referenced. The song was not a dedicated diss track in the lineage of "Hit 'Em Up" or "No Vaseline," but it functioned as one for Jackson, who had no comparable tradition of engaging in musical feuds and for whom the references touched on the most serious accusations of his life.

**The Famous Music acquisition.** In 2007, Sony/ATV Music Publishing acquired Famous Music LLC from Viacom. Famous Music was a publishing company with rights to roughly 125,000 songs, including compositions by Eminem, Shakira, Christina Aguilera, and numerous film scores. The acquisition was a major transaction in the music publishing world — the kind of deal that generated trade-press headlines but little mainstream attention at the time. Michael Jackson held a 50% stake in Sony/ATV, a position he had held since 1995 when he merged his ATV Music catalogue with Sony's publishing division in a deal reportedly worth $90 million. (The other 50% was held by Sony.)

This is the factual foundation of the rumor. Eminem released a song that mocked Jackson. A company Jackson co-owned acquired the rights to Eminem's back catalogue. The two events are separated by approximately three years. And that is where the clean facts end and interpretation begins.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes correctly identifies the key distortions in the viral narrative. The most significant of these is the claim that Jackson "stayed silent" after "Just Lose It" was released. He did not. As Snopes notes, The Guardian reported in October 2004 that Jackson threatened to sue Eminem and demanded that all US cable music-video channels cease showing the video. Jackson's statement was direct and forceful: "I am very angry at Eminem's depiction of me in his video. I feel that it is outrageous and disrespectful." This is not the response of a man practicing strategic silence; it is the response of a man who was publicly and vocally outraged. The rumor's narrative — silence followed by stealthy financial maneuvering — requires Jackson to have been quiet when he was in fact loud.

Snopes also correctly notes that the acquisition of Eminem's catalogue was not a personal purchase by Jackson. It was a corporate transaction by Sony/ATV, a joint venture in which Jackson held a 50% stake. The decision to acquire Famous Music was a business decision made by a corporation, not an act of personal revenge executed by an individual. Sony/ATV was run as a professional publishing operation with its own management, its own strategic priorities, and its own fiduciary obligations. Jackson was a co-owner, but there is no evidence that he directed the Famous Music acquisition or that the acquisition was motivated by his feelings about Eminem.

Where Snopes' analysis could have gone further is in several directions that would have enriched the context considerably.

**First, the scale and nature of Jackson's publishing empire.** Snopes mentions that Jackson held a 50% stake in Sony/ATV but does not fully convey what this meant. Jackson's entry into music publishing began in 1985, when he acquired ATV Music Publishing — the company that owned the Northern Songs catalogue, which contained the publishing rights to the majority of the Beatles' songbook — for $47.5 million. This acquisition, which reportedly came at the suggestion of Paul McCartney himself (who had advised Jackson that music publishing was where the real money was), was one of the most consequential moves in the history of music business. It gave Jackson ownership of some of the most valuable copyrights in popular music and established him not merely as a performer but as an intellectual-property magnate.

The merger of ATV with Sony's publishing division a decade later created Sony/ATV, which became one of the largest music publishers in the world. By the time of the Famous Music acquisition in 2007, Sony/ATV administered hundreds of thousands of songs across virtually every genre. The Famous Music deal was not an anomaly or a targeted acquisition; it was a routine — if large — expansion of an already enormous catalogue by a company whose business model was the accumulation of music copyrights. The Eminem catalogue was a component of this deal, not its purpose. Famous Music's value lay in its breadth — 125,000 songs — not in any single catalogue within it.

Snopes could have made this point more emphatically: the structure of the rumor implies a targeted act of revenge, but the structure of the acquisition reveals a portfolio acquisition in which Eminem's songs were a fraction of a percent of the total value. This is not to say that Jackson was unaware that the deal included Eminem's catalogue — co-owners of publishing companies are generally informed of major acquisitions — but there is a vast difference between being aware that a deal includes a particular catalogue and orchestrating the deal to acquire that catalogue.

**Second, the "Just Lose It" incident in the broader context of Jackson's public life.** Snopes provides the essential timeline of Jackson's legal troubles but could have situated the Eminem incident more fully within the pattern of Jackson's responses to mockery and parody throughout his career. Jackson was a figure who had been subjected to decades of satire, from comedians to tabloid caricatures to, notably, Eminem's predecessor in musical parody — "Weird Al" Yankovic, who parodied Jackson's "Beat It" as "Eat It" and "Bad" as "Fat." Jackson reportedly enjoyed Yankovic's parodies, or at least tolerated them graciously. The difference with Eminem was not the act of parody itself but the specific content: Eminem's references were to child molestation, the most damaging accusation Jackson faced. Jackson's outrage was not about his image being used; it was about his image being connected to the specific allegations that threatened his freedom and his legacy.

**Third, the question of timing and causation.** Snopes states that "there is no evidence to assess whether the song had anything to do with the media-rights acquisition." This is technically correct — there is no smoking-gun document linking Jackson's anger over "Just Lose It" to the Famous Music deal. But the framing of this absence is important. In the absence of evidence, the rumor fills the gap with narrative causation: Jackson was angry, therefore he bought the catalogue. The actual chain of causation is almost certainly more banal: Sony/ATV was in the business of acquiring catalogues, Famous Music was available, the deal made financial sense, and it included — among 125,000 songs — some of Eminem's compositions. The coincidence is real, but coincidence is not causation.

**Fourth, the broader culture of music-industry revenge narratives.** Snopes does not situate this rumor within the larger pattern of pop-culture stories about musicians using business acumen as a form of retaliation. The most famous precedent is, of course, Jackson's own acquisition of the Beatles catalogue, which was widely interpreted — then and now — as Jackson one-upping Paul McCartney, who had wanted the catalogue for himself. The narrative that Jackson "took" the Beatles songs from McCartney is a simplification of a complex business transaction, but it has become culturally entrenched, and the Eminem rumor draws on this established narrative template. The rumor works because audiences have been primed to believe that Michael Jackson used music publishing as a weapon, and the Eminem story slots neatly into that preexisting frame.

## 4. Why This Matters

The endurance of this rumor — its ability to circulate widely on social media two decades after the events it describes, and nearly fifteen years after Jackson's death — says something important about how popular culture processes the relationship between art, commerce, and personal conflict.

At its core, the rumor reflects a desire for narrative coherence. The actual music industry is a complex web of corporate structures, publishing rights, master recordings, mechanical royalties, performance royalties, synchronization licenses, and joint ventures. It is not easily reducible to a story. But the rumor reduces it: one artist insulted another, the insulted artist struck back through ownership, and the system itself became the instrument of revenge. This is a satisfying story because it makes the opaque machinery of music publishing legible. It translates a corporate acquisition into a personal drama.

The rumor also reflects the cultural fascination with Michael Jackson as a figure who was simultaneously vulnerable and powerful. Jackson's public image in the mid-2000s was that of a man under siege — facing criminal charges, hounded by media, subject to open mockery by a rapper at the peak of his commercial powers. The rumor reclaims agency for Jackson within this narrative. It says: you thought he was weak, but he was playing a longer game. This is a deeply attractive proposition for Jackson's fans and for anyone who identifies with the figure of the powerful person brought low by circumstance. The rumor is not just about Eminem and Jackson; it is about the fantasy that the powerful are always more powerful than they appear, that silence is strategy, and that the last laugh belongs to those who understand the system.

But this appeal is precisely why the rumor is dangerous in its simplified form. It encourages a misunderstanding of how music publishing works. It suggests that an individual artist can unilaterally "buy" another artist's catalogue as an act of personal will, when in reality these transactions involve corporate entities, boards, management teams, and financial analyses that have nothing to do with personal feelings. It also encourages the belief that the music industry is a venue for personal scores to be settled — a belief that, while not entirely false, obscures the extent to which the industry operates on logics of capital accumulation that are indifferent to the personal dramas of the artists whose work it controls.

Furthermore, the rumor's endurance highlights the peculiar dynamics of social-media-era fact-checking. Snopes published its fact-check in February 2024, but the rumor continued to circulate. This is because the rumor's appeal is not primarily informational — it is narrative. A fact-check can correct the factual record, but it cannot easily dislodge a story that people find emotionally satisfying. The "Mixture" rating, while accurate, may actually reinforce the rumor's credibility: if Snopes says parts of it are true, many readers will round up to "basically true" and move on. This is a known limitation of the "Mixture" rating as a communication tool, and it is worth noting that the gap between a fact-check's precision and the public's reception of it is itself a kind of distortion.

## 5. Research Notes

**Primary Sources.** The Snopes article by Aleksandra Wrona, published February 17, 2024, draws on reporting from The Guardian (October 2004), public court records from the 2005 People v. Jackson trial, and corporate announcements regarding the Sony/ATV acquisition of Famous Music. The Guardian's reporting on Jackson's response to "Just Lose It" is the key corrective to the "silence" element of the rumor. The article also references the broader context of Jackson's legal troubles, including the 1993 settlement and the 2003–2005 criminal proceedings.

**Corporate Structure Context.** Sony/ATV Music Publishing was formed in 1995 as a joint venture between Michael Jackson (through his ATV Music catalogue) and Sony Corporation. The structure of the joint venture meant that both parties had to agree on major decisions, and the day-to-day operations were managed by Sony's publishing executives. Jackson's role was that of a co-owner and beneficiary, not an operational manager. The Famous Music acquisition in 2007 was part of Sony/ATV's broader strategy of catalogue expansion, a strategy that continued well after Jackson's death in 2009. In 2016, Sony exercised its option to buy out the Jackson estate's 50% stake in Sony/ATV for $750 million, a transaction that valued the entire publishing company at approximately $2.3 billion — a figure that gives some sense of the scale at which Sony/ATV operated and the relative insignificance of any single artist's catalogue within the broader portfolio.

**The Beatles Precedent.** Jackson's acquisition of the ATV catalogue (containing the Beatles' Northern Songs) in 1985 for $47.5 million is the foundational event in the "Jackson as catalogue raider" narrative. The story, often told, is that Paul McCartney advised Jackson on the value of music publishing — reportedly saying "Think about getting some music publishing" — and Jackson then outbid McCartney for the catalogue that included McCartney's own songs. The accuracy of this account is disputed, but its cultural resonance is undeniable. It established the template that the Eminem rumor later followed: Jackson the savvy businessman, acquiring the rights to others' work and profiting from their success. The irony — if irony is the right word — is that McCartney himself had been similarly outmaneuvered years earlier, when he and John Lennon had lost control of Northern Songs in a complex series of corporate transactions in the late 1960s. The history of music publishing is, in many ways, a history of artists losing control of their own work and then watching it be acquired by others who understand its value better than they did.

**Eminem's Catalogue and Publishing.** Eminem's publishing situation is itself more complex than the rumor suggests. Eminem's early catalogue was administered by various entities over the years, and the specific rights acquired through the Famous Music deal pertained to publishing administration for certain compositions — not necessarily the master recordings, and not necessarily the entirety of Eminem's output. The distinction between master recordings (the actual recorded audio) and publishing (the underlying compositions — the music and lyrics as intellectual property) is crucial and is routinely conflated in popular discussions of music catalogue sales. When the rumor says Jackson "bought Eminem's whole catalogue," it implies ownership of the recordings, which was not the case. The Famous Music deal involved publishing rights, which generate revenue through royalties when songs are performed, recorded, or synchronized — but do not constitute ownership of the recordings themselves.

**The "Diss Track" Tradition.** "Just Lose It" occupies an ambiguous position in the history of hip-hop diss tracks. Eminem is, of course, one of the most accomplished practitioners of the form — his feuds with Machine Gun Kelly, Ja Rule, Canibus, and others produced some of the most memorable diss tracks in the genre's history. But "Just Lose It" was not framed as a diss track; it was a pop single, a lead single from a major-label album, and its references to Jackson were part of Eminem's broader pop-culture satire mode, which also touched on MC Hammer, Madonna, and others. The fact that it functioned as a diss track for Jackson — who was not a hip-hop artist and had no framework for responding in kind — is part of what made the incident so culturally charged. Jackson's response, threatening legal action, was culturally coded as a non-response in the hip-hop context, where the expected move is a counter-track. The rumor's claim that Jackson "stayed silent" may reflect this cultural framing: Jackson did not respond in the way that hip-hop culture demands, therefore he was "silent," even though he was in fact quite vocal through other means.

## 6. Verdict

The rumor that Michael Jackson purchased Eminem's catalogue as revenge for the "Just Lose It" diss is, in the words of Snopes, a Mixture — and the word "mixture" is doing a great deal of work here. Let us be precise about what is and is not true.

**What is true:** Eminem released "Just Lose It" in 2004, a song that referenced Jackson and child-molestation accusations in terms that were, at minimum, tasteless and, depending on one's perspective, cruel. Michael Jackson was angered by the song and its video — he was not silent, as the rumor claims, but publicly condemned it and threatened legal action. In 2007, Sony/ATV Music Publishing, a company in which Jackson held a 50% stake, acquired Famous Music, a deal that included publishing rights to Eminem's back catalogue among 125,000 other songs.

**What is false:** Jackson did not personally buy Eminem's catalogue. Jackson did not stay silent. There is no evidence that the Famous Music acquisition was motivated by Jackson's feelings about "Just Lose It." The acquisition did not give Jackson ownership of Eminem's recordings — it involved publishing administration rights, which is a different (though related) form of intellectual property.

**What is distorted:** The rumor compresses a complex corporate transaction into a personal act of revenge. It takes a coincidence — Jackson's company acquired a catalogue that happened to include Eminem's songs — and converts it into causation. It strips Jackson of his actual response (anger, legal threats, public condemnation) and replaces it with a more narratively satisfying response (strategic silence and financial maneuvering). It also flattens the distinction between publishing rights and master recordings, which are entirely different assets, and which the rumor treats as interchangeable.

The verdict, then, is this: the rumor is a story that uses real events as raw material to construct a narrative that is more emotionally satisfying than the truth but less accurate than the truth. The real story — a corporation making a portfolio acquisition, a pop star publicly expressing outrage, a music publishing industry that operates on logics of capital rather than personal feeling — is less dramatic but more illuminating. It tells us something about how the music industry actually works, whereas the rumor tells us something about how we want the music industry to work: as a stage for personal dramas, where the powerful settle scores through ownership and where silence is never really silence but always the prelude to a masterstroke.

The irony, perhaps, is that the real Michael Jackson — the man who acquired the Beatles catalogue, who merged with Sony, who built one of the most valuable publishing portfolios in music history — was indeed a figure who understood the power of ownership. But his understanding was not the petty, retaliatory understanding of the rumor. It was the understanding of a man who grasped, perhaps earlier and more clearly than any other performer of his generation, that the real money in music was not in performing or recording but in owning the underlying intellectual property. That insight — Paul McCartney's advice, allegedly, to a younger Jackson — built a fortune that outlived the performer himself. The Eminem rumor is a distortion of that legacy: it takes Jackson's genuine business acumen and reduces it to a meme about revenge, when the actual story is both more interesting and more instructive than any meme could be.

Snopes' "Mixture" rating is accurate. The rumor contains truth, falsehood, and distortion in roughly equal measure. But the most important thing to understand about this rumor is not what it gets right or wrong about Michael Jackson and Eminem — it is what it reveals about our desire for the music industry to be a venue for personal drama, and our willingness to sacrifice accuracy for narrative satisfaction. The catalogue was real. The revenge was not. And the distance between the two is where the real story lives.
