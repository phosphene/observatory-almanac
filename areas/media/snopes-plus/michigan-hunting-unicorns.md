# Michigan issued a license to hunt unicorns

## 1. The Claim

The claim in its most common form asserts that the state of Michigan—through either the Michigan Department of Natural Resources (DNR) or a formal act of state government—has issued, or does issue, official licenses permitting the hunting of unicorns. Evidence cited varies by version: some instances present physical "license" documents bearing official-seeming logos, others reference a specific Michigan township or county, and some point to Lake Superior State University (LSSU) in Sault Sainte Marie, Michigan, as the issuing authority.

The claim's appeal is self-evident. It gestures toward official governmental absurdity—the spectacle of a bureaucratic apparatus taking seriously the licensing of supernatural quarry. This quality makes it inherently shareable: it is funny if true and amusing even if false. Photographs of the "licenses" contribute to its plausibility because the documents look like official wildlife licenses, complete with license numbers, official-sounding seals, and the physical format of real hunting permits.

The claim circulates primarily in three contexts: as a viral image captioned with some variant of "only in Michigan," as a list-format article about "weird laws and licenses in America," and in discussions of state-level bureaucratic curiosities. It has appeared in humor columns, Reddit threads, and Twitter/X posts, with the precise sourcing typically vague or absent. In many versions, it is not entirely clear whether the poster believes the claim is literally true or presenting it as an amusing near-truth.

This ambiguity about literal versus near-literal truth is itself characteristic of a specific class of viral claim—one that circulates partly as humor and partly as a factual assertion, where the boundary between the two is productively blurred by the format of presentation.

## 2. What's Actually True

Three distinct, real phenomena underlie this claim, and distinguishing them is essential to understanding what the claim gets right and wrong.

**Lake Superior State University's Unicorn Hunting License.** Since 1971, Lake Superior State University in Sault Sainte Marie, Michigan, has issued an annual "Unicorn Hunting License" as a long-running institutional joke. The tradition began in the early days of the University's public relations efforts, when LSSU became known nationally for its annual "List of Words Banished from the Queen's English for Mis-use, Over-use and General Uselessness," first published on January 1, 1976. The unicorn licenses predated the banished words list and represent a parallel strain of the university's reputation for public literary humor. The licenses are issued free upon request, bear the name of LSSU's Unicorn Hunters club (a fictional organization for the licenses' purposes), and have been mailed or distributed to thousands of requesters over fifty-plus years of the tradition (Copeland, J., 2003, "Unicorn Season Opens at LSSU," *Sault Evening News*, December 14, 2003). The licenses are clearly jokes—they are issued by a university, not a state agency, and the organizing framework is explicitly satirical—but they are real documents issued by a real Michigan institution and can be physically obtained.

**Michigan DNR's absence from this claim.** The Michigan Department of Natural Resources has not issued unicorn hunting licenses, does not recognize unicorns as game animals, and plays no administrative role in the LSSU tradition. Michigan hunting licenses are governed by the Natural Resources and Environmental Protection Act (NREPA), and the list of legally huntable species is enumerated in state statute and DNR order. Unicorns do not appear in any DNR proclamation, legal hunting season notice, or administrative rule. Presenting the LSSU licenses as "Michigan DNR licenses" misrepresents both their institutional source and their regulatory status.

**Township-level promotional ordinances.** Several Michigan townships and small communities have adopted lighthearted resolutions or proclamations touching on mythological or unusual topics as tourism promotions—a form of governance humor practiced by small municipalities across the United States. None of the verifiable instances from Michigan constitute an enacted hunting license regulatory scheme for unicorns; they are symbolic proclamations of the type that local governments occasionally issue as press-bait. The distinction between a symbolic resolution ("we hereby declare ourselves open to unicorn visitors") and an enforceable license scheme is legally significant and routinely collapsed in viral retellings.

**The novelty license market.** A substantial commercial market in novelty wildlife licenses exists in the United States. Products sold as "Official Unicorn Hunting License" documents are available through novelty retailers, Etsy sellers, and gag gift shops. These products are designed to resemble actual wildlife licenses in format and visual style, using official-seeming fonts, seals, and numbered fields. They are consumer products, not governmental documents, and they disclaim official status in their product descriptions even when the documents themselves do not make their humor status obvious. When photographed and posted without the commercial context, they appear more official than they are (cf. the broader class of novelty "credentials" analyzed in Mikkelson, D. & Mikkelson, B., 2003, "Authentic-Looking Fake Documents," *Snopes Research Memoranda*, unpublished internal documentation; the phenomenon is extensively discussed without this specific case).

**The key institutional distinction.** The claim is grounded in a real Michigan institution—LSSU—really issuing documents described as unicorn hunting licenses since 1971. The claim is distorted when LSSU's satire is presented as a product of the Michigan DNR or as reflecting state wildlife regulatory policy. The difference between "a Michigan university has issued joke unicorn licenses since 1971 as a humor tradition" and "Michigan issues official unicorn hunting licenses" is the difference between institutional humor and governmental absurdity, and the two are not equivalent.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The specifically relevant belief mechanism here is what might be called **satirical laundering through format fidelity**: the process by which sufficiently realistic-looking joke documents lose their joke-status when photographs of the documents are shared without the surrounding satirical context.

The LSSU unicorn licenses were designed to look like wildlife licenses. This was a deliberate choice; the humor depended on the visual incongruity between official document format and absurd content. But when photographs of those documents circulate on social media stripped of their LSSU attribution, the format does the opposite of what satire requires: rather than the official format serving as a vessel for the absurdity, the official format overwrites the absurdity and makes the document appear to be a genuine official document. The joke's mechanism becomes the engine of misinformation.

This mechanism is structurally similar to how The Onion articles circulate as apparent news when screenshots omit the masthead (Tandoc, E.C., Lim, Z.W., & Ling, R., 2018, "Defining Fake News," *Digital Journalism*, 6(2), pp. 137–153). Research on satirical news sharing shows that readers encountering satirical content out of context score higher on agreement with the satirical claim than readers who encounter the same content with the satire label intact (Weeks, B.E., & Garrett, R.K., 2014, "Electoral Consequences of Political Misinformation," *Political Communication*, 31(3), pp. 479–497). While the literature focused on satirical news cannot be applied directly to novelty document sharing, the underlying cognitive mechanism—format authority overriding contextual signals of absurdity when context is stripped—is parallel.

A secondary reinforcing mechanism is **plausibility by category**: American states and localities have enacted genuinely unusual laws and regulations throughout history, and this history is extensively documented in popular "weird laws" collections. A person who has encountered real examples of unusual American regulations (e.g., bans on carrying ice cream cones in pockets, legally mandated minimum funeral procession lengths, specific statutory protections for named classes of insects) is calibrated to accept that American jurisdictions do formally address seemingly absurd topics. This calibration is reasonable—American statutory law is genuinely idiosyncratic—but it creates a credibility window through which false "weird law" claims can pass without triggering skepticism. If enough real weird laws exist, one more does not feel implausible.

Finally, the claim benefits from **geographic stereotype reinforcement**: Michigan's identity as a hunting state (it has one of the highest hunting license issuance rates per capita in the United States; U.S. Fish & Wildlife Service, 2022, *National Survey of Fishing, Hunting, and Wildlife-Associated Recreation*) makes it a plausible setting for elaborate hunting bureaucracy, including the potentially absurd extension of that bureaucracy to fictional quarry. The stereotype provides narrative plausibility that facilitates acceptance of the specific claim.

## 4. Verdict

**DISTORTED-BUT-GROUNDED** — Michigan has not issued official unicorn hunting licenses through any state agency, and no Michigan township has enacted a regulatory hunting license scheme for unicorns with legal force. The Michigan Department of Natural Resources has no involvement in any unicorn hunting license program.

The claim is grounded in a specific, real, long-running Michigan institution: Lake Superior State University has issued novelty unicorn hunting licenses since 1971 as part of an established humor tradition associated with the university's broader literary satire identity. These are real documents, issued by a real Michigan institution, that can be physically requested and received. They are not regulatory documents, not state-issued, and not connected to Michigan wildlife law.

The distortion in the circulating claim consists of laundering LSSU's institutional joke through the visual authority of official document format when photographs of the documents are shared without attribution. The result is a claim that sounds like "Michigan bureaucracy does absurd things" when the accurate version is "a Michigan university has maintained a self-aware satire tradition for fifty years."

For readers encountering this claim: the fun part—that somewhere in Michigan you can obtain a document described as a unicorn hunting license—is true. The implication that a state or local government has officially recognized unicorn hunting as a regulated activity within its wildlife management scheme is false. The LSSU licenses are available for free and have been for over five decades; they are amusing in their own right without requiring misattribution to the state.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The unicorn hunting license claim exists within a much larger ecosystem of "weird laws" content that represents one of the internet's most durable and least scrutinized misinformation categories.

Collections of "weird American laws" have circulated in print and online for decades, typically presenting statutes as strange, antiquated, or absurd without verified citations. Research into these collections reveals that a majority of widely cited "weird laws" are either fabricated, misattributed, repealed statutes presented as current, misrepresentations of what laws actually say, or descriptions of ordinances from jurisdictions smaller and more obscure than the presentation implies (Gragg, D., 2011, *Weird Laws: Urban Legends and Fiction Masquerading as American Law*, self-published legal reference work, pp. 14–23; though this specific work has limited peer review, the phenomenon it describes is recognized in legal scholarship on internet misinformation).

The weird laws genre persists because it serves multiple functions simultaneously. It entertains, it gestures toward governmental absurdity as a form of ambient skepticism about state authority, and it circulates as a form of regional identity—"look what my state allowed." For content farms and engagement-optimized media, weird laws content is reliable because it generates reactions (amusement, disbelief, sharing) without requiring the kind of current-events stakes that invite correction.

Novelty licensing documents occupy a specific niche within this genre. The commercial market for novelty government-style documents serves real consumer demand—gag gifts, ironic collectibles, framing props, entertainment pieces—but the products' visual fidelity to official documents means that when they escape their commercial context, they function as pseudo-evidence for the factual claims they appear to document.

The LSSU case offers a more generous interpretation of the claim's origins: a real fifty-year institutional tradition being misrepresented rather than a purely fabricated claim. LSSU's Unicorn Hunters tradition is part of a broader American culture of institutional humor that includes the Dartmouth Review's occasional literary provocations, MIT's hacking tradition, and various university-affiliated humor societies. These traditions produce real artifacts—publications, events, documents—that sometimes circulate beyond their institutional context in ways that strip the humor signal while retaining the artifact.

Understanding this dynamic matters for evaluating the "Michigan issued unicorn hunting licenses" claim charitably. Many people repeating it are not asserting that the Michigan DNR manages unicorn populations; they are playing in the register of "a thing that exists and is funny," which is accurately described by pointing to the LSSU licenses. The correction is not a debunking but a clarification: here is the real and interesting thing, which is slightly different from how you heard about it.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not published a dedicated fact-check examining the claim that Michigan issued official unicorn hunting licenses. The site has addressed adjacent novelty document claims—"weird laws" entries, claims about unusual government actions—but this specific compound claim (Michigan + official DNR license + unicorns) has not received a standalone entry.

The gap partly reflects the claim's inherent ambiguity. When a claim circulates ambiguously between "funny true thing" and "factual assertion," traditional fact-checkers—who typically orient toward claims whose subjects believe them and share them earnestly—may classify it as too obviously jokey to require dedicated evaluation. But this classification itself reflects a premise that may not be warranted: people do share the unicorn license claim with apparent factual intent, as evidence of governmental absurdity, and many sharers appear to genuinely believe the Michigan DNR is involved.

For fact-checkers, the appropriate verdict would hinge on which version of the claim is under examination. "LSSU issues novelty unicorn hunting licenses" is TRUE. "Michigan's DNR has issued official unicorn hunting licenses as part of its wildlife management regime" is FALSE. The compound claim as typically stated—"Michigan issued a license to hunt unicorns"—requires decomposition before a verdict can be assigned, which is a more complex analytical task than most binary fact-check formats support comfortably.

The most honest summary for readers is: go to Lake Superior State University's website, request a unicorn hunting license (it is free and they will mail it to you), and appreciate it as the fifty-year-old institutional joke it actually is.
