## The Claim

On April 11, 2024, a TikTok user operating under the handle @microzoomguy posted a video in which he appeared to place a standard Oreo cookie under a microscope. As the microscope's view was shown to the audience, insects became visible moving across and within the cookie. The video accumulated more than 5.6 million views and approximately 64,900 shares, and copies spread across Facebook, Instagram, and X. The implicit claim of the video — powerful in its absence of explicit statement — was that an ordinary, commercially sealed Oreo cookie, as purchased from a store, was alive with insects when examined at microscopic scale.

The visceral quality of this claim is immediately understandable. Oreo cookies are among the most recognisable and widely consumed packaged food products in the world, sold in more than 100 countries and manufactured in controlled industrial environments with food safety certifications. The idea that such a product harbours macro-scale insect life — not contaminants at the level of incidental particulate, but live, moving, visually recognisable bugs — violates a fundamental assumption most consumers hold about sealed packaged food: that it is bug-free by modern industrial standards.

The video played to food anxiety in the same register as a long tradition of content designed to provoke disgust and concern about hidden contaminants in everyday food. Whether or not the video was explicitly framed as a consumer warning, viewers received it as one.

## What's Actually True

The insects shown in the video are real and correctly classifiable. The brown insects visible in the footage are weevils belonging to the *Sitophilus* genus, which encompasses three common stored-grain pests: the granary or wheat weevil (*Sitophilus granarius*), the maize weevil (*Sitophilus zeamais*), and the rice weevil (*Sitophilus oryzae*). Adult weevils in this genus are easily recognisable by their characteristic long rostrum (snout), which they use to bore into grain kernels. Reproduction occurs when a female deposits an egg into a hole bored in a grain kernel; the larva spends approximately a month inside the kernel, consuming it as a food and developmental substrate, before emerging as an adult.

The smaller, semi-transparent, white-to-pale creatures also visible in parts of the video appear to be mold mites, a collective name for multiple arachnid species in the broader mite taxonomic grouping that are frequently found in stored grain, flour, cheese, and similar products. Their diet is fungal rather than starchy — mold mites consume the mold that develops on food stored in damp or humid conditions rather than the food itself. Their presence in a food sample therefore indicates mold development, not simply that the food has been stored in proximity to grain.

These identifications were confirmed by Laura Iles, an entomologist and the director of the North Central Integrated Pest Management Center, an institution funded by the United States Department of Agriculture. Iles reviewed the video and confirmed to Snopes that the insects depicted appear to be *Sitophilus* weevils and at least one species of mold mite. She also identified three features of the video that, taken together, led Snopes and Iles to conclude that the footage was staged rather than authentic documentation of a factory-sealed product infestation.

The first anomaly concerns weevil behaviour. At approximately the 00:32 timestamp of the video, weevils appear to be struggling within the Oreo's white frosting centre. Grain weevils are not attracted to sugary frosting — they evolved as stored-grain pests and are specifically adapted to grain kernels as their reproductive substrate. An adult weevil placed in frosting has no functional reason to be there; it would not voluntarily travel into that food substrate, because frosting does not provide the grain-kernel structure in which the species reproduces. The appearance of weevils in the frosting therefore suggests they were placed there deliberately rather than found there independently.

The second anomaly concerns the mite substrate. At approximately the 00:56 timestamp, mites are shown moving on a crumbly brown substance. If this substance is Oreo cookie crumble, it appears to have been soaked in liquid and allowed to develop mold — evidenced by small white filaments consistent with fungal hyphae visible in the footage. Mold mites depend on mold for food; their presence indicates a substantially degraded food substrate, not a cookie under normal storage conditions. A cookie that has developed mold through liquid exposure is, as Iles noted, "not good for human consumption for reasons beyond any mites present" — it is a tampered, deliberately compromised food sample, not a representative grocery product.

The third anomaly is methodological. The microscope shown on camera at the beginning of the video, under which the Oreo is visibly placed, does not appear to be the same type of microscope used to generate the microscopic footage. The visible-on-camera microscope is of a type that does not appear capable of producing the magnification and resolution of the cellular-scale images shown subsequently. This inconsistency suggests either the use of a different instrument for actual filming, or stock footage assembled and presented misleadingly as showing one continuous operation.

The video shows no evidence of digital manipulation — the insects are not CGI, and the footage is not digitally fabricated. But the combination of behavioural anomalies, substrate condition, and apparent equipment inconsistency strongly suggests the video's creator intentionally placed insects on a compromised cookie for the purpose of creating footage that would be received as discovering hidden contamination in a normal product.

## Why People Believe This

The video's 5.6 million TikTok views are explained by several overlapping factors in the psychology of food disgust, the social media ecology of viral content, and the specific anxieties around food safety in an era of industrial food production.

**Food disgust and contamination sensitivity.** Food disgust is one of the most evolutionarily ancient and psychologically powerful emotional responses. Research by Jonathan Haidt and colleagues on moral foundations theory identified disgust as a foundational moral emotion with deep evolutionary roots in pathogen avoidance (Haidt & Joseph, 2004, *Daedalus*). Paul Rozin's extensive research on the psychology of food disgust demonstrated that contamination perceptions — including the "once-in-contact, always-contaminated" principle — operate with high emotional intensity and are largely resistant to rational deactivation (Rozin, Millman & Nemeroff, 1986, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*). Seeing macro-scale insects inside a familiar food product triggers these disgust mechanisms at near-maximal intensity.

**The normalization-disruption dynamic.** Much of the emotional power of the Oreo bug video derives from the specific contrast between the normality of the product (a globally familiar packaged snack) and the alleged content (live insects). The Oreo is not an exotic food or an artisanal product whose origins and handling might be opaque to consumers — it is one of the most industrialised and quality-controlled consumer food products in the world. The claim that such a product harbours insect life violates a normalised trust assumption in a particularly destabilising way.

**The microscope as epistemic authority.** The use of a microscope in the video performs a specific rhetorical function: it presents itself as the kind of objective, technical, scientific instrument whose outputs should be trusted as unmanipulated documentation of reality. Microscopy is associated in public understanding with authentic scientific inquiry rather than theatrical production. The choice to present the footage as microscope output — rather than, say, drawing attention to insects visible with the naked eye — leverages this association to claim a kind of documentary authority that the footage, in fact, does not legitimately possess.

This is an instance of what might be called epistemic novelty in staged content: the staged video is epistemically interesting not because it shows something true about Oreos but because it exploits and reveals the public trust infrastructure around scientific instrumentation. The appearance of objectivity is itself the deceptive mechanism.

**Food safety discourse and the industrial food system.** The video reached social media audiences in a period of heightened consumer attention to food safety and food sourcing. Documentary films, investigative journalism, and social media content have spent two decades expanding public awareness of food production conditions that were previously opaque, including documented cases of insect contamination in industrial food production. The US Food and Drug Administration maintains published "Defect Action Levels" specifying the maximum permissible levels of natural or unavoidable defects in commercial food products — including insect fragments — that do not pose health hazards (FDA, 2005, *The Food Defect Action Levels*). Public knowledge that regulatory standards permit some level of insect-derived material in processed food creates a background anxiety that video content of this type activates.

**The "what if it's real" threshold effect.** One of the distinctive features of food safety viral content is that viewers often recognise they cannot definitively rule out authenticity from the content itself. The threshold for believing "this is genuinely alarming" is lower than the threshold for believing "this is definitely fake" — if in doubt, the safer assumption (from an evolutionary risk management perspective) is caution. This asymmetric threshold means that viral food safety content accumulates engagement even from viewers who are uncertain about its authenticity: both "I believe this" sharing and "can anyone confirm if this is real?" sharing drive traffic.

## Verdict

**The claim is a mixture, and the mixture is epistemically important.** The insects in the video are genuine *Sitophilus* weevils and mold mites — real, identifiable organisms correctly categorised as food pests. The footage is not digitally fabricated. These elements are true.

But the video's implicit primary claim — that these insects would be found on a normal, factory-sealed Oreo cookie — is not established and is actively undermined by the forensic evidence examined by entomologist Laura Iles. The weevils' presence in the frosting is behaviourally anomalous. The mites' substrate appears to be a degraded, mold-bearing, liquid-exposed cookie fragment rather than a representative product. The microscope equipment visible on camera does not appear to match the equipment that generated the microscopic footage.

The most accurate characterisation is that @microzoomguy appears to have staged the video by deliberately placing known food pests on an intentionally compromised Oreo, then presented the footage as though it documented a natural infestation which consumers might encounter. This is a specific type of misleading content distinct from digital fabrication: it uses real insects and real microscopy to create a false impression about the representativeness of the depicted phenomenon.

## The Wider Picture

The Oreo bug video case raises substantive questions about several intersecting topics: the actual biology of food-associated insects, the regulatory framework for food safety standards, the problem of staged "scientific" content on social media, and the epistemics of entomological evidence.

**The biology of grain weevils in the food supply.** *Sitophilus* grain weevils are genuine and economically significant pests. They are responsible for substantial losses in stored grain globally. Ruiz Moreno and colleagues document that in developing countries, post-harvest losses to stored-grain insects — including *Sitophilus* species — can account for 10–30% of grain production (Ruiz Moreno et al., 2018, *Journal of Stored Products Research*). In industrialised food production contexts, grain processing facilities implement integrated pest management protocols — temperature controls, fumigation, airtight storage, monitoring programs — that are specifically designed to prevent *Sitophilus* infestations in finished food products.

The FDA's Defect Action Levels for cookies (inclusive of Oreos) allow up to 75 insect fragments per 50 grams — fragmentary evidence of insects in the production system — but do not permit live insects in sealed packaged products. A packaged cookie containing live, active weevils and mites of the kind shown in the video would represent a significant food safety failure requiring product recall. No such recall was associated with Oreo products in or around April 2024, which is itself evidence against the video's representational claim.

**Mold mites and food storage conditions.** The presence of mold mites (*Tyrophagus putrescentiae* and related species) is a reliable indicator of specific storage failure conditions: high humidity, extended temperature fluctuation, or direct liquid contact, all of which promote the mold growth on which these organisms feed. Research by Hay and colleagues confirms that mold mite populations in stored food are strongly correlated with mold infestation levels and ambient humidity (Hay & Williams, 2019, *Experimental and Applied Acarology*). Their presence in the video footage is therefore not just evidence of insect contamination but evidence of a severely degraded storage environment — one inconsistent with a factory-sealed cookie under normal conditions.

**Staged scientific content as a misinformation vector.** The Oreo bug video belongs to a distinct and epistemically challenging category of misleading content: material that uses genuine scientific instruments, genuine biological specimens, and genuine footage to create a misleading impression about the phenomenon depicted. This category is more difficult to counter than digitally fabricated content because the component elements are each authentic. The deception lies in the experimental design — what was placed under the microscope, in what condition, and whether it represents what the framing suggests it represents — rather than in the recording technology.

This epistemic structure is analogous to what philosopher Ian Hacking calls the "argument from coincidence" problem in scientific instrumentation (Hacking, 1983, *Representing and Intervening*): the authenticity of an instrument's outputs does not guarantee that what is captured by the instrument is what the operator claims to have been studying. A microscope accurately records what is under it; whether what is under it is representative of the claimed phenomenon is a separate question requiring methodological transparency that the video does not provide.

**The TikTok science content ecosystem.** The @microzoomguy TikTok account (and similar accounts using microscopy, spectrometry, and other scientific instrumentations) exists within a broader TikTok content category in which scientific equipment is used to generate visually striking footage. This genre includes legitimate science communication content alongside staged or misleading content. The genre's conventions — show an ordinary object through a technical instrument, reveal something surprising — create a presentation format that is visually compelling regardless of the underlying accuracy of the implicit claim.

Research on science communication and social media suggests that visually striking scientific-format content achieves high engagement partly because it activates both the emotional systems associated with discovery and surprise and the cognitive associations with scientific authority (Brossard & Scheufele, 2013, *Science*). The microscope content genre leverages both simultaneously. Content creators operating within this genre have structural incentives to maximise visual impact — which staged infestations achieve more reliably than waiting for naturally occurring contamination events that may never arise.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check, authored by Caroline Wazer and published June 22, 2024, demonstrates a sophisticated approach to evaluating content where digital authenticity and staged staging are independent questions.

**Expert entomological consultation.** The central methodological strength of this fact-check is the engagement of Laura Iles, an USDA-funded entomologist, to evaluate the footage's biological elements. This consultation went beyond the standard "is this image real?" verification to ask "if the biological elements are genuine, is the scenario depicted represented accurately?" Iles's identification of the behavioural anomalies — weevils in frosting rather than grain, mold-substrate mites in what appears to be liquid-damaged cookie material — required exactly this kind of domain-expert evaluation that visual forensics alone could not provide.

**Distinguishing authenticity from representativeness.** The fact-check clearly articulates the distinction between two questions that viewers typically collapse into one: "Is the footage authentic?" (Is it digitally fabricated?) and "Is the implied claim accurate?" (Would I find these insects on a cookie I purchased at a store?). These are logically independent questions, and a fact-check that only answered the first would miss the more important second. The "Mixture" rating reflects this distinction: authentic footage, misleading claim.

**Transparency about follow-up limitations.** Snopes noted that it had attempted to contact the @microzoomguy TikTok account to ask how the video was produced, and that it would update if a response was received. This transparency about the limits of available evidence is appropriate — the conclusion that the video was staged is supported by strong circumstantial evidence but not by the creator's direct admission, and the fact-check correctly represents that epistemic status rather than overstating its certainty.

The Mixture rating in this case captures a genuinely complex evidentiary situation with appropriate precision: neither dismissing the insects as fake (they are genuine organisms) nor accepting the implicit claim about typical cookie contents (the video's staging undermines that claim entirely).
