## The Claim

In April 2020, an image spread rapidly across social media platforms showing what appeared to be an aerial photograph of a large crop circle in an agricultural field. The design of the alleged crop circle was visually distinctive: it combined the four-panel, flag-like structure of the Microsoft Windows logo with a ring of protruding spikes resembling the "crown" of a coronavirus, as depicted in the widely reproduced NIAID imagery that had become the visual symbol of SARS-CoV-2.

The image circulated primarily in two framings. The first was neutral wonder — "protest art" that had supposedly appeared somewhere in the United Kingdom, described as a powerful visual statement about perceived corporate involvement in the pandemic. The second framing was conspiratorial — the image was shared by accounts promoting the theory that former Microsoft CEO Bill Gates was using COVID-19 to implement population-level microchip surveillance through vaccines, and the alleged crop formation was presented as evidence of or commentary on that conspiracy. Both framings treated the image as a genuine photograph of a real field installation.

This claim is false. The image is a digital manipulation. No such crop circle was created or reported. The image was produced by digitally altering a genuine photograph of a real crop circle that appeared in Wiltshire, England, in the summer of 2004 — a photograph archived by The Temporary Temples project, which documents UK crop circle appearances. The Microsoft Windows design and coronavirus spikes were inserted into the original photograph, replacing the original circle's actual design.

Snopes journalist Dan Evon published a fact-check on May 6, 2020, establishing the image's false provenance and rating the claim False.

## What's Actually True

The underlying real photograph shows a legitimate crop circle from the summer of 2004 in Wiltshire, England. The Temporary Temple, a group that archives crop circle imagery, holds several photographs of this real circle from multiple angles and perspectives. The original circle had its own design, which was replaced in the manipulated version with the Microsoft Windows four-panel logo and coronavirus spike profile.

The real history of crop circle production in Wiltshire is itself multifaceted and documented. Wiltshire has been the epicentre of the crop circle phenomenon since the 1970s. The origins of the modern crop circle craze trace to Doug Bower and Dave Chorley, two English pranksters who created a "flying saucer nest" in a Wiltshire wheat field in 1976, drawing on local UFO mythology associated with the town of Warminster. Their creation sparked enormous interest among extraterrestrial enthusiasts and led directly to the proliferation of crop circle culture — despite Bower and Chorley's eventual public admission that their formations were human-made hoaxes. Smithsonian Magazine (Irving and Brookesmith, December 2009) documented this history extensively, noting that scattered reports of crop circles date back to a 17th-century pamphlet and an 1880 account in *Nature*, but the phenomenon as a cultural institution is traceable to the 1976 Bower-Chorley creation.

Crop circles — regardless of their 1970s–1990s association with extraterrestrial speculation — are produced by human beings using tools ranging from primitive planks and ropes to sophisticated GPS-guided mechanical systems. The artistic and promotional range of modern crop circle creation is illustrated by an incident Evon cites in the Snopes fact-check: in January 2014, technology company NVIDIA created a 310-foot diameter crop circle in a California field as a publicity stunt for the release of a new mobile processor. NVIDIA's president and CEO, Jen-Hsun Huang, personally confirmed the stunt at a press event. The NVIDIA crop circle was a legitimate field installation created by human design. The Microsoft Windows crop circle was a digitally manipulated image — a different category of fabrication.

No reporting, no drone or aerial photography, and no Temporary Temple documentation supports the existence of the alleged COVID-era crop circle. The absence of corroborating evidence for a claimed formation that would have been visually spectacular and widely photographed is itself significant: genuine unusual crop circles in Wiltshire and surrounding areas are almost invariably documented by multiple sources almost immediately upon discovery, given the active community of crop circle researchers and aerial photographers operating in the region.

The manipulated image was primarily deployed within the broader ecosystem of Bill Gates/COVID conspiracy theories. The most prominent of these — given its own separate Snopes fact-check — concerns "ID2020," a digital identity initiative supported in part by the Gates Foundation, which conspiracy theorists misrepresented as a scheme to implant microchips in vaccine recipients. The digitally manipulated crop circle image functioned within this ecosystem as a piece of visual propaganda, giving a symbolic form to the conspiracy narrative and lending it the apparent authenticity of photographic evidence.

## Epistemic Novelty

The Microsoft Windows crop circle case sits at the convergence of three significant epistemological domains: the forensics of digital image manipulation, the sociology of pandemic conspiracy theory, and the semiotics of visual propaganda. Its interest as a Truth Vault entry lies not merely in its falseness — that is easily established — but in what it reveals about how fabricated visual evidence functions within belief systems.

**The "false floor" of photographic evidence.** Photographs carry a culturally inherited epistemic authority that text does not possess. The phrase "photographs don't lie" encodes a presumption — now outdated but deeply ingrained — that photographic images are indexically connected to the physical world in a way that makes them harder to fabricate than written claims. Digital imaging technology dissolved this presumption, but the psychological response to photograph-as-evidence has not updated correspondingly. Psychologists have examined this phenomenon extensively: a single experiment by Reber and Unkelbach (2010, "The Epistemic Status of Processing Fluency as Source for Judgments of Truth," *Review of Philosophy and Psychology*, Vol. 1, pp. 563–581) demonstrated that processing ease — including the smooth, immediate visual legibility of a photographic image — elevates rated truth credibility even when the image content is uncorroborated.

More directly relevant is research by Nightingale et al. (2017, "Can People Identify Original and Manipulated Photos of Real-World Scenes?", *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*, Vol. 2, No. 30), which found that participants correctly identified manipulated images in only 60% of cases — barely above chance — and correctly located the manipulated region in only 45% of explicitly manipulated images. The visual appearance of authenticity was sufficient to deceive the majority of participants independent of the content's plausibility.

**Semantic loading through symbol combination.** The specific design of the manipulated crop circle — combining the Windows logo with the coronavirus spike profile — is an example of what semioticians call *motivated iconicity*, the use of visual symbol combination to imply conceptual connections between their referents. Neither the Windows logo nor the coronavirus crown is visually natural to a crop circle; combining them into a single design in a "discovered" formation implies that whoever "created" the circle (whether humans or unknown forces) was making a deliberate claim about the relationship between Microsoft and the virus. The image thus encodes a conspiracy theory not in text but in icon — making the viewer a discoverer of meaning rather than the recipient of a direct verbal claim.

This mechanism is well-documented in visual persuasion research. Mitchell and Hill (1984, in "The Image and Its Context," contributing to the literature on visual argument) observed that visual images implying causal or associative relationships through juxtaposition are processed as assertions without being syntactically analysable as assertions — they carry propositional force without the logical connective tissue that would make the assertion debatable. A meme juxtaposing Bill Gates's photograph with a coronavirus image makes an implicit claim while providing no verbal proposition that can be formally rebutted.

**Conspiracy ecosystem function.** The pandemic of 2020 produced a cascade of COVID-adjacent conspiracy theories that shared a functional architecture: each provided an explanatory frame that transformed a terrifying, complex, and largely incomprehensible event (a novel respiratory virus crossing from animals to humans and killing at unprecedented rates) into a comprehensible narrative of intentional human agency. Many of these narratives converged on the same small set of actors — Bill Gates, Anthony Fauci, the World Health Organisation — because these actors were real, publicly visible, and associated with pandemic response in ways that made them available as conspiracy protagonists.

The cognitive function of such narratives is documented in the psychological literature on conspiracy ideation. Van Prooijen and van Dijk (2014, "When Consequence Size Predicts Belief in Conspiracy Theories: The Moderating Role of Perspective Taking," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, Vol. 55, pp. 63–73) experimentally demonstrated that the severity of an event — operationalised as its perceived scale and consequence — significantly increases belief in conspiracy explanations, even when the evidence for those explanations is held constant. Large events, the reasoning goes, should have large, intentional causes. A pandemic killing hundreds of thousands of people is too large to be an accident; it must have been planned. The Microsoft Windows crop circle image circulated into a belief system already primed to accept its implied claim.

**Digital forensics methodology.** The identification of the image as a manipulation rested on a straightforward approach: finding the original unmanipulated photograph. The Temporary Temple archive, by maintaining a systematic photographic record of crop circle appearances since the 1970s, functioned as the ground-truth reference against which the manipulated image was compared. This reverse-image-search approach — now systematised through Google Reverse Image Search, TinEye, and related tools — is one of the primary methodologies in digital forensics for visual misinformation. The approach does not, however, detect all forms of manipulation, particularly when fabricated images do not incorporate identifiable original photographs. As sophisticated generative AI imagery becomes more prevalent, source-tracing approaches will encounter increasing difficulty — a limitation not relevant to the 2020 Bill Gates crop circle image but increasingly significant for the broader practice of image verification.

## The Evidence Base

**The Temporary Temple (temporarytemples.co.uk), 2004 UK Crop Circle Archive.** The specialist archive documenting crop circle appearances in the UK. Holds multiple angle photographs of the 2004 Wiltshire formation that served as the source for the digital manipulation, enabling comparison of the original versus the modified version.

**Irving, R., and Brookesmith, P., Smithsonian Magazine, "Crop Circles: The Art of the Hoax," December 15, 2009.** The authoritative popular history of crop circle culture, documenting the Bower-Chorley origins of the modern phenomenon, the scale of the cultural response, and the mechanisms through which human-created formations came to be attributed to non-human forces.

**Radford, B., Live Science, "Crop Circle Was a Publicity Stunt: Why 'Experts' Were Fooled," January 7, 2014.** Covers the NVIDIA crop circle publicity stunt, establishing the modern commercial use of genuine field formations as a contrasting case demonstrating what verified field crop circles involve.

Directly relevant to why digitally manipulated images like the crop circle spread without detection across non-expert audiences.

"When Consequence Size Predicts Belief in Conspiracy Theories: The Moderating Role of Perspective Taking." *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, Vol. 55, pp. 63–73.** This experimental study demonstrated that the scale of perceived consequence systematically inflates acceptance of conspiracy explanations, providing the psychological mechanism behind COVID conspiracy proliferation of which the crop circle image is an instance.

**Reber, R., and Unkelbach, C. (2010). "The Epistemic Status of Processing Fluency as Source for Judgments of Truth." *Review of Philosophy and Psychology*, Vol. 1, pp. 563–581.** Demonstrates that processing ease — including the visual smoothness and legibility of photographic images — elevates credibility ratings independently of content evaluation, relevant to understanding why photograph-form misinformation is disproportionately effective.

**Snopes, "ID2020: Does Bill Gates Want to 'Use COVID-19 Vaccine to Implant Microchips'?"** The companion fact-check on the broader conspiracy ecosystem within which the crop circle image circulated, documenting the vaccine-microchip narrative and its relationship to the Gates-COVID family of conspiracy theories.

## Why People Believe This

The crop circle image amplified an existing conspiratorial structure rather than seeding a new one. By April 2020, the cluster of theories connecting Bill Gates, COVID-19, vaccines, and digital surveillance was already extensively developed and circulating across platforms. The image did not need to construct that framework; it needed only to offer a visual instantiation of it — a photograph that made the connection seem not just asserted but physically manifested in the world.

The crop circle format was particularly effective for this purpose because it carried its own pre-existing mystery. Crop circles have cultural associations with secrecy, hidden knowledge, and non-ordinary forces — associations cultivated over fifty years of popular culture engagement with the phenomenon, from books claiming alien construction to television documentaries treating the circles with varying degrees of earnestness. Using the crop circle format to deliver a conspiracy message embedded the message in a medium that already indexed "hidden truth known only to those paying attention." The form mirrored the function: this is secret knowledge, visible only to those who look.

The pandemic context also heightened what psychologists call *illusory pattern perception* — the tendency to see meaningful patterns in random or ambiguous stimuli. Proulx and Heine (2010, "Death and Black Diamonds: Meaning, Mortality Salience, and the Detection of Meaningful Patterns," *Psychological Science*, Vol. 21, No. 10, pp. 1306–1312) and Whitson and Galinsky (2008, "Lacking Control Increases Illusory Pattern Perception," *Science*, Vol. 322, pp. 115–117) both documented experimentally that conditions of threat, loss of control, and mortality salience increase participants' tendency to perceive meaningful patterns — including conspiratorial connections — in neutral material. The pandemic was precisely such a condition: widespread, life-threatening, and outside individual control. Under these conditions, a visual image that organised disparate anxieties (the pandemic, technology, Bill Gates, loss of bodily autonomy over vaccination) into a coherent symbol was psychologically highly functional, and that function was independent of whether the image was real.

The social media environment of April 2020 also exhibited specific structural features that amplified visual misinformation. Major platforms had taken steps to limit textual misinformation about COVID-19, reducing algorithmic promotion of text posts making false health claims. Visual content that encoded the same claims in image form was less easily detectable by automated systems and circulated more freely. False crop circle images would not trigger the same content moderation systems as false text claims about vaccines — even though the conspiracy encoding was functionally equivalent.

## The Verdict

The claim that a photograph shows a genuine crop circle shaped like the Microsoft Windows logo is **false**. The photograph is a digital manipulation. The original image shows a real 2004 Wiltshire crop circle, documented in The Temporary Temple archive, which was digitally altered to insert the Microsoft Windows four-panel design and a ring of coronavirus spike-protein shapes. No evidence — aerial photography, field reports, local media coverage, or Temporary Temple documentation — supports the existence of any such formation.

The manipulated image operated primarily within the ecosystem of COVID-19 conspiracy theories linking Bill Gates, vaccine development, and surveillance technology. Its visual encoding of the conspiracy narrative in crop-circle form combined two powerful meaning-systems — the mystery culture around crop circles and the emergent pandemic conspiracy framework — into a compound image designed for maximum resonance with primed audiences.

The episode illustrates what might be called the *fabrication premium* in visual misinformation: images that embed false claims in photographic form rather than textual assertion are disproportionately credible, share at higher rates, and encounter less cognitive friction than equivalent text-based claims. They are also, when the manipulation involves an identifiable source photograph, tractable to forensic verification through reverse image search. The Bill Gates crop circle failed this basic verification — its source image was found in a specialist archive, the manipulation made evident by comparison — but the majority of people who shared it never applied that verification and would have no reason to do so absent a specific prompt.

Maintaining a healthy epistemic posture toward striking photographic "evidence" for extraordinary claims — especially when those images arrive within narratives already shaped to receive them — remains among the most practically important skills a media-literate person can develop in the contemporary information environment.
