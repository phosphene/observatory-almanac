# "The Milgram Experiment Proved Ordinary People Will Torture Others When Ordered To"


## 1. The Claim

The claim asserts that Stanley Milgram's famous obedience experiments from the 1960s demonstrated that ordinary people will inflict torture on others when commanded to do so by an authority figure. This interpretation typically emphasizes the shocking willingness of approximately 65% of subjects to administer what they believed were potentially lethal electric shocks to another person, simply because a researcher in a lab coat told them to continue. The claim positions these findings as proof of humanity's disturbing capacity for compliance-driven cruelty and is frequently invoked to explain historical atrocities, particularly the Holocaust.

The narrative usually proceeds as follows: average citizens were recruited for what they thought was a learning experiment, then systematically coerced into administering increasingly painful electric shocks to a "learner" (actually an actor) who screamed, pleaded, and eventually fell silent. Despite obvious distress from both the "victim" and themselves, most subjects continued to the maximum 450-volt level when prodded by the experimenter. This supposedly reveals that under the right authoritarian conditions, normal people become willing executioners.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim contains both accurate elements and significant distortions. The factual core is solid: Milgram did conduct a series of obedience experiments beginning in 1961, approximately 65% of subjects did administer the maximum shock level, and the studies are among the most famous and replicated in psychology. The basic experimental setup is also correctly described—subjects believed they were participating in a learning study and were instructed to deliver electric shocks of increasing intensity.

However, the claim's interpretive framework—that this "proves" ordinary people will "torture" others when "ordered to"—embeds several problematic assumptions that decades of subsequent research have complicated:

**The "torture" framing oversimplifies the psychological reality.** Subjects weren't sadistically inflicting pain but were caught in a complex psychological situation involving competing moral imperatives—helping science, obeying legitimate authority, and avoiding harm to another person. Many subjects showed extreme distress and repeatedly questioned the procedure.

**The "ordinary people" characterization obscures important individual differences.** While Milgram's initial samples were reasonably diverse, subsequent research has identified personality factors, cultural backgrounds, and situational variables that significantly predict compliance rates. The behavior isn't uniformly distributed across all populations.

**The "ordered to" description mischaracterizes the experimental manipulation.** Subjects weren't given direct orders but were encouraged through a series of standardized prods: "Please continue," "The experiment requires that you continue," "It is absolutely essential that you continue," and "You have no other choice, you must go on." Many subjects who reached the final prod actually refused to continue.


The epistemological gap between Milgram's actual findings and their popular interpretation reflects a broader problem in how psychological research gets translated into cultural narratives. The experiments provide strong evidence for situational influences on moral behavior, but they don't support deterministic conclusions about human nature or simple predictions about real-world behavior.

**What the research actually shows:** People can be led to act against their moral intuitions when caught between competing social pressures, when authority figures provide seemingly legitimate justification, when responsibility is diffused, and when the consequences of their actions are psychologically distant. The process is gradual, involves significant internal conflict, and varies considerably across individuals and situations.

**What the research doesn't show:** That humans are inherently evil, that ordinary people are one command away from becoming torturers, or that obedience to authority inevitably leads to atrocity. The experimental context—with its scientific legitimacy, graduated commitment process, and ambiguous moral landscape—doesn't directly map onto most real-world authority relationships.

The epistemological status of conclusions depends heavily on the level of abstraction. At the concrete level—"In Milgram's specific experimental setup, most subjects continued to the maximum shock level"—the evidence is strong. At intermediate levels—"Situational pressures can override individual moral judgments under certain conditions"—the evidence is substantial but requires careful specification of those conditions. At abstract levels—"People will torture when ordered"—the evidence becomes much weaker and the conclusion potentially misleading.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemological foundation of Milgram's experiments is robust in terms of basic methodology and replication. The core finding—that situational pressures can lead people to act against their moral intuitions—has been confirmed across cultures, decades, and various experimental modifications. Jerry Burger's 2009 partial replication at Santa Clara University found similar compliance rates even with enhanced ethical protections, suggesting the basic phenomenon is real and persistent.

However, the epistemological status of popular interpretations is far more problematic. The shift from "subjects administered shocks in this specific experimental context" to "ordinary people will torture when ordered" involves several inferential leaps that subsequent research has challenged:

**Gina Perry's archival research** revealed significant methodological complexities that weren't apparent in Milgram's published reports. Her 2013 book "Behind the Shock Machine" documented that many subjects expressed suspicion about the reality of the shocks, that experimenters often deviated from the prescribed script when subjects resisted, and that Milgram's data was considerably messier than his clean statistical summaries suggested.

**Reanalysis by Alexander Haslam and colleagues** (2015) found that subjects who genuinely believed they were harming another person were actually more likely to disobey the experimenter. perceived legitimacy of the authority and belief in the scientific purpose were crucial mediating factors—not blind obedience to arbitrary commands.

**Cross-cultural replications** have found significant variation in compliance rates, ranging from around 15% to 90% depending on cultural context, suggesting that the behavior isn't a universal human tendency but is mediated by cultural values around authority, individualism, and social obligation.

The epistemological challenge is that Milgram's experiments are simultaneously over-interpreted (as proof of universal human evil) and under-analyzed (in terms of the specific psychological mechanisms involved). The research demonstrates something important about human psychology, but that "something" is more complex than the popular narrative suggests.

The narrative's emotional impact also plays a powerful role in its persistence. The Milgram experiment functions as a modern parable—a compact, dramatic story with a clear moral lesson about human weakness. Parables survive not because they are accurate but because they are useful: they give people a framework for making sense of otherwise incomprehensible events like genocide. The "65% will torture" headline is psychologically satisfying in a way that a nuanced account of graduated commitment, role confusion, and variable compliance rates never could be. This narrative economy makes the simplified version almost impossible to dislodge from popular culture, because the simplified version does more emotional and explanatory work per sentence than the accurate version can.

Pedagogical inertia compounds the problem. Introductory psychology textbooks have presented the simplified Milgram narrative for over fifty years, and many instructors themselves learned it in this form. Even when updated editions include caveats about Perry's archival findings or Haslam and Reicher's reanalysis, these qualifications tend to appear as footnotes to the central dramatic arc rather than as fundamental reframings. Students leave introductory courses with the parable firmly installed, and only a small minority ever encounter the revisionist literature. The result is a self-perpetuating cycle in which each generation of psychology graduates carries the oversimplified narrative into journalism, policy discussion, and popular nonfiction.

There is also a motivational dimension to belief persistence: the simplified Milgram narrative serves as a moral warning that people find genuinely useful. If "anyone can become a torturer under the right conditions," then vigilance against authority is always warranted. Accepting the more nuanced interpretation—that compliance depends on specific mediating factors and varies across individuals and cultures—feels less urgent, less actionable, and even potentially dangerous, as though admitting complexity might lower people's guard against authoritarianism. This perceived moral utility of the oversimplified version creates resistance to correction that operates independently of the evidence.

The experiment's visual and narrative aesthetics also contribute to its cultural persistence. The image of an ordinary person in a lab coat methodically flipping switches while someone screams behind a wall is cinematically powerful—it has been dramatized in films, television shows, and documentary reenactments dozens of times. Each dramatization reinforces the simplified narrative because the dramatic medium rewards clean storylines over methodological nuance. A film that accurately depicted the messiness of Milgram's actual data—the subjects who saw through the deception, the experimenters who went off-script, the wide variation across conditions—would make for a much less compelling viewing experience. The cultural reproduction of the myth is thus partly a consequence of the narrative constraints of the media that transmit it.


## 4. Verdict

**AUGMENT:** The claim accurately describes the basic findings of Milgram's obedience experiments but embeds them within an interpretive framework that oversimplifies the psychological mechanisms involved and overgeneralizes from laboratory findings to real-world behavior.

The experiments genuinely demonstrated that situational pressures can lead people to act against their moral intuitions under specific conditions: when authority figures provide seemingly legitimate scientific justification, when responsibility is diffused through institutional context, when the harmful consequences are psychologically distant, and when commitment is built gradually through small initial steps.

However, the popular narrative of "ordinary people will torture when ordered" mischaracterizes both the experimental manipulation and the psychological reality of subjects' experience. The experiments didn't involve direct orders to torture but rather a complex moral situation involving competing pressures and ambiguous responsibilities. Many subjects showed extreme distress and questioned the procedure throughout.

Subsequent research has revealed that the effect is mediated by factors including perceived legitimacy of authority, belief in the scientific purpose, cultural values around hierarchy and individualism, and individual personality differences. The behavior isn't uniformly distributed across all populations or situations.

The claim serves an important cultural function by highlighting humanity's capacity for situationally-influenced moral failure, but it risks promoting an oversimplified deterministic view of human behavior that could paradoxically reduce sense of moral agency and responsibility.

A more accurate interpretation would emphasize the experiments as demonstrating the power of graduated commitment, role confusion, and legitimate authority to override moral intuitions—while noting that this process is neither inevitable nor uniform, and that understanding the specific mechanisms involved is crucial for developing effective safeguards against real-world applications.

The research remains scientifically important and culturally relevant, but its implications are more complex and conditional than the popular narrative suggests. The goal should be augmenting the simplified interpretation with nuanced understanding rather than dismissing the underlying findings or their significance for understanding human behavior in institutional contexts.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Milgram's experiments exist within a broader ecosystem of social psychology research on conformity, authority, and moral behavior. This wider context both supports and complicates the popular interpretation:

**Supporting research** includes Asch's conformity studies, Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment (though this has faced significant criticism), and various studies of bystander intervention, social proof, and commitment consistency. These collectively suggest that situational factors can powerfully influence behavior in ways that people don't anticipate.

**Complicating research** includes work on moral psychology by Jonathan Haidt and others showing that moral reasoning is often post-hoc rationalization rather than cause of behavior, research on individual differences in susceptibility to authority, and studies showing that many historical atrocities involved active participation rather than reluctant obedience.

**Recent developments** in the field have emphasized the importance of social identity, in-group loyalty, and ideological conviction in explaining harmful behavior. Research by Haslam, Reicher, and others suggests that people often engage in harmful acts not because they're blindly following orders, but because they identify with the group's goals and see their actions as morally justified within that framework.

The Holocaust research that originally motivated Milgram's work has also evolved. Historians like Christopher Browning ("Ordinary Men") have documented how Nazi perpetrators often had opportunities to refuse participation without severe consequences, yet chose to continue. ideological commitment, careerism, peer pressure, and gradual habituation were more important than simple obedience to authority.

**Methodological critiques** have also expanded the conversation. Beyond Perry's archival revelations, researchers have questioned whether laboratory studies can meaningfully model real-world atrocities, whether the ethical violations involved in deception studies compromise their scientific value, and whether the focus on individual psychology deflects attention from structural and institutional factors.

The field has increasingly moved toward more nuanced models that consider multiple pathways to harmful behavior: blind obedience (Milgram's focus), social identity and group loyalty (Reicher and Haslam), moral disengagement (Bandura), and ideological conviction (Staub). Each pathway involves different psychological mechanisms and would require different preventive interventions.

The implications for institutional design deserve particular attention. If the simplified narrative were correct—that ordinary people simply obey harmful orders—then the primary safeguard would be removing authority structures or teaching individual resistance. But the more nuanced understanding points toward different interventions entirely. Graduated commitment (the "foot-in-the-door" escalation that Milgram's procedure itself embodied) can be disrupted by institutional circuit breakers: mandatory review points, rotation of personnel, explicit opt-out protocols, and structured dissent channels. Diffusion of responsibility can be countered by clear chains of accountability. Role confusion can be addressed by separating the authority to assign tasks from the authority to evaluate their ethical legitimacy. These structural safeguards are both more practical and more effective than appeals to individual moral courage, which Milgram's own data shows to be unreliable under pressure.

The relationship between Milgram's findings and the emerging science of moral injury also warrants exploration. Many of Milgram's subjects who complied to the maximum shock level reported lasting psychological distress—guilt, shame, and disturbed self-concept—that mirrors what clinicians now recognize as moral injury in military veterans, healthcare workers, and first responders. This suggests that compliance in Milgram's paradigm was not evidence of moral indifference but of a situation in which people acted against their own values and suffered for it. The psychological cost of compliance is itself evidence against the "ordinary evil" interpretation: genuinely indifferent people would not experience moral injury.

Finally, the digital age has created new contexts for studying obedience and authority that both extend and complicate Milgram's legacy. Online radicalization, algorithmic content curation, and the diffusion of responsibility in platform governance all create conditions structurally analogous to Milgram's laboratory—graduated escalation, perceived legitimacy of the system, psychological distance from consequences, and ambiguity about individual responsibility. Understanding these parallels requires the nuanced, mechanism-specific interpretation of Milgram rather than the blunt "people obey orders" summary. The experiments remain profoundly relevant, but their relevance depends on getting the interpretation right.

The therapeutic and clinical dimensions of the Milgram legacy also deserve recognition. The concept of "obedience to authority" has been incorporated into clinical ethics training for healthcare professionals, where it serves as a cautionary framework for understanding how institutional hierarchies can suppress dissent and enable medical errors. The aviation industry's Crew Resource Management (CRM) training was directly influenced by research on authority gradients, including Milgram's work, and has demonstrably reduced accidents by empowering junior crew members to challenge senior officers' decisions. These practical applications represent the best use of Milgram's findings: not as evidence of human depravity, but as diagnostic tools for identifying the specific structural conditions under which compliance overrides judgment, and engineering those conditions out of high-stakes systems.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed this specific formulation of Milgram's findings, though the experiments themselves are occasionally referenced in their fact-checks of related psychological claims. The absence of a dedicated Snopes analysis likely reflects the complexity of the topic—it's neither a simple true/false claim nor a contemporary rumor, but rather an interpretation of established scientific research that requires nuanced evaluation.

When Snopes does mention Milgram's work, it's typically in the context of debunking oversimplified applications of the findings to current events or conspiracy theories. They tend to cite the experiments as legitimate research without diving into the interpretive controversies that have emerged in recent decades.
