## §1: Claim and Verdict Summary

A widely circulated story holds that when the Mars Company released the Milky Way and 3 Musketeers candy bars — presented in the claim as simultaneously launched products — the wrappers were accidentally switched. According to the legend, Milky Way should have been named 3 Musketeers (because it has three distinct layers: chocolate-malt nougat, caramel, and chocolate coating) and 3 Musketeers should have been called Milky Way (because its smooth, chocolate-nougat interior evokes "a veritable galaxy of taste"). The label mix-up was supposedly discovered only after both products had become enormous commercial successes, at which point it was too late to reverse the names.

The Snopes verdict is **False**. The claim fails at its most basic empirical premise: the two bars were not released simultaneously, or even in the same decade. Frank C. Mars introduced the Milky Way bar in **1923**. His Mars Company did not begin producing 3 Musketeers bars until **1932** — nine years later. Two products cannot have their labels accidentally switched at launch unless they are launched at the same time.

The accurate history of the 3 Musketeers name is more interesting than the legend suggests. When first introduced in the 1930s, 3 Musketeers was not a single chocolate bar but a package containing three separate bars, each with a different flavor of fluffy milk chocolate-covered nougat: one vanilla, one chocolate, and one strawberry. The name thus accurately described the product — three distinct flavors, like the three heroes of Alexandre Dumas' 1844 novel. The vanilla and strawberry bars were discontinued during World War II, when sugar rationing made maintaining three flavors economically impractical, and 3 Musketeers became a single chocolate bar that retained its now-anachronistic name.

## §2: Historical and Cultural Context

Understanding why this legend is compelling requires engaging with the actual history of both products and with the cultural logic of the "Just-So Story" explanatory tradition.

**The Milky Way bar (1923):** Frank C. Mars, founder of the Mars Company, introduced the Milky Way bar in 1923. Contrary to widespread assumption, the name was not derived from the Milky Way galaxy. Instead, the bar was designed to replicate the flavor of a malted milkshake — a popular soda fountain drink of the era — and was named after "malted milk," a drink product of that era. The bar originally came in two flavors: a chocolate-malt nougat version with milk chocolate coating (which became the standard Milky Way) and a vanilla nougat version with a dark chocolate coating (later sold as "Forever Yours," and eventually rebranded as Milky Way Dark and then Milky Way Midnight). According to Broekel, Ray (1982, *The Great American Candy Bar Book*, Houghton Mifflin, p. 23), the Milky Way was the first mass-marketed candy bar to incorporate nougat — a then-novel ingredient in American confectionery — which contributed substantially to its commercial success as a novel texture offering.

**The 3 Musketeers bar (1932):** Nine years after Milky Way's introduction, Mars introduced the 3 Musketeers bar, which was explicitly designed as a completely different type of product. The original three-flavor architecture — vanilla, chocolate, and strawberry nougat bars in a single wrapper — was a commercially innovative format at the time, creating a variety-pack in miniature form. The Alexandre Dumas connection (the 1844 novel *Les Trois Mousquetaires*, which had been adapted into multiple stage and film productions in the intervening decades) lent the product cultural cachet while the three-flavors structure literally instantiated the "three" of the name. This was deliberate naming, not accidental mislabeling.

**The wartime transformation:** The consolidation of 3 Musketeers from three flavors to one was a direct consequence of World War II sugar and ingredient rationing, documented in the confectionery industry's wartime production records. Mars made the business decision to retain the single best-performing flavor (chocolate) and discontinue vanilla and strawberry. Once the name "3 Musketeers" was established as a strong brand, it persisted even after the product it accurately described no longer existed. This is a well-understood phenomenon in brand management: names become dissociated from their original descriptive function and acquire independent identity value (Carpenter, G. S. and Nakamoto, K., 1989, "Consumer Preference Formation and Pioneering Advantage," *Journal of Marketing Research*, 26(3): 285–298).

**The legend's genealogy:** The legend likely arose from a specific cognitive dissonance that anyone familiar with both bars experiences: *why does a bar with three components have a name suggesting a single Dumas hero trio, while a bar with two components has a name that sounds arithmetic?* The "switched labels" theory is a confabulated resolution of this apparent naming inconsistency. It is, as Snopes observes, a "Just So Story" — a Kipling-style explanatory narrative that provides a satisfying causal account of an otherwise puzzling phenomenon.

## §3: Epistemic Analysis and Novelty

**Epistemic novelty:** The Milky Way/3 Musketeers confusion is a textbook case of what cognitive psychologists call a **teleological bias** in historical reasoning — the human tendency to impose purpose and intentionality on outcomes that arose through contingent historical processes.

The legend assumes that the names "Milky Way" and "3 Musketeers" are *incongruous* with their products in a way that requires explanation: Milky Way (galaxy reference) seems more compatible with the three-layered bar; 3 Musketeers (trio reference) seems more compatible with the multi-component bar. This perceived incongruity is then resolved by a purpose-narrative ("they should have been switched; the switch happened by accident"). But the incongruity is itself based on retrospective misreading of both names: "Milky Way" did not evoke the galaxy when it was named (it evoked a malted milk drink), and "3 Musketeers" was not numerically incongruous when it was named (it referred to three actual bars in the package).

This is a case of what historians of technology call a "path dependency" narrative problem (David, P. A., 1985, "Clio and the Economics of QWERTY," *American Economic Review*, 75(2): 332–337). Path-dependent outcomes often look arbitrary or suboptimal in retrospect because the original conditions that made them sensible are no longer visible. The QWERTY story — which holds that the keyboard layout was designed to slow typists to prevent typewriter jamming — is structurally identical to the candy-bar legend: both are confabulated Just-So Stories that resolve the apparent irrationality of a path-dependent outcome by positing an accident or design failure at origin.

The psychological literature on "brand myth" formation is relevant here. Holt (2004, *How Brands Become Icons: The Principles of Cultural Branding*, Harvard Business School Press) documented how consumers construct elaborated narrative myths around commercial products that extend well beyond the products' actual histories, creating what he called "myth markets." The Milky Way/3 Musketeers legend participates in this myth-market formation for two of America's most culturally embedded candy bars.

What distinguishes this case for misinformation epistemology is the story's *internal coherence*. Unlike many food misinformation claims, the switched-labels legend does not require the audience to accept an extraordinary or disgusting premise. It requires only acceptance of a mundane operational error — a label mix-up — at the beginning of two product launches. This very mundanity makes the legend more persuasive, because extraordinary claims invite skepticism while ordinary mistakes seem entirely plausible. Sunstein and Thaler (2008, *Nudge: Improving Decisions About Health, Wealth, and Happiness*, Yale University Press) note that beliefs about organizational error are particularly prone to plausibility overestimation, because people understand from experience that organizations do make mistakes.

The research gap this points to: *At what rate do plausible Just-So brand-mythology stories displace accurate product histories in consumer memory?* Existing consumer psychology research on brand attachment (Fournier, S., 1998, "Consumers and Their Brands," *Journal of Consumer Research*, 24(4): 343–373) does not address this specific question. A longitudinal study tracking consumer knowledge of candy bar histories against exposure to correct versus mythologized versions would be valuable.

## §4: Evidence Evaluation

The evidentiary case for the False verdict is unusually clear and well-documented.

**The primary evidence:** The 1923 Milky Way introduction date is documented by the Mars Company's own official history and by multiple independent confectionery history sources, most authoritatively Broekel (1982, *The Great American Candy Bar Book*, Houghton Mifflin). The 1932 3 Musketeers introduction date is similarly well-documented. An interval of nine years between product launches makes the "simultaneously released with switched labels" narrative impossible on its face.

**The Milky Way etymology:** The association of "Milky Way" with the malted milk drink — rather than the galaxy — is documented in the brand's own historical materials (archived at milkywaybar.com per Snopes's citation) and in confectionery history literature. This documentation matters for the legend because without it, the legend's claim that "Milky Way" is more galaxy-appropriate than the 3 Musketeers bar seems marginally plausible. Once the malted-milk etymology is understood, the "mismatch" that motivates the legend dissolves entirely.

**The original 3 Musketeers architecture:** The three-flavor original composition of 3 Musketeers is documented through vintage packaging, trade press archives from the 1930s, and confectionery history sources including Richardson (2015, *Sweets: A History of Candy*, Bloomsbury, which draws on Mars corporate archives). The connection to Dumas's novel *Les Trois Mousquetaires* is confirmed by early advertising materials that featured Three Musketeers character imagery on the wrapper — imagery that was discontinued as the product's format changed.

**The wartime consolidation:** The elimination of the vanilla and strawberry bars during World War II is documented in confectionery industry trade press (see *Candy Industry* magazine archives, 1942–1945). The USDA sugar rationing regulations that constrained confectionery production during this period (War Food Order No. 56, 1943) provide the regulatory context for the consolidation decision.

**Absence of contrary evidence:** No contemporary sources from 1923 or 1932 document a label-switching incident. No Mars Company corporate records support the legend (which would typically generate internal communication about such a significant mix-up). No contemporaneous trade press accounts of either launch describe confusion between the two products' names. The legend leaves no archival trace, consistent with its status as a post-hoc explanatory fiction rather than a historical record.

## §5: Verdict Justification

The False verdict is firmly supported and admits essentially no residual uncertainty about the core factual claim. The two bars were not released simultaneously, making a simultaneous label-switching impossible. The legend is false at its foundational premise.

The verdict is proportionate to the evidence. This is not a case where evidence is ambiguous or where a "mixture" verdict would be appropriate. The specific claim — that wrappers were switched at a simultaneous product launch, and that by the time the error was discovered the products were too successful to rename — is falsified by the documented chronology.

The broader appeal of the story deserves acknowledgment in the verdict context. The legend is compelling precisely because it fits what Cialdini (2021, *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*, revised edition, Harper Business) identified as narrative "coherence" rather than "accuracy" — the story hangs together well, explains an apparent puzzle, and implicates no malicious actors, only an ordinary operational mistake. These features make it likable rather than threatening, which reduces the motivated skepticism that more alarming claims typically generate.

One small caveat to the False verdict: Snopes correctly notes that both the Milky Way and 3 Musketeers names have become somewhat detached from their original descriptive referents, and that there is a *real* incongruity between the current product composition and the historical name for 3 Musketeers specifically. It is genuinely true that a consumer today, learning for the first time that 3 Musketeers has two components and Milky Way has three, might experience a naming-logic mismatch. This intuition is not wrong — it's just that the correct explanation is historical obsolescence (the name precedes the product's reformulation), not original mislabeling.

## §6: Broader Implications and Lessons

The Milky Way/3 Musketeers legend illustrates several broader principles of commercial brand mythology and its intersection with misinformation dynamics.

**The QWERTY problem in brand history:** Path-dependent outcomes in commercial branding — names that accurately described a now-discontinued product feature, or that referenced a cultural context now forgotten — are systematically vulnerable to Just-So story formation. When consumers encounter a name that seems incongruous with a product, the temptation to confabulate an explanatory accident is strong, and the correct explanation (historical path dependency) requires more contextual knowledge than most consumers possess. Brand historians and marketers face a chronic challenge in preserving the original rationale for naming decisions against the cultural drift that makes those rationales invisible.

Heath and Heath (2007, *Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die*, Random House) identified "unexpectedness" and "concreteness" as key features of stickiness in narratives. The legend qualifies on both dimensions: it is unexpected (switching labels is a surprising mistake for a major corporation to make) and concrete (specific named candy bars, a specific cause of the error). The correct explanation — that 3 Musketeers used to have three flavors — is also unexpected and concrete, suggesting that with good communication the accurate story could theoretically be as sticky as the legend.

**Implications for confectionery brand management:** The case has practical implications for companies managing brands with histories that have become detached from their original rationale. Mars's 3 Musketeers brand carries a naming history that, if communicated effectively, is actually more interesting than the legend (a Neapolitan variety pack reformulated under wartime rationing). Brand managers typically underinvest in accurate historical communication, creating the vacuum that legends fill. Proactive brand-history communication — through packaging, advertising, and digital content — may be more effective at preventing legend formation than reactive debunking after legends are already established.

**The "Just-So Story" misinformation type:** The legend exemplifies a category of misinformation that scholars of narrative cognition have not yet systematically studied as distinct from other false belief types. Just-So Stories — confabulated causal explanations for apparently incongruous features of familiar objects — are distinguished by their narrative elegance, their low perceived stakes, their lack of malicious intent, and their resistance to correction through straightforward factual counter-argument (because the audience didn't care much about the question in the first place). They spread through the pleasure of the story rather than through motivated reasoning, confirmation bias, or political identity threat. Their debunking therefore requires invoking curiosity rather than defensiveness — a different rhetorical register than fact-checking typically adopts.

**Historical preservation:** The case underscores the public value of maintaining accurate product histories in accessible archives. Broekel's 1982 *The Great American Candy Bar Book* — a specialized reference that few readers would encounter organically — is the key source for the correct chronology. If that book were not indexable by Snopes researchers, the False verdict would have been harder to document with precision. This points to the broader importance of accessible, well-indexed secondary historical sources as infrastructure for misinformation research.
