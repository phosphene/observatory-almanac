## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

One of the most persistent and long-lived folk etymologies in the English language holds that the beef cut known as "sirloin" received its name because an English monarch—variously identified as Henry VIII, James I, or Charles II—was so pleased with a particularly fine joint of beef at dinner that he drew his sword and knighted it, declaring "Arise, Sir Loin." The tale has been repeated in prestigious reference works including Samuel Johnson's 1755 *Dictionary of the English Language*, Jonathan Swift's 1738 *A Treatise on Polite Conversation*, William Kitchiner's 1822 *The Cook's Oracle*, and as recently as 2005 in a BBC News report about the Lancashire tradition attributing the knighting to James I at Hoghton Tower in 1617.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson rated this claim **False**. The sirloin etymology is demonstrably false on both chronological and linguistic grounds. Chronologically, the word "sirloin" first appeared in English in the early sixteenth century, and while Henry VIII might conceivably have been its originator (he reigned 1509–1547), James I (reigned 1603–1625) and Charles II (reigned 1660–1685) could not have coined a word already in English use before their births. The decisive evidence is linguistic: the original English spelling was "surloyn" or "surloine," not "sirloin"—a spelling that transparently derives from Middle French *surlonge* (literally "over the loin," referring to a cut taken from above the loin), not from the French or English word "sir." The spelling shift from "sur-" to "sir-" is documented as a later development that created the punning possibility that generations of storytellers then backdated as an origin.

This entry examines the full documentary record of the sirloin legend from its earliest print appearances through the 2005 BBC account, the Middle French etymology and the anatomical meaning of "surlonge," the self-reinforcing dynamics of prestigious sources repeating folk etymologies, the cultural significance of food-knighting as a symbolic act, and the broader history of "sur-" and "over" prefixes in French-derived English food vocabulary.

## §2 The Documentary Record: Four Centuries of a False Etymology

Few folk etymologies have been as thoroughly and respectably endorsed as the sirloin knighting story. The narrative appears across nearly four centuries of English print culture, endorsed by writers whose reputations would ordinarily grant credibility to the claims they reported.

The earliest print evidence connecting a royal knighting story to the word "sirloin" appears in a 1694 note in the *Athenian Mercury* (March 6, 1694), which attributed the tale to Henry VIII and the Abbot of Reading—a story purportedly drawn from Thomas Fuller's 1655 *Church History of Britain*, where Fuller wrote of "a Sir-loyne of beef, so knighted, saith tradition, by this King Henry." Fuller's phrasing is careful: he attributes the claim to "tradition" rather than asserting it as historical fact. By 1694, someone had converted Fuller's cautious attribution to an apparently definitive historical account.

Jonathan Swift's 1738 *A Treatise on Polite Conversation* assigned the knighting to "our King James First," whose royal hospitality the punning knight was said to have exercised at a noble's dinner. Swift's text is itself a manual of polite conversation clichés—deliberately ironic—which raises the question of whether he was reporting the etymology in good faith or including it as a representative example of the kind of dinner-party bon mot that passed for sophisticated conversation in his era.

Samuel Johnson's 1755 *Dictionary of the English Language* is the most prestigious endorsement in the list. Johnson's definition noted that "sir" was "A title given to the loin of beef, which one of our kings knighted in a fit of good humour." The telling vagueness ("one of our kings") suggests that even Johnson was aware that the attribution to a specific monarch was contested—there were already multiple candidates in circulation by his time—while still accepting the general framework of the story as credible tradition. Johnson's *Dictionary* was so authoritative in setting the standard for English lexicography that its inclusion of the sirloin etymology gave the story several additional decades of uncritical circulation.

William Kitchiner's 1822 *The Cook's Oracle* assigned the creation definitively to Charles II. The BBC News report of 16 April 2005 assigned it to James I at Hoghton Tower, citing a specific location and year (1617)—the most geographically and temporally precise version of the legend, which is predictably also the most unsupported. A tourist marker or interpretive sign at Hoghton Tower had apparently been maintaining the James I tradition as a regional heritage claim.

The royal gallery of candidates—Henry VIII, James I, Charles II—tells its own story about the folk etymology process. Once a story about "an English king knighting a piece of beef" enters cultural circulation, different regions and eras attach different monarchs to it based on their own narrative preferences. Henry VIII gets the attribution because he is England's paradigmatic glutton-king, famous for enormous appetite. James I appears because the Hoghton Tower tradition needed a specific historical anchor. Charles II appears in the Restoration-era cookbooks because that period represented a revival of aristocratic dining culture after the Puritan interregnum. The story migrates to whichever king best embodies "royal appetite" for the particular audience receiving the tale.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Middle French Etymology and the Anatomy of Beef Cuts

The definitive linguistic refutation of the sirloin folk etymology is available in the original spellings of the word, and understanding why those spellings settle the question requires examining both the Middle French derivation and the anatomical logic of beef cutting traditions.

The Middle French word *surlonge* is a transparent compound: *sur* ("over," "above") + *longe* ("loin"). The *longe* component derives from Latin *lumbulus* (diminutive of *lumbus*, "loin"), via Old French *longe*. The *sur* prefix is one of the most productive in French, appearing in dozens of English borrowings: *surface* (over + face), *surname* (over + name, i.e., the name used in addition to the Christian name), *surcharge*, *surtax*, *surmount*, *surgeon* (originally *chirurgeon*, but compare the "sur-" structure), and many culinary terms (Ayto, John, 1990, *Dictionary of Word Origins*, Arcade Publishing, New York, p. 479). Mikkelson (2001) notes that "surname" stems from the same French root, indicating a family name used "over" (in addition to) one's given name.

In the context of beef cuts, *surlonge* refers anatomically to the cut taken from above (over) the loin—the short loin region of the cow's back, superior to the tenderloin and posterior to the ribs. This is the correct anatomical description of what is sold as sirloin today: the primal cut between the short loin and the round, typically located over the hip area and constituting a relatively tender, flavor-rich cut that benefits from dry-heat cooking methods including roasting and grilling. The French anatomical cut terminology is documented in English culinary history (Davidson, Alan, 1999, *The Oxford Companion to Food*, Oxford University Press, pp. 724–726), confirming that the nomenclature of beef primal cuts was largely organized during the period of extensive French culinary influence on English practice.

The spelling shift from "surloyn/surloine" to "sirloin" is documented as occurring somewhat gradually in English from the early to mid-17th century onward. This is consistent with normal phonological processes in English borrowing: the French *sur* prefix, when borrowed into a language that had no similar productive prefix, underwent phonological reanalysis toward the more familiar English element "sir." Once the spelling stabilized as "sir-loin" rather than "sur-loin," the pun became not just available but seemingly invited—a joint of beef with "Sir" in its name obviously cries out for a story about how it was knighted. The folk etymology is in this sense an almost inevitable response to the spelling shift, generated locally and repeatedly by anyone who noticed the pun without knowing the earlier spelling.

Ackermann (1923, *Popular Fallacies Explained and Corrected*, The Old Westminster Press, London, p. 529) and Room (1986, *The Fascinating Origins of Everyday Words*, NTC Publishing, Chicago, p. 160), both cited by Mikkelson (2001), confirm this analysis in their independent treatments of the sirloin etymology. Room specifically traces the French derivation and the documentary evidence against the knighting story. The existence of two independent early-to-mid 20th century reference works making the same correction confirms that professional lexicographers have been aware of and publishing the correct etymology for at least a century, without eliminating the folk etymology's popular circulation.

## §4 Why the False Etymology Persists: Narrative Pleasure and Institutional Amplification

The sirloin folk etymology exemplifies a general principle of folk etymology persistence: when a prestigious source endorses a false etymology, it becomes self-reinforcing in ways that correct scholarly accounts struggle to overcome.

The normal social epistemology of etymology in lay discourse assigns authority to lexicographers, historians, and similarly credentialed sources. When Samuel Johnson—the era's supreme lexicographic authority—included the knighting story in his *Dictionary*, he gave it a social proof that overrode individual skepticism. "If Johnson believed it, it must be true" is not an unreasonable heuristic for an 18th-century reader with no access to medieval French manuscripts. The story subsequently propagated through later works citing Johnson's authority, and through popular writers who repeated it as an entertaining historical fact without checking primary sources.

The BBC News report of 2005—cited by Mikkelson (2001) from a 16 April 2005 article about Hoghton Tower—represents a modern recurrence of the same dynamic. BBC News, as an authoritative and widely-consumed media outlet, reporting on a local heritage tourism claim about a specific king, place, and date, gave the story a fresh round of credibility. The Hoghton Tower tradition serves tourist and heritage purposes—it provides a reason to visit a specific site and a locally-grounded narrative—which means institutional incentives exist to maintain the claim even against linguistic evidence.

Taylor's 1630 *The Great Eater of Kent* contains the earliest clear pun on "Sir Loin of Beef" identified by Mikkelson (2001), noting a fictional combat with "a worthy knight called Sir Loin of Beef." This is a deliberate literary pun rather than an etymological claim—Taylor knew perfectly well he was making a joke—but it demonstrates that the pun was available in English by 1630. Once the pun was in literary circulation, it was easy for later writers to transform a deliberate witticism into a retroactively claimed historical origin, forgetting that they were working from a joke rather than a chronicle.

The 10 July 1880 *Notes and Queries* entry that Mikkelson cites is particularly interesting for its meta-textual quality: a 19th-century scholarly journal systematically collecting evidence about the competing etymology stories, which already included Henry VIII, the Abbot of Reading, Fuller's 1655 citation of "tradition," the Athenian Mercury's 1694 elaboration, and James I. By 1880, there was already enough variant material that a scholar was compiling it as a problem of historical attribution. The existence of this 19th-century scholarly thread is a reminder that the sirloin etymology debate is not a product of internet-era skepticism but has been traceable as a scholarly concern for at least 150 years.

## §5 Comparative Cases: Food Words Derived from French Culinary Terminology

The sirloin etymology fits within a broader pattern of French-derived English food vocabulary that has been misunderstood or folk-etymologized due to the opacity of the French prefix or root to English speakers.

The French culinary vocabulary that entered English during and after the Norman Conquest (1066) and through centuries of subsequent French gastronomic influence is extensive. Many English food terms that seem purely English turn out on examination to be French-derived: "beef" (from Old French *boeuf*), "pork" (from Old French *porc*), "veal" (from Old French *veel*), "mutton" (from Old French *mouton*)—all the names for the meats of animals that English farmers raised but French-speaking Norman aristocrats ate. This food vocabulary split (English names for live animals, French names for their meat when served at table) is itself one of the most cited pieces of evidence for the social stratification of post-Conquest England.

Within this pattern, "surloin/sirloin" is one of a set of French terms for primal beef cuts that entered English through the medieval and early modern periods. Room (1986) notes that the French anatomical cut terminology—organizing beef into primal cuts by location relative to anatomical landmarks—was professional butcher knowledge that traveled with French culinary practice into England. The "sur" prefix in surlonge distinguished the cut above (over) the loin from the loin itself, a distinction that made anatomical and commercial sense to anyone working with the full carcass.

The word "surname," which Mikkelson (2001) notes as sharing the same *sur* root, is a helpful comparative case for English readers. A surname is a name that goes "over" or "above" (i.e., is added to, used in conjunction with) the given name—not a name that is knighted or elevated above other names by a royal decree, but simply a name positioned in the over/additional relationship to another name. The same structural logic applies to surloin: a cut positioned "over" (above, superior to) the loin, just as a surname is positioned "over" (in addition to) the given name.

## §6 Broader Significance: The Sirloin Case and the Long Debate About False Etymologies

The sirloin etymology is one of the most thoroughly documented examples of a false folk etymology that has been repeatedly corrected in authoritative sources without losing popular circulation. Its presence in Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755), Swift's *Treatise* (1738), Fuller's *Church History* (1655), and the 2005 BBC News means that it has had more than four centuries of public life and has been endorsed by sources ranging from the greatest English lexicographer to a national broadcast news service.

Room (1986) and Rawson (1994)—both cited by Mikkelson—wrote popular-audience books specifically cataloguing false folk etymologies and replacing them with documented derivations, yet neither eliminated the sirloin story from popular circulation. Ayto's (1990) *Dictionary of Word Origins* and the *Compact Oxford English Dictionary* (1993) both contain the correct Middle French derivation available to anyone who consults them. The persistence of the folk etymology in the face of readily available corrections is a clean case study in what information scholars call "continued influence effects"—the documented tendency for false beliefs to persist even after explicit correction, particularly when the false belief provides a more satisfying or memorable narrative than the truth (Lewandowsky, S., Ecker, U. K. H., Seifert, C. M., Schwarz, N., & Cook, J., 2012, "Misinformation and Its Correction: Continued Influence and Successful Debiasing," *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, Vol. 13, No. 3, pp. 106–131).

The EARC gap designation of **A** (Archival) reflects an interesting remaining question: precisely when did the English spelling "surloyn/surloine" shift to "sirloin," and can that shift be dated with enough precision to identify the period in which the folk etymology (the knighting story) first became plausible—i.e., the period after which the spelling look like "sir" + "loin"? The existing popular scholarship consulted by Mikkelson treats this as an incidental point. A proper historical lexicographic study using digitized early modern English corpora could potentially identify the precise decades in which "sir-" spelling became standard, which would constrain the origin date of the folk etymology itself and potentially identify the first text to transform the pun into an etymological claim rather than a joke. Such a study would contribute both to English lexicographic history and to the scholarship of folk etymology formation.

The broader cultural significance of the sirloin story is that it captures something genuine about the relationship between English aristocratic food culture and the French language: the presence of "sir" in the cut's name is not coincidental disguising of French "sur" but rather an English reshaping of a French word toward an English form with pleasingly aristocratic connotations. Even the false etymology reflects a real cultural truth—that beef was royal food, that the best cuts had elevated social status, and that the language of fine dining in England was deeply intertwined with French linguistic prestige. The story of a king knighting his beef, while false as etymology, is culturally coherent as a myth about the social meaning of eating well in pre-modern England.
