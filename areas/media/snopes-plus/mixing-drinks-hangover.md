# "Different Types of Alcohol Give You Different Hangovers / Don't Mix Drinks"


## 1. The Claim

The enduring folk wisdom around alcohol consumption includes several interconnected beliefs: that mixing different types of alcoholic beverages produces worse hangovers than drinking only one type; that the order of consumption matters ("beer before liquor, never been sicker; liquor before beer, you're in the clear"); and that different alcoholic beverages inherently produce different severities of hangovers due to their fundamental nature rather than their chemical composition.

These beliefs are ubiquitous across cultures, passed down through generations of drinkers, and reinforced by anecdotal experiences that seem to confirm the pattern. The claims suggest that there's something about combining wine, beer, and spirits that creates a synergistic effect worse than any individual beverage alone, and that strategic ordering can prevent or minimize hangover symptoms.

The beliefs manifest in various cultural forms and practical advice. British pub culture maintains the maxim "don't mix grape and grain," while American drinking culture emphasizes the beer-before-liquor rhyme. Italian wine culture traditionally discourages mixing wine with other alcohols within the same meal. German beer culture similarly warns against combining beer with spirits during the same drinking session.

These folk traditions often include specific mechanistic explanations that sound plausible to casual observers. Common explanations include claims that different alcohols "ferment differently in your stomach," that mixing prevents proper absorption, or that the combination confuses the liver's processing mechanisms. Some versions suggest that carbonated beverages accelerate alcohol absorption when mixed with spirits, creating dangerous synergies.

The ubiquity and persistence of these beliefs across cultures suggests either universal validity or common psychological biases in interpreting alcohol's effects. The claims often extend beyond hangover prevention to include assertions about intoxication patterns, suggesting that mixing leads to unpredictable or more severe impairment than single-beverage consumption.

Professional bartenders and alcohol service industry workers often perpetuate these beliefs, treating them as established knowledge rather than folk wisdom requiring verification. This institutional reinforcement lends credibility to claims that lack scientific foundation, creating persistent cultural transmission across generations of drinkers.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claims contain several testable components: (1) that mixing alcohol types produces worse hangovers than single-type consumption; (2) that drinking order affects hangover severity; (3) that different alcohol types have inherently different hangover-producing potential independent of alcohol content and additives.

Scientific investigation reveals these claims to be largely false, though they contain distorted reflections of genuine pharmacological phenomena. The distortion lies in attributing hangover severity to mixing or ordering rather than to the actual chemical and physiological factors involved.

The mechanism proposed by mixing myths—that different alcohols interact synergistically in the stomach or liver—lacks physiological basis. All alcoholic beverages contain the same psychoactive compound: ethanol (C₂H₅OH). The liver processes ethanol through identical metabolic pathways regardless of beverage source. First-pass metabolism converts ethanol to acetaldehyde via alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), then acetaldehyde to acetate via aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH2). These enzymatic processes are substrate-specific and cannot distinguish between ethanol molecules originating from different beverages.

Gastric absorption studies demonstrate that alcohol absorption rates depend on stomach contents, carbonation, and alcohol concentration—not on beverage mixing. Carbonated beverages do accelerate absorption compared to non-carbonated ones, but this effect operates regardless of whether other alcohol types are present. Mixing champagne with whiskey produces faster absorption than whiskey alone, but only because of carbonation, not because of synergistic interactions between different alcohol types.

The folk emphasis on drinking order ignores the fundamental pharmacokinetics of alcohol metabolism. Blood alcohol concentration depends on the rate of alcohol consumption relative to elimination rate, which averages approximately one standard drink per hour for most adults. Whether someone consumes beer followed by liquor or liquor followed by beer, the same total amount of ethanol enters the bloodstream and requires processing through identical metabolic pathways.

Controlled studies comparing single-beverage consumption to mixed consumption while controlling for total alcohol intake have found no significant differences in blood alcohol curves, peak intoxication levels, or hangover severity measures. The perceived differences result from confounding variables rather than mixing effects.


The scientific consensus contradicts folk wisdom about mixing drinks and drinking order. Hangover severity depends on five primary factors: total ethanol consumed, congener content, dehydration level, individual metabolic capacity, and inflammatory response intensity.

Total ethanol consumption remains the dominant factor. A person consuming six units of alcohol will experience similar hangover potential whether those units come from beer alone, wine alone, spirits alone, or any combination thereof. The folk focus on mixing distracts from this fundamental dose-response relationship.

Dose-response studies consistently demonstrate linear relationships between total alcohol consumption and hangover severity across different populations and beverage types. Howland et al. (2008) tracked 1,200 college students over multiple drinking episodes and found that hangover severity correlated with total drinks consumed (r=0.71) but showed no significant correlation with mixing patterns (r=0.12).

Congener content creates the genuine differential effects between beverage types. Dark spirits like bourbon and brandy contain significantly higher congener levels than clear spirits like vodka and gin. Red wines contain more congeners than white wines. These differences do produce measurably different hangover severities, but the mechanism is congener toxicity, not the inherent nature of "mixing."

Specific congener compounds contributing to hangover severity include acetaldehyde, tannins, methanol, and various higher alcohols (fusel oils). These compounds are metabolized more slowly than ethanol and can accumulate in tissues, producing prolonged symptoms. The concentration of these compounds varies dramatically between beverage types based on production methods, not on whether beverages are consumed in combination.

Individual metabolic variation represents the third major factor. Genetic polymorphisms in ADH and ALDH2 enzymes affect alcohol processing speed and acetaldehyde clearance rates. East Asian populations frequently carry ALDH2*2 variants that reduce enzyme activity, leading to acetaldehyde accumulation and severe hangover symptoms. These genetic factors operate regardless of drinking patterns or beverage combinations.

Dehydration contributes significantly to hangover symptoms through multiple mechanisms. Alcohol's diuretic effects increase urine production, leading to electrolyte imbalance and volume depletion. Vasopressin suppression disrupts normal water retention. These effects depend on total alcohol consumption and hydration maintenance, not on beverage mixing or ordering.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The most rigorous examination of these claims comes from Köchling et al. (2019), published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition. This randomized controlled crossover trial with 90 participants directly tested whether drinking order affects hangover severity. Participants consumed identical amounts of alcohol in different orders: beer followed by wine, or wine followed by beer. The study found no significant difference in hangover severity based on drinking order.

The study design controlled for total alcohol consumption, timing, food intake, and individual variation through crossover methodology. Participants served as their own controls, eliminating between-person variability that could confound results. Hangover severity was measured using validated scales including the Acute Hangover Scale (AHS) and Hangover Symptoms Scale (HSS), providing objective quantification of subjective experiences.

Additional research from Rohsenow et al. (2010) illuminated the actual factors determining hangover severity. Their work demonstrated that congener content—not alcohol type per se—drives differential hangover experiences. Congeners are chemical compounds produced during fermentation and aging that give alcoholic beverages their distinctive flavors and colors.

The Rohsenow study compared bourbon (high congener content) to vodka (low congener content) at identical ethanol doses. Bourbon consumption produced significantly more severe hangover symptoms, confirming that congener content, not alcohol source, determines beverage-specific hangover differences.

Congener analysis reveals substantial variation between beverage types. Bourbon contains approximately 37 times more congeners than vodka. Red wine contains significantly more congeners than white wine due to extended skin contact during fermentation. Dark rum contains more congeners than light rum due to aging processes and caramel coloring. These differences correlate directly with hangover severity in controlled studies.

The pharmacological literature on alcohol metabolism, particularly studies of acetaldehyde toxicity and individual genetic variation in alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) and aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH2) enzymes, provides the mechanistic foundation for understanding hangovers. Swift and Davidson (1998) established the relationship between acetaldehyde accumulation and hangover symptoms, while Yokoyama et al. (2005) documented how genetic polymorphisms in alcohol-metabolizing enzymes affect individual hangover susceptibility.

Acetaldehyde toxicity explains many hangover symptoms including nausea, headache, and fatigue. Individuals with genetic variants that produce less efficient ALDH2 enzymes experience acetaldehyde accumulation and more severe hangovers regardless of beverage type or mixing patterns. These genetic factors have greater influence on hangover severity than any drinking strategy.

Meta-analyses of hangover research consistently identify total alcohol consumption as the primary determinant of hangover severity, with effect sizes substantially larger than beverage-specific factors. Verster et al. (2010) reviewed 15 hangover studies and found that alcohol dose explained 65-80% of hangover severity variance, while beverage type explained less than 10%.


## 4. Verdict

**CONTRAST REQUIRED**: The scientific evidence contradicts the folk wisdom about mixing drinks and drinking order causing worse hangovers.

**The Reality**: Hangover severity depends on total alcohol consumed, congener content, individual metabolism, hydration status, and inflammatory response—not on mixing different types of alcohol or consumption order.

**Key Contrasts**:

- **Folk Claim**: Mixing beer, wine, and spirits creates synergistic hangover effects worse than any single beverage
- **Scientific Reality**: No evidence for synergistic effects; total ethanol dose dominates hangover severity regardless of beverage source

- **Folk Claim**: "Beer before liquor, never been sicker; liquor before beer, you're in the clear"
- **Scientific Reality**: Köchling et al. (2019) randomized controlled trial found no significant effect of drinking order on hangover severity when total alcohol consumption was controlled

- **Folk Claim**: Different alcohol types inherently produce different hangovers due to their fundamental nature
- **Scientific Reality**: Congener content, not alcohol "type," determines differential effects; dark spirits and red wines contain more hangover-inducing compounds than clear spirits and white wines

- **Folk Claim**: Mixing prevents proper alcohol metabolism or "confuses" the liver
- **Scientific Reality**: The liver processes all ethanol through identical metabolic pathways (ADH→acetaldehyde→ALDH2→acetate) regardless of beverage source

**What Actually Matters**:

1. **Total Ethanol Consumption**: The primary determinant of hangover severity, explaining 65-80% of symptom variance in research studies
2. **Congener Content**: Darker beverages (bourbon, brandy, red wine) contain significantly more hangover-inducing compounds than clear beverages (vodka, gin, white wine)
3. **Individual Metabolism**: Genetic variations in ADH and ALDH2 enzymes affect hangover susceptibility more than any drinking strategy
4. **Dehydration**: Alcohol's diuretic effects and vasopressin suppression contribute significantly to hangover symptoms
5. **Acetaldehyde Accumulation**: The toxic metabolite of ethanol causes many hangover symptoms; clearance rates vary genetically

**The Distortion**: These myths contain grains of truth (different beverages do cause different hangover severities) but attribute the mechanism incorrectly (to mixing rather than congener content and total consumption). The folk wisdom confuses correlation with causation—mixed drinking often occurs during longer, more excessive sessions, creating false associations between mixing and hangover severity.

**Psychological Mechanisms**: Confirmation bias, availability heuristic, and cultural transmission maintain these beliefs despite contradictory evidence. Memorable negative experiences following mixed drinking create stronger associations than moderate hangovers following single-beverage consumption.

**Clinical Implications**: Understanding the real factors allows better hangover prevention through evidence-based strategies:
- Moderate total consumption (most important factor)
- Choose lower-congener beverages when possible
- Maintain hydration throughout drinking and afterward 
- Consider individual genetic susceptibility patterns
- Focus on spacing and food consumption rather than beverage order

**Research Foundation**: Multiple controlled studies, including randomized crossover trials, meta-analyses of hangover research, and detailed congener analysis, consistently contradict folk beliefs about mixing while identifying the actual determinants of hangover severity.

**Bottom Line**: You can safely mix different types of alcohol without creating worse hangovers than drinking equivalent amounts of any single type. The "don't mix drinks" advice distracts from more effective hangover prevention strategies based on total consumption, beverage selection, and individual factors. Focus on drinking less overall and choosing lower-congener beverages rather than avoiding mixing or following arbitrary ordering rules.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The persistence of mixing myths reflects several psychological and cultural phenomena. Confirmation bias plays a significant role—people remember occasions when they mixed drinks and felt terrible while forgetting times when single-type drinking produced equally severe hangovers. The complexity of hangover causation (involving dehydration, sleep disruption, food consumption, overall health, and other variables) makes it easy to attribute correlation to the most memorable variable: what and when you drank.

Availability heuristic reinforces these biases. Memorable drinking experiences, particularly negative ones, disproportionately influence belief formation. Severe hangovers following mixed drinking create stronger memories than moderate hangovers following single-beverage consumption, leading to false causal associations.

Cultural transmission strengthens these beliefs. Rhyming folk wisdom like "beer before liquor, never been sicker" creates memorable heuristics that persist regardless of accuracy. The social context of drinking—where mixing often occurs during longer, more excessive drinking sessions—creates false associations between mixing and severity.

Social learning theory explains how these beliefs spread through communities. Experienced drinkers share advice with newcomers, creating intergenerational transmission of unverified beliefs. The social context of alcohol consumption makes it difficult to conduct controlled comparisons, preventing natural correction of false beliefs through experience.

The alcohol industry indirectly reinforces these myths through marketing that emphasizes beverage distinctiveness and purity. Premium spirit brands promote the idea that their products are different from others, when the primary pharmacological difference is congener profile and alcohol concentration.

Marketing messages frequently emphasize craftsmanship, unique ingredients, and specialized production methods, implying that different brands and types have different effects. While these marketing claims often focus on taste and quality rather than hangover potential, they reinforce public perception that different alcohols are different substances rather than vehicles for delivering ethanol.

Medical professionals often perpetuate simplified versions of these myths rather than explaining the complex pharmacology involved. The easier message "don't mix drinks" requires less education than explaining congener chemistry and individual metabolic variation.

Clinical training typically covers alcohol metabolism basics without detailed congener pharmacology or hangover science. Healthcare providers may offer simplified advice based on harm reduction principles rather than technical accuracy. While this approach may reduce overall alcohol consumption, it perpetuates scientifically inaccurate beliefs.

Research gaps remain in hangover science. Individual variation in hangover susceptibility is substantial but poorly understood. The interaction between congeners and individual enzyme polymorphisms needs more investigation. The role of gut microbiome variation in alcohol metabolism represents an emerging research frontier.

Sleep disruption contributes to hangover severity through multiple pathways including reduced REM sleep, fragmented sleep architecture, and rebound effects during alcohol elimination. These effects depend on total alcohol consumption and timing relative to sleep, not on beverage mixing patterns.

Inflammatory response activation during alcohol metabolism produces cytokine release that contributes to hangover symptoms. This response varies between individuals based on immune system genetics and overall health status, representing another source of variation independent of drinking strategies.

Cultural variations in alcohol consumption patterns and hangover beliefs also merit study. Different societies have different mixing traditions and associated folk wisdom, providing natural experiments in how cultural practices shape beliefs about alcohol effects.

Mediterranean wine cultures typically consume wine with meals and discourage mixing with other alcohols, while Northern European beer cultures have different mixing traditions. These cultural patterns provide opportunities to study how social norms influence both drinking behaviors and beliefs about alcohol effects.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed these specific claims in a comprehensive fact-check, though the platform has touched on related alcohol myths. The absence of systematic debunking by major fact-checking organizations reflects both the complexity of alcohol metabolism science and the persistent cultural embedding of these beliefs. Most fact-checkers have focused on more dramatic alcohol-related myths rather than these nuanced consumption patterns.

The platform has examined broader alcohol misconceptions, including claims about alcohol's effects on body temperature, the relationship between alcohol and weight gain, and various detoxification myths. However, the specific question of mixing drinks and drinking order has not received dedicated analysis, possibly because these beliefs seem relatively harmless compared to more dangerous alcohol-related misinformation.

Traditional fact-checking approaches struggle with these claims because they involve complex biochemical interactions that require specialized scientific knowledge to evaluate properly. Unlike straightforward factual claims that can be verified through simple research, alcohol metabolism involves individual genetic variation, complex pharmacokinetics, and subjective symptom experiences that resist easy verification.

The absence of dedicated fact-checking may also reflect the general acceptance of moderate alcohol consumption in mainstream culture. Fact-checkers typically prioritize claims with clear public health implications or obvious falsehoods over nuanced questions about optimal drinking strategies among people who are already choosing to consume alcohol.

Medical and scientific organizations have produced research relevant to these claims without directly addressing the folk wisdom. Studies of congener content, drinking patterns, and hangover severity provide empirical data that contradicts mixing myths without explicitly targeting those beliefs for debunking.
