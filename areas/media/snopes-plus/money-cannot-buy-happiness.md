## 1. The Claim

*"Money can't buy happiness."*

This is one of the most durable pieces of folk wisdom in the Western world—present in Stoic philosophy, Christian teaching, the Romantic rejection of materialism, and the self-help canon. It implies a sharp distinction between material wealth and genuine flourishing, and carries a moral undertone: the pursuit of money is a category error, a substitution of means for ends, a chasing of the wrong thing.

In its empirical form, the claim is often supported by pointing to research—most famously a 2010 paper by Nobel laureate Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton—showing that emotional wellbeing stops improving beyond a certain income level (~$75,000/year in 2010 U.S. dollars). The finding was widely interpreted as scientific confirmation of the folk wisdom: once you have enough, more money doesn't help.

But subsequent research, including a high-powered 2021 study by Matthew Killingsworth using a much larger dataset and real-time measurement, found no such plateau—wellbeing continued rising with income across the full range studied. Then Killingsworth and Kahneman collaborated on a joint reconciliation paper in 2023, finding that *both* were partly right, in ways that depend on which people and which happiness measure you look at.

The science here is not settled. The relationship between money and happiness is one of the most actively contested empirical questions in the psychology of wellbeing.

## 2. What's Actually True

**Money is positively associated with wellbeing across a wide income range, but the relationship is non-linear, depends heavily on how happiness is measured and what aspects of life it covers, and is moderated by how money is spent and the baseline needs of the population studied.**

### The Kahneman & Deaton 2010 finding

Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton published "High Income Improves Evaluation of Life but Not Emotional Well-Being" in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences* in 2010. They analyzed Gallup data from approximately 450,000 Americans, distinguishing two aspects of happiness:

- **Life evaluation**: How satisfied are you with your life overall? (Survey responses rating life on a scale from 0–10.)
- **Emotional wellbeing** (also called experienced wellbeing or hedonic wellbeing): How often do you experience positive emotions (joy, smiling, feeling well-rested) versus negative emotions (stress, worry, sadness) in daily life?

Their finding: **life evaluation** continued rising with income across the full range—richer people consistently rated their lives as better overall. But **emotional wellbeing** showed a satiation effect: it improved strongly with income at lower levels and then *flattened out* around $75,000/year (household income in 2010 dollars). Above that threshold, more money was not associated with more positive daily emotional experience or less negative emotional experience.

This distinction is crucial and often lost in popular accounts. The study did not say "money doesn't matter"—it said that *day-to-day emotional experience* may saturate at moderate income levels while *overall life satisfaction* continues to track income.

### The Killingsworth 2021 challenge

Matthew Killingsworth, then at the University of Pennsylvania, published "Experienced Well-Being Rises with Income, Even Above $75,000 Per Year" in *PNAS* in 2021. His methodology differed from Kahneman & Deaton in a key respect: he used an experience sampling app ("Track Your Happiness") that prompted over 33,000 employed U.S. adults at random intervals during the day to report their current feelings. This produced over 1.7 million real-time data points—a much larger dataset with a more granular, ecologically valid measure of experienced wellbeing.

Killingsworth found **no evidence of a plateau**. Experienced wellbeing continued rising log-linearly with income across the full range studied, including above $75,000 and extending to over $500,000. Higher earners were not just more satisfied with their lives in the abstract—they were reporting better moment-to-moment emotional experiences.

This was a direct challenge to the Kahneman-Deaton plateau finding.

### The 2023 reconciliation: Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers

In 2023, Killingsworth and Kahneman collaborated with psychologist Barbara Mellers on an adversarial collaboration designed to resolve the apparent contradiction. Their joint analysis found something subtle: **both findings were correct for different populations**.

For most people, wellbeing does continue rising with income without a plateau—Killingsworth's result holds for the majority. But for a specific subgroup—approximately the 20% of people who are the *least happy* at low incomes—there is indeed a plateau effect: additional income beyond a certain point does not improve their wellbeing further. These are people whose unhappiness appears rooted in factors that money cannot address: grief, mental illness, poor relationships, deep dissatisfaction.

The picture that emerges: **money buys wellbeing for most people, at all income levels studied. But for those whose unhappiness has non-financial roots, the relationship breaks down at moderate income levels.**

### The spending dimension: Matz et al. 2016

Beyond how much money you have, research by Sandra Matz and colleagues adds another wrinkle: **how you spend money matters enormously**. Their 2016 study in *Psychological Science* found that spending aligned with one's personality profile generated more happiness than spending misaligned with it. Extraverts who spent money on social activities and experiences reported more happiness than extraverts who spent on solitary goods; introverts showed the reverse.

More broadly, a well-replicated literature (Dunn, Gilbert, & Wilson, 2011) shows that spending on experiences tends to generate more lasting happiness than spending on material goods; spending on others tends to generate more happiness than spending on oneself; and spending that reduces "time poverty" (commuting, chores) improves wellbeing substantially. Money, in this frame, is a resource whose happiness-generating potential depends entirely on how skillfully it is deployed.

### The comparative and absolute income puzzle

An important complication: people's happiness appears to depend not just on their *absolute* income but on their income *relative to those around them*. Easterlin's famous paradox (1974) observed that within any country at a given time, richer people are happier than poorer people—but as average national income rises over time, average national happiness does not reliably increase. This was interpreted as evidence that relative standing matters more than absolute wealth beyond a basic threshold.

The Easterlin paradox has been re-examined many times, with mixed results. Some researchers find that absolute income matters, full stop; others find strong relative effects. The weight of evidence suggests *both* matter: absolute income matters most at low levels (when basic needs are at stake) and relative income matters more at higher levels (when social comparison and status are the drivers).

## 3. Why People Believe This

**The "money can't buy happiness" claim persists because it is partly true, morally appealing, and fits with real but selective experience.**

**Basic needs satiation.** Below a certain income, money buys relief from concrete suffering: hunger, insecurity, chronic illness, housing instability. Once those needs are met, additional wealth does become less directly linked to felt wellbeing—especially if spent poorly. People who have crossed this threshold into security often *feel* that additional money matters less, and this experience is real.

**Hedonic adaptation.** People consistently overestimate how much positive events (promotions, new purchases, salary increases) will improve their long-term wellbeing and how long that improvement will last. After the initial boost, life returns to its baseline level. This is the "hedonic treadmill"—gains produce adaptation, not permanent elevation. This creates a subjective experience in which getting more money seems to help less than expected.

**Visibility of wealthy but unhappy people.** Public figures who are fabulously wealthy but visibly miserable—addiction, broken relationships, public breakdown—provide salient counterexamples that confirm the folk wisdom. The availability of these examples creates disproportionate impressions. Less salient: the large literature showing that wealth *is* consistently associated with better outcomes on nearly every measure of life quality.

**Moral and religious framing.** Western cultural traditions—Stoic, Christian, Romantic—carry persistent anti-materialist themes. The idea that prioritizing wealth is a moral failing, that "the love of money is the root of all evil" (1 Timothy 6:10), shapes how the empirical question is framed and what answers are culturally accepted.

**Confirmation bias in self-help.** The "money doesn't buy happiness" claim is enormously useful to the self-help and wellness industry, which sells inner transformation, mindfulness, and meaning as alternatives to material striving. A finding that more money actually does improve wellbeing at all income levels competes with product positioning.

## 4. Verdict

**Contested: the relationship between money and happiness is real, positive, and non-trivial—but its shape, magnitude, and moderators are genuinely debated in active research.**

The simplistic claim that "money can't buy happiness" is false as a general principle. The best available evidence suggests that income is positively associated with both life satisfaction and experienced wellbeing across a wide range, with no universal plateau. For most people, more money is associated with better lives.

But the claim contains a real insight: the relationship is not linear, does not substitute for psychological foundations, and depends heavily on how money is spent, relative standing, and baseline need. For a significant minority of people whose unhappiness has non-financial causes, additional wealth above moderate levels does little.

The useful truth: **money matters, and how it is spent matters even more. The question is not whether money buys happiness but what it buys happiness at, and what it cannot purchase at any price.**

## 5. The Wider Picture

The money-happiness research sits within a larger program in wellbeing science that asks what actually determines human flourishing. Income is one of many predictors, but consistent large-scale surveys—the Gallup World Poll, the World Happiness Report, the UK's Office for National Statistics wellbeing data—find that health, social relationships, freedom, and sense of meaning explain at least as much variance in happiness as income does. The richest countries are not always the happiest; Nordic countries regularly top happiness rankings, and income is only part of why.

Within-country inequality matters independently of absolute income: more unequal societies show lower average wellbeing even controlling for mean income, a relationship mediated partly through social trust, status anxiety, and the erosion of public goods. The Wilkinson and Pickett argument (*The Spirit Level*, 2009) that inequality is independently harmful has generated significant debate but has survived methodological scrutiny at a population level.

The practical implications for individuals are reasonably clear from the spending literature: spending on experiences over objects, on others over self, and on time-saving services generates robust wellbeing gains. The "buy your way to happiness" frame is not wrong—it is just that most people purchase in ways that systematically underdeliver on wellbeing, and the research provides correctable guidance.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Fact-checkers have addressed specific adjacent claims—"scientists proved money can't buy happiness"—with appropriate verdicts of misleading or lacking context. The Kahneman-Deaton finding was widely misreported as stronger and simpler than it was; the subsequent Killingsworth challenge and joint reconciliation received far less popular coverage.

Snopes has not directly addressed the general claim. Science journalism has generally covered the Killingsworth finding accurately, but that coverage has not displaced the original folk wisdom in popular culture.

The claim "money can't buy happiness" is best characterized as **misleading as stated**: it overstates the evidence for satiation effects, ignores the consistent positive association across most of the income range, and elides the crucial question of how money is spent. The more accurate claim is: "money is positively associated with wellbeing, but the relationship is non-linear, moderated by individual circumstances and spending behavior, and insufficient on its own to produce flourishing." That version is less poetic and less culturally resonant—which is precisely why the simpler, more satisfying folk version persists.
