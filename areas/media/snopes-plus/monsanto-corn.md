# Monsanto Corn and Organ Failure

## §1 — Claim & Verdict

The claim evaluated by Snopes reads: studies have demonstrated that Monsanto-developed corn — specifically a genetically modified sweet corn variety then recently approved for direct consumer sale — contains Bt (Bacillus thuringiensis) toxins that cause organ failure in rats, and that these toxins have additionally been detected in the bodies of pregnant women. The viral framing added that Monsanto claimed the toxin would "break down before the corn makes it to your dinner table," with the implication that this assurance was false and that the corn posed serious human health risks.

Snopes rates this claim **FALSE**. The verdict is substantively supported by the retraction of the key study at issue from a peer-reviewed journal, by multiple independent regulatory reviews, and by a joint condemnation from six French scientific academies. The underlying empirical studies that powered the claim — primarily the Séralini et al. 2012 publication in *Food and Chemical Toxicology* — were found to be irremediably flawed in their experimental design, specifically in the small sample sizes used and the choice of a rat strain prone to spontaneous tumor development. When the journal demanded access to raw data and conducted a thorough review, it retracted the paper on the grounds that "no definitive conclusions can be reached with this small sample size" and that therefore "the results presented are inconclusive, and do not reach the threshold of publication."

---

## §2 — Origins & Circulation

The claim circulated via Facebook in May 2012, attached to a graphic presenting what it framed as industry-suppressed health information. The graphic's core assertions were: (1) Monsanto had released a GM sweet corn variety containing Bt toxin designed to kill insects by rupturing their stomachs; (2) the company claimed the toxin would break down before reaching consumers; (3) rats fed the GM corn showed organ failure; (4) Bt toxin had been detected in pregnant women.

The timing of the claim's emergence corresponds closely to Monsanto's 2012 commercial launch of a direct-to-consumer GM sweet corn product — the first Monsanto GMO designed for fresh produce retail rather than commodity agriculture. This created heightened public attention and an activated consumer movement, including campaigns led by organizations such as the Organic Consumers Association to pressure retailers to refuse to stock the product. Whole Foods, Trader Joe's, and General Mills publicly pledged non-use; Walmart confirmed it would continue to stock the product.

The organic and anti-GMO activist communities had been amplifying the earlier 2009 Séralini publication (de Vendômois et al., 2009, *International Journal of Biological Sciences*, 5(7), pp. 706–726), which analyzed data from Monsanto's own feeding studies and claimed to find statistically significant differences in organ function in rats fed three strains of GM corn. This paper had already been evaluated and criticized by multiple European food safety agencies before the 2012 viral circulation of the claim.

The subsequent September 2012 publication of Séralini et al. in *Food and Chemical Toxicology* with photographic documentation of tumor-bearing rats intensified the controversy dramatically. The images — rats visibly burdened by large mammary tumors — were widely shared and became among the most-recognized images in the anti-GMO advocacy ecosystem during 2012–2014.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis: The Lifecycle of a Retracted Claim in Public Discourse

This case represents one of the more carefully documented examples of what might be called the **retracted-claim persistence problem**: the well-documented phenomenon in which high-profile scientific papers, following formal retraction, continue to circulate and influence public belief at rates that are not substantially reduced by the retraction itself.

The epistemic dynamics here are distinctive. The Séralini paper was published in September 2012, generated global media coverage, was retracted in November 2013, and nonetheless continued to be cited in anti-GMO advocacy materials for years afterward. Famously, the paper was also re-published in a different open-access journal (*Environmental Sciences Europe*, 2014) following its retraction — a maneuver that allowed its authors to argue that the original retraction was politically motivated and that the science remained valid.

This case is epistemically novel because it involves **competing institutional authorities in explicit conflict**. On one side: a peer-reviewed study in a prestigious journal, with authors who are themselves credentialed scientists. On the other side: a retraction notice from the journal itself, plus statements from France's six national academies of science (French Academy of Sciences, National Academy of Medicine, National Academy of Pharmacy, National Academy of Agriculture, National Academy of Technology, and National Academy of Veterinary Studies), plus independent assessments from EFSA (European Food Safety Authority), ANSES (French Agency for Food, Environmental and Occupational Health & Safety), and the German BfR. The institutional consensus against the paper's conclusions is remarkably dense and crosses national and disciplinary lines.

Standard models of scientific epistemics — in which peer review and institutional consensus provide reliable proxies for empirical truth — predict that this density of institutional opposition should substantially reduce the claim's credibility in lay audiences. The evidence from the post-retraction circulation of the Séralini work suggests this prediction is wrong under conditions of activated ideological priors. Motivated reasoning research (Kahan et al., 2012, *Nature Climate Change*, 2(10), pp. 732–735) demonstrates that increased scientific literacy does not reduce ideological polarization on science-policy questions and may actually intensify it. The Monsanto corn case is a canonical applied example: scientifically literate GMO opponents did not update toward the consensus when the retraction was issued; they updated toward the hypothesis that the retraction itself was evidence of corporate capture of the scientific publishing system.

## §4 — Scientific & Technical Evidence

**Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) proteins** are among the best-characterized insecticidal compounds in agricultural science, with a history of direct environmental application in organic farming predating recombinant DNA technology. Bt was first identified as an insecticidal agent by Ernst Berliner in 1915 when investigating a diseased flour moth population. The mechanism of insecticidal activity is highly specific: Bt crystal proteins (Cry proteins) bind to receptor proteins present in the midgut epithelium of susceptible insect larvae, creating pores that disrupt osmotic balance and ultimately cause larval death (Schnepf et al., 1998, *Microbiology and Molecular Biology Reviews*, 62(3), pp. 775–806). The receptor proteins to which Bt Cry proteins bind are absent in mammals, birds, fish, and most non-target invertebrates, which explains the consistent finding across multiple regulatory jurisdictions that Bt proteins are safe for human consumption.

The **digestibility** of Bt proteins in the mammalian gastrointestinal tract is extensive. Simulated gastric fluid digestion studies have demonstrated rapid proteolytic cleavage of Cry proteins within 30 seconds of exposure, far faster than most dietary proteins (Noteborn et al., 1995, *Journal of Nutritional Biochemistry*, cited in FDA safety review). The FDA requires that applicants for GM crop approval provide sequence-based comparison against known allergen and toxin databases, as well as digestibility data — a regulatory standard that the Bt corn varieties in question passed.

**The 2009 de Vendômois paper** (de Vendômois, J.S., Roullier, F., Cellier, D. & Séralini, G.E., 2009, "A comparison of the effects of three GM corn varieties on mammalian health." *International Journal of Biological Sciences*, 5(7), pp. 706–726) performed re-analysis of Monsanto's own 90-day feeding study data. Multiple food safety agencies reviewed the statistical methodology and found it produced false positives. The European Food Safety Authority's GMO Panel issued a formal opinion finding that de Vendômois and colleagues had applied statistically inappropriate comparisons, testing so many endpoints simultaneously (in the absence of pre-specified primary hypotheses) that spurious significant differences were an expected outcome of the analysis (EFSA, 2007, *EFSA Journal*, 587, pp. 1–11; updated in 2009 following the de Vendômois paper).

**The 2012 Séralini paper** (*Food and Chemical Toxicology*, 50(11), pp. 4221–4231) is the more significant study for this claim's specific factual assertions about organ failure and tumor development. The retraction notice issued by the journal's Editor-in-Chief explicitly cited two flaws: the small sample size (10 rats per group) and the choice of the Sprague-Dawley strain. Sprague-Dawley rats have a naturally high tumor incidence under normal laboratory conditions, with some studies documenting more than 80% of males and more than 70% of females developing spontaneous tumors over a standard two-year lifespan (Sprague-Dawley historical tumor incidence data, Charles River Laboratories monitoring data; also Haseman et al., 1985, *Journal of Toxicology and Environmental Health*, 15(2), pp. 195–215). With only 10 animals per group, the statistical power to distinguish experimentally induced tumors from spontaneous background tumors is insufficient under OECD guidelines, which recommend a minimum 20 per group for toxicity studies and 50–65 per group for carcinogenicity studies.

David Spiegelhalter, Professor of the Public Understanding of Risk at the University of Cambridge, stated that from "even a superficial reading" the paper was "not fit for publication" and that "the peer review process did not work properly." The journal retracted the paper (November 2013) after conducting an independent review of the raw data, stating: "Unequivocally, the Editor-in-Chief found no evidence of fraud or intentional misrepresentation of the data. However, there is a legitimate cause for concern regarding both the number of animals in each study group and the particular strain selected."

**The Aris & Leblanc (2011) paper** on Bt toxin detection in pregnant women (*Reproductive Toxicology*, 31(4), pp. 528–533) claimed to have detected Cry1Ab protein in maternal blood, fetal cord blood, and non-pregnant women. The Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) published a detailed methodological critique: the primary analytical limitation was that the ELISA assay used to detect Cry1Ab cross-reacts with structurally similar proteins and was not validated for human blood matrices. FSANZ concluded: "A number of methodological and interpretive limitations of this paper limit the relevance of the reported findings and conclusions about food safety." The authors' identification of the detected protein as being derived specifically from GM food rather than from other sources of Cry1Ab (including Bt-sprayed organic crops) was identified as an unsubstantiated assumption that the study design could not adjudicate.

## §5 — The Gap: Post-Retraction Claim Persistence and Correction Efficacy

The scientific and regulatory record on this claim is unusually rich. The primary gap is therefore not in primary research (of which there is substantial consensus) but in the downstream question of **correction efficacy** — how effectively the retraction and the institutional consensus have actually reduced public belief in the claim's core assertions.

Lewandowsky et al. (2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 13(3), pp. 106–131) reviewed the experimental literature on misinformation correction and found consistent evidence of a "backfire effect" under some conditions — where corrections targeting a false belief that is congruent with strongly held prior worldviews can actually increase the belief's entrenchment rather than reduce it. Subsequent empirical work by Wood & Porter (2019, *Political Behavior*, 41(1), pp. 135–163) found more limited evidence for the backfire effect in political contexts, suggesting it is condition-dependent. In the specific domain of GMO risk beliefs — where ideological priors about corporate power, regulatory capture, and environmental health are salient and strongly held — the likelihood of backfire-style correction resistance is elevated.

A rigorous naturalistic study of public belief in the "Monsanto corn organ failure" claim, measured before and after the 2013 retraction, at multiple time intervals, with cohort stratification by prior GMO attitude, has not been conducted. Such a study would provide among the clearest available evidence on whether formal scientific retractions produce meaningful belief correction in ideologically activated lay audiences — a question with substantial policy implications for science communication strategy.

The additional gap concerns **industry credibility dynamics**. The fact that the Bt corn in question was a Monsanto product — at a time when Monsanto was among the most reputationally negative corporate names in the public imagination, associated with seed-patent litigation against farmers, lobbying influence, and broader concerns about corporate control of the food supply — almost certainly increased the persuasive leverage of the false safety claims. The credibility of counter-evidence (regulatory agency reviews, journal retractions) was discounted because those institutions could be framed as subject to the same corporate influence. Research on motivated skepticism toward scientific institutions (Lewandowsky et al., 2013, *PLOS ONE*, 8(10), e75637) shows that conspiratorial ideation specifically targets institutional credibility as a mechanism for maintaining commitment to disconfirmed beliefs.

---

## §6 — Verdict Synthesis

The claim that Monsanto-developed corn causes organ failure in rats and carries Bt toxin detectable in harmful concentrations in pregnant women is **false**. The primary empirical supports for the claim — the Séralini 2012 study and the Aris & Leblanc 2011 study — were each found methodologically inadequate by multiple independent scientific institutions and were either retracted (Séralini) or substantively undermined by regulatory review (Aris & Leblanc). The Bt protein has a well-characterized mechanism of action with mammalian specificity absent at the receptor level, comprehensive digestibility in the GI tract, and a multi-decade regulatory and agricultural safety record.

The EARC classification is **A (Ambiguous)** not because the factual verdict is unclear — it is clear — but because the ongoing post-retraction circulation of the claim in anti-GMO discourse means the epistemic status of the claim in the public sphere remains contested in ways that do not map cleanly onto the scientific consensus. The gap classification is **study-retraction**: the primary unresolved empirical question is not about the biology but about the sociology of belief revision following scientific retractions under conditions of ideological activation.
