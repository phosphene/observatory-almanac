## §1 · Claim & Verdict

In November 2016, a viral report circulated on social media claiming that a new laboratory analysis had found alarming levels of the herbicide glyphosate in common American foods, and that Monsanto — maker of the Roundup herbicide formulation containing glyphosate — had conspired with both the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) to suppress knowledge of the chemical's carcinogenicity and its residue levels in the food supply. The specific claim, amplified by the influential blog operated by food activist Vani Hari (widely known as "the Food Babe"), stated that the FDA suspended its glyphosate residue testing program as a direct result of Monsanto's pressure after initial test results revealed disturbing contamination levels — including in baby food.

Snopes rated this composite claim **False**. The original Snopes write-up, authored by investigative journalist and science writer Alex Kasprak and published on 16 November 2016, began as a "Mixture" rating but was subsequently reclassified to "False" following consultations with scientists in the field. The reclassification was explained in an editor's note appended to the original piece: the editors concluded that the original "Mixture" framing risked legitimizing the activist laboratory testing as a scientifically rigorous contribution to an ongoing debate, when in fact the analysis failed basic standards of methodological transparency and peer review. No evidence existed — and none has emerged since — connecting the FDA's suspension of its glyphosate testing program to any Monsanto intervention.

## §2 · Context & Background

Glyphosate is a broad-spectrum systemic herbicide first registered for agricultural use in the United States in 1974 (U.S. EPA, 1993). It operates by inhibiting the enzyme 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (EPSPS), a pathway present in plants and microorganisms but absent in animals — which is one biochemical basis for early safety assessments suggesting low direct mammalian toxicity at moderate exposures (Séralini et al., 2012, *Food and Chemical Toxicology*; though see contested literature below). Glyphosate became commercially and culturally transformative after Monsanto introduced "Roundup Ready" genetically modified crop lines in the 1990s, which were engineered to tolerate glyphosate application. By the early 2000s, it had become the most widely used herbicide on the planet, with annual applications measured in hundreds of millions of pounds in the United States alone (Benbrook, 2016, *Environmental Sciences Europe*).

The regulatory history of glyphosate's carcinogenicity classification is genuinely complex and contested — a fact that a fair treatment of this controversy must acknowledge. The EPA first assessed glyphosate's carcinogenic potential in 1985 and classified it as a Group C carcinogen ("possibly carcinogenic to humans") based on studies that showed some evidence of renal tubule tumors in male mice and hemangiosarcoma in male mice (EPA Classification Review, 1986). After additional investigations, including industry-sponsored rodent studies, the EPA reclassified glyphosate in 1993 as a Group E carcinogen ("evidence of non-carcinogenicity in humans"), the highest evidence-of-safety classification the agency used at the time (EPA Reregistration Eligibility Decision, 1993). This reclassification was scientifically consequential: glyphosate's safety profile for the following two decades rested substantially on it.

The landscape shifted materially in March 2015, when the WHO's International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) published a Monograph classifying glyphosate as Group 2A — "probably carcinogenic to humans" — based largely on evidence from human epidemiological studies showing elevated risk of Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL), positive results in rodent carcinogenicity bioassays, and genotoxicity studies (IARC, 2015, *The Lancet Oncology*). The IARC classification drew vigorous rebuttal from regulatory agencies that took a different view of the same evidence base, including a joint 2016 assessment by the FAO/WHO Joint Meeting on Pesticide Residues (JMPR), which concluded that "glyphosate is unlikely to pose a carcinogenic risk to humans from exposure through the diet" (JMPR Summary Report, 2016). The apparent regulatory contradiction stems partly from a methodological divergence: IARC assessed hazard (whether a substance can cause cancer under some conditions of exposure), whereas JMPR and national regulatory bodies typically assess risk (whether a substance is likely to cause cancer at the levels to which people are actually exposed). This is not a trivial distinction, and conflating hazard classification with risk assessment became a persistent source of public confusion that activist messaging — such as the Food Babe post at issue — systematically exploited.

The specific trigger for the 2016 viral episode was a report commissioned by Food Democracy Now! and The Detox Project, two organizations with stated advocacy missions around food system reform and chemical elimination respectively. The report alleged that a "FDA-registered food safety laboratory" had found glyphosate residues at concentrations far exceeding the threshold of 0.1 parts per billion (ppb), which the report described as the level at which "probable harm to human health begins." The analytical results attached to the report, however, were marked with qualifications noting that many results "may not represent an accurate representation of the sample," and the methodology used to achieve them was not disclosed in a manner permitting independent verification. The comparison threshold of 0.1 ppb was itself drawn from a single mouse study (Mesnage et al., 2015, *Environmental Health*) that observed "potential" effects — not "probable" harm — and the study concerned a mouse model, not direct human data.

## §3 · Epistemic Analysis & Novelty

The Monsanto–glyphosate–suppression narrative constitutes an instructive case study in the architecture of scientific misinformation, and specifically in what epistemologists of science call **motivated evidence aggregation** — the selective mobilization of legitimately uncertain science to support a pre-formed causal conclusion that the available evidence does not, in fact, warrant.

The epistemic novelty in this case turns on a structural asymmetry rarely made explicit in popular fact-checking: the genuine scientific controversy surrounding glyphosate's carcinogenicity (real, ongoing, and methodologically complex) was leveraged rhetorically to import into that controversy a second, entirely separate claim — that Monsanto engaged in regulatory capture to suppress evidence — for which no direct evidence existed or has since emerged. This two-step rhetorical operation is epistemically dangerous precisely because it is partially anchored in legitimate scientific uncertainty. The IARC 2A classification was real; European regulatory divergence from U.S. EPA positions was real; industry-sponsored studies in the regulatory record were real. These authentic uncertainties created narrative traction for a conspiracy scaffolding that, analytically, should have required independent evidentiary support.

Williams et al. (2016, *Critical Reviews in Toxicology*) conducted a systematic review comparing four expert panel assessments of glyphosate's carcinogenic potential with the IARC 2015 Monograph and identified a central methodological dispute: IARC's positive determination rested partly on studies that deployed supra-environmental exposure levels, a problem endemic to animal carcinogenicity testing. The Meyers et al. (2016, *Environmental Health*) consensus statement representing a different expert community argued the opposite — that low-dose, environmentally realistic exposures might carry risks underweighted in regulatory models built on threshold assumptions. Neither position supports the suppression narrative as such, but both demonstrate that the underlying science contains genuine disagreements that can be and were exploited by advocacy communicators.

A further epistemic dimension involves the sociology of food activism and "concern laundering" — a term this analysis introduces to describe the practice of borrowing the credibility of legitimate scientific controversy to launder advocacy-generated claims through the appearance of evidence. The Food Babe's post did not merely cite the commissioned laboratory report as possible evidence warranting investigation; it presented it as definitive proof of a cover-up. This rhetorical transformation — from "contested evidence" to "proof of conspiracy" — is characteristic of the genre and represents a failure of epistemic honesty independent of one's position on the underlying herbicide safety questions.

Importantly, the cessation of the FDA's glyphosate testing program in 2016 — cited as the smoking-gun evidence of suppression — had a documented technical explanation: methodological disputes among FDA laboratories about standardization protocols and instrumentation sensitivity (GAO-15-38, 2014). The Government Accountability Office report that originally compelled the FDA to initiate glyphosate testing predated any commercially sensitive test results; the FDA's internal methodological difficulties were documented independently of any Monsanto communication. Inferring suppression from program suspension required ignoring this documented bureaucratic explanation in favor of an undocumented conspiratorial one.

## §4 · Scientific Evidence

The scientific literature relevant to this claim subdivides into two threads that should be carefully distinguished: (a) the actual state of evidence on glyphosate's health effects, and (b) the specific claim of regulatory suppression. These are logically independent questions, and conflating them confounds evaluation.

**On glyphosate carcinogenicity:** The IARC Working Group's determination (IARC Monograph Vol. 112, 2015) was based on sufficient evidence of carcinogenicity in experimental animals and limited evidence in humans, with the human evidence most consistently associating glyphosate exposure with Non-Hodgkin lymphoma. The IARC process explicitly aggregates hazard across exposure scenarios rather than performing site-specific risk assessment. Subsequent epidemiological analyses have been mixed: Andreotti et al. (2018, *Journal of the National Cancer Institute*) analyzed the Agricultural Health Study cohort — a large prospective study of U.S. farm families — and found no statistically significant association between glyphosate use and NHL overall, though the data for specific NHL subtypes required cautious interpretation. Zhang et al. (2019, *Mutation Research/Reviews in Mutation Research*) conducted a meta-analysis and concluded there was a 41% increased risk of NHL in highly exposed groups, though this figure has been contested on methodological grounds by several re-analyses. The scientific debate remains active and unresolved at the population level, and the IARC/EPA divergence reflects genuine methodological pluralism rather than simple error by either agency.

**On pesticide residues in food:** The FDA's maximum residue limits (MRLs) for glyphosate in food vary by commodity. For grains, the tolerance is typically set at 30 parts per million (ppm), which is 30,000 ppb — four orders of magnitude above the 0.1 ppb threshold cited by the Food Democracy Now! report as the onset of "probable harm." The JMPR (2016) concluded that dietary exposure to glyphosate residues, even at maximum levels, fell well below the acceptable daily intake. The commissioned laboratory results, to the extent their methodology could be evaluated, found residues in the low ppb range — orders of magnitude below the FDA tolerance and not inconsistent with what independent analytical laboratories have found in similar food sampling exercises.

**On the regulatory capture claim:** No peer-reviewed publication, investigative journalistic exposé, or government investigation has documented a specific mechanism by which Monsanto communicated pressure to the FDA resulting in the suspension of residue testing. The GAO-15-38 report (Gruenberg, 2014, U.S. Government Accountability Office) explicitly noted equipment and methodology standardization challenges across FDA laboratories as the driver of testing delays. Carey Gillam's reporting in the Huffington Post (November 2016) — a journalist who has written critically about Monsanto — attributed the suspension to "confusion, disagreement and difficulties with establishing a standard methodology," without citing suppression by industry.

## §5 · Verdict Evaluation

Snopes' "False" rating is well-supported by the evidence, with the acknowledgment that the reclassification from "Mixture" illuminates something important: the original "Mixture" designation was itself potentially misleading, because it could imply that the commission-funded laboratory report contributed legitimate scientific information to the carcinogenicity debate. The editors' subsequent correction — explaining that the "Mixture" framing risked "legitimizing" activist-commissioned tests — reflects unusual methodological self-awareness in popular fact-checking and models good epistemic practice.

The core evaluative standard here is evidentiary warrant: the suppression narrative made a specific causal claim (Monsanto pressured the FDA to suspend testing) for which no evidence was presented. The laboratory results, even if we bracket the methodological deficiencies, provide residue concentration data — they do not and cannot demonstrate suppression. The narrative stitched together (a) genuine scientific uncertainty about glyphosate toxicity, (b) undisclosed-methodology laboratory results from advocacy organizations with stated anti-glyphosate missions, (c) the FDA's documented methodological difficulties with testing standardization, and (d) the general documented history of industry lobbying and regulatory interaction — and presented this assemblage as proof of conspiracy. Each element, evaluated independently, fails to support the specific suppression inference.

The "False" verdict for the suppression component is therefore robustly warranted. For the subsidiary claim that high levels of glyphosate were found in food: the commissioned test results were marked by the lab itself as potentially unreliable, employed undisclosed methodology, and were produced by organizations with advocacy stakes in the findings. These characteristics do not meet minimum standards for scientific credibility. The EARC classification for this article is **E** (empirically contested with an advocacy-generated evidentiary core), indicating that while the underlying science involves genuine expert debate, the specific claim as formulated was not supported by credible evidence.

## §6 · Epistemic Gap & Further Research

The epistemic gaps here are significant and worth enumerating for future researchers. First, the regulatory divergence between IARC and national agencies on glyphosate's carcinogenic classification represents a genuine unresolved methodological dispute about how to weigh hazard versus risk, how to treat animal carcinogenicity data from supra-environmental exposures, and how to handle heterogeneity in epidemiological study populations. These questions are not answered by the Snopes fact-check, nor could they be — they require primary scientific investigation. Second, the sociology of how industry-sponsored research enters the regulatory record for pesticides remains an active area of concern for scholars of science policy (Séralini et al., 2014, *Food and Chemical Toxicology*, retraction and controversy; Mesnage & Antoniou, 2017, *Food and Chemical Toxicology*); the mere existence of industry studies in the record does not constitute suppression, but the relative weight given industry versus independent studies in regulatory decisions is a legitimate governance question. Third, the 2018 California trial verdict finding Monsanto liable for a groundskeeper's Non-Hodgkin lymphoma development (noted in the Snopes editors' update) re-energized the scientific and legal debate: legal liability verdicts reflect jury interpretation of evidence weighted under a civil standard, not scientific consensus, and the two should not be equated.

Researchers investigating downstream developments in the glyphosate regulatory landscape should consult: the updated EPA evaluation of glyphosate carcinogenicity (2020, reaffirming "not likely to be carcinogenic to humans" at realistic exposure levels); IARC Monograph 112 (2015) for the 2A classification rationale; the Agricultural Health Study prospective cohort publications (Andreotti et al., 2018); and the ongoing litigation docket arising from Roundup personal injury cases for the legal rather than scientific record. The gap between the legal determination and the scientific consensus is itself an important epistemic and policy problem warranting careful analysis.
