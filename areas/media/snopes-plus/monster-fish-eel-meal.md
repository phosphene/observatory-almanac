## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

During the spring of 2021, a bizarre short video circulated widely across social media platforms showing what was described as two large "monster fish" apparently passing an eel from one's mouth to the other in an act that superficially resembled cooperative food-sharing behavior. The clip was framed with wonder and sometimes with explicit naturalistic language—as if documenting a genuine, previously unobserved fish behavior. The implicit claim embedded in the video's framing was that this represented a real natural event: large fish engaged in active eel-transfer behavior.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2021) assigned this claim the rating **Miscaptioned**. The video is real footage but its framing is false: it does not document a natural occurrence. The clip originates from the India-based YouTube channel Discovery Site, a content-production operation that creates staged fishing and wildlife videos using what appears to be dead animals as props. The channel operates with a standard disclaimer that its content is for "entertainment purposes only."

This entry examines the verification methodology applied to the clip, the broader ecosystem of staged wildlife content on YouTube, the ethological reasons why the claimed behavior is implausible, and the implications of this content genre for misinformation literacy.

## §2 The Video's Origin and the Discovery Site Network

Evon's (2021) investigation traced the clip to the YouTube channel "Discovery Site," an India-based creator account that at the time of the fact-check had accumulated more than 100 million total views since its launch in August 2020—a striking rate of growth for a channel with less than a year of operation. The original video title, rendered in the channel's style, was: "Underground Monster hunting eelfish. Big fish swallowing mudeel. Big fish vomiting eel to big fish."

The Discovery Site channel is representative of a content genre that can be characterized as *constructed-nature video*: footage of fish, reptiles, and other animals that appears to show unusual behaviors or large specimens but is, on careful inspection, staged using dead or immobilized animals positioned by off-camera operators. The channel's broader portfolio includes videos of supposedly amphibious fish emerging from burrows in coordinated groups, footage of "climbing perch" being lured out of holes with raw egg, and other sequences involving animal behaviors that have no credible scientific basis.

Multiple videos on the channel and similar channels include explicit disclaimers stating the content is for entertainment only. The presence of these disclaimers did not prevent the eel-transfer clip from circulating without attribution or disclaimer in the viral iteration reviewed by Snopes.

Evon (2021) also identified a parallel channel, "Fisherman Animal Lover," with more than 1 million subscribers at the time of review, as producing similar content using the same genre conventions. He noted a precursor viral moment in 2019 in which a Coca-Cola and Mentos "fish extraction" video from a now-deleted channel called Technique Tools attracted international attention before being exposed as a hoax by Science Alert—demonstrating that the genre predates and outlasts individual channels, and that the format has proven resilient to debunking because new audiences encounter it without memory of previous debunkings.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Audience Cost of Ambiguity in Staged-Nature Content

The central epistemic issue in this case is neither authenticity nor fabrication in the strict sense—the footage is real footage of real fish—but rather what philosophers of testimony might call *misleading implicature*: the framing strongly implies a behavioral claim that the footage does not support, without technically making the claim explicit. This places it in a different category from a simple hoax or a digitally manipulated video.

The rating "Miscaptioned" is correct but slightly misleading as a verdict category. "Miscaptioned" typically implies that genuine footage has been mislabeled as to its source or time. Here, the footage has been *misframed* as to its nature—it is presented as documentary evidence of a behavior when it is actually a staged performance. This is a more sophisticated category of misinformation because it does not require any specific false claim to be made. The video performs legitimacy through the visual grammar of nature documentary while simultaneously insulating itself with an entertainment disclaimer available to any determined viewer.

The behavioral claim embedded in the framing is also falsifiable from basic ethology. There is no known species of freshwater or marine fish that exhibits peer-to-peer food transfer, regurgitation-sharing, or cooperative food exchange as described and visually implied by the video's framing. Food-sharing in vertebrates is documented primarily in social mammals (Wilkinson, 1984, *Nature*, on vampire bat allofeeding) and certain bird species with biparental care dynamics, but it requires costly social cognition and reciprocal altruism mechanisms that teleosts (bony fish) are not known to possess at a level that would generate the observed behavior. The fish nervous system architecture, while more sophisticated than traditionally assumed (Brown, 2015, *Animal Cognition*), does not support the kind of intentional, coordinated, other-directed food transfer implied by the "monster fish" framing.

The key epistemic novelty here is the identification of a content-manipulation technique that does not depend on digital manipulation, false identity, or fabricated events. Instead, it exploits the audience's natural tendency to interpret novel footage through a "documentary validity" heuristic: if footage looks like it could be genuine natural history, and if it lacks obvious visual markers of manipulation, it is treated as genuine. This heuristic is usually adaptive (most nature footage is real), but it creates systematic vulnerability to the Discovery Site genre, which mimics documentary aesthetics while staging its content.

The 100-million-view figure for a channel less than a year old, focused entirely on this genre, is itself an epistemic data point: it suggests a substantial global audience for whom the content's implausibility does not trigger skepticism, either because of limited exposure to ethological base knowledge or because the entertainment value of the spectacle overrides accuracy concerns at the point of social sharing.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Evon's (2021) fact-check correctly identified the source channel, summarized its content genre, and cross-referenced it with the Technique Tools precedent. Several additional analytical threads are worth developing:

**The dead-fish question**: Evon (2021) stated that the staged videos "seemingly use dead fish as their main props." The factual basis for this characterization is the visual evidence within the videos themselves—fish that exhibit no autonomous locomotion, eye films consistent with post-mortem change, and positioning consistent with manual placement. However, a formal necropsy or veterinary analysis of any individual specimen is not cited. The "dead fish" inference is reasonable but not formally documented.

**The platform monetization dimension**: A channel accumulating 100 million views in under a year on YouTube is, depending on CPM rates for the relevant audience geography, generating substantial advertising revenue. YouTube's content policies technically prohibit materially deceptive content, but the entertainment disclaimer cited by Evon provides a plausible defense. Whether YouTube's review processes ever engaged with the Discovery Site channel's content and how the platform's recommendation algorithms contributed to its virality are questions not addressed in the fact-check.

**Cross-channel coordination**: The existence of "Fisherman Animal Lover" and similar channels suggests either parallel independent development of the same genre or deliberate genre-copying. Whether these channels are operated by connected individuals or constitute an organized content farm is not established in available reporting.

**Cultural context**: The Discovery Site and similar channels are primarily targeting South and Southeast Asian audiences, where freshwater fishing in rice paddies, ponds, and rivers is culturally prominent and where videos of unusual fish are a recognized entertainment category. The epistemic norms operating for that audience—whether the content is understood as entertainment-genre fishing exaggeration rather than genuine documentary naturalism—may differ substantially from the norms of the global audience that encountered the clips through decontextualized reposting.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Staged Wildlife Content as a Misinformation Category

The "monster fish" video is representative of a category that fact-checking frameworks have been slower to address than the more familiar categories of false news or manipulated images: **authentic footage staged to produce a false behavioral narrative**. This category is distinct from:

- *Digitally manipulated footage* (visual fabrication without physical staging)
- *Misattributed footage* (genuine footage described as occurring at the wrong time or place)
- *Out-of-context footage* (genuine footage of a real event reframed as a different event)

Staged wildlife content is more accurately characterized as *fabricated behavioral documentation*: the filming is real, the animals are real (or were real), and the location is real, but the behavior depicted is artificially produced and presented as natural. This genre has a long history predating social media—the Disney "True Life Adventure" film series of the 1950s was criticized for staging animal behaviors (including the notorious, factually false lemming "mass suicide" footage in *White Wilderness* [1958])—but the low cost of digital production and the virality dynamics of social media have dramatically expanded its scale and accessibility.

From a cognitive science perspective, staged wildlife content exploits what Mercier and Sperber (2017, *The Enigma of Reason*, Harvard University Press) describe as the evolved tendency to treat perceptual evidence (seeing something happen) as more credible than testimonial evidence (being told something happened). Video of an apparent behavior is processed by human cognitive systems as perceptual evidence rather than as a testimonial claim, which is why staged footage is cognitively stickier and more resistant to correction than false verbal claims.

The discovery of the entertainment disclaimer buried in channel descriptions is a key remediation tool, but its location in the channel metadata rather than in-video means that the corrective information reaches only viewers who actively seek the channel's identity—a small fraction of the total audience in a viral distribution scenario.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: MISCAPTIONED (Staged Content)**

The "monster fish" eel-transfer video does not document a genuine natural behavior. It originates from the Discovery Site YouTube channel, which produces staged footage of fish using what appears to be dead animals as props. The channel includes an entertainment-purposes-only disclaimer. There is no known fish species that exhibits the cooperative food-sharing behavior depicted. The video is real footage; the behavioral narrative framing is false.

**EARC Classification: E (Epistemic — staged content genre)**

This case illustrates the epistemic gap category: the specific claim (real natural behavior) is false, but the falseness cannot be resolved by simple fact retrieval. It requires a combination of source provenance research (finding the original channel and its other content), behavioral ethology (establishing why the depicted behavior is implausible), and documentary media analysis (identifying the visual grammar of staged-nature content). The EARC gap is epistemic because the misinformation mechanism—exploiting documentary-validity heuristics with staged footage plus entertainment disclaimers—is a structural feature of the content type rather than a specific factual error.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Formal confirmation (necropsy or veterinary analysis) that animals used in Discovery Site videos are dead rather than live
- Whether YouTube's content moderation reviewed the channel and on what grounds
- Whether Discovery Site and similar channels are connected operations or independent genre mimicry
- Audience reception studies on whether target demographic interprets this content as documentary or entertainment

## §7 Supplementary Note: The Economics and Incentives of Staged Nature Content

Staged wildlife content of the Discovery Site variety is not a pure information-environment aberration but an economically rational response to the incentive structures of platform-based video distribution. YouTube's recommendation algorithm, as documented in audit research by Ribeiro et al. (2020) in *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction* (ACM), systematically rewards content that maximizes watch-time and click-through rate from thumbnails. Videos featuring large animals, unexpected behaviors, and mild-shock content reliably score well on these metrics, which means the algorithmic amplification of staged nature videos is a feature of the platform architecture rather than a failure of it.

For content creators in lower-income regions of South and Southeast Asia, the Discovery Site model—locally filmed, low-production-cost staged animal content—can generate meaningful income from globally distributed advertising revenue that a YouTube channel with 100 million views would access. The per-thousand-impression (CPM) rates for audience demographics in South Asia are lower than for North American or European audiences, but the total view volumes are high enough to generate substantial absolute revenue. This economic logic means that as long as platform incentives remain as structured, Discovery Site–type channels will be produced and will succeed.

The policy implication is that fact-checking individual videos, while valuable, does not address the underlying incentive structure. A durable solution would require platform-level changes: mandatory in-video disclaimers (not just channel-description disclaimers) for content that explicitly claims to document natural behavior, and algorithmic deprioritization of flagged staged-content channels. Neither of these interventions has been consistently implemented as of available review.

For educators, the Discovery Site case is illustrative material for media literacy curricula focused on the specific failure mode of documentary-implicature misinformation: the audience skill most needed is not skepticism about all wildlife footage but selective application of skepticism when (a) the claimed behavior has no known biological basis, and (b) the footage originates from an entertainment-disclaimer channel.
