## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

Beginning in January 2014 and recirculating multiple times across Pinterest, Twitter, Tumblr, and Facebook over subsequent years, a widely shared image and accompanying text described a fruit called the "moon melon" (also stylized as "moonmelon"), allegedly bearing the scientific name *Asidus*, allegedly grown in "some parts of Japan," and allegedly possessing the unusual property that consuming it would cause flavors to switch: sour things would taste sweet, salty things would taste bitter, and water would take on "a strong orange-like taste." The image accompanying the claim depicted what appeared to be a slice of watermelon with vivid blue flesh and rind, photographed in a realistic-looking style consistent with food photography from markets or nutritional contexts. The text cited an approximate retail cost of 16,000 Japanese yen (roughly US$200 at 2014 exchange rates) per fruit.

Snopes founder David Mikkelson (2014, updated 2022) assigned this claim the rating **False**. No such fruit exists. There is no plant species called *Asidus* or "moon melon" in any botanical registry, scientific database, or horticultural catalogue. The image is not a photograph of an unusual fruit indigenous to Japan; it is an ordinary watermelon slice whose flesh has been digitally recolored blue. The extraordinary flavor-switching properties claimed for the fruit are physiologically impossible in the manner described. The claim is a fabricated hoax that was created and circulated for unknown purposes, possibly as a social media engagement experiment or as creative fiction that was mistaken for genuine natural history.

However—and this is the most scientifically interesting aspect of this claim—a real fruit does exist that produces a superficially similar (though mechanistically distinct and far more limited) taste-altering effect: the miracle berry, *Synsepalum dulcificum*, which produces a protein called miraculin capable of making sour foods taste sweet for up to an hour after consumption. The existence of a real fruit with genuine taste-altering properties makes the moon melon fabrication more persuasive to uninformed audiences and more interesting analytically: the fiction is built on a real but poorly-known scientific precedent.

## §2 The Hoax: Identifying the Photo Manipulation and the Absence of Botanical Evidence

The moon melon claim is analysable at two levels: the visual claim (the photo) and the textual claim (the species description and flavor properties).

At the visual level, the image of blue watermelon flesh is a straightforward digital modification of a conventional watermelon photograph. Mikkelson (2014) presents the original unmodified image alongside the manipulated version, making the recoloring immediately obvious on direct comparison: the texture, light reflection, and seed pattern of the "blue" melon are identical to the original red-fleshed watermelon. The manipulation is a simple hue rotation applied to the flesh—a color-channel adjustment that takes approximately thirty seconds in any standard photo editing application. The rind transitions, seed positions, and photographic grain are all consistent with a single original image subjected to color channel manipulation.

The absence of botanical evidence for any plant called *Asidus* or "moon melon" is equally conclusive. No entry for *Asidus* appears in the Catalogue of Life (the standard reference for accepted scientific species names), the International Plant Names Index (IPNI), the Plant List maintained by Kew Gardens and Missouri Botanical Garden, or any national botanical registry for Japan. Japan's National Agriculture and Food Research Organization (NARO), which maintains comprehensive databases of Japanese cultivated and wild fruits, lists no variety of blue-fleshed melon or any fruit with the claimed flavor-switching properties. The scientific binomial *Asidus* follows no established naming convention in plant taxonomy: binomials require both a genus and a species epithet (e.g., *Homo sapiens*, *Cucumis melo*), and a single word name is not valid nomenclature under the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants.

The claimed price—16,000 yen—was probably included in the original fabrication to add verisimilitude by suggesting a luxury or rare item. Japan has a genuine market for premium fruits (Mikkelson, 2014, notes this without elaboration), including branded strawberries, melons, and grapes sold at prices that can reach thousands of yen per unit. Matsunaga (2019, *Food Policy*, Elsevier) documents Japan's "gift fruit" market, where highly selected specimens of common fruit varieties are sold at auction prices exceeding ¥100,000 for exceptional specimens. By citing a specific high price, the moon melon claim anchors itself in a real cultural phenomenon—Japan's luxury fruit market—to increase perceived plausibility.

The flavor-switching claim is perhaps the least plausible element of the fabrication from a biochemical perspective. The description—everything sour tastes sweet, everything salty tastes bitter, water tastes like orange—describes not a selective taste-receptor modifier but a wholesale reconfiguration of gustatory perception affecting multiple distinct receptor types simultaneously. As documented by Chandrashekar et al. (2006, *Nature*, Nature Publishing Group), the five basic taste modalities (sweet, sour, salty, bitter, and umami) are mediated by distinct receptor classes located on separate subsets of taste receptor cells. A single compound capable of simultaneously modifying all four of the claimed modalities (sweet, sour, salty, bitter) would need to interact with multiple structurally unrelated receptor families—an unprecedented and pharmacologically implausible property for a single plant compound.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Miracle Berry Precedent and Plausibility Spillover in Botanical Misinformation

The moon melon claim is epistemically interesting because it parasitizes a genuine scientific phenomenon: the taste-modifying properties of miraculin, the active protein in the real miracle berry (*Synsepalum dulcificum*).

As Mikkelson (2014) documents, the miracle berry is a small red fruit indigenous to West Africa. The plant (*Richardella dulcifica*, formerly classified as *Synsepalum dulcificum*; see Swamy et al., 2014, *African Journal of Biotechnology*, Academic Journals) has been used by local populations for centuries to make sour or fermented foods palatable. Miraculin, the protein responsible for the taste-modifying effect, was isolated and characterized by Kurihara and Beidler (1968, *Science*, AAAS). Their study established that miraculin is a glycoprotein that binds to sweet-taste receptors (T1R2/T1R3 heterodimers) on the tongue and—uniquely—activates them in the presence of acidic (sour) compounds: miraculin itself does not taste sweet, but when the oral environment becomes acidic, the bound miraculin shifts conformation and strongly activates the sweet receptor, making the source of the acidity (lemon juice, vinegar, sour candy) taste intensely sweet.

The mechanism is specific: miraculin acts only on the sweet receptor (T1R2/T1R3), and only in the presence of acidic conditions. It does not modify salty, bitter, or umami taste. The duration of effect—typically 30 minutes to one hour—reflects the time required for miraculin to be removed from receptor binding sites through normal oral processes (Ye et al., 2001, *Chemical Senses*, Oxford University Press).

The epistemic novelty in the moon melon case is the phenomenon of *plausibility spillover*: the genuine existence of a real taste-modifying fruit in West Africa makes the claims about a hypothetical taste-modifying fruit in Japan more believable to people who have heard of miracle berries. Crucially, most people who had heard of the miracle berry had heard only vague second- or third-hand accounts—"there's a berry that makes sour things taste sweet"—without any mechanistic or botanical detail. This vague prior makes the moon melon hoax's claim—"there's a fruit that makes flavors switch"—sound like a continuous variation on a known phenomenon rather than a fundamentally new and implausible assertion.

This plausibility spillover mechanism operates via what Tversky and Kahneman (1983, *Psychological Review*, American Psychological Association) called "base rate neglect" combined with representativeness heuristics. The relevant base rate is: almost no plant species produce pharmacologically potent taste-modifying compounds detectable at tiny doses. This base rate is vanishingly low—miraculin and gymnemic acid (from *Gymnema sylvestre*) are among the only well-characterized examples from decades of plant screening. But because the moon melon claim is framed as being similar to miracle berries (a known real thing), the brain pattern-matches to "one member of a category (taste-modifying fruits)," ignoring the fact that the category itself is nearly empty of members.

The implication for misinformation analysis is that fabricated botanical claims are more difficult to evaluate than purely invented claims because they often invoke real but poorly-known biological phenomena as precedents. When a claimed phenomenon has a real, weakly-known analog, fact-checking must engage with the science of the real phenomenon—not merely the falsity of the specific claim—to provide effective correction, because a correction that dismisses the possibility of taste-modifying fruits entirely appears to conflict with what the audience (correctly) believes about miracle berries.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Mikkelson's (2014, updated 2022) investigation correctly identifies the photo manipulation, establishes the species' non-existence, and introduces the miracle berry as context. However, several significant threads received minimal treatment.

**Origin and purpose of the hoax**: The investigation does not attempt to trace the moon melon fabrication to its creator or original posting context. Memes of this type are sometimes created as deliberate social experiments ("what can I get people to believe?"), as creative fiction that escaped its original context, or as deliberate misinformation for unknown motives. No investigation of the original Pinterest or social media post context appears in the fact-check or in subsequent coverage.

**The aspartame–miraculin interaction**: The *Wired* article cited by Mikkelson (2014) notes that miraculin also interacts with sweet foods in complex ways, including making aspartame taste sweeter in acidic conditions and suppressing sweetness in neutral conditions. These secondary effects of miraculin are not explored in the Snopes investigation; they are important for understanding the full pharmacological profile of the real compound and distinguishing it from the moon melon's claimed wholesale flavor reversal.

**The blue fruit design choice**: No analysis is offered of why the hoaxer chose blue coloring for the fictitious fruit. Blue is essentially absent from common fruits and vegetables in naturalistic settings (naturally occurring anthocyanin pigments produce blue-purple colors in berries and some varieties of corn and potato, but vivid bright blue as visible in the manipulated image is not found in any common fruit). The choice of blue maximizes visual novelty—it triggers the "this is unusual and memorable" response—which is likely deliberate. The study of color choices in viral hoax imagery would be a productive direction for future research.

**Gymnemic acid**: Mikkelson (2014) does not mention gymnemic acid (from *Gymnema sylvestre*), which produces a different kind of taste alteration: it suppresses sweetness, making sugar taste like plain starch and sweet foods taste flat. This is another real taste-modifying compound that would be relevant to explaining why the category of "flavor-altering plants" is real and scientifically interesting, even while the specific moon melon claim is fabricated.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Botanical Misinformation, the "Fantastic Japan" Template, and the Visual Semiotics of Blue Food

The moon melon belongs to a recognizable genre of online image-based fabrication that might be called the "exotic Asian food" hoax template. This template combines four elements: a photograph of or relating to a real (but digitally altered or misrepresented) food item; a claimed Japanese or broadly East Asian provenance; a price point high enough to imply luxury or rarity; and a claimed fantastical property (flavor alteration, health benefit, unusual coloring) that exceeds biological plausibility but aligns with popular cultural stereotypes about Asian cuisine as mysterious, exotic, and nutritionally potent.

The choice of Japan as provenance is not arbitrary. Japan's genuine reputation for premium agricultural products, innovative food culture, and unusual flavor approaches (umami, fermented condiments, unusual preparations) provides cultural scaffolding that makes Japanese-origin food claims more immediately plausible to Western audiences. Cwiertka (2006), writing in *Modern Japanese Cuisine: Food, Power and National Identity* (Reaktion Books), documents the structural role of "exotic Japanese foodways" narratives in Western popular culture: Japanese food is consistently framed as sophisticated, mysterious, and sometimes possessing quasi-medicinal properties that Western cuisine lacks.

This cultural scaffolding allows botanical fabrications attached to "Japan" to exploit pre-existing audience priors that make the claims seem more plausible before detailed scrutiny. The same claim—a color-manipulated watermelon photo attributed to Wyoming farmland with flavor-switching properties—would likely generate much less virality than the same image attributed to Japanese specialty agriculture.

The visual semiotics of blue coloring in food are also significant. Blue is strongly associated with synthetic, artificial, or fantastical food in Western cultural contexts (Garber et al., 2000, *Food Quality and Preference*, Elsevier). Laboratory studies on food color expectations (Spence, 2015, *Flavour*, BioMed Central) document that subjects rate blue-colored foods as less natural, less appetizing, and more likely to be artificially manipulated than equivalently composed foods in expected colors. However, the same studies note that blue coloring in a *foreign* food context—where the audience has no "expected color" baseline—may not trigger the same naturalness discount: if a Japanese fruit is imagined as sufficiently exotic, blue flesh may read as "unusual but possible" rather than "clearly artificial."

The conjunction of these factors—real precedent (miracle berry), cultural scaffolding (Japan as exotic food source), visual novelty (blue coloring that reads as fantastical but not necessarily artificial in a foreign context), and specific price-point verisimilitude—makes the moon melon one of the more carefully constructed botanical hoaxes in the genre, even if its construction was not consciously strategic.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: FALSE**

No fruit called "moon melon" or bearing the scientific name *Asidus* exists. The species name is not found in any botanical registry, taxonomic database, or horticultural catalogue. The image circulated with the claim is a digitally recolored photograph of an ordinary watermelon. The claimed flavor-switching properties—simultaneous alteration of multiple taste modalities including sweet, sour, salty, and water taste—are biochemically implausible given the mechanistic specificity of mammalian taste receptor biology. A real taste-modifying fruit (the miracle berry, *Synsepalum dulcificum*) exists, but its mechanism is limited to sweet-receptor activation in acidic conditions, not wholesale flavor reversal. The visual presentation in the moon melon hoax misleads by invoking a real fruit's properties in an exaggerated and mechanistically impossible form.

**EARC Classification: C (Confabulation — fabricated species attached to real phenomenon)**

The primary knowledge gap is one of contextual confabulation: the fabricated claim is structured to parasitize a real but poorly-known scientific phenomenon (miraculin and miracle berries), making it resistant to simple denial without engagement with the actual science of taste modification. The C classification captures the manufactured plausibility through real-phenomenon scaffolding.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Origin and identity of the moon melon hoax's creator and original posting context
- Secondary interactive effects of miraculin with aspartame and other synthetic sweeteners
- Comprehensive comparison of all documented taste-modifying plant compounds (miraculin, gymnemic acid, curculin, etc.) in a single analytical frame
- Sociological analysis of why "exotic Japanese food" provides particularly effective scaffolding for botanical misinformation
