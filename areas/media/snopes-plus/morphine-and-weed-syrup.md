# Did This Cough Syrup Really Contain Morphine and Weed?


## 1. The Claim

A photograph showing the ingredient label of a product called "One Night Cough Syrup" — manufactured by Kohler Manufacturing Co. — periodically achieves viral circulation on Reddit and other platforms. The label, as depicted in the photograph, lists the active ingredients as alcohol, cannabis indica, chloroform, and "morphia, sulph" — an archaic abbreviation for morphine sulphate. Posts sharing the image present it as evidence of the extraordinary pharmaceutical freedom once enjoyed (or suffered) by American consumers, frequently drawing comparisons to contemporary over-the-counter cough remedies or drug prohibition frameworks.

The implicit argument embedded in most reshares operates on at least two levels: first, as a commentary on the arbitrary historical contingency of drug scheduling (substances now classified as Schedule I or II narcotics were once sold over the counter at pharmacies); and second, as a form of dark comedy about the past — the recognition that ancestors confidently administered to themselves and their children compounds that modern medicine would classify as powerful narcotics.

The claim's factual status is the most straightforward of any entry in this collection: the product was real, its ingredients are documented from multiple independent contemporaneous sources, and the photograph accurately represents an authentic historical label. Snopes investigative journalist Alex Kasprak, who covered the claim in May 2023, rated it "True."


## 2. What's Actually True

**The product existed.** Kohler Manufacturing Co. was a real enterprise and a significant participant in the patent medicine industry of the late 1800s and early 1900s. Newspaper advertisements for One Night Cough Syrup appear in digitised archives from the period — including a January 1893 advertisement in *The Indianapolis Journal* boasting "17 Gross Sold in 6 Days!" Promotional pamphlets advertising Kohler's proprietary remedies are held in bibliographic collections and confirm the product's commercial presence across multiple decades.

**The ingredients are independently confirmed.** The strongest corroborating evidence is a 1910 publication by Lyman Frederic Kebler of the U.S. Department of Agriculture, titled *Habit-Forming Agents: Their Indiscriminate Sale and Use a Menace to the Public Welfare* (U.S. Government Printing Office, 1910). This document — produced as part of federal scrutiny of the patent medicine industry in the Progressive Era — explicitly lists One Night Cough Syrup among habit-forming products and confirms its ingredient profile as matching the viral label photograph: alcohol, cannabis, chloroform, and morphine sulphate.

**The product was already criticised in its own time.** Importantly, the compound was not regarded as entirely unproblematic even by its contemporaries. A 1914 newspaper piece presents an imagined conversation in which a pharmacist and a physician use One Night Cough Syrup — referred to as "One-Night Cough Cure" — as an example of the dangers of patent medicines, with the pharmacist noting that it contained "both morphine and hasheesh." The physician's response — "By reading the label carefully" — frames the revelation as a failure of consumer vigilance rather than of regulatory oversight, a framing that reveals the pre-regulatory assumptions of the era: the information was technically available; the consumer's failure to scrutinise it was the attributed cause of harm.

**Historical context: the patent medicine market.** One Night Cough Syrup was a product of the elaborate patent medicine industry that flourished in the United States between roughly 1820 and 1906. Patent medicines were proprietary concoctions whose active ingredients were frequently kept secret or listed in archaic nomenclature (like "morphia, sulph") that obscured their nature from lay consumers. The Smithsonian Institution's Balm of America patent medicine collection documents the scope of this industry and the range of compounds — including opiates, cocaine, alcohol, and various other psychoactive substances — routinely incorporated into remedies marketed for conditions including coughs, infant teething pain, "female troubles," and general malaise.

The effects of patent medicines, to the extent they had any, were frequently attributable to their narcotic or alcohol content rather than to any condition-specific therapeutic mechanism. An opiate-containing cough suppressant would, in fact, suppress coughing — morphine acts directly on the cough reflex's medullary control centres — just as it would also create physical dependence in regular users.


## 3. The Epistemic Gap

The claim is rated True, and the documentation is strong. The epistemic gap here is not about the accuracy of the central claim but about what the claim implies that goes largely unexamined in most social media discourse around it.

**The regulatory history is the more important story.** The reason One Night Cough Syrup no longer exists is not simply that attitudes changed — it is that a specific regulatory apparatus was constructed to eliminate it. The pivotal instrument was the Pure Food and Drug Act of 1906, the direct precursor to the modern Food and Drug Administration. The Act was passed following a decade of investigative journalism — most prominently by Samuel Hopkins Adams in his 1905 *Collier's Weekly* series "The Great American Fraud" — that documented the false therapeutic claims, dangerous ingredients, and misleading labelling practices of the patent medicine industry. Adams's series named specific products and compounds, forced public debate, and created the political conditions for federal intervention (Young, 1967, *The Medical Messiahs: A Social History of Health Quackery in Twentieth-Century America*, Princeton University Press).

The subsequent Harrison Narcotics Tax Act of 1914 specifically required registration and taxation of opiates and cocaine, effectively ending their unregulated over-the-counter availability. By 1914 — the same year the newspaper satirising One Night Cough Syrup appeared — the product's commercial days were numbered by regulatory force rather than merely by shifting consumer preference.

**The specific compound chemistry is rarely analysed carefully.** The four ingredients listed on the label warrant individual attention that viral resharing never provides.

*Morphine sulphate* (morphia, sulph) is an opioid derived from the opium poppy, acting on mu-opioid receptors throughout the central nervous system. As a cough suppressant, it is genuinely effective — morphine's antitussive action is well-established (Calignano et al., 2000, *British Journal of Pharmacology*, vol. 130, no. 7, pp. 1651–1658). The same receptor binding produces analgesia, euphoria, respiratory depression, and physical dependence. A consumer dosing themselves with One Night Cough Syrup would receive meaningful morphine exposure.

*Cannabis indica* — now understood to contain tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD) as primary psychoactive compounds — was used in nineteenth-century medicine primarily as a sedative and analgesic. Its antitussive properties are less well-established than those of morphine, but the sedative effect would contribute to the product's reported ability to stop "that tickling in the throat almost instantly" — not by eliminating the irritation but by sedating the patient sufficiently to suppress the behavioural response to it.

*Chloroform* (CHCl₃) was the first widely used general anaesthetic, introduced into medical practice in 1847 by James Young Simpson. By the late nineteenth century, it was recognised as having a narrow therapeutic index — the gap between anaesthetic dose and lethal dose is dangerously small — but small amounts in a cough syrup formulation would contribute a local anaesthetic and pleasant-tasting solvent effect to the preparation.

*Alcohol* served both as a preservative and as a solvent for the other active ingredients, while also contributing its own central nervous system depressant effects and presumably some of the product's consumer appeal.

The combined effect of these four compounds would have been profound sedation, cough suppression, and — with regular use — substantial dependence risk. The product would have been literally habit-forming in the modern pharmacological sense, which is precisely why it appears on Kebler's 1910 USDA list.

**The information was available but structured to obscure.** The 1914 newspaper exchange — in which Mrs. Clyde is horrified to learn her household cough remedy contains morphine and cannabis — reveals a critical asymmetry. The information was technically present on the label, but in archaic nomenclature ("morphia, sulph") that the average consumer could not be expected to interpret. This is a sophisticated regulatory problem that predates contemporary debates about ingredient labelling: formal disclosure can coexist with practical opacity when language is chosen to satisfy the letter of transparency while defeating its purpose. The physician's advice to "read the label carefully" is inadequate as a public health response to a market structured around information asymmetry.

**The "it was fine back then" inference is not warranted.** A significant risk embedded in how this content circulates is that audiences may draw the inference that because these products existed and were sold openly, they were not causing significant harm. The evidence does not support this inference. Addiction to patent medicines containing opiates was a recognised and documented social problem throughout the late nineteenth century. Medical literature from the period documents widespread opiate dependence attributable precisely to the patent medicine market — a phenomenon particularly affecting women, who were disproportionately targeted as consumers of tonics and soothing preparations (Courtwright, 1982, *Dark Paradise: Opiate Addiction in America before 1940*, Harvard University Press).


## 4. Who Spread It and Why

**Retro shock value and dark comedy.** The image's appeal is primarily aesthetic and comedic: the incongruity between the sedate Victorian package design, the respectable pharmaceutical typography, and the ingredient list reads as a joke. The Reddit community r/interestingasfuck — where the image gained significant traction — is explicitly oriented toward exactly this kind of surprising historical curiosity.

**Drug policy libertarianism.** A secondary but meaningful audience engages with the image as a drug policy argument — evidence that substances currently treated as maximally dangerous narcotics were once commercially available and that the scheduling system represents a historically contingent political choice rather than a purely scientific determination. This framing is not entirely illegitimate: the Harrison Act of 1914 was enacted under significant political pressure from both medical establishment gatekeeping interests and racial animus (the Act was partly justified by racist claims about cocaine use among Black Americans), and the modern drug scheduling framework carries real historical contingencies that merit examination. But One Night Cough Syrup is a poor exhibit for this argument — the product genuinely caused widespread dependence in a poorly informed consumer population.

**Journalistic and educational dissemination.** The photograph has also circulated through more clearly educational channels — history teachers, pharmacy historians, and science communicators use it as an opening for discussion of regulatory history and pharmaceutical evolution.


## 5. What Matters for Evidence Literacy

The One Night Cough Syrup case is nearly unique in this collection in having a clearly "True" rating with strong primary documentation. The evidence literacy questions it raises are accordingly different from the usual ones of misinformation — they are questions about what the true fact implies.

**Verified historical artefacts carry unverified implications.** When a true historical fact circulates virally, audiences import interpretive frames that are not themselves fact-checked. The One Night Cough Syrup image is accurate; the inference that drugs were fine when unregulated is not accurate and is not supported by the historical record.

**Archival documentation is a particularly strong form of corroboration.** The combination of newspaper advertisements, government regulatory documents (the 1910 USDA report), and contemporaneous newspaper commentary (the 1914 piece) provides unusually robust corroboration for a historical claim. This multi-source, cross-institutional documentary record is why the rating is "True" rather than "Unproven."

**Technical label disclosure is not equivalent to informed consent.** The historical case anticipates modern debates about ingredient labelling, terms-of-service agreements, and pharmaceutical disclosure. The information being technically present does not mean it was practically accessible or that consumers could reasonably be expected to interpret it correctly.


## 6. Verdict and Confidence

**Snopes rating: True.** Contemporaneous records — newspaper advertisements, USDA government reports, pamphlets, and contemporary press — firmly establish that One Night Cough Syrup was a genuine product manufactured by Kohler Manufacturing Co. with the exact ingredient profile shown in the viral photograph.

**EARC assessment:** The gap here is in *Evidence* — not because the core claim lacks evidence (it has abundant evidence), but because the evidence provided in viral resharing is minimal (a photograph), and audiences lack access to the deeper corroborating record that makes the rating confidently True. The USDA documentation in particular is the crucial piece that elevates this above a plausible-but-unverified historical curiosity to a fully verified historical fact.

**Gap category: verified-with-context.** The claim is accurately rated True. The important contextual additions — what happened next (regulatory intervention), why it mattered (widespread contemporary dependence), the chemistry of its effects — are not present in viral transmission and are necessary for honest assessment.

**Practical upshot:** One Night Cough Syrup is a genuine historical product and a useful starting point for discussions of pharmaceutical regulatory history, drug scheduling, and the informed consent problems of pre-regulatory medical consumer markets. Its viral status reflects the dark comedy of its ingredient list, but its real significance is as evidence of why the regulatory infrastructure created between 1906 and 1914 was urgently necessary.
