# Mountain Dew Contains Brominated Vegetable Oil (BVO), a Dangerous Chemical

*Series: The Truth Vault — Claim Forensics from the Observatory*

---

## 1. The Claim

The claim, which has circulated in various forms since at least 2012 and resurged dramatically in 2013 and 2018, states that Mountain Dew and a range of similarly coloured citrus-flavoured sodas contain brominated vegetable oil — an ingredient that is also used as a flame retardant for plastics, banned in Japan and across the European Union, and linked to severe neurological damage, thyroid disruption, cancer, and organ failure.

The viral versions of this claim range from moderately alarming to apocalyptic. Earlier iterations described BVO as a "toxic chemical" that "competes with iodine for receptor sites in the body" and can cause "hypothyroidism, autoimmune disease, and cancer." Social media posts in 2018 escalated the rhetoric significantly: a widely shared Facebook post reported that a doctor had told a Mountain Dew–drinking patient that BVO was responsible for nerve damage and possible blindness, driving hundreds of thousands of shares. 

The language used in these posts tends to rely on evocative contrasts: the fact that BVO has an industrial application as a flame-retardant for plastics is repeatedly foregrounded, with posts describing Mountain Dew as containing "flame retardant" as though the drink itself were designed to stop fires. The claim also leans heavily on the asymmetry between the United States, which permitted BVO's use, and more cautious regulatory regimes in Japan and the EU, which did not. The implication is straightforward — and emotionally persuasive — that something is being sold to American consumers that other, wiser countries have banned because they know something damning.

This is an archetypal example of a claim that is grounded in a real, verifiable controversy — BVO's regulatory status and safety profile were genuinely unsettled for decades — but whose emotional framing far outruns what the evidence actually supports.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

Let's establish the factual baseline. Several things in this claim are straightforwardly accurate.

**BVO was in Mountain Dew.** Brominated vegetable oil was indeed used as an emulsifier in Mountain Dew and a range of other citrus-flavoured sodas including Squirt, Fresca, Sunkist Peach, Fanta Orange, and certain flavours of Powerade. Its function was practical and specific: without it, the citrus-oil flavour compounds would separate from the water-based liquid and float to the top of the bottle. BVO's greater density relative to water helped hold those oils in suspension throughout the drink.

**BVO is also patented as a flame retardant.** This is true. Brominated compounds have a variety of industrial uses, including as flame retardants, because the presence of bromine atoms interferes with combustion chemistry. The fact that a compound has industrial applications does not, however, tell us anything directly about its toxicity at trace food-grade concentrations.

**BVO is banned in Japan and the European Union.** Also accurate. Many compounds that are permissible as food additives in the United States are not approved in Japan or the EU, and vice versa. This cross-jurisdictional variation reflects different regulatory philosophies, risk tolerances, and the timing of when each jurisdiction last evaluated a particular compound — not necessarily a simple hierarchy of safety wisdom.

**The FDA's status on BVO has been genuinely complicated.** The FDA originally classified BVO as "generally recognised as safe" (GRAS) in 1958. This classification was withdrawn in the early 1970s after studies linked brominated vegetable oil consumption to heart disease in rats. BVO was then reclassified as an "interim food additive" — a designation meaning its continued use was permitted pending the outcome of further studies, capped at 15 parts per million in fruit-flavoured beverages. What is remarkable is how long "interim" stretched. As of the time of most major viral coverage of this story, BVO's interim approval status had not been permanently resolved for over thirty years. In 2023, the FDA finally moved to revoke BVO's authorisation as a food additive entirely, and the formal rule was finalised in 2024.

**Consumer pressure worked.** After a then-15-year-old named Sarah Kavanagh launched a Change.org petition in late 2012 asking PepsiCo to remove BVO from Gatorade, the company pledged to do so. By 2013 and 2014, both PepsiCo and Coca-Cola announced they would phase out BVO from their beverages. By mid-2020, BVO was no longer listed as an ingredient in Mountain Dew's label.

**What is not established by the evidence** is the strongest version of the claim: that BVO, at the concentrations found in commercially sold sodas, was causing harm to consumers. The handful of documented cases of BVO-related health problems in humans involved extraordinary consumption — individuals drinking two to four litres of BVO-containing sodas daily over prolonged periods. A 1997 case study and a 2003 case study both documented patients who had developed memory loss, skin lesions, and nerve-related symptoms consistent with bromine toxicity; in both cases, the patients were consuming amounts of soda so far beyond normal consumption as to constitute a unique exposure category. The medical literature on BVO's effects on humans at typical consumption levels does not establish causation.

The studies showing adverse effects in animals used doses far in excess of anything a human could realistically ingest through beverages where BVO was legally capped at 15 ppm — and where actual concentrations in products were typically around half that maximum.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The BVO claim is an unusually effective piece of health misinformation because it is not constructed from whole cloth. Every major building block is factually present in some form.

**The "flame retardant" framing is viscerally alarming.** Few metaphors are more powerful for generating disgust than telling someone their soda ingredient is also used to stop plastics from catching fire. The framing exploits a cognitive shortcut — if a substance has a dangerous industrial application, it must be dangerous at any dose. This is a version of what toxicologists call dose-ignorance: the conflation of hazardous versus safe exposure levels. Almost every biologically active substance is dose-dependent in its effects. Water consumed in extreme quantities is fatal.

**The "banned in Europe" heuristic is genuinely compelling.** For many American consumers, European regulatory standards function as a proxy for better science and less industry capture. There is a real basis for this perception — the EU operates on a stricter precautionary principle than the FDA — but the inference that "banned in Europe = proven dangerous" is too simple. Regulatory differences reflect divergent risk philosophies and administrative timelines, not always different bodies of evidence.

**The FDA's genuine ambiguity gave the claim staying power.** The fact that BVO spent over three decades in "interim" approved limbo was not reassuring. It was a legitimate policy failure — the regulatory system had identified a potential problem in the 1970s and then failed to resolve it definitively for an extraordinarily long time. Critics who pointed to this as evidence of industry capture were not obviously wrong. The FDA's eventual 2023–2024 decision to ban BVO outright validated the core direction of consumer concern, even if it took fifty years.

**Social media amplifies personal medical testimony.** The 2018 Facebook post from the woman whose doctor allegedly attributed her neurological symptoms to Mountain Dew was shared over 200,000 times. This kind of personal testimony — a named patient, a specific medical encounter, precise symptoms — is epistemically compelling even when it cannot be verified and even when it involves a single case that may reflect idiosyncratic factors. We are wired to update more strongly on personal narrative than on population-level epidemiological data.

**The wellness and clean-eating movement provided ideological infrastructure.** BVO concerns flourished in a cultural moment when processed food was already under intense scrutiny, when "food with ingredients you can't pronounce" had become a widely shared shorthand for unhealthy, and when distrust of major corporations like PepsiCo and Coca-Cola was commercially useful for the wellness industry selling alternatives. The anti-BVO campaign was partly organic and partly channelled through platforms with financial incentives.

## 4. Verdict

Snopes rated this claim **Mixture** — and that's the right call.

The claim as frequently stated contains a true component (BVO was in Mountain Dew; it has contested safety history; it is banned in some other countries) and a false component (it was not proven dangerous at quantities found in soft drinks, and typical consumers were not being harmed by drinking Mountain Dew in normal amounts).

The more useful framing for the Observatory's purposes is that this is a **grounded distortion**: the undergirding facts are real, but their significance has been amplified and decontextualised. BVO's industrial use as a flame retardant is real, but weaponised rhetorically to imply inherent toxicity. The European and Japanese bans are real, but treated as definitive pronouncements on safety rather than jurisdictional policy choices. The animal studies showing harm are real, but extrapolated from doses unrelated to human consumption.

What makes this particularly instructive is the outcome: consumer pressure succeeded. Kavanagh's petition, the viral sharing, the sustained media attention — these created enough reputational liability for major beverage companies that they voluntarily reformulated before any definitive regulatory ban was in place. The FDA's eventual 2023–2024 rule change arrived after the market had already moved. Whether the health risk was ever as acute as claimed, the advocacy campaign produced a real public-health outcome.

That ambiguity — a potentially overstated risk producing a real regulatory response — is one of the most interesting structural features of food safety advocacy.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The BVO story is a window into several interlocking problems in how food safety gets communicated and regulated.

**The "interim" problem.** The FDA's concept of interim food additive approval was designed to handle genuine scientific uncertainty — a substance would be permitted while further studies resolved open questions. In practice, the interim category became a holding pen where regulatory decisions could stall indefinitely. By 2023, the FDA had more than 60 food additives still in interim status, many of them for decades. This is not a sign of thorough ongoing review; it is a structural failure of administrative follow-through. BVO's story illustrates that the interim category can persist long past the point where it offers meaningful consumer protection.

**Dose-response and the limits of popular toxicology.** One of the most persistent challenges in public health communication is explaining dose-response relationships to audiences who reasonably worry about any exposure to a compound with known harm potential. The principle that "the dose makes the poison" is correct and important — but it requires trusting expert institutions to set appropriate exposure thresholds, and that trust has been eroded by decades of cases where regulatory thresholds were later found to be too permissive (lead in gasoline, asbestos in buildings, PFAS in drinking water). The BVO debate is partly a symptom of that credibility gap.

**Consumer advocacy as regulatory driver.** Sarah Kavanagh was fifteen years old when she launched her petition. Her petition attracted over 200,000 signatures within months. PepsiCo, calculating reputational risk, moved faster than the FDA. This is a pattern that appears repeatedly in food safety history: consumer-facing market pressure outpacing administrative process. The lesson is not simply that petitions work, but that the regulatory system has structural lags that make informal market pressure a meaningful corrective force.

**The reformulation story matters.** After BVO was removed from most US sodas, manufacturers replaced it with glycerol ester of rosin (SAIB) or sucrose acetate isobutyrate (SAIB) — compounds that perform a similar emulsification function. Neither of these replacements has attracted anything like similar scrutiny or viral concern, though neither has an entirely resolved safety profile either. The pattern of consumer pressure producing reformulation without necessarily producing better safety understanding is worth tracking.

**BVO's actual chemistry.** Bromine is element 35, a halogen like fluorine, chlorine, and iodine. Brominated compounds have a wide range of applications — pharmaceuticals, pesticides, fire retardants, photographic chemistry — and a wide range of safety profiles depending on the compound and dose. The human body does metabolise bromine, and there is a genuine competitive dynamic between bromine and iodine for certain biochemical sites. Whether this competition is clinically relevant at trace dietary exposures is the genuinely contested scientific question that popular coverage rarely explored with appropriate precision.

The 2013 Environmental Health journal paper that called for renewed safety assessments of BVO using modern toxicological tools was a legitimate scientific intervention: its authors were not alarmists but researchers noting that the standard safety assessments had used blunt 1970s-era tools and that newer methods might yield different results. That kind of careful scientific advocacy is different from viral oversimplification, but both contributed to the eventual regulatory outcome.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes's handling of this claim is exemplary in several respects. The site rated it **Mixture**, which is both accurate and honest about complexity — the claim is not simply true or false, and forcing it into a binary would misrepresent the underlying situation.

The Snopes entry's architecture is instructive: it separates what's true from what's false within the same claim, it provides regulatory history with named FDA officials quoted directly, it notes the specific documented medical cases of harm (while contextualising them as involving extreme consumption), and it acknowledges the outcome — BVO's removal from products — without treating that removal as retroactive confirmation that consumers were being harmed at typical intake levels.

This is the correct forensic approach for a compound whose real status is genuinely uncertain: not reassuring false certainty, not maximising alarm, but walking through the actual state of the evidence with appropriate caveats.

What fact-checkers do less well with claims like this is address the structural question that the regulatory history raises. Snopes notes that BVO remained in "interim" status for over thirty years but does not pursue what that institutional failure means for how we should think about other interim additives. This is perhaps beyond the scope of a single fact-check, but it is the kind of systemic question that the BVO story most urgently raises.

The broader lesson for media literacy: **"it's in interim approval" is not the same as "it's been shown safe."** The FDA's interim category is essentially an administrative acknowledgement that the question has not been definitively answered. This distinction — between absence of proven harm and proven absence of harm — is one that popular health journalism consistently flattens in both directions, sometimes reassuring and sometimes alarming, rarely reflecting the genuine epistemic uncertainty that drives the regulatory ambiguity in the first place.

For readers encountering claims about food additives: the questions worth asking are not "is this banned somewhere?" or "does this have an industrial use?" but rather "at what doses has harm been demonstrated, are those doses relevant to normal consumption, and what does the most recent systematic review of the evidence conclude?" Those are harder questions. But they are the ones that actually track the underlying reality.
