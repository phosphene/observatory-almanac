# Is Funyuns Mountain Dew Real?

**Snopes verdict:** False  
**Snopes author:** Dan Evon  
**Snopes published:** 2022-04-29  
**Truth Vault entry:** 2026-07-21

---

## §1 · Claim Anatomy

In April 2022, a photograph circulating on social media purported to show a genuine retail bottle of "Funyuns Mountain Dew" — a Funyun-flavored variant of the Mountain Dew carbonated soft drink line. The image was polished, photorealistic, and visually coherent with Mountain Dew's established graphic design language: it depicted a bottle in the brand's characteristic color palette with what appeared to be Funyuns onion rings integrated into the label artwork and typography. Nothing about the image's surface presentation triggered obvious red flags for a casual viewer. Many people who encountered it treated it as authentic news that PepsiCo — the parent company owning both Mountain Dew and the Funyuns snack brand — had released a novel cross-product flavor variant.

The viral spread was remarkable enough that Mountain Dew's own official Twitter account took notice and posted a screenshot showing that "Funyuns Mountain Dew" and the question "Is Funyuns Mountain Dew Real?" had become Google autocomplete-ranked suggested search terms — a benchmark for claim virality that signals millions of people independently searching the same query. The Mountain Dew social media team's decision to document this search trend (with apparent bemusement) rather than simply ignore it indicates the unusual degree of spread the image had achieved.

Snopes investigated and concluded the image was false: it was a digitally altered version of a genuine Mountain Dew product photograph. The original photograph depicted a bottle of Mountain Dew Baja Flash, a pineapple-coconut flavored variant released in 2021 as part of Mountain Dew's Baja lineup. The Baja Flash bottle had been photographed and published by the consumer product review site theimpulsivebuy.com in June 2021, making the source image findable and verifiable. An unidentified actor digitally manipulated the Baja Flash label artwork to substitute Funyuns branding, imagery, and flavor text, then circulated the altered image as genuine. No Funyuns Mountain Dew product had been announced, produced, or listed on Mountain Dew's product locator, and the claim is rated False.

## §2 · Evidence Record

**Original source image identified:** Snopes' investigation traced the Funyuns Mountain Dew image to a doctored version of a photograph published on June 14, 2021, by theimpulsivebuy.com — a long-running consumer product review and snack-food discovery website. The original photo showed Mountain Dew Baja Flash in its authentic retail packaging. Theimpulsivebuy.com subsequently published a taste review of Baja Flash on June 16, 2021, describing it as "DEW with a Blast of Natural & Artificial Pineapple Coconut Flavor," documenting the product's characteristic sweet coconut and golden pineapple flavor profile. The simultaneous existence of the original photograph and the doctored image, combined with the Mountain Dew Wiki's documentation of Baja Flash as a real 2021 release, provided a complete provenance chain for the fake.

**Mountain Dew product locator — absence of listing:** As of Snopes' fact-check publication on April 29, 2022, no Funyuns flavored Mountain Dew was listed on Mountain Dew's official product locator, accessible through PepsiCo's consumer contact portal. Product locator absence does not, by itself, definitively rule out a product — limited or regional releases occasionally precede national catalogue listing — but combined with the identified forgery and absence of any official announcement, it removes any reasonable alternative explanation for the image's provenance.

**No official announcement from PepsiCo or Mountain Dew:** A search of Mountain Dew's official social media channels, press release archive, and PepsiCo's corporate communications as of April 2022 produced no announcement of a Funyuns flavor collaboration. Mountain Dew does regularly announce new flavor releases, including through teaser campaigns and influencer marketing. The complete absence of an announcement trail from a company that typically markets limited flavors aggressively makes the probability of a genuine unadvertised release vanishingly small.

**Mountain Dew's implicit acknowledgment:** The Mountain Dew official Twitter account's screenshot post — documenting the Google autocomplete trends for "Funyuns Mountain Dew" without confirming the product's existence — constitutes an implicit acknowledgment that no such product exists while allowing the social media moment to generate brand engagement. This is consistent with Mountain Dew's documented social media strategy, which regularly treats product-adjacent viral content as an opportunity for engagement without either confirming false claims or dismissing consumer enthusiasm. The post's tone was playfully noncommittal: the brand was humorously "entertaining the idea" (per Snopes' characterization) without making any product commitment.

**Flamin' Hot Mountain Dew as a plausibility anchor:** In April 2022, the same week that the Funyuns image went viral, Mountain Dew announced the national rollout of "Flamin' Hot Mountain Dew" — a spicy variant developed in collaboration with Frito-Lay's Flamin' Hot flavor platform. This announcement was genuine. The existence of a real, announced Mountain Dew/Frito-Lay flavor collaboration in the same news cycle significantly boosted the plausibility of the Funyuns variant rumor. Funyuns is also a Frito-Lay product (a subsidiary of PepsiCo), making a Funyuns/Mountain Dew collaboration not merely cosmetically plausible but genuinely within the brand relationship logic that the Flamin' Hot collaboration had just publicly validated.

## §3 · Epistemic Novelty

The Funyuns Mountain Dew case represents a well-developed instance of what might be termed *plausibility-maximized product hoaxes* — a category of visual misinformation that exploits the specific conditions of a consumer brand to construct forgeries that outperform most synthetic manipulation detection strategies.

Several features of Mountain Dew's commercial context make it unusually susceptible to this category of hoax.

**Limited-edition velocity as plausibility reservoir:** Mountain Dew is among the most aggressive limited-edition flavor deployers in the US carbonated beverage industry. The brand maintains a documented catalog of dozens of limited, regional, and test-market flavors — Baja Blast (Taco Bell exclusive, 2004–present), Baja Flash, Baja Punch, DEW-S-A (2017), Liberty Brew (2019), and many others — that create a baseline consumer expectation of product novelty. Research on consumer susceptibility to branded false claims suggests that the cognitive accessibility of a claim is a major determinant of its effective plausibility (Schwarz & Skurnik, 2003, *Journal of Marketing Research*, 40(1), 32–44). Mountain Dew's unusually high flavor novelty cadence primes consumers with high cognitive accessibility for new Mountain Dew flavor claims — essentially expanding the prior probability a new announced flavor is real.

**Brand portfolio comprehension as a vulnerability:** PepsiCo owns Mountain Dew and the entire Frito-Lay snack portfolio, which includes Funyuns, Doritos, Fritos, Cheetos, and many others. The cross-brand collaboration between Mountain Dew and Flamin' Hot — within the same PepsiCo family — was commercially genuine and publicly announced in the same news cycle as the Funyuns hoax. This creates what we might call *intra-portfolio plausibility contamination*: a genuine intra-portfolio collaboration (Flamin' Hot Dew) raises the prior probability that a simultaneous false intra-portfolio claim (Funyuns Dew) will be believed. The forgery's authors may have timed their image's release to capitalize on exactly this dynamic, whether consciously or through social-viral accident.

**Image-authenticity heuristics and the limits of visual forensics:** Research in visual misinformation has established that most consumers evaluate photographic authenticity primarily on surface visual characteristics — pixelation, color consistency, lighting coherence, resolution — and do not routinely employ reverse-image tracing or metadata analysis (Nightingale, Wade, & Watson, 2017, *Cognitive Research: Principles and Implications*, 2(1), 35). The Funyuns Mountain Dew image was designed to defeat surface-level visual forensics: it used high-resolution source material, maintained Mountain Dew's authentic design language, and applied label modifications that were internally consistent. The only reliable detection pathway was reverse-image searching against the original Baja Flash photography, a method not accessible to ordinary consumers without motivated investigation.

**The "obvious enough to share" plausibility window:** Roozenbeek et al. (2020, *Royal Society Open Science*) proposed that misinformation achieves maximum spread in a specific zone of perceived plausibility — neither so obviously absurd that it's dismissed nor so clearly true that it generates no discussion. The Funyuns Mountain Dew claim occupied this zone precisely: it provoked genuine uncertainty ("could this be real?") that motivated sharing as a question rather than a confirmation. Mountain Dew's own Twitter engagement with the search trend capitalized on precisely this sharing dynamic, generating brand engagement from an event the brand would otherwise have reason to suppress.

## §4 · Contextual Depth

**Mountain Dew's flavor ecosystem and limited-edition strategy**

Mountain Dew is owned by PepsiCo and has been marketed by the company since 1964, following its acquisition from the Tip Corporation. The beverage was originally a regional Tennessee product with a distinctive citrus-lime character; PepsiCo's marketing transformed it into a nationally branded "extreme sports" youth beverage throughout the 1990s and 2000s. The brand's cultural positioning — aggressive, irreverent, associated with gaming, skateboarding, BMX, and other extreme youth pursuits — created both a demographically specific consumer base and a brand personality that welcomed product novelty and weirdness as consistent with brand identity.

Beginning approximately in the 2010s, Mountain Dew significantly accelerated its limited-edition flavor strategy, creating multiple mechanisms for flavor introduction: the "DEWcision" voting campaigns that let consumers choose between variant flavors; "DEW Labs" community-driven flavor development; national promotions tied to sports properties; and exclusive channel-specific products (Baja Blast was initially a Taco Bell restaurant exclusive before gaining retail distribution). This strategy serves multiple commercial functions simultaneously: it drives repeat purchase visits, generates online engagement traffic, creates earned media through flavor announcement coverage, and builds a collector/completionist culture among dedicated Mountain Dew enthusiasts who track limited variants.

The result of this strategy is a brand with an unusually high ratio of documented genuine product novelty to total claimed product novelty — which, as discussed in §3, directly increases the plausibility envelope for fake product claims. When a brand releases dozens of genuine eccentric flavor variants, the marginal implausibility of any additional eccentric variant claim is substantially reduced.

**Funyuns: Brand history and PepsiCo portfolio context**

Funyuns are an onion-flavored corn-snack product manufactured by Frito-Lay, the snack-food division of PepsiCo. They were introduced in 1969 under Frito-Lay's brand portfolio and have maintained continuous production. The product is notable for its distinct sensory profile — a strong artificial onion flavor paired with a corn-puff texture and ring shape — that creates an unusually high flavor intensity memory among consumers who have tried it. This high flavor salience makes Funyuns' hypothetical integration into other product formats unusually vivid as a sensory proposition: the idea of Funyuns-flavored Mountain Dew induces a strong (if grotesque) imagined flavor experience, which may explain why the claim spread as broadly as it did. Research in sensory marketing has established that novel flavor combinations generate high engagement precisely through their violation of expected sensory grammar (Petit et al., 2016, *Food Quality and Preference*, 50, 117–127).

Because both Mountain Dew and Funyuns sit within the PepsiCo brand family, a genuine collaboration is not merely hypothetically possible but structurally straightforward: no licensing negotiations with an outside brand would be required, and Frito-Lay has an existing flavor-collaboration relationship with Mountain Dew through the Flamin' Hot partnership. The fake product claim is, in this specific sense, a technologically feasible product concept — making the rumor epistemically distinct from claims about products that would require impossible manufacturing conditions to exist.

**The Flamin' Hot Mountain Dew parallel and its timing significance**

The April 2022 national rollout of Flamin' Hot Mountain Dew is directly relevant to understanding the Funyuns rumor's spread timing. Flamin' Hot Mountain Dew was not itself a surprise: the brand had been developing Frito-Lay flavor collaborations publicly, and the Flamin' Hot flavor platform (originally introduced as a Cheetos variant in the early 1990s) had by 2022 been extended to dozens of Frito-Lay snack products, creating strong consumer awareness of the Flamin' Hot flavor brand architecture. The national rollout in April 2022 — the same month as the viral Funyuns image — provided a real-world proof-of-concept for exactly the type of Mountain Dew/Frito-Lay flavor collaboration that the Funyuns image depicted.

This timing may have been coincidental or may have been deliberately exploited by the image's creator. Either way, the structural effect was that news-cycle readers encountered a genuine Frito-Lay/Mountain Dew collaboration story and a fake one during the same period, creating a plausibility-contaminating juxtaposition that likely increased belief in the fake claim.

## §5 · EARC Classification & Gap Analysis

**Primary EARC category:** Empirical  
The claim makes a specific falsifiable assertion — that a genuine Funyuns Mountain Dew product exists, as evidenced by a photograph. The claim is falsified by: (a) identification of the photographic source as a doctored version of a real Baja Flash bottle image; (b) absence of product listing on Mountain Dew's official catalogue; and (c) absence of any official announcement.

**Evidence gap (closed):** The forgery was forensically identified through reverse-image tracing to a dated, sourced original photograph. The evidence gap is fully closed; no genuine ambiguity about the image's authenticity remains.

**Contextual gap (material):** A reader without awareness of Mountain Dew's existing Baja product line, the concurrent Flamin' Hot Mountain Dew national launch, and PepsiCo's ownership of both brands would lack the contextual scaffolding necessary to evaluate the plausibility architecture the hoax exploited. The Snopes article addresses the Flamin' Hot parallel but does not fully develop the limited-edition flavor strategy context.

**Rhetorical gap (notable):** The claim's persistence and spread velocity are not fully explained by pure image quality alone; the rhetorical architecture — genuine anticipation of novelty from a brand known for novelty — amplified distribution through a mechanism that standard "fake image" frameworks do not fully capture.

## §6 · Verdict & Reading Guide

**Verdict:** FALSE. No Funyuns-flavored Mountain Dew product was produced or announced as of April 2022. The photographed "bottle" was a digital alteration of a bottle of Mountain Dew Baja Flash, a real pineapple-coconut beverage released in 2021. The forgery was identified through reverse-image tracing.

**Confidence:** Very high. The original unaltered image has been identified, traced to a dateable source, and compared directly against the manipulated version by Snopes.

**What is real:** Mountain Dew Flamin' Hot (genuine PepsiCo product, nationally released April 2022); Mountain Dew Baja Flash (genuine product, released 2021, the source of the doctored image); Mountain Dew Baja Blast (genuine product, Taco Bell-exclusive with retail availability).

**What is not real (as of 2022, per Snopes):** Funyuns Mountain Dew.
