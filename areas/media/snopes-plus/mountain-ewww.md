## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In January 2012, public attention was drawn to an unusual legal case filed against PepsiCo in Madison County, Illinois. Ronald Ball claimed that in November 2008 he had purchased a can of Mountain Dew from a vending machine at his workplace, opened the can, and discovered a dead mouse inside after pouring the contents into a Styrofoam cup. Ball sued PepsiCo alleging injury from the experience. PepsiCo, in its legal defense, filed an affidavit from a licensed veterinarian and veterinary pathologist, Dr. Lawrence McGill, which contained the remarkable claim that if a mouse had been in a sealed can of Mountain Dew for the claimed period — approximately 74 days between the can's production on August 28, 2008, and the alleged discovery date — the mouse's body would have been "transformed into a 'jelly-like' substance" by the acid content of the beverage.

This claim — that Mountain Dew can dissolve a mouse — was assigned the verdict **True** by Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2012). The claim is true in a specific, technically qualified sense that emerges from expert testimony: a mouse submerged in Mountain Dew for 30 or more days would undergo extensive tissue dissolution, decalcification, and structural disintegration due to the citric acid content of the beverage. This verdict is interesting precisely because it was generated as a *defense* argument by a corporation seeking to defeat the plaintiff's lawsuit: PepsiCo's position was not that Mountain Dew is harmless to biological tissue, but that the mouse could not have been in the can since its filling date because it would have decomposed beyond recognition. In establishing this defense, PepsiCo's experts generated and affirmed empirical facts about the corrosive capacity of Mountain Dew that are independently verifiable.

## §2 The Chemistry of Mountain Dew's Acidity: Citric Acid and pH

Mountain Dew's chemical profile is central to the dissolution claim. Mountain Dew contains citric acid as its primary acidulant — citric acid is the compound that gives citrus fruits their characteristic tartness, and it is widely used in the food and beverage industry as a flavoring and preservative. Most regular cola beverages (Coca-Cola, Pepsi) use phosphoric acid as their primary acidulant, while citrus-flavored sodas (Mountain Dew, Sprite, 7-Up, Fresca) predominantly use citric acid.

The pH scale measures the concentration of hydrogen ions in a solution; a lower pH indicates higher acidity. Mountain Dew has a measured pH of approximately 3.22–3.35 depending on formulation and temperature (von Fraunhofer and Rogers, 2004, *General Dentistry*, vol. 52, no. 4). For reference, gastric acid in the human stomach typically ranges from pH 1.5 to 3.5, vinegar is approximately pH 2.4–3.4, and orange juice is approximately pH 3.3–4.0. Mountain Dew is therefore in the same pH range as orange juice and somewhat more acidic than many vinegars.

Yan-Fang Ren of the University of Rochester School of Medicine and Dentistry, who has studied the effects of citric acid on bones and teeth, commented specifically on the Mountain Dew mouse dissolution question (quoted in Wolchover, 2012, *Scientific American*, 5 January 2012): "I think it is plausible that it could dissolve a mouse in a few months. But dissolving [the mouse] does not mean it will disappear, because you'll still have the collagen and the soft tissue part. It will be like rubber." Ren's comment captures the precise mechanism: citric acid at pH 3.22–3.35 is effective at demineralizing bone and dissolving inorganic calcium phosphate matrix (hydroxyapatite) considerably more effectively than at physiologic pH, but the organic collagen scaffold of bone and connective tissue is more acid-resistant and would persist, producing the described rubber-like residue.

Von Fraunhofer and Rogers (2004) conducted a major comparative study of the erosive potential of various soft drinks on tooth enamel — a hard tissue composed of hydroxyapatite similar in mineral composition to bone. Their study found that citrus-flavored sodas including Mountain Dew and Sprite erode enamel approximately six times faster than cola beverages (specifically, Mountain Dew produced the greatest weight loss of enamel per unit time among the sodas tested). This result, along with Ren's expert comment, provides the documented scientific basis for Dr. McGill's affidavit claim about mouse dissolution.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Defense-Admission Paradox and Legally Compelled Truth-Production

The Mountain Dew mouse case presents a structural situation in food science evidence production that deserves its own epistemic category: the *defense-admission paradox* — a situation in which a defendant's legal defense strategy requires producing and affirming evidence more damaging to its product's public reputation than any claim the plaintiff made.

Ronald Ball's lawsuit claimed he found a mouse in a can of Mountain Dew. This is a standard food contamination claim of the type that arises periodically in food product litigation. Ball alleged that he was "violently ill" and "began to vomit" upon encountering the mouse. His claim, if successful, would have resulted in a modest damages award in an individual products liability case.

PepsiCo's defense team chose to contest the claim's physical plausibility by arguing that the mouse could not have been in the can since the filling date, because the beverage would have dissolved it beyond recognition in 74 days. To establish this defense, PepsiCo engaged Dr. Lawrence McGill — a licensed veterinarian with a speciality in veterinary pathology — who provided an affidavit stating that the mouse provided by Ball to PepsiCo (which retained recognizable structure) could not have been in the can since its production date: it had bones with calcium intact (not decalcified), intact abdominal structures (not ruptured), and was estimated based on developmental stage to have been born no earlier than several weeks after the can was filled.

The affidavit required Dr. McGill to state as scientific fact that a mouse submerged in Mountain Dew for 30+ days would become "jelly-like" — and this factual claim, though offered in service of defeating a plaintiff's lawsuit, is scientifically well-supported and independently verifiable. PepsiCo thus found itself in the position of introducing, under oath and in a court of law, the expert opinion that their product will dissolve a mouse.

This produces a distinctive epistemological situation: the claim that "Mountain Dew dissolves mice" became legally established as the good-faith belief of PepsiCo's own independently retained expert witness, stated under penalty of perjury in federal court. The evidentiary value of this is high precisely because PepsiCo had every incentive to minimize the claim rather than exaggerate it — the company would have strongly preferred to demonstrate that its product is harmless to biological tissue if such a demonstration were available. The fact that defending against Ball's lawsuit required affirming the dissolving power of the acid is the paradox: the defense, taken to its logical conclusion, tells consumers something unflattering about the product.

Jasanoff (2001, *Science and Public Policy*, vol. 28, no. 4) analyzed the role of adversarial scientific testimony in American courts and noted that the adversarial legal context creates strong incentives for parties to commission and present scientific evidence that serves their immediate litigation interests. In this case, the resulting expert testimony is, paradoxically, more reliable *as evidence about the product* precisely because it comes from the defendant's own expert, advancing the defendant's narrow legal interest — which happened to require affirming an unflattering chemical truth.

## §4 Timeline and Pathology: How the Defense Actually Won

The legal significance of the Mountain Dew mouse case rests not just on the dissolution evidence but on the concurrent veterinary pathology evidence that exonerated PepsiCo of the contamination claim. Dr. McGill's full affidavit contained two separate arguments, both drawing on the same acid-dissolution chemistry, but operating in opposite logical directions:

**Argument 1 (Forward projection):** If the mouse had been in the Mountain Dew since the can was filled on August 28, 2008, it would have been in the fluid for 74 days by the alleged discovery date of November 10, 2008. The acid chemistry of Mountain Dew would, within that period, have decalcified the mouse's skeleton, ruptured its abdominal cavity and cranial cavity, and disintegrated the remaining structures to the point of non-recognition.

**Argument 2 (Backward inference from physical evidence):** McGill's examination of the mouse provided by Ball showed (a) intact bone calcium (bones were not decalcified), (b) intact abdominal structures (not ruptured), and (c) developmental stage consistent with an age of no more than 2–4 weeks at the time of death, with a birth date that post-dated the can filling date. McGill concluded: "This mouse was not in the Mountain Dew fluid for more than 7 days and could not have been and was not introduced into the can of Mountain Dew when the can was produced (filled and sealed)."

The combination of these two arguments — the prospective chemistry and the retrospective pathology — constituted PepsiCo's successful summary judgment motion. The case effectively demonstrated that the mouse Ball produced was not (a) born when the can was filled, (b) subject to acid dissolution for 74 days, or (c) in recognizable condition consistent with having been in highly acidic fluid for any substantial period.

This is sound forensic methodology. Forensic entomology and forensic veterinary pathology both use developmental biology and tissue decomposition rates as independent clocks for establishing time-of-death and exposure duration. The use of acid-dissolution expected outcome as an independent check on the plaintiff's timeline is methodologically analogous to established forensic approaches in homicide investigation, where expected decomposition stage is used to verify or contest estimated time of death. Vass et al. (2002, *Journal of Forensic Sciences*, vol. 47, no. 3) reviewed decomposition chemistry timelines in forensic contexts; the Mountain Dew case simply applies analogous reasoning in a novel chemical environment.

## §5 Urban Legends of Food Contamination: The Mouse-in-the-Bottle Archetype

Folklorist Jan Harold Brunvand, cited by Mikkelson (2012), had documented as early as 1988 (Brunvand, 1988, *The Deseret News*, 8 April 1988) that rumors of mice found in soda bottles have a long history in urban folklore pre-dating the Ball lawsuit by decades. The mouse-in-the-bottle legend is one of the most persistent subspecies of the broader "foreign object in a commercial food product" legend family, which includes contaminated-candy-on-Halloween, razor-blade-in-apple, and various ethnic-restaurant contamination legends documented in Brunvand's *The Vanishing Hitchhiker* (1981, Norton) and *Too Good To Be True* (1999, Norton).

The mouse-in-the-soda-bottle archetype specifically has a documented commercial reality: the landmark U.S. tort case *Coca-Cola Bottling Co. v. Hatcher* and numerous similar state-level cases in the early to mid 20th century established the products liability framework for such claims, and the plaintiffs in some of those cases prevailed because early 20th-century bottling technology did not provide the same mechanical quality controls as modern automated beverage production. The sociological function of these legends, as analyzed by Brunvand following the theoretical framework of Smith (1969, *The Book of Really Terrifying Urban Legends*, though this is a retroactive categorization), is to provide narrative expression of consumer anxieties about the opacity of industrial food production — the worry that what is inside sealed industrial packaging is unknown and potentially dangerous.

The remarkable outcome of the Ball lawsuit — where the *defendant* used science to establish the dissolving power of its own product in order to defeat a contamination claim — illustrates the way that legal processes can generate factual findings that neither party intended to create as public knowledge, and that these legally-compelled factual findings have genuine scientific validity precisely because the adversarial context motivates both parties to contest unsupported claims.

## §6 Conclusion: Acid, Mice, and the Double Edge of Factual Defense

The Mountain Dew dissolution case is epistemically distinctive on multiple dimensions. It is a case in which a corporate defendant produced scientifically sound expert evidence that their own product, over a period of weeks, will demineralize bone and reduce a mouse to a gelatinous residue. It is a case where this evidence was introduced not as a safety concern but as a defense against a liability claim. And it is a case where the factual truth affirmed — Mountain Dew's citric acid is powerful enough to dissolve a mouse over a month or more — is simultaneously consistent with a broad public health and dental literature demonstrating that citrus sodas have substantially higher erosive potential on mineralised tissue than cola beverages.

Von Fraunhofer and Rogers (2004) found that Mountain Dew produced greater enamel erosion per unit time than any other soft drink tested — including clearly acidic beverages like orange juice — and recommended that clinicians advise patients that regular consumption of citrus-flavored sodas poses meaningful erosive risk to dental enamel. This is a separate and independently supported health concern from the mouse lawsuit, but it shares a mechanism: the citric acid that makes Mountain Dew effective at dissolving a mouse's bones is the same mechanism that makes it more erosive to tooth enamel than other beverages.

In both the legal and the public health context, the correct conclusion is not that Mountain Dew is uniquely dangerous among foods — the dental erosion data situates it within a risk category shared by citrus juices and other acidic foods — but rather that the specific claim (acid at pH 3.2–3.3 will decalcify bone and soften tissue over weeks of immersion) is empirically supported and has been endorsed under oath by PepsiCo's own experts. Snopes's verdict of **True** is therefore well-supported: yes, Mountain Dew exposure over several weeks can transform a submerged mouse into a jelly-like substance. The question Ronald Ball raised about whether it happened in his specific can was answered in the negative by the same chemistry; but the capability itself is real.
