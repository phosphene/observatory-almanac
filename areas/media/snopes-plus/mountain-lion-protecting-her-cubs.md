## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In October 2020, a video went viral showing a Utah man named Kyle Burgess being followed for more than six minutes along a trail by a mountain lion (*Puma concolor*) in the Slate Canyon wilderness area near Orem, Utah. The encounter had all the hallmarks of what many social media commenters described as an active predatory stalk: the mountain lion moved steadily, deliberately, and aggressively toward Burgess along the trail, making several short lunges or bluff charges. The footage was widely viewed and prompted substantial public discussion about whether the animal was, in fact, stalking the man as prey—or, as a significant subset of social media users argued, protecting cubs that Burgess had unknowingly walked near.

Snopes investigator Madison Dapcevich (2020) rated this claim **True**: it was a genuine encounter, and expert analysis strongly supported the interpretation that the mountain lion's behavior was defensive—protecting her cubs—rather than predatory. Burgess himself confirmed afterward in his YouTube post that he had first encountered what he initially thought were bobcats, only to later realize they were cougar (mountain lion) cubs. That discovery triggered the prolonged, aggressive following behavior from the adult female.

This entry examines the specific behavioral evidence that differentiates defensive parental behavior from predatory stalking in *Puma concolor*, the expert testimony obtained by Snopes, Burgess's response behavior during the encounter and its evaluation by wildlife professionals, the broader ecology of human-mountain lion conflict, and what the public misunderstanding of cougar predatory strategy reveals about the epistemics of wildlife encounter interpretation.

## §2 The Encounter: Narrative Reconstruction and Expert Commentary

Kyle Burgess was hiking alone in Slate Canyon in the Wasatch Range of central Utah on October 10, 2020, when he came upon what he initially took to be bobcat kittens on or near the trail. The Utah Division of Wildlife Resources (UDWR) later confirmed these were mountain lion cubs. Within moments of this discovery, the adult female appeared and initiated the behavior captured on video: a sustained, close-range following of Burgess as he backed slowly up the trail.

Burgess narrated the encounter breathlessly while filming, repeating "this is not fun" at multiple points. The mountain lion made short, aggressive rushes toward him on several occasions before retreating a few steps. Burgess made himself appear larger, spoke loudly, and avoided turning his back or running—behaviors consistent with official mountain lion encounter protocols. The encounter lasted approximately six minutes and twenty seconds before being resolved when Burgess threw a rock toward the lion, which caused her to retreat and abandon the pursuit.

Darren DeBloois, game mammals program coordinator with the Utah Division of Wildlife Resources, was interviewed by Snopes and provided the key expert assessment: the mountain lion's behavioral pattern—specifically the combination of encountering cubs first and then being pursued persistently rather than ambushed—was consistent with a defensive, cub-protective motivation. DeBloois explained: "The fact that he ran into kittens first and was then pursued is considered a defensive action." He also noted that Burgess "appeared to do everything right" given the circumstances and observed that the lion "delivered a handful of bluff charges" rather than committing to a full predatory attack: "She knew that he knew that she was there and wasn't going to cross that line—but it could have happened. At any moment, she could have decided that this threat was big enough, and she could have done something more."

This careful circumscription is important: DeBloois did not declare the situation entirely safe or the lion's motivations entirely certain. Rather, the behavioral evidence strongly pointed toward defensive cub-guarding but left open the theoretical possibility that a defensive situation could have escalated into a predatory or fear-based attack.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Distinguishing Predatory Stalking from Maternal Defense in *Puma concolor* Behavior

The epistemic novelty in this case lies in the behavioral diagnostics that allow—imperfectly but meaningfully—the differentiation of predatory stalking from defensive maternal behavior in a large felid. This is not a trivial distinction, and it is not primarily a question of folklore or lay interpretation: it is a question in applied animal behavior with real policy and safety implications.

Mountain lions are obligate ambush predators. Their primary predatory strategy, documented across their range, is to approach prey—typically deer—from concealment, position themselves for a short explosive charge, and deliver a killing bite to the nape of the neck or throat. This is fully described in Logan and Sweanor (2001), *Desert Puma: Evolutionary Ecology and Conservation of an Enduring Carnivore* (Island Press), the most comprehensive ecological monograph on the species. The predatory stalk is characterized by: concealment, minimal vocalization, patient waiting for an opportunity, and a terminal high-speed charge of short duration. Critically, it is not characterized by prolonged, visible open-ground pursuit of aware, upright prey. Prey awareness disrupts the ambush advantage that is the foundation of the strategy.

Beier (1992), writing in *Wildlife Society Bulletin* (The Wildlife Society), analyzed 10 mountain lion attacks on humans in the United States and Canada and identified that nearly all predatory attacks involved prey-type behavior by victims: jogging, running, bending over, or being small (children). In no documented case in Beier's dataset did a mountain lion sustain a six-minute visible pursuit of an alert, standing, loud, rock-throwing adult human. This is not what predatory mountain lion behavior looks like in the scientific record.

By contrast, maternal defense behavior in felids—including both large and small cats—is characterized precisely by the pattern observed in the Burgess encounter: persistent, visible following at a distance compatible with monitoring the threat, bluff charges designed to increase the perceived threat to the intruder rather than to make contact, and graduation in response intensity based on the perceived responsiveness of the intruder. This behavioral pattern is consistent with a risk-cost tradeoff model in which a mother lion assesses that the intruder is too dangerous to attack directly but that abandoning the cubs' vicinity would expose them to predation risk by other animals (bobcats, coyotes, or another male lion, all of which prey on unguarded cubs).

The epistemic novelty for fact-checking purposes is that the behavioral diagnostics here—duration of pursuit, pattern of approach, visible vs. concealed strategy—allow a reasonably confident determination about motivation from video evidence alone, when combined with the spatial-temporal context of cubs being encountered first. This represents a case where biological behavioral analysis, applied carefully to documented footage, can resolve what might appear to be an ambiguous "expert opinions vary" situation into a well-supported evidential determination.

Morse et al. (2020), writing in *Human–Wildlife Interactions* (Jack H. Berryman Institute), documented that human perception of mountain lion encounters is systematically biased toward threat-inflation: people who have had encounters with mountain lions consistently report perceiving the animal as more predatorily motivated than expert post-hoc behavioral analysis suggests. This perception bias is relevant to the public interpretation of the Burgess video and helps explain why the "defensive behavior" interpretation, despite being supported by expert testimony, was received skeptically by many social media viewers who interpreted the persistent following as unambiguous predatory behavior.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Dapcevich's (2020) investigation effectively verified the core facts and secured strong expert commentary. Additional dimensions merit examination:

**Outcome for the cubs**: The investigation confirms that Burgess encountered cubs and that the presence of cubs explains the mother's behavior, but does not report whether any follow-up monitoring by the UDWR confirmed the cubs' survival or subsequent fate. Cougar cub mortality rates are high; cubs are vulnerable to infanticide by adult males, starvation if separated from their mother, and accidental death. Whether the October 10, 2020 encounter had any lasting effect on the family group is not documented.

**Regulatory aftermath**: Slate Canyon is a public wilderness area within the Wasatch Mountain range. The investigation does not address whether the UDWR posted any advisories or warnings following the incident, whether Burgess's encounter location was temporally or geographically communicated to other trail users, or whether any management response was triggered.

**The rock-throwing resolution**: Burgess ended the encounter by throwing a rock near the mountain lion. The effectiveness of this response is consistent with professional recommendations (the Mountain Lion Foundation recommends fighting back if attacked; throwing objects is generally supported as a deterrent). However, the investigation does not discuss the potential risk of throwing at an animal that is bluff-charging—in some documented cases, counter-aggression from humans has escalated mountain lion encounters. DeBloois's commentary does not explicitly evaluate this specific action.

**Mountain lion identification verification**: While DeBloois and Burgess both confirmed these were mountain lion (cougar) cubs rather than bobcat kittens, the footage showing the cubs is brief and the identification is based primarily on witness account rather than frame-by-frame biological verification. The cubs' images were not analyzed in detail in the fact-check. Adult pumas and adult bobcats are not easily confused, but cubs are smaller and more ambiguous.

**Broader safety communication gap**: The fact-check does not address the public guidance question of what a hiker should do upon encountering cougar cubs on a trail, separate from what to do during a direct cougar encounter. Current North American mountain lion safety literature (e.g., the Mountain Lion Foundation guidelines, USDA guidelines for recreation in lion habitat) focuses primarily on responding to an encounter already in progress. There is limited public guidance on what to do when you encounter cubs before the adult appears—back away slowly, do not approach, leave the area immediately—which is arguably the more time-sensitive decision point.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Mountain Lion Ecology, Conservation Status, and the Human Safety Intersection

Mountain lions (*Puma concolor*) have the largest natural range of any native terrestrial mammal in the Western Hemisphere, extending from the Canadian Yukon to Patagonia in South America. They are known by more than 40 names in English alone—cougar, puma, panther, catamount, painter—and occupy virtually every terrestrial habitat type within their range, from tropical rainforest to high-altitude alpine zones above 5,000 meters. In the United States, the species is found predominantly west of the Rocky Mountains, with a small isolated population (*P. c. coryi*, the Florida panther) clinging to survival in southern Florida and classified as Federally Endangered under the Endangered Species Act (U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, 2008).

Beier (1991), in *Journal of Mammalogy* (American Society of Mammalogists), provided foundational analysis of mountain lion habitat use relative to human residential areas and established that mountain lions are increasingly encountered in periurban settings as development expands into former wildland habitats and as lion populations recover following sport hunting restrictions in California (1972) and other western states. The California Department of Fish and Wildlife estimated in 2020 that the state hosts 4,000–6,000 mountain lions, a population that interacts regularly with tens of millions of recreationists.

The Cougar Fund (cited in the Snopes article) reports that North American cougars have killed 25 people since 1890. This extremely low number—relative to the scale of potential interactions, given the vast overlap of human recreational activity with mountain lion habitat—reflects precisely the species' natural avoidance behavior. Mountain lions are not inherently dangerous to humans, but the combination of human encroachment into lion habitat, the attractiveness of domestic animals and deer in suburban settings, and occasional loss of fear conditioning in habituated individuals does produce genuine risk events. The Burgess encounter serves as a vivid illustration of how close, prolonged human-mountain lion interactions can be without resulting in injury when the human responds appropriately and the lion's motivation is defensive rather than predatory.

The Slate Canyon incident entered a canon of mountain lion encounter narratives that wildlife managers and safety educators use to illustrate correct response behavior. The behavior Burgess demonstrated—maintaining an upright stance, avoiding running, making noise, backing away slowly, and ultimately using a projectable object—is consistent with the most current North American recommendations (Halfpenny et al., 1991, *A Field Guide to Mammal Tracking in North America*, Johnson Books; Mountain Lion Foundation, 2019, *Safety in Mountain Lion Country*).

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: TRUE**

The viral video of a mountain lion following Utah hiker Kyle Burgess through Slate Canyon for more than six minutes in October 2020 is genuine. Expert analysis by UDWR game mammals program coordinator Darren DeBloois, combined with the spatial-temporal context of Burgess having encountered what were later confirmed to be mountain lion cubs immediately before the adult female appeared, strongly supports the interpretation that the lion's behavior was defensive and cub-protective rather than predatory in character. The distinguishing behavioral evidence includes: the duration and visibility of the pursuit (inconsistent with ambush predatory strategy), the pattern of bluff charges rather than committed attacks, and the triggering sequence (cub encounter first, adult appearance second). Burgess's behavior during the encounter—maintaining upright stance, avoiding flight, making noise, throwing an object—was consistent with expert recommendations and likely contributed to the encounter's safe resolution.

**EARC Classification: R (Reasoning — behavioral analysis required to distinguish predatory from defensive motivation)**

The analytical gap here is one of reasoning: the video is genuine and the facts of the encounter are not in dispute, but determining whether the mountain lion was in predatory or defensive mode requires behavioral analysis grounded in the scientific literature on *Puma concolor* predatory and maternal defense strategies. This reasoning is accessible to experts (wildlife biologists, UDWR staff) but not to lay viewers parsing the footage without specialized knowledge, explaining why the claim required fact-checking at all.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Outcome of the specific cubs encountered by Burgess (survival, subsequent monitoring)
- Whether UDWR issued formal management responses or trail advisories following the incident
- Biological verification of the cubs' species identification from video evidence independent of witness accounts
- Public safety guidance on behavior when encountering unaccompanied mountain lion cubs before adult appearance
