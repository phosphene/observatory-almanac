## §1 Claim and Verdict

Snopes rates this **LEGEND**—a category indicating that the story has no verifiable factual basis as an actual hiring practice of any named figure, but that it circulates widely, carries cultural meaning, and is understood by its tellers as illustrative of genuine wisdom about character evaluation. The specific claim that IBM, or Thomas Edison, or Henry Ford, or any other named individual made hiring decisions on the basis of food-salting behavior is unsupported by contemporaneous documentation, biography, or institutional record.

The legend is notable for two structural features that make it resilient in its circulation. First, it is protean: Snopes identified at least eight distinct figures and institutions to whom the story has been attributed, and the Snopes article notes that the earliest documented print reference is from 1977—making this legend's modern circulation at least five decades old, though the story is almost certainly older. Second, the story carries its own epistemological argument: it presents the rejection of a job candidate for pre-tasting salting as obviously wise, embedding its premise in the apparent common sense of the conclusion. To question the legend is, implicitly, to question whether character inferences drawn from small behaviors are valid—which is itself a contested empirical question with genuine scientific literature behind it.

The inversion variant documented by Snopes—where a job *applicant* rejects an interviewing company because the company's president salted food before tasting it—inverts the power relationship while preserving the same underlying logic about decision-making character. This structural flexibility suggests the core cultural content is the epistemological claim itself (drawing character inferences from trivial food behavior), not any particular individual's actual practice.

## §2 Historical and Factual Record

The documented print history of this legend begins with a December 1977 *New York Times* piece by Elizabeth Fowler ("Of Fish and Beef, Cabbages and Kings," December 2, 1977, p. D1), which is the earliest Snopes citation and features the inversion variant: a Chicago executive rejecting an employer because the company president salted his steak without tasting it. The article presents this as a real anecdote from a specific job candidate, though without sufficient detail for independent verification.

Subsequent print appearances documented by Snopes span the 1980s through the 2000s, with the story appearing in business advice books (McCormack, 1984, *What They Don't Teach You at Harvard Business School*, Bantam Books, p. 231), newspaper features on business dining etiquette (Bonnin, 1994, *South Bend Tribune*, May 23, p. D4; Holmes, 1997, *Florida Times-Union*, December 8; Seltz, 2000, *The Boston Globe*, March 12), and in the form of collected email testimonials from individuals who claim to have heard the story in bespoke professional contexts (e.g., "when I interviewed with IBM in the 1980s," "Edison died in 1931 so the IBM version couldn't be original").

The attribution roster is itself informative. Thomas Edison (d. 1931) was a historically real figure genuinely associated with unconventional hiring practices—he administered a famous written examination to job applicants at his Menlo Park laboratory, a fact that is well-documented and distinct from the salting legend. Edison's association with unusual tests makes him a natural magnet for additional stories about eccentric hiring behavior, whether true or fabricated. Henry Ford's inclusion reflects his status as a legendary figure in American industrial mythology, to whom many apocryphal characterization stories attach. Admiral Hyman Rickover (d. 1986) was genuinely famous for aggressive and unconventional personnel interviews in his nuclear submarine program—documented instances of his interview style include stacking chairs in a room to make interviewees uncomfortable, and "failing" chairs to see how candidates responded. The attribution to Rickover may therefore represent a partial conflation with his real (if exaggerated) reputation.

The earliest plausible origination for the specific food-salting framework may lie in traditional etiquette discourse rather than business hiring. Pre-tasting salting had long been considered a breach of formal dining protocol—it signals to the chef and host a preemptive judgment about the inadequacy of their preparation before the evidence has been sampled. In formal dining settings from at least the Victorian era onward, the etiquette literature (e.g., post-Civil War American etiquette manuals) treats this as a social failure analogous to other forms of premature or uninvited judgment. The business legend may have simply transplanted this etiquette concern into the hiring context, activating the same underlying logic in a new frame.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty and Scientific Context

The epistemic novelty of this entry is substantial and exists independent of the legend's factual status. The legend's underlying premise—that trivial, habitual behaviors under minimal self-monitoring conditions reliably reveal character traits relevant to professional judgment—is a genuine empirical question addressed by several bodies of research.

The most directly relevant framework is what psychologists term "thin-slice" accuracy: the degree to which brief windows of behavior enable reliable predictions about personality or behavioral tendencies. Ambady and Rosenthal (1992, *Psychological Bulletin*, Vol. 111, No. 2) established that brief behavioral samples—sometimes as short as 30 seconds—can predict meaningful outcomes (e.g., end-of-semester teacher effectiveness ratings from brief video clips of classroom behavior). Their work inspired a large literature on when and how thin-slice judgments are accurate.

However, the salting legend invokes a more specific claim: not that thin-slice behavioral observation is generally valid, but that a specific habitual behavior (pre-tasting salting) reliably indicates a specific cognitive tendency (making decisions without gathering evidence). This is a much more constrained and less empirically defensible claim. The behavioral act of salting food before tasting it is overdetermined as a social behavior—it reflects habit formed over years of personal sensory preference, familiarity with a specific food (knowing from experience that one prefers highly seasoned scrambled eggs, for example), cultural norms around food preparation, and individual variation in salt taste receptor density (Dias et al., 2013, *Chemical Senses*, Vol. 38, No. 2) that makes some people systematically under-season the same food that another person correctly anticipates will need salt.

There is no peer-reviewed behavioral science literature supporting the specific leap from "salts food before tasting" to "makes professional decisions without gathering adequate evidence." The two behaviors involve entirely different cognitive systems (habitual sensory preference formation versus deliberative judgment under genuine uncertainty), and there is no principled psychological mechanism connecting them.

What does have empirical support is the general principle that interviewers make rapid character assessments from superficial behavioral cues, and that these assessments are often self-consistent (interviewers believe they are accurate) but tend to show poor predictive validity for actual job performance (Schmidt & Hunter, 1998, *Psychological Bulletin*, Vol. 124, No. 2, found structured interviews significantly outperform unstructured interviews precisely because unstructured interviews let interviewers rely on just such inferences). The salting legend thus describes a real psychological phenomenon—character inference from trivial behavior—while presenting it as reliable wisdom when the evidence suggests such inferences are often noise.

This is the true epistemic interest of the legend: it functions as folk validation of a cognitive process (character inference from behavioral observation) that people naturally perform and that feels accurate from the inside, but that may not reliably deliver the predictive accuracy that gives it its apparent legitimacy. The legend's persistence across eight decades is partly explained by the fact that every time a hiring manager acts on such an inference and the hire works out well, the inference feels confirmed—but the counterfactual (how many excellent candidates were passed over for salting their soup?) is invisible.

EARC rating: **C** (Contextually Contested)—the legend's factual status is settled (it describes no documented historical practice), but the epistemic content it embeds is a genuine and contested scientific question.

## §4 Psychological and Behavioral Mechanisms

The psychological content of this legend operates at two levels: the level of attribution theory (how people infer stable traits from single behaviors) and the level of implicit theory about self-monitoring and social performance.

Attribution theory, as developed by Fritz Heider (1958, *The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations*, Wiley) and extended by Lee Ross's concept of the "fundamental attribution error" (Ross, 1977, in *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, Vol. 10), predicts that observers systematically overweight dispositional factors (character, personality) and underweight situational factors (habit, cultural norms, sensory biology) when interpreting behavior. Pre-tasting salting, viewed through this lens, gets interpreted as evidence of a disposition ("makes decisions without evidence") rather than a situational habit ("this person typically oversalts food and has learned through experience to correct for restaurant under-seasoning").

The implicit theory about self-monitoring is equally interesting. The legendary tycoon's hiring logic implicitly assumes that the dining context is a *test situation* that the candidate should recognize and in which they should modulate their behavior accordingly. From this perspective, the candidate who fails to self-monitor—who behaves habitually rather than self-consciously—is revealing that they do not perceive or respond to situational testing cues. This connects to Snyder's (1987, *Public Appearances, Private Realities: The Psychology of Self-Monitoring*, W.H. Freeman) framework of high versus low self-monitoring individuals. High self-monitors adjust their behavior based on situational cues; low self-monitors behave more consistently across contexts. The legend implicitly values high self-monitoring—the ability to perform appropriately in a formal assessment context.

However, the value of high self-monitoring in professional performance contexts is itself contested. Kilduff and Day (1994, *Academy of Management Journal*, Vol. 37, No. 4) found that high self-monitors were more likely to achieve early career advancement, but also found that the adaptive behavior of high self-monitors could be perceived as inconsistency or opportunism by colleagues. For positions requiring intellectual integrity and independent judgment—the kind of senior executive role most versions of the legend describe—a candidate who self-monitors so carefully as to notice and change a habitual dining behavior in the presence of a potential employer might reasonably be characterized as more socially anxious and more susceptible to social pressure, rather than more principled, than one who remains behaviorally consistent.

The inversion variant of the legend (the candidate who rejects the employer because the interviewer salted their food) adds a further layer: it treats the same behavior as evidence not of the candidate's inadequacy but of the employer's inadequacy. This structural dualism demonstrates that the behavioral signal is genuinely uninformative—it can be elaborated to support any conclusion about any party, which is characteristic of folk wisdom that feels resonant but lacks predictive specificity.

## §5 Etiquette, Folklore, and Institutional Mythology

The salting legend belongs to a well-identified genre in American business folklore: the "character test at the dinner table" story. McCormack's reference in *What They Don't Teach You at Harvard Business School* (1984) treated the salting story as one of several examples of how observant executives read character from behavioral minutiae in informal settings—a genre of wisdom literature that presents informal assessment as superior to, or at least complementary to, formal evaluation processes.

This genre serves a specific ideological function in business mythology: it legitimizes intuitive, informal, and relationship-based hiring over formalized, credentialed, and structured evaluation. If the truly excellent executive can be identified by watching how they handle a salt shaker at dinner, then formal resumes, academic credentials, and structured interview processes are revealed as insufficient or even misleading. The legendary tycoon's wisdom is precisely his ability to see through formal performance to authentic character.

Gary Alan Fine and Joel Best's analysis of legend dynamics (Best & Horiuchi, 1985, *Western Folklore*, Vol. 44, No. 3) notes that legends which attribute wisdom to prominent figures serve to validate not just the specific behavior described but the *type of reasoning* the figure exemplifies. Edison with his written exam, Rickover with his unstacked chair trick, the unnamed president with his salt shaker: each legend teaches that great executives assess through unconventional means, that conventional assessment is for ordinary people, and that superior judgment operates through channels inaccessible to HR departments.

The naming pattern—IBM, Henry Ford, Edison—reflects the cultural logic of legend attachment. Stories about informal character detection are most compelling when attributed to figures already coded as having superior judgment and unusual methods. A story about a mid-level regional manager who rejects a candidate for salting their food carries no cultural weight; the same story attributed to Edison or Ford acquires the authority of legendary wisdom. The legend's multi-attribution is therefore a sign of its cultural vitality rather than confusion about its origins: it is being continuously recycled through figures whose names amplify its core message.

## §6 Research Gaps and Verdict Summary

The genuine research gaps in this area are as follows. First, there is no systematic study of whether real hiring decisions based on informal behavioral observation at business meals produce outcomes distinguishable from hiring decisions made through structured interviews alone. While the broader literature firmly establishes that structured interviews outperform unstructured ones (Schmidt & Hunter, 1998; Wiesner & Cronshaw, 1988, *Journal of Occupational Psychology*, Vol. 61, No. 4), no study has specifically isolated dinner-table behavioral observation as a hiring criterion to test its predictive validity.

Second, the social dynamics of business meals as assessment contexts have received limited systematic ethnographic study relative to their cultural prominence in executive hiring folklore. How prevalent is the practice of using business meals primarily or explicitly as character assessment opportunities? Survey data on this question would be illuminating but does not appear to exist in published form.

Third, the cross-cultural variation in salt-before-tasting norms has not been systematically documented in relation to the business-hiring mythology. In culinary traditions that rely heavily on table condiments (many East Asian traditions, for example), pre-seasoning is neither aberrant nor discouraged; the entire etiquette of pre-seasoning varies substantially across national and regional cultures. The legend as circulated in the United States implicitly assumes American formal dining norms as universal, which is an ethnocentric framing that limits its applicability as hiring guidance even on its own terms.

**Verdict summary:** The claim that business tycoons historically practiced the "salted food test" as a literal hiring criterion is a **Legend** with no documentary support—it is a recurring folk narrative attached to successive cultural symbols of business acumen (Edison, Ford, IBM, etc.) without verifiable historical instantiation. The legend is analytically significant as a vehicle for anxieties about character inference, self-monitoring, and the limits of formal evaluation in hiring. The underlying psychological premise (that habitual behavior reveals stable character relevant to professional judgment) is a genuine empirical question that the available behavioral science literature does not support for this specific behavioral signal. EARC rating: **C** (Contextually Contested). Epistemic gap: **Medium**.
