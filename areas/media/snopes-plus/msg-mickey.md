# Coca-Cola and MSG


## 1. The Claim

The claim at issue is a forwarded warning message, transmitted primarily by email and later by messaging application, which has circulated in various forms since approximately September 2005. Its canonical version, reproduced in Snopes' archived example from November 2008, reads as follows:

> *"Coca-cola + Ajinomoto/monosodium glutamate = a medicine which will cause drowsiness and excitement in the victim. This mixture is poisonous if used too often on the victim. Please send this to all your female friends, sisters, & your loved ones and ask them to beware. DO NOT accept coca-cola or any other drinks from stranger / even if it is your friend that you are not very close with."*

The message frames this chemical claim as a documented and active street-crime tactic, invoking the high-profile 2003 rape and murder of Canny Ong in Kuala Lumpur, Malaysia, as purported evidence. The invocation of a real, widely-publicized crime lent the message a degree of urgency and credibility that generic warnings about drink-spiking would otherwise lack. The claim's core scientific proposition is that monosodium glutamate, when mixed into Coca-Cola, functions as a pharmacological agent capable of inducing "drowsiness and excitement"—a dual-state effect that the message characterizes as analogous to date-rape drug pharmacology.

The message specifies the brand name "Ajinomoto," which is both the name of a major Japanese chemical and food manufacturing company and the brand name for their MSG product—a specificity that made the warning feel grounded in commercial reality rather than abstract fear. The underlying scientific claim drew further surface plausibility from the well-known public discourse around MSG sensitivity, which ensured that many recipients had at minimum heard that MSG could cause physiological effects.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim is false in every substantive respect.

MSG is not an ingredient in Coca-Cola. The Coca-Cola Company has confirmed this directly in its official response to the MSG-related rumors: "MSG is a flavor enhancer used in many foods, but it is not an ingredient in Coca-Cola. There is no factual or scientific basis for concluding that Coca-Cola is an aphrodisiac — whether or not it is combined with MSG." The formulation of Coca-Cola, while occasionally litigated over its trade-secret "secret formula," has been extensively analyzed through industrial chemistry; no analysis has ever detected MSG as a component. Cola beverages do contain glutamate naturally occurring in caramel color and in the coca-leaf extract used in the formula, but these trace amounts are not pharmacologically significant and are present in scores of other common foods at higher concentrations.

MSG is not pharmacologically active as an aphrodisiac or as a reliable sedative at any dose achievable through dietary exposure. Monosodium glutamate is the sodium salt of glutamic acid, an amino acid that is one of the most abundant neurotransmitters in the mammalian central nervous system and a ubiquitous component of dietary protein. Its role as an excitatory neurotransmitter in the brain is fully mediated by the blood-brain barrier, which prevents dietary glutamate from significantly altering central nervous system glutamate levels following normal oral ingestion. The pharmacological literature consistently finds that dietary MSG does not produce psychoactive effects in healthy individuals at any realistic intake level (Walker & Lupien, 2000, *Journal of Nutrition*).

The concept of "MSG symptom complex"—formerly called "Chinese Restaurant Syndrome" in the medical literature, a term now recognized as ethnically loaded and scientifically dubious—describes a cluster of self-reported symptoms in individuals who believe they are MSG-sensitive. Controlled double-blind challenge studies have repeatedly failed to confirm a consistent causal relationship between MSG ingestion and adverse symptoms when subjects are unaware of whether they received MSG or a placebo (Geha et al., 2000, *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*). However, where sensitivity has been documented in susceptible individuals at high doses (approximately 3 grams or more, according to FDA characterization), the symptom profile includes headache, flushing, tingling, chest tightness, and occasionally drowsiness—not the specific pharmacological profile of a sedative-aphrodisiac capable of incapacitating a person for assault purposes.

Even setting aside the lack of pharmacological mechanism, the message's claim fails on practical grounds. The adverse symptom complex documented by the FDA requires large doses of MSG consumed by susceptible individuals. Adding MSG to a cola drink would require identifying a susceptible target in advance, administering several grams of MSG (a challenging amount to dissolve discretely in a carbonated beverage), and hoping that the target experiences drowsiness specifically rather than the much more commonly reported symptoms of headache or flushing—which would alert rather than incapacitate. An effective drug-spiking operation requires a reliably sedating compound at low doses: MSG appears in double-digit quantities of grams per day in normal diets across MSG-consuming populations worldwide without producing mass incapacitation.

Regarding the Canny Ong case: Ong, a 28-year-old Malaysian woman, was abducted from the car park of the Bangsar Shopping Centre in Kuala Lumpur on June 13, 2003, raped, and murdered. Her assailant, Ahmad Najib Aris, was apprehended and convicted. The case's evidence, trial records, and contemporaneous press coverage document an abduction conducted at knifepoint from a parking facility. There is no documented evidence from the investigation, prosecution, or trial that a spiked drink played any role in the crime. The inclusion of Ong's name in the warning message is factually inaccurate and instrumentalizes a real murder victim's tragedy to amplify a false scientific claim.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The anchoring function of real-world crime invocation**: The Canny Ong reference is the most decisive factor in the warning message's credibility architecture. Research on persuasion and narrative cognition indicates that anecdotal and specific case references dramatically increase belief in causal claims, even when the causal link is asserted rather than demonstrated (Green & Brock, 2000, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*). The specific naming of a victim, a location (Bangsar Shopping Centre, Kuala Lumpur), and a perpetrator shifted the message from speculation to apparent documentation. Readers unfamiliar with the actual details of the Ong case—most of the message's global audience—had no mechanism to verify the implicit claim that MSG was involved. The horror of the genuine crime created what may be called an "evidential halo": the reality of the murder was read as evidence for the biochemical claim, which was presented as a lesson from the murder.

**The MSG sensitivity heuristic**: By the mid-2000s, widespread discourse about MSG sensitivity—particularly in the broader context of "Chinese Restaurant Syndrome"—had established a rough popular consensus that MSG causes physiological reactions in some people. This diffuse prior belief did not specify what those reactions were or how they worked, leaving the content ambiguous enough to be recruited into almost any health claim about MSG. The warning message exploited this ambiguity: since "MSG causes effects in your body" was broadly accepted, the jump to "MSG causes the specific effects of drowsiness and excitement in a drug-spiking scenario" required only a modest cognitive leap for readers who lacked the specific pharmacological knowledge to evaluate the distinction.

**The epistemic novelty of the MSG-as-weapon framing**: The most underappreciated feature of this claim's durability is that it reframed a familiar, apparently safe food additive as a covert threat. This inversion—making the mundane dangerous—is a known feature of health anxiety misinformation. MSG had been a topic of contested safety discussion since the 1960s, when neurologist John Olney published research on excitotoxicity in neonatal mice (Olney, 1969, *Science*) that was widely misapplied in the popular press as evidence for human harm from dietary MSG. This research was conducted at doses far exceeding normal dietary exposure and in a non-human model; subsequent human research did not replicate its implications. Nevertheless, MSG entered public consciousness as a substance with physiological power. The 2005-era warning message effectively harvested that latent anxiety and redirected it toward a specific criminal application. This category of claim—"ordinary thing you are already suspicious of is also a covert weapon"—is structurally difficult to rebut because it reinforces rather than challenges existing risk perceptions.

**Gendered fear circuits in message forwarding**: The explicit targeting of the warning toward women, with forwarding instructions directed at "your female friends, sisters, & your loved ones," activates well-documented gender-specific risk communication pathways. Research on protective communication behavior demonstrates that messages framed as protecting women from sexual assault travel especially rapidly through female social networks, and that recipients of such messages are less likely to critically evaluate their factual basis than they would be for neutral informational claims (Bröder et al., 2017, *Psychonomic Bulletin & Review*). The emotional stakes suppress epistemic interrogation.


## 4. Empirical Record

The biochemistry of MSG has been extensively studied since Kikunae Ikeda's 1908 isolation and characterization of glutamic acid from seaweed stock (*Journal of the Chemical Society of Tokyo*, cited in Lindemann et al., 2002, *Physiology & Behavior*), which identified a fifth basic taste—now termed umami—distinct from sweet, sour, salty, and bitter.

The pharmacokinetics of oral MSG have been directly studied in human volunteers. Stegink and colleagues (1983, *Journal of Nutrition*) found that while free plasma glutamate does increase after oral MSG ingestion, the rise is modest and short-lived, and blood-brain barrier transport of glutamate is tightly regulated by saturable carrier proteins operating at a small fraction of maximum capacity under normal physiological conditions. Dietary glutamate does not meaningfully perturb central nervous system glutamate levels.

The double-blind challenge studies are particularly instructive. Geha et al. (2000, *Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology*) conducted what is regarded as the definitive placebo-controlled study of MSG sensitivity, challenging subjects who had reported MSG-related symptoms under blind conditions with MSG and placebo. They found that subjects did not reliably identify which capsule contained MSG based on symptom profile, and that no consistent symptom complex emerged from MSG challenge at doses up to 5 grams—far above what could be dissolved discretely in a carbonated beverage. Earlier work by Tarasoff and Kelly (1993, *Food and Chemical Toxicology*) reached similar conclusions.

The FDA's current classification of MSG as GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe) reflects the totality of this evidence. The agency requires MSG to be declared on food labels when added as an ingredient, but this declaration requirement was implemented to address consumer autonomy preferences rather than demonstrated safety risk.

For completeness: the genuinely effective substances used in documented drug-facilitated sexual assault—flunitrazepam (Rohypnol), gamma-hydroxybutyrate (GHB), ketamine, and high-dose benzodiazepines—operate via specific receptor mechanisms (GABA-A agonism, NMDA antagonism) at milligram doses and produce rapid, reliable sedation without requiring any secondary triggering mechanism. Their pharmacological profiles are entirely different from MSG's, and their effectiveness is not conditional on the victim's prior sensitivity.


## 5. Gaps and Open Questions

The scientific consensus on MSG safety is robust, but several adjacent questions remain contested or underexplored.

The long-term epidemiological effects of chronically high MSG consumption remain less thoroughly characterized than the acute exposure studies. Some population-level studies in Asian dietary contexts, where MSG consumption rates are substantially higher than in Western nations, have explored relationships between MSG intake and metabolic parameters including body weight (He et al., 2011, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*); these findings remain debated and have generated substantial methodological critique regarding confounding through dietary pattern and reverse causation. They have no bearing on acute pharmacological claims, but they illustrate that MSG's long-term dose-response relationship is not fully resolved.

The persistence of MSG health anxiety in Western public opinion, despite repeated scientific rebuttals, represents a gap in science communication research. The MSG-as-harmful narrative has proved unusually durable across decades and across demographic groups, which suggests that the debunking literature has not successfully engaged the emotional and heuristic roots of the belief. Understanding why MSG specifically became a focal point for food anxiety—compared to many other additives with comparable or better evidence of safety concerns—would require ethnographic and historical analysis that goes beyond existing fact-checking literature.

The specific cultural trajectory of the MSG-spiked-Coke myth in Southeast Asian digital spaces has not been systematically documented. The claim's circulation through regional messaging apps predated the deep-platform data access that would allow for transmission network analysis. Whether similar structural myths about drink-spiking with common household chemicals persist in analogous forms in other dietary cultures remains an open empirical question.


## 6. The Takeaway

The claim that Coca-Cola mixed with MSG (Ajinomoto) functions as a pharmacological weapon capable of causing drowsiness and excitement in assault victims is false on every level at which it can be evaluated.

MSG is not present in Coca-Cola. Monosodium glutamate has no documented pharmacological activity as an aphrodisiac or reliable sedative at any dose achievable in a beverage. The adverse reactions documented in MSG-sensitive individuals require specific susceptibility and substantial doses, and they produce symptoms—headache, flushing, tingling—that would more likely alert a victim than incapacitate one. The Canny Ong case, invoked to anchor the warning in documented tragedy, involved no drink-spiking.

The message's longevity—two decades of uninterrupted forwarding across technological generations—reflects an architecture of persuasion that exploits real crime, pre-existing food anxieties, and gendered social networks to propagate a medical claim that no pharmacologist would take seriously. It is a study in how false health claims are amplified by the sincerity of those who share them.
