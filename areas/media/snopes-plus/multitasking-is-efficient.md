# Multitasking Makes You More Productive

## 1. The Claim

The modern knowledge worker wears multitasking as a badge of honor. Job listings demand candidates who "thrive in a fast-paced environment and can manage multiple priorities simultaneously." Productivity culture celebrates the person who fields emails during meetings, toggles between documents mid-sentence, and keeps six browser tabs actively in use. The underlying assumption is that handling several tasks at once is a sign of high cognitive capacity — and that it gets more done in less time.

This belief is not a fringe notion. It is baked into office design (open-plan layouts assume and enable constant interruption), management expectations, and the personal self-image of millions of professionals. When someone describes themselves as a "good multitasker," they mean it as a compliment. The implicit model is additive: if you can do Task A at rate X and Task B at rate Y, then doing A and B simultaneously gets you X + Y worth of output.

That model is almost entirely wrong.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

### Multitasking Is Mostly Task-Switching

For the vast majority of cognitive work, what humans call "multitasking" is not simultaneous processing. It is rapid sequential switching between tasks — and each switch carries a cost.

The foundational research comes from Joshua Rubinstein, David Meyer, and Jeffrey Evans at the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration and the University of Michigan. In a landmark 2001 paper in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance*, they demonstrated that switching between tasks requires two cognitive operations: a **goal shift** (deciding to do the new task) and a **rule activation** (loading the mental rules for the new task while suppressing the previous set). Each switch takes time and depletes cognitive resources. Across eight experiments, they found that mental task-switching costs amounted to **up to 40 percent loss in productive time** — not a trivial penalty, but a catastrophic one by workplace standards.

This effect is not limited to complex tasks. Even switching between simple operations — sorting numbers, categorizing words — produces measurable delays and error increases.

### Heavy Multitaskers Are Worse at Multitasking

Perhaps the most counterintuitive finding in this literature came from a 2009 Stanford study by Eyal Ophir, Clifford Nass, and Anthony Wagner, published in the *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*. The researchers divided participants into "heavy media multitaskers" (people who regularly consume multiple media streams simultaneously) and "light media multitaskers." They expected the heavy multitaskers to show some cognitive advantage — perhaps sharper attentional switching or better working memory. Instead, they found the reverse on every measure.

Heavy multitaskers were **worse at filtering irrelevant information**, more susceptible to distraction from stimuli that had nothing to do with the current task, and — paradoxically — **slower at switching between tasks** than light multitaskers. The people who multitask most are not better at it. They are, by measurable cognitive metrics, worse.

Nass summarized the finding with characteristic directness: "They're suckers for irrelevancy. Everything distracts them."

### Working Memory and Cognitive Load

The mechanism behind these effects is well-understood. Human working memory — the mental workspace where active information is held and manipulated — has a hard capacity limit. Psychologist George Miller's 1956 estimate of 7 ± 2 "chunks" has been revised downward by more recent research; Nelson Cowan's 2001 analysis suggests the true limit is closer to four chunks. When a person switches tasks, they must evict the current working memory state and load a new one. This process is not instantaneous. The technical term is "switch cost" or "set reconfiguration lag," and it is not eliminated by practice — only reduced slightly.

For tasks that require sustained attention, creative thinking, or complex reasoning, the switch cost is severe enough to disrupt the formation of deep work states (what psychologist Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi called "flow"). Frequent interruptions prevent the brain from reaching the mode of processing where its most sophisticated cognitive operations occur.

### Where Parallel Processing Actually Works

The picture is more nuanced for tasks that draw on different cognitive systems simultaneously. A skilled driver can hold a conversation while navigating familiar roads — the driving has been sufficiently automatized that it requires minimal working memory. A musician can play a learned piece while discussing technique with a student. Walking while talking is trivially achievable for most adults.

These examples share a key feature: one task is **automatized** (processed without conscious attentional resources) while the other is novel or deliberate. True dual-task performance is possible when the tasks do not compete for the same cognitive bottlenecks. This is meaningfully different from, say, drafting an email while listening to a conference call — both tasks demand the same language-processing resources and compete directly.

## 3. Why People Believe This

### The Subjective Feel Is Real

The myth persists partly because the subjective experience of multitasking does feel like productivity. When you toggle rapidly between email, a spreadsheet, and a chat window, you are generating activity — responses, edits, reactions. The behavioral output is visible and immediate. The cognitive costs — the shallow processing of each task, the errors that require later correction, the difficulty re-entering a complex task after an interruption — are less visible and often delayed.

Psychologists call this the **busyness heuristic**: we tend to equate the feeling of being busy with the feeling of being productive. Multitasking generates busyness. It does not generate proportional output.

### Interruption Culture Normalizes It

Modern communication technology has restructured the workplace around constant interruption. Email, Slack, phone notifications, meeting invites — the average knowledge worker now faces dozens to hundreds of interruptions per day. A 2005 study by Gloria Mark at the University of California, Irvine found that workers in an interrupted environment spent only 11 minutes on any single task before being derailed, and that after an interruption it took an average of 25 minutes to fully return to the original task. Later research by Mark and colleagues found the average time on a single screen before switching had dropped to 47 seconds by the mid-2010s.

In an environment structured around constant interruption, multitasking is not a skill — it is survival behavior. Workers adapt by lowering their expectations for focused output. Over time, the degraded cognitive state becomes the baseline, and the norms of "good multitasking" follow.

### Gender and Multitasking Mythology

The claim that women are better multitaskers than men has circulated widely enough to warrant mention. Research does not support a meaningful gender difference in multitasking performance. A 2019 study published in *PLOS ONE* tested 120 participants on a multitasking paradigm and found no significant sex-based advantage. The stereotype likely persists because women have historically been expected to manage multiple simultaneous domestic and professional responsibilities — the cultural narrative was mapped onto a presumed biological trait that was never well-evidenced.

## 4. Verdict

**The claim is false as usually stated, with a meaningful caveat.**

Multitasking in the sense of simultaneously performing multiple tasks that require conscious attention does not increase efficiency. It reduces it, by a margin large enough — up to 40 percent lost productive time by Rubinstein et al.'s measure — to matter significantly in any serious work context. The people who multitask most heavily are not better at it; they are measurably worse.

The caveat: genuine parallel processing is possible when one task is automatized and does not compete with the other for cognitive resources. The line between valid dual-tasking and inefficient task-switching is real and important, but most of what office culture calls "multitasking" falls on the wrong side of it.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### What Deep Work Research Shows

Cal Newport's popularization of "deep work" — extended, uninterrupted focus on cognitively demanding tasks — has a solid empirical grounding even if some of his specific claims outpace the evidence. The underlying research consensus is clear: the cognitive states most associated with high-quality output (problem-solving, creative synthesis, complex analysis) require sustained attention that multitasking systematically prevents.

Csikszentmihalyi's "flow" research, spanning decades and cultures, found that subjective wellbeing and performance both peak during states of uninterrupted, challenging engagement. Task-switching is structurally incompatible with flow states.

### Attention as a Finite Resource

Roy Baumeister's ego depletion research, while contested in its specific formulation, points to a broader phenomenon: cognitive resources are finite within a given time window. Whether one conceptualizes attentional capacity through working memory limits, prefrontal metabolic constraints, or attentional blink phenomena, the converging finding is that human attention is not a free good that can be multiplied by division. Treating it as if it were is a category error that modern work culture makes at scale.

### Structural vs. Individual Solutions

Much productivity advice frames multitasking as an individual habit to correct — "put your phone away," "use the Pomodoro technique," "block notifications." This framing is not wrong, but it misses the structural dimension. Organizations that normalize constant interruption, reward visible busyness over deep output, and design open-plan offices to maximize informal communication are engineering multitasking into their workflows regardless of individual preference.

The research implication is structural: protecting time blocks, reducing communication overhead, and designing work around periods of sustained focus — not just as a personal hack, but as an organizational policy — is what the evidence points toward.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The multitasking claim occupies a slightly awkward position for traditional fact-checkers. It is not a specific empirical claim that can be rated true or false in a single sentence — it is a culturally embedded assumption that research has systematically eroded. As a result, it tends to appear in fact-checking ecosystems as debunkery in science journalism (pieces covering the Stanford or Michigan studies) rather than as a rated claim on Snopes or PolitiFact.

**Snopes status:** Not addressed as a formal claim. The relevant research findings have been widely covered by science journalists, but the claim itself does not map neatly onto Snopes' political misinformation focus.

**Science journalism coverage:** The Rubinstein et al. and Ophir et al. findings have been extensively popularized in publications including *Scientific American*, *The New Yorker*, *NPR*, and *The Atlantic*. The translation to mainstream audiences has been mostly accurate, though sometimes stripped of nuance about when parallel processing is genuinely feasible.

**The complexity challenge:** Fact-checkers are most effective at evaluating specific, falsifiable claims. "Multitasking is efficient" is a diffuse cultural belief, not a discrete factual statement. The mismatch between the claim's form and the fact-checking format means it rarely gets the rigorous treatment the underlying research warrants.

**What responsible coverage looks like:** The most accurate coverage of this topic specifies what kind of tasks are being discussed (attention-demanding vs. automatized), distinguishes task-switching from genuine parallel processing, reports the actual effect sizes (not just direction), and notes that the 40-percent productivity loss figure is a ceiling estimate under controlled experimental conditions — real-world impacts vary but are reliably negative.

The bottom line is clear enough to state simply: for the cognitive work that dominates modern professional life, multitasking does not make you more productive. It makes you less productive, and it feels like the opposite.
