# "Humans Use More Muscles to Frown Than to Smile"


## 1. The Claim

The widespread assertion that "it takes more muscles to frown than to smile" appears in countless motivational posters, self-help books, and casual conversations about the benefits of positive thinking. The most commonly cited version claims that frowning requires 43 muscles while smiling uses only 17, though variations range from "it takes 62 muscles to frown but only 26 to smile" to "frowning uses 72 muscles compared to 14 for smiling." This claim is invariably presented as established anatomical fact supporting the conclusion that smiling is "easier" and therefore more natural or beneficial than frowning.

The claim's appeal lies in its apparent scientific precision—specific muscle counts suggest rigorous measurement—combined with its motivational message that happiness requires less effort than sadness. It's become a staple of positive psychology discourse, workplace wellness programs, and parenting advice, often accompanied by encouragements to "choose the easier path" of smiling.

The earliest traceable appearances of specific muscle counts in print date to the mid-twentieth century, though none cite a primary anatomical source. The claim gained particular momentum in the 1970s and 1980s alongside the rise of pop psychology and corporate motivational culture. By the time the internet accelerated its circulation, the numbers had become so firmly embedded in popular consciousness that they were routinely attributed to unnamed "scientists" or "researchers at a university"—the hallmark attribution pattern of a factoid that has detached from any original source.


## 2. What's Actually True

The fundamental problem with this claim is not that it's false, but that it's meaninglessly imprecise. Human facial anatomy does involve approximately 43 muscles total (though some anatomists count as few as 20 paired muscles, or as many as 50+ when subdivisions are included), but the claim conflates several distinct anatomical and definitional issues:

**Definitional Problems:**
- What constitutes a "smile"? A polite social smile involves different muscles than a genuine Duchenne smile. Guillaume Duchenne de Boulogne's pioneering electrical stimulation experiments in the 1860s demonstrated that authentic enjoyment smiles recruit the orbicularis oculi in ways that voluntary social smiles do not—a distinction that renders any single "muscle count for smiling" inherently misleading.
- What defines a "frown"? Clinical depression expressions differ from momentary disapproval, and the corrugator-dominated "brow frown" is anatomically distinct from the depressor-dominated "mouth frown." These are different motor programs recruiting different muscle populations.
- How do we count muscle involvement? Full contraction versus partial activation versus stabilization all yield different numbers. Electromyographic studies routinely show low-level activation in muscles not considered "primary movers" for a given expression.

**Anatomical Variability:**
The Facial Action Coding System (FACS), developed by Paul Ekman and Wallace Friesen in 1978 and revised in 2002, identifies 46 distinct Action Units (AUs) corresponding to individual muscle movements or combinations. FACS provides the most rigorous available taxonomy of facial movement, but it deliberately avoids mapping AUs to simple muscle counts because the relationship between muscles and visible movement is not one-to-one. A single muscle can produce multiple AUs depending on contraction intensity and co-activation patterns.

A genuine Duchenne smile primarily involves:
- Zygomatic major (AU12—raises corners of mouth)
- Orbicularis oculi, pars lateralis (AU6—creates crow's feet around eyes)
- Supporting muscles for facial stability, including buccinator and risorius in many individuals

A basic frown typically engages:
- Corrugator supercilii (AU4—pulls eyebrows together and downward)
- Depressor anguli oris (AU15—pulls mouth corners down)
- Procerus (AU9—wrinkles nose bridge)
- Depressor labii inferioris, mentalis, and additional stabilizing muscles
- Orbicularis oris and platysma may activate depending on frown intensity

**The Counting Problem:**
Different anatomical authorities provide wildly different muscle counts because they're measuring different things. Some count only prime movers, others include stabilizing muscles and synergists. Some consider muscle groups as units, others count individual muscle bellies. The "43 vs. 17" ratio appears in no peer-reviewed anatomical literature and likely originated from motivational rather than medical sources. David H. Song, a plastic surgeon at the University of Chicago Medical Center, investigated the claim and concluded that a genuine smile likely requires about 12 muscles while a frown requires about 11—almost the exact opposite of the popular claim's implied ratio. His analysis, widely cited in debunking contexts, highlights how the motivational version inverts or fabricates the actual anatomy.


The epistemological status of conclusions drawn from this claim reveals deeper issues about how scientific-sounding information circulates in popular culture. The claim's trajectory from unknown origin to widespread acceptance illustrates several concerning patterns:

**Authority Transfer:** The claim gains credibility through repetition rather than verification. Each repetition in a new context—a doctor's office poster, a corporate wellness newsletter, a children's science book—adds a thin layer of implied endorsement. After sufficient accumulation, the claim feels verified even though no single repetition involved actual verification.

**Scientific Laundering:** Pseudo-anatomical language makes motivational advice appear medically validated. The specific muscle counts transform a subjective preference ("smiling is nice") into an apparent empirical discovery ("science shows smiling is more efficient"). This laundering process strips the claim of its actual epistemic status—opinion—and replaces it with a false one—established fact.

**Resistance to Correction:** Once established in popular consciousness, false precision becomes harder to dislodge than obvious misinformation. Correcting "frowning uses 43 muscles" requires explaining why the question itself is malformed—a more demanding cognitive task than simply replacing one number with another. Research on the "continued influence effect" (Johnson & Seifert, 1994) demonstrates that corrections are less effective when the original misinformation provided a satisfying causal explanation, as this claim does.

The motivational conclusion—that choosing positive expressions benefits wellbeing—has legitimate psychological support through separate research pathways, but this specific anatomical justification undermines rather than strengthens the case for positive psychology interventions. When a valid conclusion rests on fabricated evidence, correcting the evidence risks discrediting the conclusion—a phenomenon known as the "backfire vulnerability" of poorly sourced claims.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemological foundation of this claim reveals how folk wisdom adopts scientific language to gain credibility. The claim exhibits several characteristic patterns of pseudoscientific thinking:

**False Precision:** Specific numbers (43, 17) suggest measurement when none occurred. This is a well-documented rhetorical strategy—quantitative claims carry implicit authority because they suggest a methodological backstory. The listener assumes someone must have counted, and the specificity of the numbers discourages questioning.

**Category Error:** The claim conflates muscle count with effort, energy expenditure, or difficulty. Even if frowning did engage more muscles, this would not mean it requires more "work" in any physiological sense. Muscle recruitment depends on contraction intensity, duration, and neural efficiency, not simply the number of muscles activated. A heavy deadlift engages fewer distinct muscles than a complex yoga pose, but few would argue the yoga pose requires more "effort."

**Motivational Reasoning:** The claim works backward from a desired conclusion (smiling is better, therefore it must be easier) to supporting "evidence." This is a textbook case of confirmation bias operating at the cultural level—the factoid persists because it confirms what people want to believe about the relationship between effort and emotional valence.

**Scientific Standards:**
Legitimate facial expression research relies on:
- Electromyography (EMG) to measure actual muscle activation patterns and timing
- FACS coding for systematic, reproducible expression analysis
- Controlled studies measuring energy expenditure, neural activation, or metabolic cost
- High-speed video analysis for temporal dynamics of expression formation
- Peer-reviewed publication with reproducible methods and transparent data

None of these standards apply to the popular muscle-counting claim. The numbers exist in a methodological vacuum—asserted without measurement protocol, sample size, or replication.


## 4. Verdict

**ENHANCE:** This claim represents distorted-but-grounded information that requires enhancement rather than simple debunking.

The anatomical assertion about specific muscle counts (43 vs. 17, or any of its variants) is **fabricated**—no credible anatomical source provides these figures. The numbers appear to have been invented to support a motivational message rather than derived from empirical measurement. The one serious investigation of the underlying question (Song's analysis) suggests that smiles and frowns engage roughly comparable numbers of muscles, with neither dramatically outnumbering the other.

However, the underlying territory contains legitimate scientific insights:
- Facial expressions do involve complex, coordinated muscle activity measurable by EMG and coded by FACS
- Different expressions engage different muscle groups and different neural pathways
- There may be meaningful differences in neural and muscular effort between expressions, though these differences are not captured by simple muscle counts
- Facial expression patterns can influence emotional states through the facial feedback mechanism, even if the effect is smaller than early studies suggested

**Enhanced Understanding:**
Rather than simply declaring the claim false, a complete understanding recognizes that:

1. **The specific numbers are fabricated**, but facial expressions do involve measurable muscular differences that legitimate researchers have spent decades characterizing
2. **The motivational conclusion is oversimplified**, but facial expression research does support connections between expression and wellbeing through mechanisms unrelated to muscle counts
3. **The claim reflects genuine curiosity** about the relationship between anatomy and emotion, even though it provides incorrect information
4. **The "effort" framing is a category error**—but the question of whether positive and negative expressions differ in their neural or metabolic costs remains a legitimate research question that has not been definitively answered

**Recommendations:**
- Replace the fabricated muscle counts with accurate information about facial expression anatomy, citing FACS as the standard taxonomy
- Acknowledge legitimate research on facial feedback and emotional contagion without overstating effect sizes
- Recognize cultural and individual variation in expression patterns and norms
- Separate anatomical facts from motivational interpretation—the case for positive emotional expression stands on its own psychological evidence and does not need fabricated anatomy

The claim's persistence demonstrates how scientific-sounding misinformation can gain credibility through repetition, while its motivational appeal reveals genuine human interest in understanding the connections between body and mind. Enhancement involves preserving the legitimate curiosity while correcting the fabricated details and pointing toward the real science that the factoid obscures.

This case illustrates why the EARC framework is particularly valuable for claims that mix authentic insights with manufactured precision. Simple fact-checking might dismiss the entire claim, losing the opportunity to engage with the legitimate scientific questions it raises, while uncritical acceptance perpetuates misinformation that undermines public understanding of both anatomy and psychology. The enhanced version replaces a comforting fiction with a more interesting truth: that the relationship between facial expression and emotional experience is real, complex, and far more fascinating than any muscle count could capture.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Understanding this claim requires examining the broader context of facial expression research, motivational psychology, and the commodification of scientific-sounding advice.

**Legitimate Facial Expression Science:**
Real research in this field has produced a rich and nuanced literature. Key findings include:
- Emotional contagion and facial mimicry: Ulf Dimberg's EMG studies (2000, 2002) showed that people unconsciously mimic observed facial expressions within 500 milliseconds, and that this mimicry can influence emotional experience.
- The facial feedback hypothesis: Originally formulated by Silvan Tomkins and elaborated by Carroll Izard, this hypothesis proposes that facial expressions can modulate emotional states. Fritz Strack's famous 1988 pen-in-mouth study appeared to confirm this, but a high-powered replication by Wagenmakers et al. (2016) involving 17 laboratories failed to reproduce the effect under controlled conditions. Subsequent work by Coles et al. (2022) in a meta-analysis of 138 studies found a small but statistically reliable facial feedback effect, suggesting the phenomenon is real but more modest than initially claimed.
- Paula Niedenthal's embodied simulation framework proposes that facial mimicry is integral to emotion recognition—we understand others' emotions partly by simulating their expressions on our own faces. This work connects facial muscle activity to social cognition rather than simple energy expenditure.
- Cultural variations in expression interpretation have been documented extensively, challenging the universality assumptions underlying the claim.

**The Motivational Industrial Complex:**
The muscle claim exemplifies how the self-help industry appropriates scientific language to sell simple solutions to complex problems. The pattern includes:
- Citing specific numbers without sources
- Mixing legitimate concepts with fabricated details
- Presenting correlation as causation
- Ignoring individual and cultural variation
- Packaging complex findings as actionable one-liners

This phenomenon extends well beyond facial expression claims. Similar fact-free factoids—"we only use 10% of our brains," "you swallow eight spiders per year in your sleep," "body language is 93% of communication"—follow identical circulation patterns: invented specificity, motivational framing, and institutional repetition without verification.

**Cultural Context of Expression:**
Cross-cultural research reveals that expression norms vary dramatically. Masaki Yuki's work (2007) demonstrated that Japanese observers weight the eyes more heavily in reading emotional expressions, while Americans weight the mouth—a finding that challenges the assumption that smiling is universally "readable" in the same way. What Americans interpret as a friendly smile might be seen as aggressive, nervous, or inappropriate in other cultural contexts. The assumption that smiling is universally "easier" or more natural reflects Western, particularly American, biases about emotional display rules.

**Anatomical Accuracy:**
Modern facial anatomy research using techniques like high-resolution MRI, diffusion tensor imaging, and 3D computational modeling has produced detailed maps of facial muscle interaction. This work reveals that:
- Expression involves coordinated muscle groups operating as functional synergies, not isolated contractions
- Individual anatomical variation is significant—some people lack certain facial muscles (notably the risorius, absent in approximately 20% of the population)
- Expression dynamics change with age, health, neurological status, and lifelong expression habits
- Energy expenditure depends more on expression intensity, duration, and individual neuromuscular efficiency than on the number of muscles nominally involved


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed this specific anatomical claim in their fact-checking database. The claim appears to fall into a category of widely circulated "facts" that combine pseudo-scientific precision with motivational messaging—a type of content that often escapes systematic fact-checking because it seems both harmless and intuitively appealing. The absence of Snopes coverage may reflect the claim's ubiquity and apparently benign nature, despite its questionable factual foundation.

This gap in coverage is itself instructive. Fact-checking organizations necessarily triage, prioritizing claims with political consequences, public health implications, or viral momentum around contested events. A feel-good anatomical factoid—even one that is demonstrably wrong—rarely rises to the urgency threshold. Yet its very persistence and apparent harmlessness make it a useful case study in how misinformation circulates when no institutional actor is motivated to challenge it. The claim has effectively been grandfathered into popular knowledge through decades of unchecked repetition.
