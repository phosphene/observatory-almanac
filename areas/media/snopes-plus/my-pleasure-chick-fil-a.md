# Does Saying 'My Pleasure' at Chick-fil-A Earn Free Food?

## 1. The Claim

On December 2, 2020, TikTok creator Jordan Lewis—known on the platform as @driftyjayy—posted a video that rapidly became one of the most viewed pieces of fast-food content of the holiday season. The video showed Lewis pulling through a Chick-fil-A drive-thru, ordering a meal with four sauces. When asked by the drive-thru voice whether that would be all, Lewis responded "Yes, thank you." After a brief pause, Lewis preemptively said "my pleasure"—before the Chick-fil-A employee had a chance to say it—and was then told his total was $0.

The video purported to reveal a secret Chick-fil-A hack: by saying "my pleasure" before the employee could, you would, through some unspecified mechanism of policy or tradition, receive your meal for free. The video accumulated more than 3.8 million views in less than a week, generating enormous discussion. Twitter threads debated whether the hack was real. Users shared screenshots of earlier tweets suggesting similar claims had circulated for years. Some claimed the payoff was specifically a free ice cream cone rather than a free meal. The phrase "my pleasure" had been a documented feature of Chick-fil-A's customer service script for decades, and its distinctive cultural presence made it a natural candidate for the kind of elaborate lore-building that attaches to beloved consumer brands.

The claim is rated False. The TikTok video was a prank.

## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes Senior Reporter Jordan Liles investigated the claim and published his findings on December 7, 2020. The investigation confirmed several things while refuting the central claim.

**The TikTok was a prank.** Jordan Lewis's @driftyjayy account was devoted to pranks generally—a review of his other content confirmed that the drive-thru hack video fit a consistent comedic pattern on his channel. Liles noted that Lewis appeared to potentially be performing both voices in the exchange—the customer's and the drive-thru employee's—and that the "your total is $0" response does not represent anything resembling actual Chick-fil-A policy. No Chick-fil-A company representative confirmed the existence of any such policy. No independent users were able to successfully replicate the result. The viral video was created and designed to be entertaining, not to document a real discount mechanism.

**No "my pleasure" free food policy exists.** The widespread belief—circulating in variant forms for years before the December 2020 TikTok—that saying "my pleasure" at Chick-fil-A earns customers a discount, free ice cream, or a complimentary item has no factual basis. Multiple independent social media users over the years had attempted variations of the hack: saying "my pleasure" before the employee, saying it like a code word at the beginning of an order, saying it at the drive-thru and at the counter. None of these approaches was confirmed to produce a reward by any credible independent report. Chick-fil-A's actual discount and reward programs operate through its One loyalty app and through traditional promotional mechanisms—not through verbal exchange rituals.

**The ice cream variant is also false.** A separate but related rumor held specifically that saying "my pleasure" earned a free Icedream Cone or Cookie Icedream Sandwich. This variant drew on the genuine existence of Chick-fil-A's "Cell Phone Coop" program—a 2016 initiative where families who dined in and placed all their phones into a special box ("the coop") during the meal would receive a free ice cream cone if no family member checked their phone. This program is real, documented on Chick-fil-A's company blog, and earned participants an actual ice cream reward. But it involved abstaining from phone use for the duration of the meal, not saying a specific phrase. The "my pleasure = free ice cream" claim appears to be a distorted derivative of the Cell Phone Coop concept, detached from its actual mechanism.

**"My pleasure" has a genuine and documented origin at Chick-fil-A.** The phrase is not arbitrary or decorative. Its use at Chick-fil-A traces directly to founder S. Truett Cathy, who encountered the phrase when a hotel employee responded to his thanks with "My pleasure" during a visit to a luxury hotel. The interaction stayed with Cathy; the employee's response struck him as more genuinely expressive than "you're welcome," which he felt was "an empty statement." He subsequently asked his restaurant owners, team members, and corporate staff to adopt the phrase. As Cathy is quoted on the Chick-fil-A company blog: "You expect that from a five-star hotel. But to have teenagers in a fast-food atmosphere saying it's their pleasure to serve—that's a real head-turner."

## 3. Epistemic Novelty: Lore-Formation Around Service Rituals

The "my pleasure" free food myth belongs to a specific subspecies of consumer mythology: the secret code hypothesis. In this genre, a distinctive verbal pattern associated with a consumer brand is hypothesized to function as an unlock mechanism—producing rewards, discounts, or access unavailable to those who do not know the phrase. The structure of these myths is consistent across many examples: a brand has a distinctive verbal marker; the marker has some emotional salience; a reward structure is hypothetically attached to the marker through social transmission.

The cognitive mechanism underlying the myth's plausibility is worth examining. "My pleasure" is distinctive at Chick-fil-A specifically because it departs from the industry-default "you're welcome." This departure is branded, intentional, and repeated consistently across thousands of employees at thousands of locations—one of the most successful examples of language policy in fast-food customer service. Because the phrase is salient (it stands out), and because it is associated with a brand that has a strong reputation for exceptional customer service and employee management, it acquires the kind of cultural weight that generates lore. The reasoning implicit in the myth runs something like: "This phrase exists because Chick-fil-A cares deeply about hospitality; Chick-fil-A uses it to reward customers with a great experience; perhaps there is a further, more tangible reward tied to the phrase that operates as an insider secret."

Cultural folklorist Jan Harold Brunvand's framework for understanding contemporary legend formation is applicable here (Brunvand, 1981, *The Vanishing Hitchhiker: American Urban Legends and Their Meanings*, W.W. Norton). Brunvand argued that urban legends typically attach to sites of cultural anxiety or fascination—they encode concerns or aspirations that the community finds meaningful. The Chick-fil-A "my pleasure" legend encodes a specifically American aspiration: the idea that knowing the right words, being in on the right cultural knowledge, can unlock rewards that the unknowing majority does not access. This is a form of cultural capital myth—the belief that social and brand literacy can be converted into material benefit.

The myth's persistence across years, in multiple variant forms, without ever being verified by independent replication, is itself epistemically instructive. Effective myths propagate forward on the basis of their structure and emotional resonance rather than their evidentiary basis. Each attempt to "try the hack" that fails produces a private disconfirmation that does not enter the publicly visible information environment; successful replication (impossible here, since the policy doesn't exist) would have. The asymmetry of confirmation bias in social sharing means that the myth can sustain indefinitely even in the face of universal private disconfirmation.

## 4. Context: S. Truett Cathy and the Cultural DNA of "My Pleasure"

S. Truett Cathy founded Chick-fil-A in 1946 with an original restaurant called The Dwarf Grill in Hapeville, Georgia. He remained personally involved in the company's culture and philosophy until his death in 2014, and his influence on the chain's customer service ethos is so thoroughly documented that it amounts to a corporate founding mythology in the most literal sense—a story of origin that actively shapes present practice.

The "my pleasure" story reflects a specific strand of Cathy's philosophy: that language is hospitality. His framework was explicitly differentiated from industry norms. The prevailing customer service language at quick-service restaurants—"you're welcome," "no problem," "sure thing"—was, in Cathy's assessment, communicatively empty. These phrases acknowledge the transaction without conveying genuine regard. "My pleasure" reframes the transaction: rather than accepting thanks, the employee is asserting that the interaction itself was a source of satisfaction. This is a subtle but psychologically distinct posture, and Cathy was apparently acutely aware of the distinction.

The WANE 15 News interview with Chick-fil-A franchise owner Jeff Hoffman (May 14, 2018) articulated this philosophy explicitly: "Mr. Cathy said if you can only say three words or two words to somebody today, and you said the words 'you're welcome,' that's an empty statement. What does that even mean? What is that? But if you say 'it's my pleasure,' it's from your heart and genuine, that's a really meaningful thing. You leave that positive impression with the guest."

This philosophical framing has had measurable cultural impact. "My pleasure" at Chick-fil-A has become so associated with the brand that the phrase alone functions as a cultural shorthand for the chain—something demonstrated by the enormous secondary culture of memes, parody videos, unofficial rap songs, and TikTok content that the phrase has generated. Chick-fil-A's customer service ratings have consistently ranked among the highest in the quick-service restaurant sector; American Customer Satisfaction Index data from multiple years across the last decade has placed Chick-fil-A either at or near the top of its category. The degree to which "my pleasure" contributes to this perception versus other service factors is not quantifiable, but the phrase is documented as a differentiator that customers notice and discuss.

The phrase has also generated linguistic commentary outside fast-food contexts. Language bloggers and commentators have noted that "my pleasure" is socially dominant in the upper-service-tier environments typically rather than in mass-market service—hotels, fine dining, concierge service—and that Cathy's strategic deployment of it at a mass-market chain was a deliberate class-boundary crossing: importing a linguistic marker of elite hospitality into a democratic consumer context. This framing gives "my pleasure" an aspirational quality that "you're welcome" lacks, which is precisely what Cathy intended and what has made the phrase sticky enough to generate folklore.

## 5. The TikTok Prank Industry and Manufactured Virality

The specific mechanism by which @driftyjayy's video achieved its 3.8 million views represents a distinct form of manufactured virality that TikTok's algorithmic infrastructure has made notably efficient. The video exploited a structural feature of the platform: the short-form video format prioritizes immediate engagement signals (likes, comments, shares, watch time through completion) over accuracy signals. A video that generates debate—is this real?—produces dramatically higher engagement than a video that clearly resolves its own claim. The "my pleasure" hack video was epistemically ambiguous for a casual viewer because the drive-thru audio was consistent with what a real exchange might sound like, the creator's demeanor was confident rather than openly joking, and the claimed reward (total = $0) was specific enough to seem like a real policy detail.

This exploited what might be called a verification-plausibility asymmetry: verifying the claim requires contacting Chick-fil-A, reviewing its actual policies, or attempting the hack and failing—none of which happens before sharing. Believing the claim is plausible enough to share requires only that one accepts the video's surface presentation, which carries apparent evidential weight (you can see the drive-thru, hear the exchange, observe the outcome claimed). In a media environment where verification follows engagement rather than preceding it, plausible pranks can achieve extraordinary scale before the corrective truth has any opportunity to slow them.

Liles noted that @driftyjayy's channel featured multiple prank videos with similar structures, suggesting the creator had developed competence in manufacturing this kind of viral engagement. The specific prank—a fast-food "life hack"—was optimized for a category of content that had proven reliably shareable: consumer insider knowledge claims. The audience for such content over-indexes for younger demographics who are both active social media users and regular quick-service restaurant customers. The video succeeded not because it was credible to careful analysis but because it was credible enough to be worth sharing before analysis.

The longer-term consequence of prank virality in this genre is a kind of epistemic fatigue: future genuine life hacks, loyalty program tips, or consumer information about Chick-fil-A are harder to evaluate credibly because the information environment has been seeded with plausible-seeming fabrications. This is not unique to Chick-fil-A; the same dynamic has emerged across virtually every major consumer brand with a strong social media presence. Consumer protection researchers and marketing scholars have begun to characterize this as a form of "brand misinformation ecosystem"—the accumulation of false consumer-facing claims about a brand that distorts the information environment even for users who are actively trying to make accurate decisions (Swire-Thompson & Lazer, 2020, *Annual Review of Public Health*, "Public Health and Online Misinformation: Challenges and Recommendations"). The principles apply transposably to brand-context misinformation.

## 6. Verdict and Implications

The claim is False. Saying "my pleasure" before a Chick-fil-A employee does not result in free food, a $0 total, or any other discount. This policy has never existed. The December 2020 TikTok video that catalyzed the latest wave of the myth was an acknowledged prank. The broader mythology—including the free ice cream cone variant—is unsupported by any credible evidence and has been consistently contradicted by independent attempts at replication.

The genuine history of "my pleasure" at Chick-fil-A is itself worth knowing: it is a deliberate, philosophically grounded language policy implemented by the company's founder with the specific intention of conveying authentic hospitality rather than transactional acknowledgment. The phrase's cultural distinctiveness—the very feature that makes it a candidate for legend formation—is the result of exactly this intentional policy, not of any embedded reward mechanism.

What the "my pleasure" myth reveals most acutely is the longing that attaches to brands perceived as exceptional: people who value Chick-fil-A's service culture want to believe there is a further depth of excellence to discover, a reward structure only accessible to the knowing. That this perfectly structures a recurring prank format capable of reaching millions in days is a feature of the contemporary information environment that neither Chick-fil-A's customer service team nor S. Truett Cathy's philosophy was designed to address.
