# Myths About Masturbation Side Effects Spreading Online vs. What Science Says

## 1. The Claim

A persistent web of claims holds that masturbation produces serious physical and mental side effects. These claims circulate across multiple distinct cultural strata simultaneously: in social media wellness communities, on self-improvement forums, in religious abstinence advocacy materials, in the "NoFap" and "semen retention" movements that gained significant online traction during the 2010s, and in peer-forwarded health misinformation that resists clear ideological categorization.

The specific claimed side effects vary by source and tradition, but the most common include: hair loss and male-pattern baldness (attributed to dihydrotestosterone or testosterone depletion); acne and worsened skin conditions; impaired vision or eye weakness; significant testosterone reduction leading to fatigue and muscle weakness; testicular atrophy from overuse; memory loss and cognitive impairment; depression and anxiety; "post-nut clarity" as evidence of neurochemical depletion; sexual dysfunction and erectile problems from "desensitization"; and what is framed as behavioral addiction entailing compulsive escalation and withdrawal symptoms comparable to substance dependency.

The online ecology around these claims is elaborate. Forum communities with hundreds of thousands of members document personal experiences of alleged harm, track "streaks" of abstinence, and report subjective improvements attributed to the cessation of masturbation. These communities have produced their own quasi-scientific vocabularies — "flatline" for libido suppression during abstinence periods, "dopamine receptors" invoked as explanatory mechanism, "superpowers" for the alleged cognitive and physical enhancements of long abstinence streaks. The claims are not easily dismissed as fringe; they recur in mainstream wellness spaces, fitness communities, and occasionally in popular media coverage of male health trends.

This article examines what the scientific literature actually says about the physiological and psychological effects of masturbation, evaluates the specific claims circulating online, and analyzes why the myths remain so culturally durable.

## 2. What's Actually True

The scientific literature on masturbation is extensive and methodologically sophisticated enough to draw clear conclusions about the major claimed harms. The short summary: essentially none of the commonly circulated negative claims are supported by the evidence. Masturbation is physiologically normal human sexual behavior that occurs across the lifespan in the vast majority of people, and the research consistently fails to find causal links to the claimed harms.

**Hair Loss:** The claim that masturbation causes hair loss typically invokes one of two mechanisms: testosterone depletion (a misconception about the physiology of ejaculation) or dihydrotestosterone (DHT) increase. The DHT version is more sophisticated and circulates in fitness communities aware of basic endocrinology. DHT is indeed linked to androgenetic alopecia (male-pattern baldness) in genetically susceptible individuals, and DHT is produced from testosterone via the enzyme 5-alpha reductase. However, research by Schlechte, Sherman, & Martin (1985, Fertility and Sterility, 44(2), 191–196) and later studies confirm that ejaculation has no meaningful impact on systemic DHT levels or testosterone concentrations that would accelerate hair loss. Testosterone levels fluctuate in response to sexual arousal and recover rapidly; there is no cumulative depletion mechanism with repeated ejaculation. A study by Kraemer et al. (1976, Archives of Sexual Behavior, 5(2), 125–132) measured testosterone levels in men following orgasm and found transient changes within normal physiological variation, followed by rapid return to baseline, with no evidence of sustained hormonal disruption.

**Testosterone and Muscle Weakness:** The testosterone depletion model underlies multiple claimed effects. The physiological reality is that spermatogenesis is metabolically cheap relative to total body resources; the nutritional cost of ejaculation is trivial, and testosterone is not "used up" in the process of sexual response. A study by Exton et al. (2001, European Journal of Endocrinology, 144(5), 503–508) examined testosterone, prolactin, and luteinizing hormone levels before and after orgasm and found short-term elevation in prolactin coupled with transient testosterone changes, all reverting to baseline within minutes to hours. Critically, a study by Jiang et al. (2003, Journal of Zhejiang University Science, 4(2), 236–240) found that testosterone levels were actually elevated after 7 days of sexual abstinence, suggesting that short-term abstinence may slightly increase circulating testosterone — but the effect was transient and disappeared at longer abstinence durations. This finding has been widely cited in NoFap communities as evidence that abstinence preserves testosterone, but the study's own data showed the elevation was temporary and returned to baseline by day 10.

**Vision and Cognitive Impairment:** Claims about vision loss or eye weakness from masturbation are among the oldest in the popular mythology, traceable to Victorian-era medical literature. There is no physiological mechanism by which masturbation or ejaculation would affect visual acuity or ocular health, and no peer-reviewed study in the modern literature has found such a relationship. Similarly, claims about memory loss or cognitive impairment have no evidentiary support. Research on sexual activity and cognitive function tends to find neutral or positive associations. A study by Fracher & Kimmel (within the broader literature on sexual behavior and aging, reviewed in: Kimmel, 1974, Social Psychology Quarterly, 37(2), 101–114) found no cognitive impairment associated with sexual frequency. More recent neuroimaging research, including work by Kühn & Gallinat (2014, JAMA Psychiatry, 71(7), 827–834), examined brain structure in relation to pornography use but found no evidence of the "desensitization" or cognitive damage claimed by online communities.

**Acne:** Acne is driven primarily by sebum overproduction, colonization with Cutibacterium acnes (formerly Propionibacterium acnes), and inflammatory response in the pilosebaceous unit. The primary hormonal driver is androgens, particularly during puberty. Research by Lucky et al. (1994, Archives of Dermatology, 130(3), 308–312) and subsequent dermatological literature has identified genetic predisposition, diet, stress, and androgen sensitivity as primary drivers of acne. There is no published research establishing masturbation frequency as a predictor of acne severity. The temporal coincidence of increased masturbation frequency during puberty with acne onset likely drives the folk association, but this is confounding by a third variable (androgens) rather than causal relationship.

**Addiction:** The question of masturbation or pornography addiction is more complex and contested. While the popular online framing treats addiction as easily established, the clinical and research picture is substantially more nuanced. The American Psychological Association's DSM-5 does not include masturbation addiction or pornography addiction as a recognized diagnosis. The World Health Organization's ICD-11 added "compulsive sexual behavior disorder" as a recognized condition, but its criteria require significant impairment and distress, and the category is deliberately designed to avoid pathologizing normative sexual behavior. Research by Prause, Steele, Staley, Sabatinelli, & Hajcak (2015, Biological Psychology, 109, 192–199) found that self-reported problematic pornography use was not predictive of attenuated P300 brain responses (a neurological marker of reward sensitivity degradation) — undermining the desensitization model. Research by Kohut & Fisher (2013, Archives of Sexual Behavior, 42(8), 1477–1489) found no evidence that pornography use causes sexual dysfunction in the general population. Studies examining whether masturbation meets behavioral addiction criteria consistently find that the compulsive-use pattern affects a small minority of users and is typically associated with pre-existing anxiety, depression, or obsessive-compulsive spectrum conditions rather than being a primary disorder caused by pornography or masturbation itself.

The overall picture from research reviewed by Coleman (2002, Archives of Sexual Behavior, 31(5), 377–393) and updated in subsequent reviews is that masturbation is physiologically benign for the vast majority of people, is associated with normal development across the lifespan, and carries no documented causal links to the physical harms commonly claimed.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The myths about masturbation represent a uniquely dense convergence of forces — historical, religious, psychological, social-technological — that operate synchronously to maintain false beliefs in the face of comprehensive contradicting evidence. Disentangling these forces illustrates exceptional clarity about how misinformation operates.

**The Victorian Medical Legacy.** The systematized pathologization of masturbation in Western medicine began in earnest with Samuel Auguste Tissot's 1760 treatise *L'Onanisme*, which collected and amplified existing folk beliefs about seminal loss into a medical doctrine. Tissot claimed that ejaculation cost the body the equivalent of significant blood loss and that masturbation was more harmful than coitus because it was not offset by the physiological benefits of a sexual partner. His work became enormously influential across European medicine and was cited approvingly for over a century. By the Victorian era, anti-masturbation medicine was a substantial institutional enterprise: John Harvey Kellogg (inventor of corn flakes) published extensively on the physical and mental harms of masturbation; the medical literature catalogued dozens of supposed symptoms; and interventions ranging from dietary regulation to surgical procedures were employed to prevent what physicians portrayed as a progressive, debilitating vice. This medical tradition — legitimate medical authority stating these harms as established fact — was absorbed into cultural consciousness and transmitted intergenerationally. The specific claims Tissot and his successors popularized (weakness, vision problems, mental deterioration) match almost exactly the claims circulating online today. The modern mythology is not newly invented; it is the fossil record of a discredited medical paradigm, preserved and retransmitted through cultural inheritance rather than through evidence.

The persistence of Victorian-era medical beliefs deep into the twenty-first century is partly explained by what we might call **cultural lag of medical folklore** — the phenomenon whereby health beliefs, once established as authoritative, continue to circulate and be reproduced long after the scientific consensus has moved on. Medical folklore is stickier than scientific knowledge for several reasons: it is transmitted through emotionally salient personal and family contexts (parental warnings, peer group norms), it is rarely directly contradicted by the same authorities who originally promulgated it, and it maps onto systems of moral significance that give it motivational reinforcement independent of its empirical status. The harm narrative of masturbation was attached to moral frameworks (self-indulgence as corrupting, semen as precious life force to be conserved) that predated Tissot and extend into multiple religious and traditional medicine systems globally. Scientific refutation of the physical harm claims does not automatically dislodge the moral framing that gave those claims their original urgency.

**The NoFap and Semen Retention Communities as Epistemic Environments.** The online communities that have developed around masturbation abstinence since approximately 2012 constitute a complex epistemic environment that repays careful analysis. These communities — centered on Reddit's r/NoFap (founded 2011), the broader "semen retention" discourse on various platforms, and adjacent men's self-improvement spaces — generate a continuous stream of first-person testimony about the effects of abstinence. This testimony follows a characteristic narrative arc: pre-abstinence life described in terms of lethargy, social anxiety, and physical malaise; a "flatline" period of reduced libido and depressed mood shortly after beginning abstinence; followed by reported "superpowers" including increased energy, social confidence, mental clarity, and physical improvements. The narrative structure is remarkably consistent across testimonials, which creates an apparent evidentiary consensus.

The consistency, however, is largely an artifact of the community's narrative norms rather than independent convergent evidence. Research on recovery communities and self-improvement forums consistently shows that testimonial norms — the expected story structure for accounts shared in the community — strongly shape what gets reported and how it is framed. Lichtenstein & Slovic (1973, Journal of Experimental Psychology, 101(1), 76–83) demonstrated that vivid anecdotal evidence displaces statistical reasoning in conditions of uncertainty; forum testimonials are optimized for vividness. The "superpowers" reported by long-term abstainers are physiologically implausible at the magnitude described and are consistent with the psychological effects of any committed self-improvement program — increased self-efficacy, more regular sleep, reduced compulsive behavior patterns — rather than specific consequences of semen retention. What the research on placebo effects and expectancy-driven behavioral change suggests is that committed belief in a behavioral intervention can produce real subjective changes through psychological mechanisms entirely independent of the proposed physiological mechanism. People who commit to NoFap often simultaneously change sleep habits, reduce pornography use, engage more socially, and increase physical activity — confounds that would predict positive subjective outcomes regardless of the masturbation frequency variable.

**Religious and Cultural Frameworks as Belief Anchors.** The belief that masturbation is harmful coexists with — and is partially sustained by — religious frameworks that treat it as morally impermissible. When a belief is anchored in a moral framework, it acquires motivational defenses against disconfirmation that purely empirical beliefs do not have. Research by Ditto & Lopez (1992, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 63(4), 568–584) on "motivated skepticism" demonstrated that people apply far more stringent evidentiary standards to claims that threaten desirable beliefs than to claims that support them. For people whose religious framework condemns masturbation as sinful, scientific evidence that it is physiologically benign is not merely a competing claim — it is a threat to a morally structured worldview, and it will be evaluated with proportionally greater scrutiny and resistance. This dynamic operates across multiple religious traditions simultaneously: conservative Christianity, Orthodox Judaism, some streams of Islam, and various Hindu ascetic traditions all have traditional teachings that assign negative spiritual or physical significance to ejaculation outside of procreative sex. The breadth of this cultural anchoring means that the harm beliefs are reproduced through independent channels and cannot be addressed by correcting any single community.

**The Confounds of Normal Adolescent Development.** Masturbation typically increases during puberty and young adulthood — the same period during which acne, social anxiety, occasional depression, hair loss (for genetically susceptible individuals), and physical development producing performance variation all naturally emerge. This temporal alignment creates a near-ideal environment for the perception of spurious correlations. Post hoc reasoning — "I started masturbating more and then my acne got worse, therefore masturbation caused the acne" — is cognitively natural. Disconfirming the correlation requires data the individual does not have: counterfactual trajectories, population comparisons, and control for confounding variables. The developmental context means that personal experience consistently confirms the harm narrative even when the underlying causal claim is entirely false, and this experiential pseudo-confirmation is more persuasive than abstract statistical evidence for most people.

## 4. Verdict

**False — Comprehensively and Consistently**

The overwhelming verdict of the scientific literature is that masturbation does not cause hair loss, acne, vision impairment, testosterone depletion, muscle weakness, testicular damage, memory loss, or cognitive impairment in normally healthy individuals. The claimed effects lack both biological plausibility (no known mechanism) and empirical support (no causal studies demonstrating the claimed effects under controlled conditions).

The behavioral addiction question is more nuanced: compulsive sexual behavior disorder is a recognized condition in the ICD-11, and some individuals do experience distressing, impaired-control sexual behavior patterns. However, this is distinct from the general claim that masturbation is addictive in the manner of a substance, and the evidence does not support the escalation-and-desensitization model widely promoted in online communities.

The most accurate summary of the scientific consensus is that masturbation is a normal component of human sexuality across the lifespan, carrying no documented causal risks for physical health and — in the context of moderate frequency and positive personal relationship to sexuality — is associated with neutral to positive wellbeing outcomes.

The false claims are not benign. Young people who absorb these myths may experience significant shame, anxiety, and impaired sexual development; may avoid seeking help for legitimate sexual health concerns; or may misattribute unrelated health issues to sexual behavior, delaying accurate diagnosis. Clinical research on sexual shame, reviewed by Tangney, Stuewig, & Mashek (2007, Annual Review of Psychology, 58, 345–372), documents its associations with depression, anxiety, and impaired intimate relationships.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The masturbation myth complex is a particularly instructive case in the sociology of health misinformation because it operates simultaneously at multiple scales: individual psychology, community epistemic norms, cultural transmission, and institutional legacy.

At the individual level, the myths exploit several well-documented cognitive vulnerabilities: temporal contiguity producing false causal attribution, availability heuristics favoring vivid testimonial over statistical base rates, and motivated reasoning protecting beliefs anchored in moral or religious frameworks. These are general features of human cognition, not specific failures of education or intelligence, which is why these beliefs persist across highly educated populations and in communities that pride themselves on rationalist self-examination.

At the community level, the NoFap and related forums function as **epistemic bubbles** in the sense described by Nguyen (2020, Mind, 129(516), 141–168): communities in which the testimony and norms of fellow members systematically amplify certain types of evidence (personal testimonials matching the expected narrative) while filtering out disconfirming information (scientific literature, experiences that don't fit the arc). This is not a new development — social support groups and religious communities have always had selective epistemic properties — but online forums drastically scaled both the reach and the self-reinforcing efficiency of these dynamics. Reddit's r/NoFap had over a million active members at peak; similar communities on other platforms show comparable engagement. The scale means that for many young men, the community epistemic environment provides more apparent evidence for the harm hypothesis than the scientific literature ever will — simply because testimonial content production massively outpaces peer-reviewed publication at the individual consumer level.

The public health implications are real in both directions. On one side: shame-driven avoidance of legitimate sexual health care, misattribution of unrelated symptoms, and the psychological costs of unnecessary guilt and anxiety imposed on normative behavior. On the other side, and less frequently discussed: the legitimate concern that compulsive pornography use and masturbation patterns that do cause distress or relationship harm in some individuals can be dismissed or poorly served by overcorrection against the myths. The claim that masturbation is uniformly harmless in all contexts and quantities is also an overstatement; the same behavior that is neutral for most people can be genuinely problematic for a minority of individuals with compulsive sexual behavior disorder. Scientific communication about sexual health faces the challenge of simultaneously correcting false harm narratives without producing a parallel false harmlessness narrative.

The history of masturbation medicine illustrates how decisively expert consensus can be wrong in ways that cause population-scale harm. The Victorian medical establishment's anti-masturbation doctrine was not fringe pseudoscience — it was published in leading journals, endorsed by prominent physicians, institutionalized in medical education, and translated into interventions ranging from dietary restriction to surgery. The fact that this consensus was completely wrong, and that its errors were sustained by the same institutional authority structures that today produce the corrective consensus, should inform how we understand medical authority. The lesson is not that medical consensus should be distrusted — the modern consensus is far better supported empirically than the Victorian one — but that medical consensus must be evaluated through its relationship to evidence rather than accepted categorically on the basis of institutional status. This is a harder epistemic position to communicate than simple trust or simple distrust, but it is the position that the history of medicine actually supports.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Masturbation myths represent an unusual challenge for mainstream fact-checking organizations because the topic's social sensitivity has historically made it underserved by professional fact-checking infrastructure. Most major fact-checking outlets — PolitiFact, FactCheck.org, the Associated Press Fact Check — focus on political and public health claims where clear institutional sources are available. Sexual health misinformation exists in a space that is simultaneously under-resourced and under-covered.

Snopes has addressed specific masturbation myths episodically — for example, fact-checking the claim that masturbation causes blindness (rated False) — but has not produced a comprehensive treatment of the full myth complex. Health journalism outlets like Healthline, Verywell Health, and similar consumer health platforms have produced more complete myth-busting content, typically organized around lists of specific claims with brief evidence summaries. These are generally accurate but lack depth and citation density, relying on statements of consensus rather than engagement with the primary literature.

The online community context creates an unusual fact-checking challenge: the communities generating these claims are self-aware about the scientific consensus against them and have developed explicit frameworks for dismissing it. Common dismissal strategies include: arguing that the scientific consensus is suppressed by the "mainstream" for cultural or commercial reasons; arguing that self-reported experience is more reliable than controlled research because researchers don't understand the specific community's practices; and arguing that the absence of the specific claimed benefits in research means research is measuring the wrong variables. These meta-level arguments against the authority of research are not straightforwardly answerable by presenting more research. Effective fact-checking of these claims requires engaging with the reasoning about evidence rather than simply presenting more evidence.

The most productive approach for communicating accurate information about masturbation and health involves: clearly separating the empirical claims (for which evidence-based evaluation applies) from the values claims (for which different frameworks apply); acknowledging the real but uncommon phenomenon of compulsive sexual behavior while distinguishing it clearly from normative behavior; engaging with the specific mechanisms proposed in online communities rather than dismissing them with authority appeals; and addressing the historical context that makes distrust of medical reassurance on this specific topic somewhat understandable given the medical establishment's historical role in creating the harm narrative.

Public health communicators who have studied effective sexual health messaging — including Braun-Harvey & Vigorito (2015, Treating Out of Control Sexual Behavior: Rethinking Sex Addiction, Springer) — emphasize that shame-reducing, non-judgmental communication frameworks consistently outperform authoritative-corrective approaches in changing health attitudes around sexuality. The most effective myth-busting in this space is less about marshaling evidence and more about creating the psychological conditions under which people are willing to evaluate evidence they had previously found threatening.
