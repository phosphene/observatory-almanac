# Do "Naked" Juices and Smoothies Contain Formaldehyde?


## 1. The Claim

On 14 March 2017, a Facebook user published a post titled "Friends don't let friends drink formaldehyde," which accumulated over 150,000 shares before the Snopes fact-check was published. The post referenced a class action lawsuit against PepsiCo's "Naked" brand of juices and smoothies — a real lawsuit that had resulted in a $9 million settlement — as implied evidence that Naked products contained the toxic preservative formaldehyde. The claim read as a consumer safety warning leveraging the credibility of litigation to assert a health danger.

The specific formulation most commonly encountered stated that Naked products contained "calcium pantothenate (synthetically produced from formaldehyde)" and used this characterization to argue that Naked products were formaldehyde-containing and therefore toxic or deceptive. The connection drawn between the lawsuit, the settlement, and the formaldehyde assertion was presented as though the lawsuit concerned dangerous ingredients; in fact, it concerned deceptive labeling practices wholly unrelated to formaldehyde or ingredient safety.

This claim is false in its core assertion and structurally misleading in its invocation of legal context. Calcium pantothenate is present in Naked products. Formaldehyde is used in one step of its industrial synthesis. Calcium pantothenate is not formaldehyde, does not contain residual formaldehyde, and has none of formaldehyde's biological properties. The claim rests on a logic error — assuming that if chemical A is used to synthesize chemical B, then B "contains" or "is derived from" A in a sense that carries A's properties — that is foundational to chemophobic reasoning and that no basic chemistry supports.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The lawsuits were real but concerned something entirely different.** PepsiCo settled a class action lawsuit in July 2013 for $9 million concerning its Naked brand. The lawsuit, filed in 2012 in the Central District of California, alleged that Naked products were falsely marketed as "All Natural," "100% Juice," "All Natural Fruit," "From Concentrate," and "non-GMO," when in fact they contained synthetic non-juice ingredients including Fibersol®-2 (a soluble corn fiber), fructooligosaccharides, calcium pantothenate, and other added vitamins, and when some ingredients were derived from genetically modified organisms. PepsiCo agreed to the settlement while denying the allegations and agreed to redesign product labels.

A second, similar lawsuit was settled in February 2017, concerning claims that labeling terms such as "no sugar added" and "only the best ingredients" were misleading to consumers. The Center for Science in the Public Interest (CSPI) argued that phrases like "only the best ingredients" and "just the healthiest fruits and vegetables" led consumers to believe the products were healthier than they actually were. The complaint pointed to Naked's Pomegranate Blueberry juice — labeled "no sugar added" (technically accurate) but containing very high sugar content relative to other beverages.

Neither lawsuit alleged that Naked products contained dangerous or harmful ingredients. Neither mentioned formaldehyde. The lawsuits were about semantic disputes over what "all natural" and "non-GMO" mean in a regulatory context, and about whether certain marketing phrases were misleading. They have nothing to say about the safety of any ingredient in the product.

**Calcium pantothenate is vitamin B5.** Calcium pantothenate is the calcium salt form of pantothenic acid, also known as vitamin B5 — a water-soluble essential vitamin ubiquitous in plant and animal tissues. As described by the National Cancer Institute Thesaurus (Code C74545), calcium pantothenate is "a component of coenzyme A (CoA) and a part of the vitamin B2 complex" that is "essential for various metabolic functions, including the metabolism of carbohydrates, proteins, and fatty acids" and "involved in the synthesis of cholesterol, lipids, neurotransmitters, steroid hormones, and hemoglobin." Pantothenic acid deficiency, while rare, causes a diffuse failure of metabolic function sometimes called "burning feet syndrome."

Calcium pantothenate is on the FDA's GRAS list (21 CFR 184.1212) and is permitted in a wide range of food products. It is also a standard component of multivitamin supplements taken by tens of millions of people daily. The FDA's approved uses include use as a nutrient supplement in infant formulas — a context that would be inconceivable if there were any legitimate concern about the substance's safety.

**Formaldehyde is used in synthesis but is not present in the product.** Synthetic calcium pantothenate is manufactured through a multi-step chemical process. The conventional synthesis involves reactions yielding racemic pantolactone from isobutyraldehyde, formaldehyde, and cyanide (as described by Shimizu and Kataoka, 2002, "Pantothenic Acid and Related Compounds," *Encyclopedia of Bioprocess Technology*, Wiley, 15 October 2002). The pantolactone is then optically resolved, condensed with beta-alanine, and isolated as the calcium salt. Formaldehyde participates in the formation of pantolactone in an intermediate step but is entirely consumed in that reaction; it does not persist as a residue in the final product.

This is basic synthetic chemistry: starting materials are transformed by chemical reactions into products that are chemically distinct from the starting materials. Formaldehyde (CH₂O) is a single-carbon aldehyde. Calcium pantothenate is a complex organic molecule with the molecular formula C₁₈H₃₂CaN₂O₁₀ and a molecular weight of 476.53 g/mol. They are different molecules with different structures, different biological activities, and different toxicological profiles.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes fact-check correctly identifies the core error: "the fact that a chemical is used to synthesize another chemical does not mean that the new chemical carries the same risks as the old chemical." This is accurate as far as it goes, but the claim is worth examining at a deeper chemical and epistemological level because the "derived from" fallacy is not simply a careless mistake — it is a durable rhetorical structure with significant persuasive force precisely because it exploits a gap in most people's mental model of chemistry.

**The ubiquity of formaldehyde as a metabolic intermediate.** What the fact-check and almost all popular coverage of this issue misses is that formaldehyde is not merely present in the industrial synthesis of vitamin B5 — it is produced endogenously by every human body as a normal metabolic byproduct and is a fundamental intermediate in one-carbon metabolism. The human body produces approximately 1.5 mg of formaldehyde per kilogram of body weight per day through normal metabolic processes including the oxidation of methanol (a trace product of fruit pectin fermentation), the demethylation of various methylated compounds, and oxidative folate metabolism (Heck et al., 1990, "Formaldehyde Concentrations in the Blood of Rhesus Monkeys After Inhalation Exposure," *Fundamental and Applied Toxicology*, 14(4), 858–867; Dhareshwar & Stella, 2008, "Your Prodrug Releases Formaldehyde: Should You Be Concerned? No!," *Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences*, 97(10), 4184–4193).

For a 70 kg adult, this represents roughly 105 mg of formaldehyde produced daily by the body. This formaldehyde is rapidly converted by formaldehyde dehydrogenase (FALDH) to formate and then to single-carbon units used in biosynthesis. The system is efficient enough that circulating blood formaldehyde concentrations in healthy humans are approximately 0.1 mM — a steady-state concentration maintained by the balance between production and oxidative elimination.

This means that anyone who has consumed a Naked juice has also, simultaneously, been producing formaldehyde internally in quantities that dwarf any conceivable trace formaldehyde from calcium pantothenate synthesis. The framing of calcium pantothenate as a "formaldehyde-containing" ingredient would, if consistently applied, make every metabolizing human being a formaldehyde-containing organism — which is simply a description of endogenous biochemistry.

**The "derived from" fallacy and its recursive structure.** The reasoning "compound B was derived from compound A, therefore B is (in some toxicologically relevant sense) A" is false in a way that is worth making explicit. By the same logic, one could note that many common pharmaceuticals — including aspirin (derived from salicylic acid, which can be obtained through the oxidation of phenol, which is toxic), acetaminophen (whose synthesis involves various toxic intermediates), and even water (which can be synthesized by combustion of hydrogen, which is explosive) — "contain" or are "derived from" substances that would horrify a consumer if presented without chemical context. The "derived from" framing systematically ignores the fact that chemical synthesis transforms starting materials, and that systematic transformation is precisely what makes chemistry useful.

Dhareshwar and Stella (2008), in a paper explicitly addressing this reasoning pattern in pharmaceutical risk assessment, document that multiple widely used pro-drugs release formaldehyde during metabolism; the paper's conclusion — that this does not constitute a clinically significant hazard — is based on the same endogenous-formaldehyde argument, namely that the quantities involved are trivial relative to basal metabolic production and elimination capacity.

**The lawsuit laundering mechanism.** The specific rhetorical power of the original Facebook post rested not just on the chemistry but on the litigation invocation. By mentioning the $9 million settlement in proximity to the formaldehyde claim, the post implied a connection that does not exist: that the lawsuit was, in some way, about formaldehyde contamination, or that regulatory and legal scrutiny of Naked products had revealed safety issues rather than labeling issues. This is a form of what might be called *lawsuit laundering* — using the prestige of legal proceedings to certify claims that those proceedings do not address and never made.

The labeling lawsuits against Naked were legitimate consumer protection actions addressing a genuine question about whether terms like "all natural" and "non-GMO" were accurately applied to products containing synthetic vitamin supplements. Those questions have real merit and were resolved through settlement. They say nothing whatsoever about whether Naked juices are safe to drink or whether their ingredients present health risks. Conflating the two categories — semantic/labeling disputes and safety/toxicology claims — is the rhetorical maneuver that gave the post its share-generating credibility.

**The asymmetry of chemophobic content spread.** Research on the spread of health misinformation on social media documents a consistent asymmetry: fear-generating health content spreads faster, travels farther, and resists correction more effectively than accurate health information (Lazer et al., 2018, "The Science of Fake News," *Science*, 359(6380), 1094–1096). The "friends don't let friends drink formaldehyde" framing was specifically engineered for this environment — the title activated protective social concern, the mention of litigation provided false credibility, and the chemistry was too technical for most readers to evaluate independently. The correction — that calcium pantothenate is not formaldehyde — is accurate but has none of these rhetorical features.


## 4. Formaldehyde: The Actual Toxicology

Formaldehyde is a genuine toxic substance at sufficient exposures, and understanding what "sufficient" means clarifies why the Naked claim is frivolous. The toxicological literature on formaldehyde is extensive, motivated primarily by occupational exposure risks (formaldehyde is widely used in manufacturing, woodworking, embalming, and laboratory settings) and by concerns about indoor air quality in buildings where formaldehyde-releasing resins are used.

Formaldehyde is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) based on sufficient evidence of nasopharyngeal cancer and leukemia in humans — but this classification is based on chronic high-level inhalation exposure in occupational settings. The IARC determination applies to workers in formaldehyde-manufacturing and formaldehyde-resin industries with long-term exposure to vapor concentrations orders of magnitude higher than any food-consumption exposure scenario.

At dietary exposure levels — including the endogenous 105 mg/day produced by a typical adult — formaldehyde does not accumulate. It is metabolized by alcohol dehydrogenase and formaldehyde dehydrogenase in a rapid enzymatic cascade. The short biological half-life of formaldehyde (approximately 1–2 minutes in circulating blood) means that even if trace formaldehyde were somehow present in calcium pantothenate (which it is not), the physiological consequence would be indistinguishable from the effect of endogenous metabolic production.

Calcium pantothenate, by contrast, has an excellent safety profile. FDA GRAS status places no upper limit for its use as a nutrient supplement, and pantothenic acid itself is consumed without adverse effects in supplement doses of 5–10 mg/day (the adequate intake level) up to pharmaceutical doses of 1,000–2,000 mg/day studied in clinical trials without significant adverse findings (Institute of Medicine, 1998, *Dietary Reference Intakes for Thiamin, Riboflavin, Niacin, Vitamin B6, Folate, Vitamin B12, Pantothenic Acid, Biotin, and Choline*, National Academy Press).


## 5. The Labeling Controversy in Context

While the formaldehyde claim is straightforwardly false, the broader context of the Naked labeling lawsuits touches on genuine and unresolved questions about food marketing that deserve treatment on their own terms.

The "All Natural" problem in U.S. food labeling is real. The FDA has historically lacked a formal regulatory definition of "natural" for food labeling purposes, creating a gap that allows manufacturers considerable latitude in applying the term. A 2015 request for public comment by the FDA received over 7,500 responses, reflecting significant consumer and industry concern about what the term should mean. Multiple class action lawsuits — against Naked and numerous other brands — have sought to establish through litigation what regulation has not established through rulemaking: that "natural" implies the absence of synthetic ingredients, GMO-derived content, or processing aids.

The Naked settlement reached in 2013 did not resolve the definitional question; it settled PepsiCo's liability for a period of prior sales while changing label language going forward. The 2017 CSPI settlement addressed different labeling terms with similar imprecision. These settlements reflect the genuine complexity of marketing claims about nutritional quality in a regulatory environment where most such terms are undefended.

The CSPI's critique — that describing products with very high sugar content using phrases like "just the healthiest fruits and vegetables" was misleading — has nutritional merit independent of any toxicological question. Naked juices do contain high concentrations of natural fruit sugars, and the marketing language may accurately describe ingredient sourcing while obscuring the product's place in a healthy diet. This is a separate and legitimate issue from the formaldehyde fabrication.


## 6. Verdict

**Snopes rating: False.** The claim that Naked juices and smoothies contain formaldehyde is false. Calcium pantothenate — a synthetic form of vitamin B5 present in Naked products — is manufactured using formaldehyde as a synthesis intermediate, but formaldehyde is entirely consumed in that synthesis and is not present in the finished ingredient. Calcium pantothenate has no chemical, biological, or toxicological relationship to formaldehyde beyond being a more complex molecule whose synthesis pathway includes formaldehyde as an intermediate.

The invocation of two real class action lawsuits as implied evidence for the formaldehyde claim is a form of lawsuit laundering: the litigation concerned deceptive labeling of "natural" and "non-GMO" claims and has no bearing on ingredient safety. The reasoning pattern "derived from A, therefore contains A" is a structural error in chemophobic content that, if consistently applied, would render virtually all synthesized substances suspect regardless of their actual chemistry or toxicology. Formaldehyde is also a normal endogenous human metabolic intermediate produced at ~105 mg/day in a typical adult and rapidly eliminated by enzymatic oxidation — making the notion of injury from trace synthetic exposure through any food route biochemically incoherent.

EARC classification: **R** (Reasoning). The core failure is not a simple factual error but a reasoning error — the "derived from" fallacy — that exploits chemical literacy gaps. Gap category: **distorted-but-grounded** — the ingredients named are genuinely present, and the lawsuits genuinely occurred, providing the true substrate on which false causal claims are constructed.
