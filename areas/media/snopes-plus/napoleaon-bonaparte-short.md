# Napoleon Bonaparte Was Unusually Short

## 1. The Claim

Few historical misconceptions have achieved the cultural sediment that the notion of Napoleon Bonaparte's diminutive stature has accumulated across two centuries. The image is immediately legible to virtually anyone in the Western world: the stocky, hat-wearing emperor, choleric in temper, compensating for an inferior physique through world-historical acts of military aggression. The claim appears in popular histories, jokes, psychological diagnoses, and caricatures stretching from Gillray's devastating Regency-era broadsides to contemporary stand-up comedy. Napoleon, the story goes, was comically, pathetically, definitionally short—perhaps five feet two inches—and his belligerence, his insatiable ambition, his very world conquest, may be read as an elaborate overcompensation for that physical deficit.

The claim does more than describe a historical figure's physical characteristics. It has become the empirical foundation for an entire psychological construct: the "Napoleon complex," a putative personality syndrome in which unusually short individuals overcompensate for their height disadvantage through heightened aggression, dominance-seeking, and risk-taking. The Napoleon complex has been seriously proposed in psychology and popular science as an explanation for patterns in aggressive behaviour, social dominance dynamics, and interpersonal competition in short-statured individuals. The entire edifice rests on Napoleon's alleged shortness being accurate.

The claim also functions as a piece of folk epistemology about the relationship between physical vulnerability and political violence. If one of modern history's most destructive military leaders was short, then perhaps there is a deep connection between embodied inadequacy and political aggression—a satisfying just-so story that maps individual psychology onto large-scale historical violence. This is not merely a claim about Napoleon's height; it is a claim about the structure of power, compensation, and human motivation.

## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rates the claim "False," and in this instance the verdict is straightforwardly well-supported. Napoleon Bonaparte's height, as best as historians and physical anthropologists can reconstruct it, was approximately 5 feet 6 or 7 inches in the modern measurement system, which corresponds to roughly 168–170 cm. This was average to slightly above average for a French man of his era and social class. The claim that he was unusually short—let alone 5 feet 2 or 5 feet 4, figures that circulate in popular accounts—does not survive scrutiny of the primary sources.

The principal source of confusion is a units-of-measurement error that has persisted for over two hundred years. French historical records, including Napoleonic-era documents, measured height in French units rather than English units. The French "pouce" (inch) was slightly larger than the English inch, and the French "pied" (foot) contained 12.79 English inches rather than 12.00. Napoleon's recorded height in French units was approximately 5 pieds 2 pouces—which in French measurement converts to approximately 1.686 metres, or 5 feet 6.5 inches in the modern Imperial system. When 19th-century British commentators read the French measurements and converted them as if they were in English feet and inches, they arrived at an apparently diminutive 5 feet 2—and the error was consequential enough to stick. The confusion is documented in detail by Ben Weider and David Hapgood and analysed by multiple historians of science and measurement (Markham, 1963, *History Today*; McLynn, 1997, *Napoleon: A Biography*).

Primary sources about Napoleon's physical appearance reinforce the measurement reconstruction. Contemporaries who knew him well, including his secretary Bourrienne and members of his guard, described him as of average or unremarkable height. At his height during the Consulate and Empire, his nickname among the Imperial Guard was "le petit caporal"—"the little corporal"—a term of affectionate familiarity reflecting his early career rank and his informal relationship with his troops, not a physical description. The Old Guard who called him "petit caporal" were themselves unusually tall, selected for impressive stature partly for ceremonial reasons; by comparison, any average-height man would appear small.

## 3. Epistemic Novelty — The Myth as Manufactured Wartime Propaganda

The epistemically most important insight about the short-Napoleon myth is not that it is false—Snopes handles that adequately—but that its origins are documented in a specific propaganda campaign that we can trace, characterise, and examine as a case study in the longevity of manufactured historical distortion.

The dominant early-propagandistic vehicle was the work of James Gillray, the British caricaturist whose representations of Napoleon were so penetrating and so widely reproduced that they substantially constituted Napoleon's popular image in Britain and eventually across Europe. Gillray consistently depicted Napoleon as a tiny, raging figure—often literally pocket-sized relative to British figures—in cartoons that functioned as mass-media propaganda during a period of active military and existential conflict. His 1803 cartoon "The Plum-Pudding in Danger" shows Napoleon (as "Little Boney") and William Pitt dividing the world, with the contrast in physical scale making explicit the intended mockery. These images were produced in a wartime context in which British morale, popular support for the war effort, and the dehumanisation of the enemy were pressing needs. Napoleon was also known for comparing himself unfavourably to British commanders—the strategic use of relative stature in propaganda has a political-economic logic.

The critical epistemological move here is to situate the myth's origin not in neutral historical perception but in the incentive structures of wartime communication. Britain was at war with Napoleonic France for much of the period 1800–1815. Propaganda that portrayed Napoleon as physically ridiculous served concrete state interests. Gillray was not a neutral observer. He was arguably the most important British propagandist of his era, and the consistency with which he physically diminished Napoleon was not incidental but structural to his political function.

What makes this case epistemically rich is the subsequent laundering of the propaganda into pseudo-factual historical record. Gillray's images circulated so widely and repeatedly that the physical representation they offered became the template through which subsequent generations visualised Napoleon—even as the original propagandistic context receded from popular memory. By the mid-19th century, the caricature had escaped its polemical origin and was being cited as evidence for characteristics of Napoleon's psychology, not as evidence of British propaganda effectiveness. By the early 20th century, psychologists were building theoretical constructs on its apparent factual accuracy.

This trajectory—from deliberately manufactured wartime distortion to psychological theoretical foundation—illustrates a broader pattern in how historical misinformation operates across time. The most persistent myths are not accidental errors but functional ones: they serve interests, they simplify complex individuals, they provide satisfying causal stories. The short-Napoleon myth serves all three of these functions and was moreover manufactured by actors with very strong incentives to make it sticky.

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** False  
**Confidence:** High  
**Epistemic Basis:** Multiple independent lines of evidence converge  
**Phenomenological Status:** Myth  
**Mechanistic Status:** Propaganda-laundered  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Negative — the myth has generated distorted psychological theory and enduring popular misunderstanding

The claim that Napoleon Bonaparte was unusually short is false. Napoleon's height was approximately 5 feet 6 to 7 inches in modern measurement—average to slightly above average for a French man of the early 19th century. The claim's origin is traceable to a combination of units-of-measurement confusion (French pieds versus English feet) and systematic British wartime propaganda, most prominently through James Gillray's caricatures. The myth's persistence for two centuries represents a case study in how manufactured distortion escapes its original context and becomes entrenched as popular historical "fact."

The downstream consequences of the myth are culturally substantial. The "Napoleon complex"—the psychological construct of aggression-driven-by-height-inadequacy—remains in popular usage despite Napoleon not being short. More importantly, experimental investigations of the "Napoleon complex" have produced inconsistent results. Timothy Judge and Daniel Cable (2004, *Journal of Applied Psychology*) found height positively associated with earnings and career success, the opposite of the overcompensation narrative. Stulp et al. (2012, *PLOS ONE*) found that shorter men do show marginally more aggressive responses in some competitive contexts, offering partial support for a height-aggression correlation in general populations—but citing Napoleon as the exemplar of this pattern is circular and historically inaccurate. The myth has also functioned as a convenient shorthand for dismissing Napoleon's military genius and political achievement by reducing extraordinary historical impact to compensatory psychology, which is a reductionism that distorts historical understanding.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**Napoleon's Actual Stature and Social Context:** Multiple independent lines of evidence establish Napoleon's height in the 168–170 cm range. Thomas-Alexandre Dumas, father of the novelist and himself a towering military figure in the French Revolutionary armies, described Napoleon in terms suggesting he was of ordinary height. The measurements taken at Napoleon's autopsy on St. Helena in 1821, conducted by multiple physicians, recorded his height as 5 feet 2 inches in French measurement, a detail that British and subsequent commentators read in English units (Chaplin, 1913, *Transactions of the Medical Society of London*). When properly converted, these measurements yield approximately 5 feet 6.5 inches. Additional confirmation comes from his uniforms, still preserved in collections including the Musée de l'Armée in Paris, which are consistent with a 5 foot 6–7 inch man of stocky build.

The social context of height in Napoleonic Europe also matters. Average male height in France circa 1800 was approximately 164–168 cm, lower than contemporary averages due to nutritional conditions of the period (Steckel, 1995, *Journal of Economic Literature*). By this baseline, Napoleon was in the upper portion of the height distribution for his time and country. He was certainly not exceptionally tall, but "average to somewhat above average" is the accurate characterisation, not "unusually short."

**Gillray's Propaganda Machine:** James Gillray's output between 1797 and 1810 represents the most influential image-manufacturing enterprise in British political history. In cartoons numbering in the hundreds, he established visual archetypes for French and specifically Napoleonic character that saturated popular British consciousness. The height trope was central: in "Maniac Ravings, or, Little Boney in a Strong Fit" (1803) and numerous companion pieces, Napoleon is literally depicted at half the size of the British figures he confronts. These were not documentary images but polemical arguments delivered in a visual vocabulary designed for maximum penetration into a largely print-literate but image-receptive public. Historian Ian Davidson (2012, *Napoleon: A Life*) notes that Gillray's physical characterisations were so powerful that contemporaries who actually saw Napoleon in person sometimes expressed surprise that he was larger than expected.

The propaganda logic was straightforward: Britain was defending against a continental hegemon. Making that hegemon seem physically ridiculous—comically undersized, raging, impotent—served to reassure a British public living under genuine existential threat. The success of this propaganda was so complete that the image outlasted the war, the Empire, and Napoleon's own life, persisting into the 21st century as apparently neutral historical description.

**The Napoleon Complex: Psychology on a False Foundation:** The Napoleon complex as a formal psychological construct developed through the 20th century as psychologists sought to name a phenomenon of overcompensatory aggression in short-statured men. Alfred Adler's concept of inferiority complex (Adler, 1927, *Understanding Human Nature*) provided the theoretical scaffolding, and Napoleon functioned as the emblematic case. The problem, as should by now be evident, is that the poster case is empirically false.

Empirical investigations of height-aggression relationships in real populations have been equivocal. Stulp et al. (2015, *PLOS ONE*) found that shorter men reported marginally greater jealousy in relationships, offering partial support for height-linked social vigilance. Bogaert and McCreary (2011, *Personality and Individual Differences*) found modest height-aggression correlations in some samples. But none of these findings reproduce anything like the dramatic, world-historical overcompensation the Napoleon case supposedly illustrates. Most researchers in the field have concluded that if a height-aggression relationship exists, it is small, contextually dependent, and in any case not exemplified by Napoleon, who was not short.

The persistence of the Napoleon complex as a cultural explanatory device despite this weak empirical foundation and historically false exemplar illuminates something important about the psychology of folk theories: once a named construct acquires a compelling narrative illustration, it becomes extremely difficult to dislodge even when both the illustration and the theoretical foundation are undermined. The Napoleon complex is invoked daily in ordinary conversation to describe assertive short-statured men, and this usage persists largely independent of any academic vetting.

**The Broader Pattern of Historical Character Assassination:** Napoleon's height is not the only physical characteristic that has been distorted in the historical record as a proxy for character. False or exaggerated physical stigmata—unusual height, unusual shortness, physical deformity—have been applied to various historical figures as a shorthand for psychological interpretation. Richard III's alleged hunchback, used in Shakespeare's representation as a metaphor for moral deformity, represents a similar pattern; subsequent archaeological and medical analysis of Richard III's unearthed skeleton (2012) confirmed moderate scoliosis but not the dramatic physical distortion of Shakespeare's villain. The pattern of projecting psychological narrative onto alleged physical abnormality is deep in Western literary and historical culture.

What distinguishes Napoleon's case is the specificity and traceable origin of the distortion. Gillray did not invent the height confusion from whole cloth—the measurement units misreading appears to have been independently circulating—but he amplified it into an indelible image. The combination of a genuine measurement confusion with a deliberate visual propaganda campaign created a myth with multiple reinforcing sources, making it far more stable than if it had been constructed from only one.

**Post-Napoleonic Legacy and Cultural Recycling:** The short-Napoleon myth accelerated after his death and defeat, when it served the retrospective narrative function of making his rise appear pathological from the start. The dominant 19th-century liberal historiography, shaped by the Whig interpretation of history as progressive emancipation from despotism, needed Napoleon to be an aberration—and physical grotesquerie was a useful marker of personal as well as political monstrousness. Victor Hugo's treatment, Thomas Carlyle's ambivalent hero-worship, and Tolstoy's withering portrait in *War and Peace* all contributed to an image in which Napoleon's physical presence was characterised as somehow inadequate or discrepant with his imperial ambition.

By the 20th century the myth was sufficiently institutionalised that it appeared in children's educational materials, popular encyclopaedias, and journalism without qualification or caveat. Corrective efforts by historians—including excellent popular accounts by Andrew Roberts (2014, *Napoleon: A Life*) and Adam Zamoyski (2018, *Napoleon: The Man Behind the Myth*)—have made inroads in specialist and educated popular audiences, but the myth remains effectively unkillable in casual cultural circulation. It is too useful, too compact, too psychologically satisfying to be dislodged by mere evidence.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates the claim as "False" and provides a reliable account of the measurement conversion error and the caricature tradition. Their treatment efficiently establishes that Napoleon's recorded height in French units converts to average stature in modern measurement. They note the Gillray connection and cite the autopsy documentation. This is adequate debunking.

What the Snopes treatment does not do in depth is situate the myth's origin within the full context of British wartime information operations, nor does it examine the downstream consequences for the Napoleon complex as a psychological construct. Treating the myth as an innocent measurement error underplays the active manufacturing role of Gillray and the broader British propaganda apparatus, which gives the myth not only an explanation for its origin but a pointed lesson about how state-sponsored disinformation can embed itself in what appears to be neutral factual description for centuries. Snopes also does not engage with the paradox that a psychological syndrome named after Napoleon continues to be invoked despite the entire empirical premise of his shortness being false—a gap that would strengthen the reader's understanding of how thoroughly this myth has colonised adjacent intellectual territory.

The verification work Snopes provides is valuable and correct. The deeper analytical work of tracing the myth's political manufacture and intellectual consequences remains underdone in the standard fact-check treatment.
