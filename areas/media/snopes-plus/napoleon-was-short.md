# Napoleon Bonaparte was unusually short for his time

## The Claim

Napoleon Bonaparte's small stature is among the most settled-seeming facts of popular history. It is not merely a neutral biographical detail; it is the interpretive key to the man's entire psychology in the popular imagination. He was short—unusually, almost pointedly short—and from that physical fact flows a personality: the chip on the shoulder, the overcompensation through conquest, the aggressive need to dominate. The "Napoleon complex" has entered the diagnostic vocabulary of everyday psychology. Small man with big ambitions; short stature as the engine of history. The image is so thoroughly naturalized that it functions less as a historical claim than as a psychological archetype.

The factual premise: Napoleon was notably shorter than average for his time and culture, and this physical characteristic is an established biographical fact.

It is not.

---

## What's Actually True

**Napoleon's actual height**

Napoleon Bonaparte stood approximately 168 to 170 centimeters tall—roughly 5 feet 6 inches to 5 feet 7 inches in modern British imperial measurement. Contemporary records and physical evidence are consistent with this range. The measurements taken after his death, the dimensions of his known clothing, accounts from contemporaries who stood near him, and the proportions of his surviving portraits all support a height in this range.

For adult men in France at the turn of the nineteenth century, this height was average to slightly above average. Average male height in France during Napoleon's lifetime has been estimated at approximately 164 to 166 centimeters based on military conscription records of the period—the best available large-sample data for male heights in the era before systematic population health surveys. Napoleon was not a tall man by modern standards, but he was not short by the standards of his own time and place.

**The measurement conversion error**

The origin of the "5 foot 2 Napoleon" claim is a unit conversion mistake with a surprisingly clear history.

France in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth century used the pied du roi—the "king's foot"—as its unit of length measurement. The pied du roi was approximately 32.5 centimeters, meaningfully longer than the British imperial foot (approximately 30.5 centimeters). Historical records and contemporary accounts in French sources consistently described Napoleon's height as approximately 5 pieds 2 pouces—five feet and two inches in French units.

Five pieds du roi and two pouces converts to approximately 168 centimeters, or about 5 feet 6 inches in British imperial. This is Napoleon's actual height.

The confusion arose when British commentators read or rendered Napoleon's height in French units and interpreted the numbers as British imperial inches. Five feet two in British imperial would be short even by the standards of the era—well below average male height. The measurement error transformed an average-height man into a conspicuously short one, and that transformation traveled through British popular culture with extraordinary speed and durability.

It is worth noting that this conversion error may not have been purely innocent. The British and Napoleon's France were at war for the better part of two decades, and Napoleon was the most consequential military and political enemy Britain had faced since the Spanish Armada. His diminishment in the British popular imagination served obvious political purposes.

**James Gillray and the machinery of propaganda**

The most important single figure in establishing Napoleon's short stature in the British imagination—and, through British cultural influence, in much of the Anglophone world—was the satirical cartoonist James Gillray. Between the 1790s and the early 1810s, Gillray produced a series of politically charged caricatures depicting Napoleon as a tiny, furious man—"Little Boney," dwarfed by the figures around him, compensating with imperial pretension and military belligerence.

These cartoons were enormously influential and widely reproduced. They constituted a major piece of British propaganda infrastructure—works that reached large audiences across class lines, that were reproduced and circulated in ways that contemporary political prints were not, and that created a visual vocabulary for Napoleon that became self-reinforcing. Each subsequent Gillray cartoon confirming little Boney's stature made the image more true-seeming, more normalized, more obviously the right way to picture the enemy.

There is a documented irony in Gillray's cartoons: Gillray was aware of what he was doing. His caricatures were deliberate political weapons, not simply incidental humor. The "little" Napoleon was a political choice before it was a popular belief. The cartoon images preceded and created the popular impression rather than reflecting it.

Napoleon was, by contrast, acutely aware of the British propaganda campaign against him and explicitly concerned about it. His own propaganda apparatus worked to construct competing images of him as heroic, commanding, and larger than life—the David portraits, the theatrical scale of his coronation imagery, the elaborate visual language of Empire. The war over Napoleon's physical image was a real cultural battle between competing representations, and in the long run, the British version won.

**The bodyguard effect and the "Petit Caporal" confusion**

Another factor contributed to the perception of Napoleon's small stature: his Imperial Guard, the elite troops who served as his personal bodyguard and ceremonial escort, had a minimum height requirement of approximately 5 feet 10 inches. These were conspicuously tall men even by today's standards. When Napoleon was seen in public, he was typically surrounded by his Guard, and the height contrast between a man of average stature and soldiers selected for exceptional height was visually striking. Observers who saw Napoleon surrounded by his towering Guard likely did perceive him as short—relative to the company he kept.

Napoleon's nickname "le Petit Caporal"—the Little Corporal—has also contributed to the short-stature myth, but its origin is not a reference to his height. The nickname was a term of affectionate familiarity given to him by his troops during the early Italian campaigns, referring to his willingness to share the hardships of ordinary soldiers and to his first commissioned rank as a junior officer. "Petit" in this context is diminutive in the sense of intimate and informal, not physically descriptive. It is a soldier's term of endearment for an officer who ate bad food with them, not a reference to his physical dimensions.

## Why People Believe This

**British propaganda was extremely effective**

Gillray's cartoons and the broader British propaganda campaign against Napoleon were not merely effective in the short term—they shaped the cultural image of Napoleon for centuries. The visual vocabulary of "little Boney" became so embedded that it survived Napoleon's defeat, the end of the Napoleonic Wars, and multiple generations of revision by historians. The image of Napoleon as small was established so early and so vividly that it functioned as common knowledge for most of the period between 1800 and the present.

This is a remarkable demonstration of how effective early cultural framing can be. The British image of Napoleon became the global image, partly through the scale of British cultural and imperial influence in the nineteenth century. It was exported to countries whose relations with Napoleon were very different from Britain's, where it had no propaganda utility, but where it persisted simply because the British framing was dominant.

**Psychological coherence**

The short Napoleon is not simply a factual claim; it is the foundation of a psychologically compelling narrative. The "Napoleon complex"—the pattern of aggressive overcompensation in short men—is a satisfying explanatory schema. It ties Napoleon's ambition, his military aggression, his imperial pretension, and his eventual downfall into a neat psychological story: a man driven by insecurity, overreaching because he always had something to prove.

This narrative has more explanatory power than "Napoleon was of average height and was driven by ambition, talent, and the particular political opportunities of post-Revolutionary France." The psychological story is simple, memorable, and provides a character key that unlocks the rest of the life. Its simplicity is a feature, not a bug, in terms of cultural persistence—even if it is historically inaccurate.

**The Napoleon complex as a real psychological phenomenon**

There is genuine psychological research on compensatory behavior in shorter individuals in status-competitive contexts, though the research is more complicated than popular usage implies. The existence of research that uses "Napoleon complex" as a frame, or that explores height and compensatory aggression, provides a retroactive pseudo-validation for the historical claim. If the Napoleon complex is a real thing, it feels reasonable to assume that Napoleon himself exemplified it. The causal arrow is actually reversed—the psychological term was named for the historical myth, not the other way around—but popular knowledge does not typically track that distinction.

**Measurement systems and historical literacy**

Most people are not fluent in the historical measurement systems of pre-metric Europe. The difference between a pied du roi and a British imperial foot is not widely known. When someone reads "5 feet 2 inches" in a historical source—without context specifying whether this is French or British measurement—the natural interpretation is modern British imperial, which would indeed describe a short man. The error requires specialized knowledge to identify; without it, the source appears to confirm the myth.

## Verdict

**False.** Napoleon Bonaparte was approximately 168-170 cm (5'6" to 5'7" in modern British imperial), average to slightly above average for French men of his era. The "5 feet 2" figure arose from misinterpreting French measurement units as British imperial ones. His image as a short man was systematically cultivated by British propaganda, particularly James Gillray's caricatures, which became the dominant visual representation of Napoleon in Anglophone culture. The myth has proved extraordinarily durable because it supports a psychologically coherent and satisfying narrative (the Napoleon complex) and because the British cultural frame established itself before historical correction was possible.

---

## The Wider Picture

The Napoleon height myth is among the most instructive examples of successful propaganda that has survived the context of its creation. It was generated in a specific political conflict, for specific strategic purposes, and has outlasted the conflict, the strategic context, and any active maintenance by the originators by more than two centuries. It is now treated as established biography in contexts where Napoleon's relationship to Britain is entirely neutral.

This illustrates a principle about the durability of early cultural framing that has implications well beyond this particular case. The image of a historical figure formed during a period of intense political conflict—when that figure was an active enemy—is shaped by the needs of that conflict, not by the demands of accuracy. When the conflict ends, the image does not automatically reset. It persists, carried forward by the cultural infrastructure (textbooks, cartoons, novels, films, psychological terminology) that the conflict generated. Correcting the image requires active intervention against the established frame, and that intervention faces an asymmetric battle: the myth has centuries of accumulated cultural authority; the correction is recent, specialized, and requires nuance.

The case also illuminates the relationship between physical description and psychological interpretation. Napoleon's height was not merely a neutral biographical fact in British propaganda; it was the explanatory key to his psychology and his politics. The short stature explained the ambition, which explained the wars, which explained the suffering. Reducing a world-historical figure to a psychological type—the short man overcompensating—is intellectually impoverishing, but it is extremely efficient as a piece of cultural shorthand. It converts a complex political and military history into a character study, and that conversion is sticky in ways that more accurate but more complex explanations are not.

The "Napoleon complex" as a psychological concept has its own interesting genealogy. The term was coined in the twentieth century to describe observed patterns of aggressive or dominant behavior in shorter individuals in certain competitive contexts. The research base is real, if narrower and more nuanced than popular usage implies. But the naming of this pattern for Napoleon presupposed the myth—it assumed Napoleon was short and overcompensating. The psychological concept, once named, then provided retroactive confirmation for the historical myth. This circular reinforcement between a historical legend and a scientific-sounding concept is a particularly effective mechanism for myth stabilization.

There is also something worth noting about the scale disproportion between Napoleon's actual historical significance and the centrality of the height myth in popular biography. Napoleon was arguably the most consequential European figure of the early nineteenth century: he reshaped the legal architecture of Europe through the Napoleonic Code, redrew the map of the continent through a decade of military dominance, accelerated the spread of revolutionary political principles, and his eventual defeat and exile set the political terms for the rest of the century. The height myth reduces this enormous historical figure to a psychological case study in compensatory behavior, which represents a profound impoverishment of historical understanding. The myth does not merely get a fact wrong; it substitutes a psychologically satisfying but historically hollow story for a genuinely interesting and complex history.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**The measurement conversion is the factual core**

The central fact-check finding—that Napoleon's recorded height in French units was misinterpreted as British imperial—is specific, documentable, and decisive. Rigorous fact-check treatments make this measurement error the centerpiece of the debunk, because it explains not just that the myth is wrong but *how* it became wrong. The error has a mechanism; it is not simply that people misremembered or exaggerated, but that a specific unit translation mistake produced a specific wrong number that then circulated.

**The propaganda context**

Gillray's role and the British propaganda context are essential for a complete fact-check. Without this context, the myth looks like an innocent error that persisted through repetition. With the context, it looks like a deliberate political construction that succeeded beyond its creators' expectations. The difference matters for understanding how such myths originate and how to evaluate similar cases.

**The Napoleon complex**

Careful fact-checkers address the "Napoleon complex" dimension without dismissing the psychological research entirely. The appropriate treatment distinguishes between: (a) the historical claim that Napoleon was short, which is false; (b) the psychological claim that there is a research-supported pattern of compensatory behavior sometimes observed in shorter individuals, which is partly supported with significant nuance; and (c) the inference that Napoleon himself exemplified this pattern, which is historically unsupported and logically confused because the psychological concept was named for the myth, not for verified observation of Napoleon's behavior.

**Height context for the period**

Good fact-check treatments provide the comparative context: what was average male height in France in Napoleon's lifetime? This requires engagement with historical anthropometric data, which comes primarily from military conscription records. The data is not perfectly precise, but it is adequate to establish that 168-170 cm was not notably short for the period. Without this context, a reader might accept that Napoleon's height was average while still not fully understanding how far from the myth that puts him.

**The Guard bodyguard visual effect**

The height contrast effect created by Napoleon's Imperial Guard is often noted in stronger fact-check treatments as an explanatory bridge: even people who saw Napoleon in person and perceived him as short were not necessarily wrong about what they saw—they were seeing a relative height comparison in a context specifically engineered to make average-height individuals look short. This is a useful piece of contextual complexity that moves the fact-check beyond a simple "the myth is wrong" to a more nuanced account of how the misperception could have arisen and persisted even among eyewitnesses.
