# Napoleon Was Unusually Short

## 1. The Claim

Napoleon Bonaparte was unusually short—a small man with an oversized ego and a compensatory hunger for power. This image is so deeply embedded in Western culture that it has its own psychological label: the Napoleon Complex, a term used to describe supposedly aggressive or domineering behavior in short men. The caricature of Napoleon is consistent across two centuries of popular representation: the little man with the hand in his coat, the cocked hat tilted at an angle, striding about with imperial ambition disproportionate to his physical stature. He is routinely cited as a famous short person, placed in lists alongside other compact historical figures, and used as shorthand for the idea that small men overcompensate. His nickname in the British press was "Little Boney." The belief in his diminutive stature is one of the most pervasive biographical myths in modern history—and it is almost entirely false.

## 2. What's Actually True

Napoleon Bonaparte was not unusually short. By the best available historical evidence, he stood approximately 5 feet 7 inches in modern Imperial measurement, or about 170 centimeters. This was average, and possibly slightly above average, for a French man of his era.

The primary documentary sources that have been examined by historians—contemporary accounts, memoirs of those who knew him, and physical records—consistently describe Napoleon as being of average or slightly above-average height. His personal physician, Francesco Antommarchi, recorded his height at 5 feet 2 inches—but in *French* inches (pouce), which were larger than English inches of the same period. Converting from the French measurement system to modern metric or English Imperial gives approximately 170 cm or 5 feet 7 inches.

This measurement unit confusion is the technical foundation of the myth. The French *pied* (foot) and *pouce* (inch) were defined differently from the English equivalents. A French inch was approximately 2.71 centimeters; an English inch was 2.54 centimeters. Napoleon's height in French inches, when read by English-speaking audiences as English inches, came out nearly two inches shorter than his actual height. The error is not dramatic, but it was enough to transform a man of average stature into a man of notably short stature, given the baseline expectations of the audience receiving the information.

The second major source of the myth was British wartime propaganda, most famously the work of the caricaturist James Gillray. Gillray's cartoons of Napoleon, produced during the Napoleonic Wars for an English audience, systematically depicted him as a small, infantile, petulant figure—"Little Boney" throwing tantrums, being held by larger figures, posturing absurdly in oversized uniforms. Gillray was producing propaganda, not portraiture. His goal was to diminish Napoleon in the eyes of the English public, and diminishment was literal as well as metaphorical. The caricatures were enormously influential, widely reproduced, and constitutive of the English popular image of Napoleon in a way that proved remarkably durable.

Contemporary accounts from people who met Napoleon complicate the caricature. Many observers described him as being of normal stature, particularly in his prime. His soldiers, who called him "le petit caporal" (the little corporal), used the term as an affectionate nickname—a reference to his willingness to be among the troops in the field, not a remark on his physical height. The affectionate nickname was taken by English propagandists and popular memory as literal description.

Napoleon's Imperial Guard—his elite personal troops—had a minimum height requirement of 5 feet 6 inches, and many guardsmen were taller than that. In photographs taken of Napoleon (daguerreotypes do not exist, but portraits from life do), he appears in proportion with the figures around him. Eugène de Beauharnais, his stepson, recorded his height as approximately average among French men of the period. There is no contemporary account that describes Napoleon as notably or unusually short; the consistent characterization among those who knew him was that he was normal in stature.

The myth was so successful that it became self-reinforcing. Once the image of the short Napoleon was established, any tall figure near him—including his Imperial Guard—was read as further evidence of his smallness. The interpretive frame shaped the interpretation of evidence rather than the reverse.

It is also worth noting what is true about Napoleon's physical presence: he was stocky and compact in build, particularly in middle age, which may have contributed to perceptions of shortness in a cultural context that associated height with elongation. He was not tall by any account, but neither was he short. He was an ordinary-sized man who became the subject of an extraordinary propaganda effort.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The myth of Napoleon's shortness is a case study in how propaganda, once successful, outlives its original context and becomes historical "fact."

The British caricatures by Gillray and others were extraordinarily effective because they were widely distributed, visually striking, and emotionally satisfying to a wartime English audience. Gillray's Napoleon was not just short—he was ridiculous, petty, and childishly dangerous. The visual grammar of the caricature communicated a political message: this is not a man to be taken seriously, merely to be defeated. The caricatures worked because they served a function beyond description; they were tools of political mobilization.

The measurement confusion, which provided a technical-seeming basis for the myth, added credibility. If someone had read that Napoleon stood 5 feet 2 inches—a figure taken from an official-looking source like a physician's report—they had what felt like documentary evidence. The fact that the figure was in a different measurement system was invisible to most readers. This is a structural feature of how misinformation often works: a real document, read without the context needed to interpret it correctly, produces a false conclusion that has the apparent authority of primary source material.

The myth was also self-reinforcing through the naming of the "Napoleon Complex." By giving his name to a psychological phenomenon—the supposed overcompensation of short men—popular psychology locked the association of Napoleon and short stature into a conceptual framework that is used in everyday conversation. Every time someone invokes the Napoleon Complex, they reinforce the belief that Napoleon was short. The psychological concept spread the myth and the myth supported the concept in a closed loop.

There is also the deeper narrative appeal. The story of a short man who conquered Europe through sheer will and overcompensation is a satisfying story about the triumph of ambition over limitation. It makes Napoleon into a comprehensible human figure, driven by recognizable insecurities. The true story—of a man of average height with exceptional strategic intelligence, enormous energy, and a particular historical moment—is more complex and less emotionally satisfying as a narrative template. The myth simplifies and humanizes in a way that makes Napoleon's ambition explicable through pop psychology.

Once the belief was embedded, cognitive confirmation bias took over. People who believed Napoleon was short tended to notice and remember details consistent with that belief (small hats, caricatures, the Imperial Guard's height) and to discount or not notice inconsistencies (contemporary accounts of normal stature, the French inch problem, the actual average height of Frenchmen in 1800).

## 4. Verdict

**Refuted.** Napoleon Bonaparte was not unusually short. He stood approximately 5 feet 7 inches (170 cm), which was average or slightly above average for a French man of his time. The myth derives from a measurement unit confusion between French and English inches, British wartime propaganda—especially the caricatures of James Gillray—and the nickname "le petit caporal," which was an affectionate soldier's title rather than a comment on his height.

This is one of the most clearly documented historical myths in modern European history. The evidence is strong, the mechanism of the error is well-understood, and the contrast between the myth and the historical record is sharp. The persistence of the myth despite its thorough debunking is itself evidence of how effectively propaganda can survive the conditions that created it.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Napoleon myth is significant beyond its factual incorrectness because of what it illuminates about the power of wartime propaganda to shape historical memory.

James Gillray is recognized by art historians as one of the most important political caricaturists in British history, and his Napoleon cartoons are cited as a paradigm case of how visual satire can constitute, rather than merely reflect, popular perception of historical figures. Gillray did not reflect a belief that Napoleon was short; he created it. The caricatures preceded the widespread popular belief, not the reverse. This is an important distinction: the myth was manufactured, and it was manufactured for specific political purposes by a specific artistic and propaganda apparatus.

The Napoleonic Wars are the historical context in which the British and French were engaged in total warfare, and propaganda was a genuine weapon of statecraft. England could not defeat Napoleon on land with any consistency in the early campaigns; it could, however, shape domestic public opinion and Allied perception of the French emperor. Making Napoleon a figure of ridicule—and specifically a figure of physical inadequacy—was politically functional. It made him contemptible rather than frightening.

The survival of the myth into the twenty-first century, two centuries after its creation and far outside the context of British wartime politics, is a demonstration of how cultural memes outlive their origins. The Napoleon Complex as a psychological concept, whether or not it describes a real phenomenon (which is itself contested in the psychology literature), has extended the myth into a new domain. When a false belief becomes the name of a psychological phenomenon, it gains a durability that is almost independent of its truth value—because the name is in common use regardless of whether the underlying belief is accurate.

This raises a broader question about named psychological phenomena derived from cultural myths. The Freudian literature is full of complexes named after mythological figures—Oedipus, Narcissus, Electra—where the mythological referent is explicit and avowedly non-literal. The Napoleon Complex occupies an uncomfortable middle ground: named after a historical figure in a way that implies the historical figure exemplified the trait, when in fact he probably did not. The phenomenon (if real) should perhaps be renamed if the field wants intellectual consistency.

For historians of science and historians of propaganda, the Napoleon myth is also a case study in how historical falsification is accomplished not through the destruction of accurate records but through the creation and amplification of competing narratives that are more emotionally satisfying or politically useful. The accurate records—physician's reports, memoirs, diplomatic correspondence—all describe Napoleon accurately. The myth was built not by destroying these but by making the caricature more vivid, more widely distributed, and more emotionally salient than the documentary record.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Napoleon height myth is one of the more tractable cases for historical fact-checking because the mechanism of error is well-documented and the evidence is clear.

The standard approach involves three layers: establishing what the actual sources say about Napoleon's height, explaining the measurement unit conversion error, and documenting the propaganda origin of the myth. All three layers are well-supported. Contemporary accounts, properly interpreted, describe a man of average stature. The French inch conversion is a matter of historical metrology. The Gillray connection is documented in art history and the history of British political propaganda.

What makes this case epistemologically interesting for fact-checkers is the durability of the myth despite thorough debunking. The fact that the Napoleon Complex is named after a false premise is widely known among historians and educated people, yet the myth persists in popular culture. This is a case where factual correction has been widely available and widely repeated, but has not dislodged the false belief. Understanding why—the emotional appeal of the narrative, the reinforcement through the psychological concept's name, the cultural saturation of the caricature—is as important for the fact-checker as establishing what the truth is.

The EARC classification of R (Refuted) reflects the clear historical consensus: Napoleon was not unusually short, the myth is documentably false, and its origin is well-understood. This is not a case of uncertain historical evidence or contested interpretation; it is a case of clear evidence that has been consistently misread or ignored because the myth it contradicts is too culturally entrenched to dislodge through ordinary factual correction.

The Napoleon case is also a useful illustration for public literacy about misinformation: propaganda that successfully shapes perception during its original context can persist long after its context has vanished, sustained by cultural inertia, self-reinforcing conceptual frameworks, and the emotional satisfaction of a good story. The question is not whether Napoleon was short—he was not—but why so many people continue to believe he was despite the evidence, and what that tells us about the relationship between historical accuracy and cultural memory.
