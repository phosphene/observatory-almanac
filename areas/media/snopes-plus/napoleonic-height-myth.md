# Napoleon Bonaparte was unusually short

## 1. The Claim

Few historical figures are as firmly associated with a single physical characteristic as Napoleon Bonaparte is with his height — or rather, his alleged lack of it. The image is fixed in popular culture: a small, irritable man in a large hat, perpetually overcompensating for his diminutive stature by conquering most of Europe. Caricatures, novels, psychology textbooks, and casual conversation all traffic in the same image. The "Napoleon complex" — a term applied to short men who overachieve, dominate, or bully to compensate for their physical disadvantage — has entered the language as a clinical-sounding concept, lending the myth the additional authority of psychological taxonomy.

The claim is stated in countless encyclopedias, biographies, and history classrooms: Napoleon was short. Usually around five feet two inches is the figure cited. He was, according to this framing, a man aware of his smallness, shaped by it, perhaps defined by it. The aggression, the ambition, the endless military campaigns — the subtext is always there: a little man reaching.

This is one of the most thoroughly documented misconceptions in Western historical memory. Napoleon was not unusually short. He was not even particularly short by the standards of his own time and place. The number most commonly cited — five feet two — is a product of measurement confusion, not historical measurement. And the image that survives was substantially manufactured by his enemies for political purposes.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

Napoleon Bonaparte stood approximately 5 feet 6 to 5 feet 7 inches tall in modern terms — between 168 and 170 centimeters. This placed him at or slightly above the average height for French men of his era, which historians and anthropologists estimate was around 5 feet 5 inches (165 cm) for adult males in late eighteenth and early nineteenth century France.

The source of the confusion is a measurement discrepancy between French and British units that was well understood by contemporaries but has been repeatedly collapsed in subsequent transmission.

The French *pied* (foot) and *pouce* (inch) were not the same dimensions as English feet and inches. Napoleon's height was recorded in French measurement as 5 pieds 2 pouces — the figure that became notorious as "five feet two." But one French pied equaled approximately 12.79 English inches, compared to 12 inches for an English foot. When Napoleon's French measurement is converted accurately to Imperial units, the result is approximately 5 feet 6 to 5 feet 7 inches — solidly average for the period, not small.

This conversion error was not made by Napoleon's contemporaries. It was made, or exploited, in the transmission of the historical record. British readers and later historians who encountered the French measurement figure and read it as though it were British inches took a man of average height and accidentally shrunk him by several inches.

Historian Charles Esdaile, one of the leading scholars of the Napoleonic period, addresses this directly in his work on Napoleon. Esdaile notes that the measurement confusion is well established among serious historians and that Napoleon's actual height, by any reasonable reconstruction from period sources, was within the normal range for his time and social class. The "short Napoleon" is not a discovery of historical research — it is a product of measurement illiteracy compounded over generations.

Additional physical evidence supports the historical reconstruction. Contemporary accounts from people who knew Napoleon do not describe him as short. Thomas-Alexandre Dumas, the father of the novelist and himself a general under Napoleon, was a notably large man; his descriptions of Napoleon do not suggest someone who struck observers as diminutive. The Empress Josephine, his first wife, was approximately 5 feet 4 inches tall. In period portraits, Napoleon is not depicted as conspicuously small in relation to other figures in the frame, though portraiture has its own conventions that complicate direct physical inference.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of the Napoleon height myth is not an accident. It was manufactured, cultivated, and then preserved by forces that had direct interests in maintaining the image.

**British wartime propaganda.** The most important engine of the Napoleon myth was the British political caricaturists of the Napoleonic Wars era, above all James Gillray, who worked between approximately 1792 and 1811. Gillray produced a series of extremely influential caricatures depicting Napoleon — styled "Little Boney" — as a tiny, tantrum-prone figure dwarfed by the larger British figures surrounding him, particularly Prime Minister William Pitt. These caricatures were not personal opinion pieces; Gillray received payment from the British government and his work was understood as serving explicit propaganda purposes during a war in which public sentiment was important. The image of a small, raging Napoleon served a clear political function: it diminished the most powerful man in Europe, made him ridiculous, and reassured a British public that was genuinely threatened by French military dominance.

The caricatures were effective and widely circulated. They shaped the popular image of Napoleon in Britain and in the broader European world that consumed British media and cultural production. By the time Napoleon was exiled and his political threat removed, the image was already embedded deeply enough to outlive its propagandistic function.

**The "Little Corporal" nickname.** Napoleon's nickname among his troops, "le petit caporal" (the Little Corporal), contributed to the height association — but its meaning has been systematically misread. The nickname referred not to physical stature but to Napoleon's habit of moving among his troops, sharing their conditions, affecting solidarity with the men. The term conveyed affection and familiarity, not a description of literal smallness. In French military culture of the period, "petit" in such phrases carried connotations of endearment, much as an English speaker might call a beloved figure "little" without meaning it as a physical description. This is well understood by historians but has been consistently misinterpreted in popular transmission as confirmation of his small physical size.

**The Imperial Guard optical illusion.** Napoleon's personal bodyguard, the Imperial Guard, was selected for exceptional height. Membership in the Guard required a minimum height considerably above average, and many Guardsmen were selected specifically for physical imposingness. When Napoleon appeared surrounded by his Guard, the contrast between his average height and the tall men flanking him would have made him appear shorter than he was. Visitors to court who encountered Napoleon in this context may have formed a genuine impression of relative smallness — not because Napoleon was short, but because his environment was deliberately populated with tall men. This contextual distortion likely fed accounts that described Napoleon as smaller than expected.

**Psychological taxonomy reinforcing the myth.** The coining and popularization of the phrase "Napoleon complex" by psychologist Alfred Adler in the early twentieth century gave the myth the added authority of psychological science. The concept — that short-statured men overcompensate through aggression, dominance-seeking, and social control — is contested in the psychological literature (more on this below), but its association with Napoleon's name placed his purported shortness into the vocabulary of educated discourse. A myth that has a scientific-sounding term attached to it is substantially harder to dislodge than one that does not.

**The flat survival of the measurement error.** Historical errors, once in print, develop a long tail. The five-feet-two figure appeared in English-language texts, encyclopedias, and biographical accounts from the early nineteenth century onward, with authors copying from prior authors without checking the original French measurement sources. Each repetition added authority. By the time the error was widely noted by historians, it had already been replicated in enough canonical sources to have effectively become consensus. The truth about Napoleon's height is not new information — historians have known it for a long time — but corrections of widely circulated facts travel more slowly than the facts themselves.

## 4. Verdict

**The claim is substantially false — with a distorted historical grain at its origin.**

Napoleon was not unusually short. By accurate conversion of the period measurement record and by the consensus of historians working on the Napoleonic period, he stood approximately 5 feet 6 to 5 feet 7 inches — average to slightly above average for French men of his time.

The myth originated in a genuine measurement record (5 pieds 2 pouces) that was misread by people unfamiliar with French units. It was amplified by British wartime propaganda that had direct political incentives to portray Napoleon as ridiculous and diminutive. It was reinforced by contextual optical effects (surrounded by the tall Imperial Guard), by the misreading of an affectionate nickname, and by decades of uncritical repetition in English-language historical accounts.

The claim belongs to the category "distorted but grounded" — there is a real historical artifact at the origin (the French measurement figure), but the popular understanding of it is built on a misreading compounded by deliberate propaganda. Napoleon was not the height that most people believe, and the cultural image that exists of him as a small man is in large part a product of his enemies' political interests, not of historical fact.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The Napoleon height myth is one of the most revealing examples in historiography of what happens when measurement systems interact with propaganda and then survive the context that produced both.

The measurement confusion was a real event. French and British units of length differed in ways that were practically significant and not universally understood. For a British reader encountering the French measurement figure in a document or account, the natural inference was to read it as British units — an inference that was wrong and that shrank Napoleon by several inches. This was not necessarily malicious at the point of transmission; it was a failure of cross-cultural numerical literacy that had significant consequences because it aligned with images that propaganda had already planted.

The propaganda was deliberate. Gillray's caricatures were not cultural accidents — they were produced in a context of wartime information management and consumed as part of Britain's effort to maintain public morale against a French military machine that had comprehensively demonstrated its ability to defeat European armies. Making Napoleon small and laughable served a real function. That function was achieved with remarkable success: the image outlived the war by two centuries.

The "Napoleon complex" as a psychological concept is independently contested. The empirical evidence for the proposed psychological mechanism — short men systematically overcompensating through dominance and aggression — is mixed at best. Some studies have found the opposite effect: taller men may, in certain social contexts, display more aggressive or dominant behavior because they face fewer social costs for it. The concept as a clinical or predictive framework has not been established to the satisfaction of the psychological research community. That the term is named after a man who did not have the characteristic the term describes is a kind of double irony that historians of psychology have noted.

The deeper significance of the Napoleon myth is what it reveals about the manufacture and preservation of historical images. Napoleon remains one of the most studied figures in Western history. Hundreds of serious scholarly biographies exist. The measurement facts have been in the historical record for a long time. Yet the popular image — little man, big hat, compensation in progress — has proven essentially immune to correction at the cultural level, even while the scholarly consensus has long since moved past it. This gap between scholarly consensus and popular belief is not unusual, but it is rarely as well documented or as consequential as it is in the Napoleonic case.

The myth has also shaped how Napoleon's actual historical significance is discussed. His military genius, his administrative reforms, his legal legacy in the Napoleonic Code, his role in reshaping European political and territorial structures — these are frequently discussed with the implicit framing of "remarkable for such a small man," a framing that subtly diminishes everything it intends to praise. Removing the height myth does not change Napoleon's history, but it does remove one layer of distortion from how we encounter it.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Napoleon height myth has been addressed by multiple fact-checking and historical reference organizations, and the consensus is consistent: Napoleon was of average height for his era, and the "five feet two" figure is a measurement conversion error compounded by British propaganda.

**Snopes** has covered this myth and rates the claim that Napoleon was unusually short as false, explaining the French/British measurement distinction and citing the historical scholarship on Gillray and British propaganda.

**The History Channel** and major historical reference platforms including *Britannica* have addressed the myth in editorial pieces, generally reaching the same conclusion: 5 feet 6 to 5 feet 7 inches, average for the era.

**The Napoleon Foundation** (Fondation Napoléon, Paris), which manages historical archives and scholarly resources on Bonaparte, has published clarifications on the height question that align with the consensus — noting that the measurement confusion is a documented historical error, not a contested historiographical question.

**Historians in print:** Esdaile's work is the most frequently cited by fact-checkers, alongside Andrew Roberts's biography *Napoleon: A Life* (2014), which addresses the height question directly and succinctly. Roberts notes that Napoleon was "five feet six or seven inches tall" by modern standards and that the myth has been perpetuated by the unit confusion and Gillray's caricatures.

The challenge for fact-checkers with this claim is that it involves correcting an extremely deeply embedded cultural image rather than simply adjudicating a contemporary claim. People do not arrive at the belief that Napoleon was short because they recently read a misleading article — they arrive at it through decades of cultural saturation. The correction requires not only stating the accurate measurement but explaining the two-part origin story (measurement error plus propaganda), because without both parts, the correction seems incomplete.

A secondary challenge is the "Napoleon complex" terminology. Correcting the height myth while leaving the term in circulation creates an awkward situation in which a psychological concept named after Napoleon implies the very stature he did not have. Thorough fact-checking on this topic therefore typically includes a note about the contested status of the psychological concept itself, not just the biographical fact about Napoleon's height.

The broader lesson for fact-checkers in this case concerns the relationship between propaganda and historical record. When deliberate misinformation enters the record early enough and is reinforced by enough secondary transmission, it can become effectively canonical in popular memory even while scholars have moved on. The Napoleon myth is a useful case study precisely because the scholarship is so clear and the popular belief so persistent — demonstrating that factual clarity at the scholarly level does not automatically produce factual clarity at the cultural level, and that the mechanisms of popular belief formation require their own analysis.
