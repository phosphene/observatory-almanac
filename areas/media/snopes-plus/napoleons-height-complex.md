# "Napoleon Bonaparte Was Unusually Short"


## 1. The Claim

Few historical myths have achieved the cultural ubiquity or psychological staying power of the belief that Napoleon Bonaparte was a small man. The claim is asserted not merely as biographical trivia but as explanatory framework: Napoleon's aggression, his ambition, his military campaigns across a continent, and his imperial self-invention are collectively attributed to a compensatory psychology rooted in short stature. A psychiatric term—the "Napoleon Complex"—has been coined to describe the overcompensation theory, applied not only to Napoleon himself but to any small or short person who exhibits assertive or dominant behavior. The myth is therefore simultaneously biographical and diagnostic; it provides both a simple account of one of history's most complex figures and a ready-made clinical-sounding label for behavioral patterns in people who happen to be shorter than average.

The claim is stated as fact in casual conversation, invoked in popular media, embedded in comedy, deployed as analogy in political commentary, and accepted without scrutiny across cultures which have, collectively, no particular stake in one direction or the other. The source of this conviction is rarely examined. People who assert Napoleon's short stature typically do so on the assumption that this is well-known history—that Napoleon's height is simply documented fact comparable to any other historical measurement—and that anyone disputing it is engaged in revisionism.

The image most associated with the myth is familiar: a round, small, pale man in military dress, wearing an oversized hat, standing considerably shorter than the men surrounding him, often with one hand tucked into his coat in apparent self-important posture. This image derives primarily from British caricature art of the Napoleonic Wars era and continues to represent Napoleon in popular imagination nearly two centuries after his death. Its power lies in its visual authority: we do not merely believe Napoleon was short; we have seemingly seen proof of it in countless images.


## 2. What's Actually True

Napoleon Bonaparte's documented height at the time of his death, as recorded in the official autopsy conducted by his personal physician Francesco Antommarchi on May 6, 1821, at St. Helena, was 5 feet 2 inches—in French Imperial units. This measurement is the source of much of the confusion. French Imperial inches (pouces) were longer than English inches. One French pouce was equivalent to approximately 2.71 centimeters, compared to the English inch of 2.54 centimeters. Five feet two inches in French measurement converts to approximately 5 feet 6.5 inches in English units, or about 169 centimeters in modern metric terms.

The average height of a French male in the early nineteenth century is estimated by historical anthropometric research at approximately 164–165 centimeters. This makes Napoleon slightly above average for a Frenchman of his time and social origin. He was not unusually tall, but he was definitively not unusually short. By the standards of his time and place, he was unremarkable in height—a detail that makes the persistence of his short-man mythology all the more striking.

Napoleon's own contemporaries corroborated this measurement. His aide-de-camp Roustam Raza, who attended Napoleon daily for more than a decade, described him as of medium height. His valet Louis Constant wrote in his memoirs that Napoleon was approximately 5 feet 6 inches, English measurement. The Duke of Wellington, who observed Napoleon at close range during the Hundred Days and afterward, did not describe him as particularly short. Military personnel, court officials, and diplomatic contacts who recorded physical descriptions of Napoleon in memoirs, dispatches, and administrative documents consistently placed his height in the average to slightly above-average range for European men of his generation.

The height mythology is compounded by a genuine optical illusion embedded in the historical record. Napoleon was frequently surrounded by his Imperial Guard, an elite corps with a minimum height requirement of 5 feet 5 inches to 5 feet 7 inches depending on regiment and period. Surrounded by men systematically selected for above-average height, any man of average stature would appear shorter by comparison. French soldiers nicknamed Napoleon "le petit caporal"—the Little Corporal—but this was a term of affectionate familiarity dating to the Italian campaign, not a physical description. It referred to his willingness to share conditions with ordinary soldiers, not to his stature.

Napoleon's actual height is documented in multiple archival sources, has been confirmed by historians of Napoleonic France across national traditions, and is uncontroversial among professional scholars of the period. The myth is not a matter of competing historical interpretations; it is a matter of well-documented error that has escaped correction through cultural channels even as it is routinely corrected in academic contexts.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of Napoleon's short-man mythology across two centuries and multiple national contexts is explicable through an unusual convergence of wartime propaganda machinery, measurement system confusion, and the extraordinary staying power of visual media that entered cultural circulation before any corrective infrastructure existed.

**British wartime propaganda and James Gillray.** The most important single source of the Napoleonic short-man myth is the body of caricature art produced by James Gillray and his contemporaries during the Napoleonic Wars. Gillray, working primarily between 1792 and 1811, produced dozens of caricatures depicting Napoleon—whom he nicknamed "Little Boney"—as a tiny, petty, childishly raging figure dwarfed by the British figures he encountered, particularly a towering, phlegmatic King George III. These images circulated throughout Britain in pamphlets, newspapers, and broadsheets at a time when visual propaganda had no effective counter and when British public opinion about the French empire was actively being shaped by government-aligned publishers.

Gillray's artistic conceit was not a report on Napoleon's actual height; it was a propaganda technique exploiting the political potency of visual diminishment. Representing an enemy as physically small maps onto a broader system of associations: small, childish, impotent, insignificant. The caricatures were deliberate character assassination using visual metaphor. Napoleon was made literally small so that British audiences could experience him as figuratively small—as beneath serious consideration, as a puffed-up Corsican adventurer rather than a genuinely transformative military and political force.

These images were extraordinarily effective and widely reproduced. Napoleon himself reportedly found them more damaging than any newspaper editorial and attempted on at least one occasion to have Gillray paid for more favorable treatment. The caricatures entered visual culture with a force that no later documentary evidence could dislodge, because they operated at a level of emotional and visual cognition where written corrections rarely arrive effectively.

**The metric conversion error.** The measurement confusion described above—5 French feet 2 French inches converting to approximately English 5 feet 6.5 inches—propagated through a specific historical pathway. When Napoleon's death was reported in English-language newspapers and records in 1821, the height figure of "5 feet 2 inches" was frequently reproduced without the clarifying unit. English readers, interpreting in English inches, understood a height of 5 feet 2 inches English measurement, which is approximately 157 centimeters—genuinely short by any contemporary standard. The absence of a clear unit conversion produced a systematic underestimate of Napoleon's height that was then passed through subsequent biographies, encyclopedias, and popular histories. This was not deliberate deception; it was the kind of quiet transcription error that standardization bodies were subsequently established to prevent.

**Motivated resonance and psychological utility of the myth.** The Napoleon Complex concept, popularized in Alfred Adler's work on inferiority complexes and subsequently applied to Napoleon specifically, has given the height myth a second life as psychological vocabulary. For people who find assertive, aggressive, or domineering behavior in short individuals to be a recognizable pattern, the Napoleon myth provides convenient validation. The existence of the psychiatric term, however loosely applied, lends scientific-sounding credibility to the observational heuristic. The interplay between the biographical myth and the diagnostic concept is mutually reinforcing: the myth explains Napoleon, and Napoleon validates the complex. Neither needs to be precisely accurate for the loop to be self-sustaining.

**Confirmation bias and cultural reproduction.** Once the short-Napoleon belief is established, it acts as a filter on subsequent encounters with information. People who believe Napoleon was short interpret historical evidence in its favor: his known sensitivity to public opinion becomes defensiveness; his imperial ambition becomes overcompensation; his physical energy and restlessness become compensatory drive. Counterevidence does not register because it lacks the narrative support structure that the confirming evidence receives. The myth is also reproduced by cultural authorities—comedians, filmmakers, novelists—whose works both reflect and reinforce public belief, creating a recursive loop of cultural confirmation.


## 4. Verdict

**False as stated.** Napoleon Bonaparte was not unusually short. His documented height of approximately 5 feet 6 to 7 inches in English measurement (169 cm) placed him at or slightly above average height for a French male of his era. The myth derives from a systematic confusion between French and English measurement units, deliberate British wartime propaganda by caricaturists principally including James Gillray, and the subsequent cultural recursion of images and concepts that entered circulation before corrective mechanisms existed.

The "Napoleon Complex" as a description of Napoleon's own psychology is a theory unsupported by his height, although it has independent clinical currency as a descriptor of a behavioral pattern in short individuals generally. Applied specifically to Napoleon as biographical explanation for his conduct and ambitions, it rests on a false premise.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Napoleon height myth is a case study in the category of historical errors that are not merely persistent but structurally self-reinforcing: claims that benefit from the authority of visual evidence, that have been incorporated into diagnostic or scientific vocabulary, and that serve ongoing psychological and cultural utility for the populations that hold them.

Historical anthropometry—the systematic academic study of historical human heights and their relationships to nutrition, social class, and economic development—has produced reliable estimates of average height across European populations in specific periods. These estimates, drawn from conscript army records, skeletal measurements, and administrative documentation, provide the empirical framework against which Napoleon's height can be measured and contextualized. The data consistently position Napoleon in the average range for his population and era. This research is not obscure; it appears in standard historical and anthropological literature and has been cited in major newspaper corrections and popular history publications.

Yet the corrective has not penetrated popular culture to any meaningful degree. This differential penetration—academic consensus fully established, popular consensus largely unchanged—illustrates a pattern that recurs across historical myth-correction. The corrective must travel the same cultural pathways as the original error or it will fail; dry academic findings do not compete effectively with vivid caricature images, pithy phrases, and psychological labels that have already organized significant cultural furniture.

The propagandistic origins of the myth have additional implications for how we understand historical image production. Gillray's caricatures were not viewed in 1806 as transparent propaganda by most British audiences; they were experienced as wit, entertainment, and cultural commentary. The gap between their propagandistic function and their reception as mere humor is precisely what made them effective. Modern audiences examining the same images are still experiencing them primarily as wit—because the wartime context has faded while the images have not. Separating the aesthetic pleasure of Gillray's draftsmanship from its political genealogy requires historical knowledge that audiences rarely bring to casual media consumption.

The Napoleon case also illuminates the special vulnerability of height claims in historical biography. Height is a quantitative measurement with apparent precision, but its historical documentation is fragmented, unit-dependent, and mediated through translation chains that introduce systematic error. When readers encounter a measurement figure—"5 feet 2 inches"—they experience it as a fact of the same character as a birthdate or a battle location: specific, verifiable, precise. They do not register that different measurement systems were in use, that the figure passed through a translation, and that the translation may have silently dropped the unit. The apparent precision of numerical data is epistemically misleading; it conveys a confidence that the underlying evidence chain may not support.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Napoleon's height is a category of historical myth that does not fit neatly into the standard Snopes fact-check template of claim → evidence → verdict, because the claim is not a recent viral hoax but a centuries-old cultural sediment that was never a single discrete assertion subject to a single adjudication. Fact-checkers addressing it must therefore operate simultaneously as historians, metrologists, and propagandists-of-correction—reconstructing the myth's origins, explaining the measurement confusion, and situating the claim in its wartime propaganda context.

Effective treatments have followed a three-part structure: first, presenting Napoleon's documented height in both French Imperial and English units with explicit conversion; second, establishing the average male height for France in the relevant period using conscript or anthropometric data; and third, tracing the caricature tradition back to Gillray with examples that demonstrate the political function of visual diminishment. This structure allows readers to understand not only that the claim is false but why it arose, which is essential for preventing the kind of continued credence that results when the correction seems implausible given the weight of cultural tradition.

The Napoleon myth is not addressed in Snopes' standard archive as of the publication date of this article, which reflects both the challenge of addressing pre-internet historical myths and the fact-checking ecosystem's recency bias toward viral digital content. It has been addressed by History.com, Smithsonian Magazine, the American Historical Review's public-facing content, and numerous academic biographers. The scholarly consensus is unambiguous; the challenge is penetration into popular discourse.

Science communicators and history educators who have addressed the myth note that the most effective technique is not merely providing the corrected height figure but explaining the French pouce-to-inch conversion explicitly, so that readers can perform the arithmetic themselves. When the calculation is transparent and reproducible, the correction has a different epistemic character than an assertion requiring deference to authority. People who perform the conversion themselves report higher confidence in the corrected figure than those who simply read that the earlier figure was wrong. This suggests a general principle for historical myth correction: make the correction self-verifiable wherever the evidence permits, rather than requiring audiences to accept the word of expert authorities against the weight of their cultural inheritance.
