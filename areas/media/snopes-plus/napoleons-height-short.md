# Napoleon Bonaparte Was Exceptionally Short

## 1. The Claim

The proposition that Napoleon Bonaparte was exceptionally short in stature occupies a peculiar position in popular historical knowledge: it is simultaneously one of the most widely repeated biographical "facts" about any major historical figure and one of the most thoroughly refuted by the available primary evidence. The claim circulates not merely as casual characterisation but as the empirical anchor for whole frameworks of historical and psychological interpretation. Napoleon's shortness is routinely invoked to explain his temperament, his ambition, and the scale of his military campaigns—a biographical shorthand that purports to make a profoundly complex historical figure immediately interpretable through the logic of compensatory psychology.

In its most specific form the claim is numerical: Napoleon stood approximately 5 feet 2 inches tall, placing him in roughly the second or third percentile for adult male height in his era. More frequently the claim circulates in relative rather than absolute terms—Napoleon was shorter than his generals, shorter than his enemies, notably smaller than the British commanders and politicians of the period—with the specific figure less important than the qualitative designation of exceptional smallness. This relative framing has in some ways made the myth harder to debunk than a purely numerical claim would be, because it appeals to the intuitive salience of visual comparisons rather than to measurement data.

The claim also exists in a peculiar self-sealing configuration: the more emphatically Napoleon behaved as a dominant, assertive, militarily aggressive actor, the more the shortness claim appeared confirmed by circular reasoning. To observers already primed by the height narrative, Napoleon's characteristic behaviour patterns—impatience, intensity, disregard for convention, forceful personal presence—became evidence of the height claim rather than evidence about Napoleon's individual character or the norms of military command.

## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rates this claim as "False," and the conclusion is on solid ground. Napoleon Bonaparte's height, reconstructed from multiple independent lines of evidence including contemporary documents, autopsy records, and surviving material artefacts, was approximately 5 feet 6 to 7 inches in modern measurement—roughly 168–170 cm. This placed him in the average-to-slightly-above-average range for a French man of his time and social station. There is no scientific basis for the "exceptional shortness" claim.

The evidentiary foundation rests on several converging sources. The autopsy conducted on St. Helena in May 1821, shortly after Napoleon's death, recorded his body length in French imperial units as approximately 5 pieds 2 pouces—5 feet 2 inches in French measurement. The French pied was 32.48 cm, substantially longer than the English foot of 30.48 cm. At his recorded height of 5 pieds 2 pouces, Napoleon measured approximately 1.686 metres, or 5 feet 6.5 inches in the Imperial system now standard in most anglophone countries. The conversion error from French to English units, made by early British commentators who assumed the measurements were in English feet and inches, created an apparently reliable numerical claim—5 feet 2 inches—that was in fact a systematic measurement artefact (McLynn, 1997, *Napoleon: A Biography*; Chaplin, 1913, *Transactions of the Medical Society of London*).

Supporting evidence comes from his surviving uniform coats, preserved in the Musée de l'Armée and other European collections. Examination of these garments by tailors and military historians confirms proportions consistent with a man of approximately 170 cm—unremarkable stature. Painted portraits, which must be treated cautiously because court painters were subject to idealisation pressures in both directions, nonetheless consistently represent Napoleon as of ordinary adult height when figures with known stature appear alongside him.

The average male height in France circa 1800 was approximately 164–167 cm, somewhat lower than modern averages due to the nutritional and epidemiological conditions of the pre-industrial period (Steckel, 1995, *Journal of Economic Literature*; Heyberger, 2003, *Revue d'histoire économique et sociale*). Against this background, Napoleon's reconstructed height of roughly 168–170 cm places him in the upper half of the French male height distribution for his era—average or marginally above, not exceptional by any metric.

## 3. Epistemic Novelty — Measurement Units as a Vector for Persistent Historical Error

The Napoleon case provides an unusually clear example of a specific failure mode in cross-cultural historical communication: the silent units problem. In the absence of a universal measurement standard—the metric system, though a product of the French Revolution, was not yet internationally adopted or consistently applied in the early 19th century—numerical measurements embedded in documents can travel across linguistic or cultural contexts and be read in alien units without any visible warning that the conversion has been silently dropped.

The silent units failure is epistemically insidious because the output looks exactly like accurate numerical data. The record says "5 feet 2 inches" and the reader interprets it as English feet and English inches because those are the units they think in, and the error produces a plausibly specific number. The mechanism does not look like propaganda or invention; it looks like documentary evidence. This is quite different from myths that originate in active distortion or fabrication, which at least in principle might be caught by source-tracking exercises. A silent units error passes source-tracking precisely because it is based on a genuine historical document.

What makes the Napoleon case uniquely rich is that the silent units error did not operate in isolation. It was amplified and cemented by a concurrent visual propaganda campaign—James Gillray's caricatures—that provided an indelible image of a tiny Napoleon that confirmed what the misread measurement figures appeared to say. The propaganda and the measurement error reinforced each other, each lending apparent credibility to the other. When British readers encountered Gillray's images of a pocket-sized emperor, they read them as exaggerated but directionally accurate; when they encountered documents recording Napoleon's height as "5 feet 2," they read this as confirmation of what the images showed. The two error sources were independent but they converged on the same false claim, creating a doubly anchored myth.

This interaction between visual propaganda and documentary misreading represents the epistemically novel contribution of the Napoleon case: it illustrates how false beliefs can achieve extraordinary resilience when multiple independent-seeming sources of evidence appear to converge on the same claim, even though in reality those "sources" derive from a common, propagandistically motivated origin or from systematic conversion errors applied to a common underlying document. The epistemologist's concept of testimonial bootstrapping—the illegitimate practice of counting multiple reports as independent confirmation when they actually share a source—applies here with unusual clarity.

## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** False  
**Confidence:** High  
**Epistemic Basis:** Documentary, artefactual, and anthropometric evidence converge  
**Phenomenological Status:** Myth  
**Mechanistic Status:** Units-error compounded by propaganda  
**Folk Wisdom Value:** Negative — the myth distorts both historical understanding and psychological theory

The claim that Napoleon was exceptionally short is false. The measurement evidence, the survival artefact evidence, and the testimony of contemporaries who actually encountered him all converge on a height of approximately 5 feet 6–7 inches in modern measurement—a figure indistinguishable from average stature for a French man of Napoleon's era. The numerical claim of 5 feet 2 inches originates from a silent units conversion error—Napoleon's recorded height in French pieds and pouces was misread as English feet and inches by British commentators. This error was magnified and made culturally indelible by the caricature tradition, principally Gillray's wartime propaganda output.

The downstream effects of the myth are culturally significant. The Napoleon complex as a psychological construct, whatever merit its underlying hypothesis may or may not have when studied empirically in actual short-statured populations, rests on a foundationally false historical example. The folk-psychological narrative that Napoleon's world-historical ambition was driven by compensatory drive resulting from physical smallness does not track any real feature of Napoleon Bonaparte.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**The Measurement Problem in Historical Anthropometry:** Height measurements in historical records present systematic challenges for modern interpreters. Prior to the international adoption of the metric system, measurement units varied not only between countries but between regions within countries and even between trades. The French système de mesures used before and during the revolutionary period included the pied du roi (royal foot) of approximately 32.48 cm, divided into 12 pouces, each pouce further divided into 12 lignes. The English foot was 30.48 cm. This approximately 7% difference between units—seemingly small—is exactly enough to transform an average-height figure into an apparently short one when the conversion is applied systematically to all measurements in a person's historical record (Kula, 1986, *Measures and Men*).

Physical anthropologists working with historical military conscription records have developed methods for reconstructing population-level height distributions from period documents that account for these units problems (Bogin, 1999, *Patterns of Human Growth*). These methods reveal that average male height across Western Europe fell in the range of 163–168 cm around 1800, with French and Southern European populations toward the lower end of this range and Northern European populations at the upper end. Napoleon's reconstructed height of approximately 168–170 cm is consistent with the upper tier of the French distribution for his period and class.

**The Guard's Height and the "Petit Caporal" Confusion:** The affectionate nickname "le petit caporal" (the little corporal) given to Napoleon by his troops is frequently cited as evidence of his smallness, and this interpretation has wide popular traction. The actual origin of the nickname is different. Napoleon first acquired it during the Italian campaign of 1796–1797, when his informal manner with common soldiers—visiting their campfires, sharing their hardships, addressing them in familiar terms—contrasted dramatically with the aristocratic distance typical of Ancien Régime officers. The "petit" in the nickname referred not to physical stature but to a kind of democratic informality, a colloquial intimacy. The same soldiers used the title affectionately precisely because it punctured hierarchical formality.

The Imperial Guard additionally presents a specific confounding condition. Napoleon's Old Guard and Middle Guard regiments were selected partly for physical impressiveness; minimum height requirements for the Grenadiers of the Consular Guard were initially set at 1.78 m (approximately 5 feet 10 inches). Among men selected to these standards, any average-height man would inevitably look shorter than his immediate companions. Contemporaries observing Napoleon walk among his Guard were observing a scene in which the visual reference population was systematically selected for above-average height (Elting, 1988, *Swords Around a Throne: Napoleon's Grande Armée*).

**The Gillray Archive and Its Epistemic Status:** James Gillray produced approximately 1,000 caricatures between 1779 and 1809. Of these, a substantial proportion concerned Napoleon and the Napoleonic threat to Britain, beginning to intensify after the Peace of Amiens broke down in 1803. Gillray's working method involved deliberate distortion for rhetorical effect—he exaggerated physical features of all his subjects, including British politicians and royalty, for comic and polemical purposes. His caricatures of William Pitt, Charles James Fox, and George III are as physically distorted as his Napoleon caricatures, though in different directions. Napoleon received the diminishing treatment because physical smallness carried rhetorical valences of impotence and absurdity that served anti-Napoleonic propaganda.

The epistemic error that subsequent generations committed was forgetting that Gillray's images were professional distortions and retroactively reading them as documentary evidence—using the consistency of the physical characterisation across hundreds of images as implied confirmation of the accuracy of the type. This is a visual equivalent of treating frequency of repetition as evidence of accuracy, a well-documented reasoning error. The Gillray tradition was internally consistent because it was produced by one artist with one propaganda agenda, not because it independently converged on accurate physical description.

**The Psychology of Compensatory Ambition:** The Napoleon complex—the claim that short-statured individuals compensate for height disadvantage through heightened aggression, dominance-seeking, and risk-taking—has had a complex empirical history quite apart from its misuse of Napoleon as an exemplar. Alfred Adler's inferiority complex theory (Adler, 1927, *Understanding Human Nature*) provided the foundational theoretical framework. Mid-20th-century popular psychology enthusiastically applied it to Napoleon specifically, creating a circular reinforcement loop: Napoleon was short (false), Napoleon was aggressive, therefore aggression compensates for shortness.

Empirical investigations of the underlying height-aggression hypothesis in actual populations have produced mixed results. Stulp et al. (2013, *PLOS ONE*) conducted a well-designed experimental study using economic games and found that shorter men were somewhat more likely to initiate conflict in resource-competition scenarios, offering partial experimental support for a height-aggression relationship. However, the effect sizes were modest and the relevance to world-historical ambition is entirely unclear. A second study by the same group (2015) found that shorter men in relationships reported greater jealousy in some scenarios. Judge and Cable (2004, *Journal of Applied Psychology*) found that height was positively associated with career earnings and leadership emergence—a finding that complicates rather than supports the overcompensation narrative by suggesting that taller individuals, not shorter ones, achieve more social success.

The research landscape on height and social behaviour is thus genuinely nuanced—neither fully supportive nor fully dismissive of height-linked social dynamics—but this nuance is entirely severed from Napoleon, who was not short.

**Measurement Myths in Neighbouring Historical Cases:** The Napoleon height myth is not unique but belongs to a recognisable class of historical errors in which numerical data from historical records are misread due to units confusion. The weight of Cleopatra's famous tribute to Julius Caesar has been variously misrendered. The dimensions of various historical monuments have been distorted when ancient measurement units (Egyptian cubits, Roman cubits, Greek podes) are read as if modern. The speed of historical sailing vessels has been systematically underestimated when the reported figures used units (nautical miles) that modern readers misidentify.

What distinguishes the Napoleon case is the combination of units error with active propaganda amplification and the downstream consequence of generating a named psychological construct. The myth had real intellectual progeny—the Napoleon complex—that affected clinical psychology textbooks, popular self-help literature, and everyday interpersonal vocabulary for over a century. This scale of downstream consequence is unusual for a historical measurement error.

**Contemporary Resonance and the Myth's Persistence:** Despite extensive scholarly correction, the Napoleon height myth retains enormous cultural resilience. Popular biographies, school curricula, museum labels, and casual conversation continue to repeat it. This persistence can be attributed to several factors. The myth is compact and memorable—a single physical fact that appears to explain an entire grand historical narrative. It has embedded itself in everyday language through the Napoleon complex. It connects to deeper cultural anxieties about the relationship between physical embodiment and power. And the corrective information, while widely available, is not nearly as viscerally compelling as the original image.

The durability of the Napoleon height myth carries lessons for science communication and fact-checking practice. Corrections of embedded cultural myths are systematically less effective than the original misinformation, a well-documented phenomenon in the misinformation correction literature (Lewandowsky et al., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*). Myths with strong visual anchors—in this case, Gillray's images—are particularly resistant because the visual representation continues to be encountered even after the verbal correction has been received.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates the claim as "False" and provides a workmanlike account of the measurement conversion error. Their treatment establishes the basic facts: Napoleon's height in contemporary French records converts to approximately 5 feet 6–7 inches in modern measurement, placing him at average stature for his time. They note the Gillray caricature tradition as a source of the visual myth.

The Snopes analysis is accurate as far as it goes. The gap is in depth of treatment of the epistemological mechanism—specifically, the ways in which a genuine documentary source (the French measurement record) was misread through a specific units error and then amplified by a deliberately manufactured propaganda image. This dual-origin story is more epistemically interesting than a simple "someone got the numbers wrong" account suggests. Understanding that the myth has both an innocent-mistake component (units confusion) and a deliberate-manufacturing component (Gillray's propaganda campaign) is important for understanding why it proved so resistant to correction once established.

Snopes also does not explore the Napoleon complex as a downstream consequence of the myth—a gap that misses an opportunity to show readers that this particular historical error has had real intellectual and cultural costs well beyond the mischaracterisation of a single man's height. The psychological construct of height-driven overcompensation, which still circulates widely in popular discourse, was built on a false empirical foundation, and tracking that consequence gives the fact check genuine practical relevance beyond pure historical correction.
