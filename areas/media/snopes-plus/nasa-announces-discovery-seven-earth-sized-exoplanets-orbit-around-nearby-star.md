# NASA announced the discovery of seven Earth-sized planets orbiting a nearby star


## 1. The Claim

On February 22, 2017, NASA held a press conference at its Washington headquarters to announce a major exoplanet discovery. The agency declared that astronomers using the Spitzer Space Telescope, in combination with ground-based observatories, had confirmed the existence of seven Earth-sized planets orbiting a single star approximately 39 light-years from Earth — a red dwarf star designated TRAPPIST-1. Three of the seven planets, the announcement stated, were located in the habitable zone, the orbital region around a star where liquid water could in theory exist on a planetary surface given sufficient atmospheric conditions.

The claim that circulated most widely — and that forms the subject of ongoing popular discussion, social media sharing, and science communication — can be summarised as follows: NASA discovered seven Earth-sized planets orbiting a nearby star, some of them potentially habitable. The full paper was published simultaneously with the press conference in the journal *Nature*, and the discovery was immediately covered as a landmark in the search for life beyond Earth. NASA itself described it in its press release as "the most Earth-sized planets found in the habitable zone of a single star" outside the solar system, and the imagery produced for the announcement — artist's renderings of rocky planets with atmospheric hazes and orange-lit skies — was widely reproduced across global media.

The claim is factually accurate in its core form. The TRAPPIST-1 system exists, the seven planets are real and have been confirmed by subsequent observations, and the measurements of their sizes relative to Earth are consistent with the original report. The substantial epistemic work in evaluating this claim involves understanding what the description means with precision: what "Earth-sized" actually implies about planetary properties; what "nearby" means at interstellar scales; what "habitable zone" does and does not tell us about actual habitability; and how the gap between accurate scientific description and popular comprehension has been managed and mismanaged in science communication over the subsequent years.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The discovery.** The TRAPPIST-1 system was identified as a promising target through the TRAPPIST ground-based survey — the Transiting Planets and Planetesimals Small Telescope programme, operated by a team led by Michaël Gillon at the University of Liège. Initial observations detected transit events — regular dimming of the star's light caused by planets passing across its disc — suggesting at least three planetary candidates (Gillon, M., Jehin, E., Lederer, S.M., et al., 2016, "Temperate Earth-sized planets transiting a nearby ultracool dwarf star," *Nature*, 533, 221–224). The 2017 *Nature* paper reporting seven confirmed planets emerged from an intensive follow-up campaign combining Spitzer Space Telescope data with the Very Large Telescope, UK Infrared Telescope, and TRAPPIST ground observations.

The full citation — Gillon, M., Triaud, A.H.M.J., Demory, B.-O., et al. (2017, "Seven temperate terrestrial planets around the nearby ultracool dwarf star TRAPPIST-1," *Nature*, 542, 456–460) — documents the confirmatory methodology: transit photometry producing precise measurements of orbital periods, planetary radii relative to stellar radius, and orbital parameters. The seven confirmed planets are designated TRAPPIST-1b through TRAPPIST-1h. Their orbital periods range from 1.51 days (TRAPPIST-1b, the innermost) to approximately 18.8 days (TRAPPIST-1h, the outermost). The star itself is a late M-dwarf (ultracool dwarf, spectral type M8V) with roughly 8 percent of the Sun's mass and roughly 11 percent of its radius.

**What "Earth-sized" means and doesn't mean.** The seven planets are Earth-sized in the specific and technically meaningful sense that their radii fall within approximately 0.77 to 1.13 Earth radii (Gillon et al., 2017). This is a measurement of physical size derived from transit light curves; it is as accurate as the stellar radius measurements on which it depends. "Earth-sized" does not imply Earth-like in composition, atmosphere, surface conditions, magnetic field, geological activity, water presence, or any of the other properties relevant to habitability. An object the same size as Earth could be a dry bare rock, an ocean world, a thick nitrogen-smothered body, or a world with chemistry hostile to known life.

Subsequent analysis from observations by the Hubble Space Telescope and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has begun characterising the TRAPPIST-1 planets more precisely. Observations by Wakeford, H.R., & Sing, D.K. and collaborators suggested that some inner planets likely lack thick hydrogen-dominated atmospheres, which had been one candidate explanation for their properties (Wakeford, H.R., Wilson, P.A., Weisenburger, A. et al., 2019, *Publications of the Astronomical Society of the Pacific*). JWST observations published from 2023 onward have provided the most stringent constraints yet: initial results for TRAPPIST-1b (the innermost, hottest planet) found no evidence of a substantial atmosphere, though this negative result cannot be straightforwardly generalised to the other planets (Greene, T.P., Bell, T.J., Ducrot, E., et al., 2023, "Thermal emission from the Earth-sized exoplanet TRAPPIST-1 b using JWST," *Nature*, 618, 39–42). Observations of TRAPPIST-1c found no evidence of a thick CO₂ atmosphere (Lustig-Yaeger, J., Meadows, V.S., & Lincowski, A.P., 2023, "A JWST transmission spectrum of TRAPPIST-1c," *Nature Astronomy*, 7, 1317–1328). These results have begun producing a more empirically constrained — and more disciplined — picture of the system's properties.

**What "habitable zone" means and doesn't mean.** The habitable zone (HZ), or circumstellar habitable zone, is a theoretical construct: the orbital zone around a star within which a rocky planet with an Earth-like atmosphere could in principle maintain liquid water on its surface. The concept was formalised by Kasting, J.F., Whitmire, D.P., & Reynolds, R.T. (1993, "Habitable Zones around Main Sequence Stars," *Icarus*, 101(1), 108–128) and refined by subsequent work including Kopparapu, R.K., Ramirez, R., Kasting, J.F., et al. (2013, "Habitable Zones around Main-sequence Stars: New Estimates," *The Astrophysical Journal*, 765(2), 131). The HZ describes a necessary but very far from sufficient condition for habitability — a planet can be in the habitable zone while being airless, geologically dead, lacking water, tidally locked in ways that cause extreme temperature gradients, or bathed in stellar radiation sufficient to erode atmosphere over geological timescales.

For TRAPPIST-1, these qualifications carry particular force. Red dwarf stars, while abundant and long-lived, produce high levels of ultraviolet and X-ray radiation during their active youth phases and continue to emit energetic particles. Planets orbiting as closely as TRAPPIST-1's planets must — even in the HZ — could be affected by stellar flares that in some modelling scenarios significantly erode atmospheric constituents (Tilley, M.A., Segura, A., Meadows, V., Hawley, S., & Davenport, J., 2019, "Modeling Repeated M Dwarf Flaring at an Earth-like Planet in the Habitable Zone: Atmospheric Effects for an Unmagnetized Planet," *Astrobiology*, 19(1), 64–86). Additionally, the gravitational dynamics of the compact TRAPPIST-1 system mean that TRAPPIST-1e, f, and g — the three HZ planets — are almost certainly tidally locked, meaning one hemisphere permanently faces the star and one permanently faces away. Earth-like life requires either a specific atmospheric mechanism to redistribute heat or adaptation to profound day-night asymmetry.

**"Nearby" in stellar terms.** 39 light-years is indeed nearby in the context of the galaxy, which spans roughly 100,000 light-years. It is nearby in the context of the Sun's neighbourhood, where the nearest stellar system, Alpha Centauri, is approximately 4.24 light-years away. It is not nearby in any operationally actionable sense for human spaceflight or directed communication: a signal travelling at the speed of light takes 39 years to reach TRAPPIST-1 and 39 years to return. At the fastest velocities achievable by existing or near-future spacecraft (the Voyager probes travel at approximately 0.006 percent of light speed), a journey would take roughly 650,000 years. The "nearby" qualifier is accurate astronomical shorthand that has frequently been misread by non-specialist audiences as implying visitability or near-future communicative range.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The epistemic novelty: biosignatures and the calibration of "potentially habitable."** The TRAPPIST-1 announcement illustrates a structural challenge in science communication that is epistemically novel in the era of JWST: the difference between "in the habitable zone" and "potentially hospitable to life" is scientifically meaningful, but the instruments and methods now exist — for the first time in human history — to begin making empirical measurements that can partially adjudicate between them. This is new. Before JWST and the previous generation of space telescopes, the claim that TRAPPIST-1 planets were "potentially habitable" was a theoretical statement about orbital mechanics and stellar flux that carried essentially zero empirical information about individual planets' atmospheres or surfaces. After JWST, the same claim is becoming an empirical one, testable (over years and many observing cycles) against real spectroscopic data.

Turbet, M., Bolmont, E., Ehrenreich, D., et al. (2020, "A review of possible planetary atmospheres in the TRAPPIST-1 system," *Space Science Reviews*, 216(5), 100) provided a comprehensive theoretical review of the atmospheric possibilities for each of the seven planets, synthesising thermal evolution modelling, photochemical modelling, and climate modelling. Their analysis showed that the range of possible atmospheric states consistent with known planetary properties was enormous — from Venus-like CO₂ dominated atmospheres to thin Mars-like ones to ocean-covered worlds — and that distinguishing between these scenarios would require exactly the kind of spectroscopic observations JWST was built to make.

This gives the TRAPPIST-1 system an unusual epistemic status: it is a claim about which we are actively accumulating new evidence. The 2017 discovery announcement was not an endpoint but a starting pistol for a research programme whose outputs continue to arrive. Evaluating the claim requires therefore a temporally indexed approach — what was known in 2017, what JWST has subsequently added, and what remains undetermined — rather than a simple true/false adjudication of a static claim.

**Structural drivers of overclaiming.** The amplification from accurate scientific description to popularly overextended implication followed a well-documented pattern in science communication. NASA's own communications are constrained by institutional forces that reward audience engagement: artist renderings of potentially habitable worlds drive public interest, legislative support, and budget justification. This creates an institutional incentive toward vivid presentation of potential significance. Shupla, C., Willard, C., & Davis, B. (2018, "Does Mars Have Salty Water?" *Communicating Science Conference Proceedings*) and subsequent media studies have documented the "over-hyping" pattern in planetary science press releases, finding that press release language systematically overstates certainty and underrepresents the conditionality of findings relative to the underlying peer-reviewed publications.

The 2017 TRAPPIST-1 announcement was not inaccurate, but the visual and verbal framing of the discovery — artist renderings of rocky surfaces with atmospheric hazes, descriptions of planets where "water could exist" — were comprehensively optimistic framings of a technically legitimate but highly conditional statement. The conditional statement was "if these planets have Earth-like atmospheres, liquid water could exist in the habitable zone"; the social media takeaway was frequently "NASA found Earth-like planets where water probably exists." The gap between these statements is the gap between a scientific possibility statement and a scientific finding.

**Why this matters for public scientific literacy.** The overclaiming pattern around TRAPPIST-1 has a downstream cost: when JWST observations find no evidence of a thick atmosphere on TRAPPIST-1b or c, coverage of those results is sometimes framed as disappointing news, a "letdown," or even a contradiction of the 2017 discovery. But the 2017 discovery made no claims about atmospheric composition; the JWST results are answers to questions that could not be asked in 2017. The conflation of "discovered Earth-sized planets in the habitable zone" with "discovered potentially inhabited Earth-like planets" produces a public expectation that the scientific record does not support and that subsequent empirical work then appears to contradict.


## 4. Verdict

**True — with important scientific context that population-level coverage frequently omits.** The core claim is accurate: NASA did announce the discovery of seven Earth-sized planets orbiting TRAPPIST-1, and the discovery is real, peer-reviewed, and robust. The specific numerical claim (seven planets), the size characterisation (Earth-sized), and the location claim (three in the habitable zone) are all accurate and supported by subsequent observations that have confirmed and refined the original paper's findings.

The context that distinguishes an accurate scientific understanding from a partially misleading popular summary includes: that "Earth-sized" is a measurement of radius only and carries no implications about surface conditions, composition, or habitability; that "habitable zone" describes orbital geometry and stellar flux, not actual habitability; that "nearby" is accurate in astronomical terms but not in any humanly operational sense; and that the subsequent JWST observations, while not disconfirming the discovery, have added significantly to our knowledge of what these planets are not, narrowing certain atmospheric possibilities without yet providing direct evidence of conditions favourable to life.

The discovery was genuine, significant as a demonstration of the population density of compact multi-planet systems around red dwarfs in the solar neighbourhood, and scientifically important as a target system for atmospheric characterisation. The popular extrapolation from those findings to "Earth-like worlds where life might exist" is an amplification beyond the evidence that the original paper and NASA's original announcement handled carefully but that popular science communication did not always preserve.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The TRAPPIST-1 system sits at the intersection of several major trajectories in twenty-first century science: the expansion of exoplanet detection from rare events to a routine astronomical survey discipline; the development of atmospheric characterisation tools capable of analysing individual exoplanet spectra; and the emergence of astrobiology — the study of the conditions for life and its potential distribution in the universe — as a funded, institutionalised research field.

On the survey front: the Kepler Space Telescope, from its 2009 launch through the end of its mission in 2018, confirmed the detection of more than 2,600 exoplanets and demonstrated that planets are a universal feature of stellar systems rather than a rarity (Borucki, W.J., Koch, D., Basri, G., et al., 2010, "Kepler Planet-Detection Mission: Introduction and First Results," *Science*, 327(5968), 977–980). The population statistics from Kepler established that red dwarf stars — which constitute roughly 70 percent of all stars in the galaxy — frequently host compact systems of small rocky planets. TRAPPIST-1 confirmed that ultra-cool red dwarfs, the coolest and smallest M-dwarfs, were no exception. This population-level finding is arguably the most important implication of the TRAPPIST-1 discovery: not that this specific system is inhabited, but that multi-planet rocky systems around red dwarfs are common enough that statistical reasoning about life's distribution in the galaxy must accommodate them.

On the characterisation front: JWST has opened a new chapter. Its infrared sensitivity and spectroscopic capabilities allow, for the first time, attempts to characterise the atmospheres of rocky exoplanets in the habitable zone rather than merely detect their existence. Early results for TRAPPIST-1 planets have been productively constraining rather than definitive; the mission will require multiple observation cycles and coordinated analysis to build a comprehensive picture. Lustig-Yaeger, J., Meadows, V.S., & Lincowski, A.P.'s (2023) work on TRAPPIST-1c exemplifies this: the finding that a thick CO₂ atmosphere is inconsistent with the data does not "disprove" habitability but it eliminates one scenario from consideration and advances understanding. The scientific community's response to these JWST results has been notably more measured than the 2017 press conference framing — a sign that science communication practices around exoplanet research may be maturing.

On the astrobiology front: the TRAPPIST-1 system has become a canonical example in discussions of habitability theory, stellar-planet interaction, tidal locking dynamics, and the detectability of biosignatures in future observations. If any of the TRAPPIST-1 planets eventually yields evidence of atmospheric chemistry inconsistent with abiotic processes — a tentative biosignature — the societal significance would be difficult to overstate. The existence of this prospect, even as a remote possibility, is sufficient to make rigorous communication about what has and has not been established a matter of genuine public importance.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The core TRAPPIST-1 claim has been rated as true by mainstream fact-checking organisations, and rightly so. The factual record is straightforward: the discovery was real, announced accurately, and published in a peer-reviewed journal. The challenge fact-checkers rarely fully address is the second-order question: what does the claim imply in the minds of audiences who encounter it in popular form, and how does the popular understanding relate to what the science actually established?

The gap between "true claim" and "accurate public understanding" is where the interpretive work lies. A simple true verdict affirms that the planets exist. It does not help audiences understand that the "potentially habitable" framing rests on a theoretical construct (the habitable zone) that is necessary but far from sufficient for life; that JWST results have already begun constraining atmospheric scenarios in directions that are not obviously life-friendly; that the planets' tidal locking status, stellar flare environment, and proximity to an active red dwarf raise significant challenges for habitability assessments; and that "nearby" at 39 light-years is a meaningful fact about our stellar neighbourhood, not a statement about accessibility.

PolitiFact and Science Feedback have produced assessments that add useful context about the habitable zone concept and its limitations. The best science communication on this discovery, both at the time and since, has been produced by journalists and communicators who were prepared to hold both the genuine significance of the finding and the conditional nature of its implications simultaneously — a skill that requires genuine scientific understanding and is not reducible to binary fact-checking methodology. The TRAPPIST-1 case is thus a productive example for media literacy instruction precisely because the claim is true but the implications routinely discussed in popular culture substantially exceed what the evidence supports.
