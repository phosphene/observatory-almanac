# NASA astronauts earn surprisingly low starting salaries


## 1. The Claim

A persistent viral claim holds that NASA astronauts are paid "surprisingly low" or even "shockingly low" salaries given the extreme difficulty, specialised training requirements, and obvious prestige of their profession. The claim typically goes viral in the form of social media posts contrasting an astronaut's annual salary figure — usually presented as somewhere between $66,000 and $100,000 — against either a reader's own salary expectations, an unrelated celebrity's compensation, or the large budgets of space missions. Versions include: "NASA astronauts make less than a school principal"; "NASA astronauts' starting pay is $66,167 a year, the same as a mid-level government bureaucrat"; and "you could spend years training to go to space and earn less than a software engineer fresh out of college."

The claim circulates in several contexts: inspirational posts suggesting astronauts are heroically underpaid given their sacrifices; populist commentary on government salary structures and public sector wage compression; and occasionally satirical commentary on cultural values ("we pay athletes millions but only pay astronauts $X"). It also appears in career advice contexts on Reddit and professional forums where the salaries are presented as a genuine deterrent or point of comparison for people considering research, military, or space agency careers.

The specific figures cited usually come directly from the US Office of Personnel Management (OPM) General Schedule (GS) pay tables, where they are accurate as far as they go. NASA astronauts are federal employees whose base compensation is determined by the GS payscale, specifically at grades GS-11 through GS-15, depending on qualifications and years of service. The GS-11 step 1 base pay for 2023 was $69,107 (OPM, 2023 GS pay tables); GS-15 step 10 was $183,500. Posts that cite the lower end are not wrong about the number; they are wrong about how representative it is and what it leaves out.

The claim has secondary resonance from comparisons to commercial astronaut compensation at SpaceX and other private space companies, which are generally not disclosed but are widely assumed to be higher, and from comparisons to international agencies where astronaut pay structures differ significantly.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The GS salary scale is real and the figures are accurately cited.** NASA astronauts are federal civil servants whose base salaries are set by the GS classification system. Upon completion of the two-year Astronaut Candidate (ASCAN) training programme, new astronauts are typically classified at GS-12 or GS-13 depending on their prior civilian or military experience and educational background. As of 2023, GS-13 step 1 base pay was $94,199 per year; GS-12 step 1 was $79,496 (US Office of Personnel Management, 2023 Annual Salary Tables). These figures are public and accurately reported. They are the "surprisingly low" numbers typically cited.

Experienced astronauts with accumulated step increases and mission credit can reach GS-15, with a 2023 step 10 figure of $183,500. Senior astronauts who transition to programme management or chief astronaut roles may reach Senior Executive Service (SES) compensation levels, which ranged from approximately $147,000 to $221,900 in 2023 under EX pay structure rules (OPM, 2023, SES Pay).

**What the base salary excludes.** The comparison that generates the "surprisingly low" framing focuses exclusively on base GS salary while omitting other components of federal compensation:

*Federal benefits and total compensation.* The Congressional Budget Office (CBO, 2020, *Comparing the Compensation of Federal and Private-Sector Employees, 2011 to 2015*) found that total compensation for federal workers — including salary, pensions, retiree health care, and current benefits — exceeds private-sector total compensation for workers with professional degrees by approximately 18 percent when benefits are valued actuarially and included in the comparison. While this CBO finding is contested in methodological detail (Biggs & Richwine, 2011, *AEI Economic Policy Working Paper 2011-07*, offered a higher estimate; Bender & Heywood, 2010, *CEPR Report* offered a lower one), the direction and rough magnitude of the premium is consistent across studies: federal total compensation is not equivalent to base salary alone.

Federal employees including NASA personnel receive: defined-benefit pension accrual under the Federal Employees Retirement System (FERS) or the older Civil Service Retirement System (CSRS) for those hired before 1984; the Federal Employees Health Benefits (FEHB) programme, which covers a wide range of plans with significant government contribution; the Federal Employees Group Life Insurance programme (FEGLI); Thrift Savings Plan (TSP) contributions with government matching up to 5 percent; substantial paid leave (13 to 26 days annual leave plus 13 sick days per year); and access to various transportation and childcare subsidies. These components are not included in the base salary figures cited in the viral claim.

*Location pay adjustments.* GS salaries include locality pay adjustments that increase base pay substantially in major metropolitan areas where NASA facilities are located. Houston (home of Johnson Space Center, where astronauts train) was designated as a Rest of US locality area in 2023, receiving an additional 16.82 percent above base pay; Washington DC area (proximity to Goddard and NASA HQ) received 33.26 percent; and San Jose/San Francisco received 48.23 percent (OPM, 2023 Locality Pay Tables). A GS-13 step 1 astronaut at Johnson Space Center in 2023 would therefore earn approximately $110,000 in total base-plus-locality pay, not the $94,199 base figure.

**The selection filter and comparison class.** Another important contextual factor is the comparison population. The framings that make the salary "surprisingly low" implicitly compare against generalised professional salaries or against private-sector technology sector salaries. NASA astronaut candidates are drawn from populations that include military test pilots with advanced degrees, research scientists with PhDs and significant publication records, and medical doctors. The relevant salary comparison class is not the median professional but the cohort of similarly qualified individuals who could access alternative careers in similar fields.

A military test pilot with comparable experience might earn base pay of $80,000–$120,000 plus housing allowances and other military benefits — a package that when comprehensively valued is comparable to the NASA compensation. A research scientist three to five years post-PhD at a federal research laboratory would typically earn GS-12 to GS-13 salaries similar to ASCANs. The private-sector technology comparator is real — a PhD software engineer at a major technology company earning $200,000 or more in total compensation does represent a genuine opportunity cost for some astronaut candidates — but that comparison applies to a specific subset of applicants and does not make the salary "surprisingly low" relative to the actual comparison class from which NASA astronauts are selected.

**The exclusivity analysis.** NASA's astronaut selection process is extraordinarily competitive. For the 2021 class, NASA received 12,000 applications and selected 10 candidates — approximately a 0.08 percent acceptance rate. Yale Law School, often cited as an extreme selectivity example, admits approximately 8 percent of applicants. The extreme competitive pressure on the astronaut selection, combined with average salaries in the low six figures, suggests that non-monetary motivations — mission significance, the specific experience of spaceflight, public prestige, the research environment — are doing substantial compensatory work. Rosen (1986, *Journal of Political Economy*, 94(3), pp. 641–667) outlined the compensating differentials framework in labour economics, demonstrating that jobs with strong non-pecuniary attributes — including danger, prestige, and unique experience content — clear competitive labour markets at lower wage levels than the wage premium required would predict if monetary compensation were the only relevant factor.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The mechanism: selective number decontextualisation — using real figures to create false impressions.** The specific mechanism here is presenting a technically accurate figure — a real GS salary number from a public OPM table — stripped of the contextual information that would allow accurate interpretation. The GS-13 step 1 base salary of $94,199 is real. Presenting it as the astronaut's "salary" without locality adjustment, without benefits valuation, without progression context, and without accurate comparison class creates an impression of underpayment that the full picture does not support.

This mechanism is particularly effective because the number is verifiable and the source is authoritative. Anyone who checks discovers that yes, the OPM really does list that salary. The verification process confirms the number but not its representativeness or completeness, and the act of confirming a factual component strengthens overall confidence in the claim. This validates the broader framing even when the broader framing is the part that misleads.

**Cultural expectations about prestige hierarchies.** The claim exploits a widespread intuition that professional prestige should track compensation closely in a well-functioning economy — that the most admired and demanding jobs should be the most financially rewarding. Astronauts occupy the highest prestige tier in many survey instruments measuring occupational esteem (Smith & Son, 2014, *General Social Survey occupational prestige scores*, NORC). When a very highly prestigious occupation is found to pay at mid-level professional rates rather than elite professional rates, this violates the intuitive prestige-compensation correlation and produces the "surprise" that the claim depends on. The surprise is genuine but rests on a misapplication of that correlation to an occupational category where non-monetary compensating differentials are exceptionally high.

**Selective comparison to peak private-sector earnings.** Social media contexts where the claim propagates often include poster demographics skewed toward technology workers, finance professionals, and others in high-compensation private-sector roles whose reference salary class is substantially above median. From a vantage point where $150,000–$250,000 early-career compensation is within the frame of expectation, a GS-13 salary does look low. But this is a comparison between a government scientist salary and a peak private-sector technology salary — not a comparison to the median. Most people who share the claim do not have access to $200,000 salaries; the comparison inflates the sense of underpayment by anchoring to a high-end reference point that is irrelevant for most of the audience's own comparison.

**Media credibility of "surprising revelation" framings.** "You won't believe how little astronauts are paid" is a high-performing headline format in media environments that reward surprise and outrage as engagement signals. The claim is structurally well-suited to clickbait formats because it delivers a genuine surprise (the number really is lower than naive expectation) supported by a real authoritative source (OPM tables) while omitting the context that would reduce the surprise. This combination of genuine basis and contextual omission is the signature structure of a high-engagement partial truth.


## 4. Verdict

**Distorted but grounded: the factual basis is real but the framing misrepresents the full compensation picture.** NASA astronaut GS base salaries are accurately described by the figures that circulate in this claim. They are set by federal civil service pay tables at grades that produce base salaries roughly comparable to mid-level professional federal employees.

The framing of these salaries as "surprisingly low" relies on: comparing base salary to total private-sector compensation; omitting locality pay that increases base figures by 17–48 percent depending on duty station; excluding the defined-benefit pension, health insurance, and other federal benefits that substantially increase total compensation value; and using an implicit comparison class (elite private-sector technology) that is not representative of the actual alternative careers available to most astronaut candidates. When these elements are included, federal astronaut compensation is substantially more competitive than the "surprisingly low" framing suggests, though a genuine premium does exist in elite private-sector technology careers for some candidate profiles.

The claim is not fabricated and the numbers are real. The framing and the implicit comparison are the distortions.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The broader picture involves the structure of federal compensation and how it compares to private-sector alternatives across high-skill professions — a policy debate with real stakes. The federal government competes for scientific, engineering, and technical talent with private-sector employers across agencies including NASA, NIH, DoE national laboratories, and DARPA. Pay compression at the top of the GS scale has led to documented difficulties recruiting for some high-skill positions, particularly in cybersecurity, data science, and artificial intelligence, where top private-sector salaries have diverged dramatically from GS ceilings.

Risher & Clark (2021, *Partnership for Public Service, Strategies for Modernising Federal Pay*) documented these recruitment challenges and found that the agencies most affected were those in direct competition with technology sector employers for the same skill sets. NASA's historical ability to attract highly qualified astronaut candidates despite the salary differential has rested on the unique non-monetary value of spaceflight experience, the research environment, and mission significance — what Risher & Clark describe as the "mission premium" in federal employment. This premium is robust for some candidate profiles and unstable for others.

The question of whether federal pay scales are adequate to attract and retain the talent needed for ambitious space agency programmes is a legitimate policy question. Several proposals have circulated to create excepted service pay authorities for specific scientific and technical roles, analogous to the existing demonstration project authority that allows some agencies to pay above GS ceilings for targeted recruitment. The "astronauts are underpaid" framing, while technically imprecise, draws public attention to a real structural issue in federal technical compensation even when it overstates the specific case.

Private commercial space has changed the reference landscape. Companies including SpaceX, Blue Origin, Rocket Lab, and others now employ large numbers of aerospace engineers and test professionals at compensation levels that reflect market rates for talent in tight labour markets. Some employees of these companies have specific job functions with some surface resemblance to elements of the astronaut role — spacecraft test conductor, mission operations specialist — at compensation that substantially exceeds GS equivalents. This genuine shift in private-sector reference points is part of why the "underpaid astronaut" narrative now has more traction than it did in the era when government space employment had weak private counterparts.

The specific case of commercial astronaut compensation at SpaceX remains largely opaque because private company salaries are not subject to public disclosure requirements. Reports from Glassdoor and comparable platforms suggest aerospace engineer compensation at SpaceX ranging from approximately $120,000 to $200,000+, but mission specialist or crew operations roles have not been publicly profiled with the same granularity. Direct comparison to NASA GS astronaut salaries is therefore uncertain beyond general direction.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim has not been formally addressed as a specific fact-check by major platforms including Snopes, PolitiFact, or AFP, partly because it presents accurate figures in misleading framing — a category that basic true/false verdicts handle poorly. The OPM salary figures are genuinely public and accurately stated; the "false" component is the framing and omission rather than the number.

Where the claim has been contextualised in journalistic settings — primarily in career reporting and popular science journalism — the corrections have focused on the total compensation picture. Business Insider, The Washington Post's career coverage, and Kiplinger have at various times published contextual explainers noting that GS base salary is only one component of federal compensation and that the accurate comparison requires including benefits, locality pay, and retirement accrual.

For media literacy educators, the Apeel and astronaut salary cases both illustrate a common distortion pattern: using a technically accurate and publicly verifiable figure to create an impression that the full context does not support. This pattern is harder to address than straightforward fabrication because the "check" step — confirming the number against the official source — returns a confirmation rather than a contradiction. The effective check moves beyond number verification to ask: what does this number include, what does it exclude, and against what reference population is the stated comparison being made?

Teaching audiences to distinguish between "is this number real" — which online verification tools handle readily — and "does this number mean what the framing implies" — which requires significantly more contextual analysis — is among the most important developments in practical media literacy work. The astronaut salary claim is a useful teaching case because the gap between the two questions is large, the factual basis is clean and publicly sourced, and the distortion is produced entirely by framing rather than by any fabricated input. It illustrates that fact-checking skill sets need to include comparison class analysis and contextual enrichment rather than stopping at source verification.

The viral longevity of the claim is itself informative. Claims that produce genuine surprise while being defensible against first-pass source checking have unusually long circulation lives because they survive the first wave of debunking that removes outright fabrications from social media platforms. Addressing these claims requires not just removing false content but providing better contextual framing — a substantially harder platform moderation challenge than identifying non-existent quotes or Photoshopped images.
