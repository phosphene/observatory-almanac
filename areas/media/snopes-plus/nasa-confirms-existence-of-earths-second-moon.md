## 1. The Claim

Headlines recur with remarkable regularity: "NASA confirms Earth has a second moon." The claim has appeared in various forms since at least 2016, typically triggered by announcements of newly discovered near-Earth objects that maintain some orbital relationship with our planet. The most prominent version centres on 469219 Kamo`oalewa (formerly designated 2016 HO3), a small asteroid discovered in April 2016, which was widely reported as Earth's "new moon," "second moon," or "mini-moon." Subsequent waves of the claim resurfaced with asteroid 2020 CD3 — a roughly one-to-two-metre rock temporarily captured by Earth's gravity — and with periodic coverage of research into quasi-satellite dynamics and temporarily captured orbiters.

The claim contains a kernel of genuine science wrapped in a conclusive-sounding assertion that NASA itself has never actually made. What NASA and affiliated research institutions have confirmed is that several small astronomical objects maintain interesting orbital relationships with Earth — relationships that are real, scientifically significant, and well-documented. What they have not confirmed is that any of these objects is a "moon" of Earth. The gap between these two statements is where the misleading framing lives, and understanding that gap requires a careful walk through orbital mechanics, naming conventions, and the surprisingly slippery definition of what a "moon" actually is.

## 2. What's Actually True

### Kamo`oalewa: The Quasi-Satellite

The centrepiece of most "second moon" claims is 469219 Kamo`oalewa, a near-Earth asteroid discovered on April 27, 2016 by the Pan-STARRS 1 asteroid survey telescope at Haleakalā Observatory in Hawaii (Chambers, K.C. et al., 2016, Pan-STARRS1 Surveys). Pan-STARRS — the Panoramic Survey Telescope and Rapid Response System — was designed to detect moving objects across the sky, particularly near-Earth objects and potentially hazardous asteroids. Kamo`oalewa was one of thousands of objects identified by the survey, but it drew particular attention because of its unusual orbital relationship with Earth.

The object's name is Hawaiian: "Kamo`oalewa" translates roughly to "oscillating celestial object," a name assigned by the International Astronomical Union's Working Group for Small Bodies Nomenclature in recognition of its distinctive motion as seen from Earth. The asteroid is small — approximately 40 to 100 metres in diameter — making it far too tiny to observe with the naked eye.

Kamo`oalewa is what astronomers call a "quasi-satellite." From Earth's perspective, it appears to loop around our planet in a slow, corkscrew-like pattern each year. But this appearance is deceptive. Kamo`oalewa does not orbit Earth. It orbits the Sun, on an orbit that happens to be very similar to Earth's — similar enough that when viewed in a rotating reference frame centred on Earth, the asteroid appears to circle us. In reality, both Earth and Kamo`oalewa are independently orbiting the Sun, and gravitational interactions between the two produce the quasi-satellite configuration. The distinction is not pedantic; it is the entire difference between a satellite and a passing companion.

In 2021, research by Sharkey, B.N.L. et al. published in *Nature Communications* ("Lunar-like silicate material forms the Earth quasi-satellite (469219) Kamo'oalewa," 2021) added a dramatic twist: spectroscopic analysis revealed that Kamo`oalewa's surface composition closely matches lunar silicate material, suggesting it may be a fragment blasted off the Moon by an ancient impact. If confirmed, this would make Kamo`oalewa a piece of our actual Moon — wandering the solar system on its own orbit, cosmically near its origin but definitively not a moon itself.

This origin story made the object even more newsworthy. China's Tianwen-2 mission was, as of 2024, targeting Kamo`oalewa for a sample-return mission, which could confirm or refute the lunar-origin hypothesis. The mission would represent the first attempt to sample a quasi-satellite, and its scientific returns could reshape understanding of impact dynamics in the early solar system.

Crucially, Kamo`oalewa's quasi-satellite relationship with Earth is temporary on astronomical timescales. Dynamical models indicate it will eventually drift away from its current configuration within a few hundred years, transitioning into a different type of near-Earth orbit. It was not always a quasi-satellite, and it will not remain one indefinitely.

### Earth's Trojan Asteroid: 2010 TK7

A separate but sometimes conflated discovery is 2010 TK7, Earth's first confirmed Trojan asteroid, reported by Connors, M., Wiegert, P. and Veillet, C. in *Nature* (2011, Vol. 475, pp. 481–483, "Earth's Trojan asteroid"). Trojan asteroids occupy stable gravitational niches called Lagrange points — specifically, the L4 and L5 points, located 60 degrees ahead of and behind a planet in its orbit around the Sun. Jupiter has thousands of known Trojans; the discovery that Earth had one was scientifically notable but not entirely surprising.

2010 TK7, approximately 300 metres in diameter, sits near Earth's L4 Lagrange point, 60 degrees ahead of Earth in its solar orbit. Like Kamo`oalewa, it orbits the Sun, not Earth. It maintains its position relative to Earth because of the gravitational balance between Earth and the Sun at that Lagrange point — a phenomenon predicted by celestial mechanics centuries before any Trojan asteroids were discovered. Calling it a moon would be akin to calling a car in the adjacent lane of a motorway a "passenger" in your vehicle: it is travelling alongside you, but it is not inside your system.

### Temporarily Captured Orbiters

Perhaps the most literally "moon-like" objects in this family are temporarily captured orbiters (TCOs) — small asteroids that Earth's gravity manages to snag for brief periods. Research by Granvik, M., Vaubaillon, J. and Jedicke, R. (2012, "The population of natural Earth satellites," *Icarus*, Vol. 218, pp. 262–277) modelled the population of these transient visitors and concluded that Earth regularly captures small asteroids as temporary satellites, typically for periods ranging from months to a few years before they escape back into heliocentric orbits.

The best-documented TCO is RH120, a roughly two-to-three-metre object confirmed to have been captured by Earth's gravity from approximately September 2006 to June 2007, spending about a year making several loose orbits of Earth before departing (Kwiatkowski, T. et al., 2009, *Astronomy & Astrophysics*). During its capture, RH120 was, in the most literal kinematic sense, temporarily orbiting Earth — making it something closer to a "moon" than either Kamo`oalewa or 2010 TK7. But it was tiny, temporary, and ultimately just a passing guest.

A more recent example is 2020 CD3, a roughly one-to-two-metre rock detected in February 2020 that was found to have been in a loosely bound orbit around Earth for approximately two years before its discovery. It subsequently escaped Earth's gravitational influence. Like RH120, 2020 CD3 was a legitimate TCO — temporarily captured, temporarily orbiting, and temporarily meeting a generous definition of "satellite." But it was a pebble on a layover, not a permanent companion.

### The Definitional Wall

Here is where the science becomes precise and the popular framing collapses. The International Astronomical Union (IAU) — the body responsible for formal definitions and nomenclature in astronomy — defines a natural satellite as an object that orbits a planet or dwarf planet. The critical requirement is that the object be gravitationally bound to the planet: it must orbit the planet, not the Sun.

Quasi-satellites like Kamo`oalewa fail this test. They orbit the Sun. Their apparent looping motion around Earth is a consequence of similar heliocentric orbits, not gravitational capture. They are not bound to Earth's gravitational well. If Earth suddenly vanished, Kamo`oalewa would continue happily along its solar orbit with only minor perturbation.

Trojan asteroids like 2010 TK7 fail even more clearly. They are held in place by the gravitational architecture of the Sun-Earth system, not by Earth alone. They orbit the Sun at a resonance point.

Temporarily captured orbiters come closest, but their transience works against the intuitive meaning of "moon." When people say "Earth has a second moon," they mean a permanent, stable companion like the Moon. A two-metre rock that drifts through for eighteen months and leaves does not match that expectation.

Planetary scientists have consistently corrected the "second moon" framing. Patrick Michel of the Observatoire de la Côte d'Azur, among other experts in near-Earth object dynamics, has emphasised the distinction between orbital resonance and gravitational capture. The science is not ambiguous. The terminology is rigorous. The framing problem emerges entirely in the translation from technical announcement to popular headline.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The obvious explanation for why "Earth's second moon" claims persist is scientific illiteracy: the public does not understand orbital mechanics, and journalists simplify for clicks. This explanation is not wrong, but it is shallow. The deeper mechanism is more interesting and more forgiving of both the public and the journalists involved.

### The Folk-Category Problem

"Moon" is a folk-category that precedes formal astronomy by millennia. Long before anyone understood gravitational physics, humans looked at the sky and named the bright object that circled overhead each month. When telescopes revealed that Jupiter and Saturn had their own circling companions, the word "moon" was naturally extended. By the time the first spacecraft reached other planets, "moon" had become a comfortable, elastic term: anything significant that hangs around a planet.

This folk definition is functional and intuitive. It captures a pattern — a smaller thing accompanies a bigger thing — without requiring any particular understanding of gravitational binding, orbital eccentricity, or the difference between heliocentric and geocentric trajectories. When NASA announces that a small object "accompanies Earth" or is a "near-Earth companion," the folk definition of moon activates immediately. The object is near Earth. It seems to go around Earth. It is smaller than Earth. Therefore: moon.

The error here is not believing a false fact. It is applying a word across a definitional boundary that the public has no particular reason to know exists. The boundary between "orbits the Sun near Earth" and "orbits Earth" is invisible to anyone who has not studied celestial mechanics. Both descriptions sound like "hangs around Earth," which is what "moon" means in folk astronomy. This is a category-boundary error, not a factual error — and it is sustained not by ignorance of the objects themselves but by the absence of a folk distinction that tracks the formal one.

This matters because it changes how correction should work. Telling someone "Kamo`oalewa is not a moon" without explaining the category boundary just sounds like pedantry. The corrective needs to install a new distinction — between orbiting-the-Sun-nearby and orbiting-Earth-directly — that the person's existing conceptual framework does not contain. This is harder than correcting a simple factual mistake. It requires building new cognitive infrastructure, not just replacing one fact with another.

### The Headline Economy of Quasi-Technical Terms

There is also a structural incentive problem in science communication. "NASA discovers quasi-satellite in 1:1 mean-motion resonance with Earth" is accurate but unreadable outside specialist contexts. "NASA discovers asteroid that follows Earth" is accurate and readable but not exciting. "NASA confirms Earth's second moon" is exciting, readable, and almost accurate — close enough that no single word in the headline is an outright lie, yet the gestalt impression is false.

This is the misleading-framing gap in compressed form. Quasi-satellites really are companions. NASA really did confirm the discovery. The object really is near Earth. The word "moon" is the only piece that is wrong, and it is wrong in a way that requires technical knowledge to detect. Headline writers operating under time pressure and engagement metrics will consistently reach for the exciting framing, especially when the correction requires a paragraph of orbital mechanics rather than a simple substitution of one fact for another.

### The Recurrence Cycle and Memory Decay

A third factor sustains the claim: it recurs. Each new discovery of a quasi-satellite, TCO, or near-Earth companion triggers a fresh wave of "second moon" coverage. Kamo`oalewa in 2016. The Sharkey et al. lunar-origin findings in 2021. 2020 CD3's detection. Periodic coverage of Granvik et al.'s TCO population models. Each wave reaches an audience that may or may not remember the previous wave's corrections.

This recurrence creates a peculiar epistemic environment where the claim accumulates apparent confirmation over time. Someone who vaguely remembers a "second moon" story from 2016 encounters a new story in 2021. The repetition feels like corroboration — "they keep finding these things" — when in fact it represents serial discoveries of different objects, none of which are moons. The claim bootstraps credibility from the sheer volume of coverage without any single article providing false information in isolation. Research into quasi-satellite capture dynamics, such as work by Grishin, E. and Perets, H.B. (2022), continues to generate genuine scientific results that can be repackaged into fresh rounds of the same misleading frame.

### Motivated Astronomical Wonder

Finally, there is a motivational component that is often overlooked. People want the solar system to be more interesting than they learned in school. The idea that Earth has a secret second moon — unknown for centuries, just discovered — taps into a deep appetite for hidden cosmic drama. It recasts the familiar night sky as containing unseen companions, which is a far more romantic narrative than "small rock shares similar orbit." The folk-category error is sustained partly because the "moon" framing is simply a better story, and people are reluctant to abandon better stories for technically correct but less evocative descriptions.

## 4. Verdict

**Mixture — real science, wrong word.**

The underlying astronomy is genuine and well-documented. Earth does have quasi-satellite companions (Kamo`oalewa being the most prominent), at least one confirmed Trojan asteroid (2010 TK7), and a population of temporarily captured orbiters that drift through Earth's gravitational influence on irregular schedules. These are real discoveries, confirmed by peer-reviewed research and observational data. NASA and associated research institutions have legitimately announced these findings.

However, none of these objects is a "moon" in any scientifically accepted sense. The quasi-satellites orbit the Sun, not Earth. The Trojan asteroid orbits the Sun at a Lagrange point. The temporarily captured objects are transient visitors, not permanent satellites. The IAU's definition of a natural satellite requires gravitational binding to the planet — an orbit around the planet rather than around the Sun — and none of the commonly cited objects meets this criterion on a permanent basis.

The claim that "NASA confirmed Earth has a second moon" takes a real discovery and passes it through a definitional filter that distorts its meaning. NASA confirmed the existence of a quasi-satellite. That is not a moon. The framing is misleading, not because it fabricates facts, but because it applies the wrong category to real phenomena. The verdict is mixture: the facts exist, but the characterisation is wrong.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### What These Objects Actually Tell Us

Stripped of the misleading "moon" framing, the objects at the centre of this claim are genuinely fascinating and scientifically important. Kamo`oalewa's possible lunar origin, if confirmed by sample-return missions like China's Tianwen-2, would provide direct evidence of ancient impact events that shaped the Moon's surface. Understanding how fragments ejected from the Moon can enter quasi-satellite configurations around Earth illuminates the dynamics of the inner solar system and the history of bombardment that both Earth and the Moon have experienced.

The existence of Earth's Trojan asteroid population — even if only one confirmed member is known — validates longstanding theoretical predictions about Lagrange-point stability and opens questions about whether larger or more numerous Trojans await discovery. The difficulty of detecting 2010 TK7 (it sits perpetually near the Sun as seen from Earth, making observation challenging) suggests that current surveys may undercount Earth's Trojan population significantly.

Temporarily captured orbiters, meanwhile, represent a dynamic population of small bodies that interact with Earth's gravitational sphere of influence on human-relevant timescales. Granvik, Vaubaillon and Jedicke's (2012) modelling work in *Icarus* suggests that at any given time, Earth is likely accompanied by at least one or two small TCOs, most too faint to detect with current technology. These objects are potential targets for future asteroid-characterisation missions precisely because of their proximity and low relative velocity — they are, in a sense, the most accessible asteroids in the solar system.

### The Definition Wars

The "second moon" confusion also connects to broader definitional tensions in planetary science. The IAU's 2006 redefinition of "planet" — which famously reclassified Pluto as a "dwarf planet" — demonstrated that celestial categories are not fixed natural kinds but negotiated conventions. The boundary between "moon" and "not a moon" is similarly convention-dependent. If the definition of natural satellite were loosened to include any object that maintains a persistent spatial relationship with a planet, quasi-satellites might qualify. If it were tightened to require long-term orbital stability, even some recognised moons of the outer planets (which may be temporarily captured objects on long timescales) might fail.

These definitional questions are not merely academic. As survey telescopes become more powerful — the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) is expected to discover thousands of new near-Earth objects — the boundary between "accompanies Earth" and "orbits Earth" will be tested by a growing catalogue of objects that resist clean categorisation. The "second moon" claim may be premature and misleading today, but it points toward a genuine taxonomic challenge that planetary science will need to address as observational capabilities expand.

### Quasi-Satellites in the Solar System

Earth is not unique in having quasi-satellite companions. Venus, Jupiter, Saturn, Neptune, and possibly other planets also host quasi-satellites — objects in 1:1 mean-motion resonance that appear to orbit the planet in a co-rotating frame but truly orbit the Sun. The study of these objects has become a subfield of its own within celestial mechanics. Theoretical work on the stability and capture dynamics of quasi-satellites, including research by Grishin, E. and Perets, H.B. (2022), has shown that these configurations arise naturally from gravitational perturbation and do not require special initial conditions. Quasi-satellite capture is a routine feature of planetary dynamics, not an exotic anomaly.

This context is largely absent from popular reporting. When a new quasi-satellite is announced, it is treated as a singular, extraordinary discovery rather than as the latest member of a well-understood dynamical class. The "second moon" framing amplifies the novelty of each discovery while obscuring the underlying science that makes quasi-satellites unremarkable in the technical literature. The gap between the scientific normality and the popular astonishment is itself a marker of how far science communication has to travel.

### Resource and Mission Implications

Near-Earth companions — whether quasi-satellites, Trojans, or TCOs — have practical significance beyond pure science. Their proximity and low relative velocities make them attractive targets for space missions aimed at asteroid characterisation, resource assessment, and planetary defence testing. The China National Space Administration's Tianwen-2 mission to Kamo`oalewa, if successful, would demonstrate the feasibility of sampling objects in quasi-satellite orbits, potentially opening a new class of accessible targets for future missions.

In the longer term, TCOs could serve as natural test cases for asteroid redirect and deflection technologies. A two-metre rock temporarily orbiting Earth is precisely the kind of object that a kinetic impactor or gravity tractor mission might target for practice, and capturing or redirecting such an object could be achievable with near-term technology. The scientific and practical value of these objects is real — it simply has nothing to do with them being "moons."

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The "NASA confirms Earth's second moon" claim presents a characteristic challenge for fact-checking: it is not cleanly true or false. The facts underneath are solid. The framing on top is wrong. This structure — real data, wrong interpretation — places the claim squarely in the "mixture" category that organisations like Snopes, PolitiFact, and Full Fact use for assertions that contain both accurate and inaccurate elements.

Effective fact-checks of this claim typically follow a three-step structure. First, they confirm what was actually discovered: a quasi-satellite, a Trojan, or a temporarily captured object, with specific citations to the relevant research (Chambers et al., 2016; Connors, Wiegert and Veillet, 2011; Sharkey et al., 2021; Granvik, Vaubaillon and Jedicke, 2012). Second, they explain why the discovered object does not meet the definition of a moon, introducing the distinction between heliocentric and geocentric orbits. Third, they identify the point of distortion: typically the translation from a technically accurate NASA announcement to a simplified headline that substitutes "moon" for "quasi-satellite" or "companion."

The main complication is temporal. Because the claim recurs with each new discovery, fact-checks have a short shelf life. A thorough debunking of the 2016 Kamo`oalewa coverage does not prevent the same claim from resurfacing in 2021 when Sharkey et al.'s lunar-origin paper generates new headlines, or in 2020 when 2020 CD3's temporary capture is reported. Each new trigger requires a fresh round of correction, often directed at an audience unfamiliar with the previous round.

Some science communicators have attempted a more structural approach: rather than debunking individual claims, they explain the full taxonomy of near-Earth objects — quasi-satellites, Trojans, TCOs, horseshoe-orbit companions — so that readers can classify future discoveries themselves. This approach is more durable but demands more of the reader. It requires installing the category-boundary distinction discussed in Section 3, which is cognitively expensive and competes with the simpler, more exciting "second moon" narrative.

The EARC mode for this claim is **E (Evolving)** because the underlying science is active. New quasi-satellites will be discovered. Tianwen-2 may return samples from Kamo`oalewa that reshape understanding of its origin. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory's LSST will catalogue near-Earth objects at unprecedented scale. Each development creates a new opportunity for the "second moon" framing to resurface. The claim is not settled in the way that a debunked conspiracy theory can be settled; it is a living interface between ongoing research and persistent public misunderstanding.

The gap category — **misleading-framing** — captures the essential character of the error. No one fabricated an object. No one invented data. The objects are real, the research is peer-reviewed, and NASA's announcements are accurate. The misleading element is purely in the categorical framing: calling these objects "moons" when they are not. This type of error is among the hardest to correct because it does not involve competing claims of fact but rather competing interpretations of what a fact means — and the misleading interpretation is shorter, simpler, and more exciting than the accurate one. That asymmetry is the core challenge, and it is unlikely to resolve as long as the folk concept of "moon" remains broader than the astronomical one.
