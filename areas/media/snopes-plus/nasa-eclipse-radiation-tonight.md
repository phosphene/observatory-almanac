# "NASA warned about dangerous radiation from tonight's eclipse"


## 1. The Claim

In the days and hours before any widely anticipated solar or lunar eclipse, social media platforms predictably generate a specific and recurring false warning: that NASA or other space agencies have issued urgent public safety advisories about dangerous radiation associated with the upcoming celestial event. The claim has appeared before lunar eclipses, total solar eclipses, partial solar eclipses, annular eclipses, and even penumbral lunar eclipses—celestial events notable primarily for their visual spectacle rather than any anomalous physical properties.

The claim takes various specific forms. In some versions, NASA warns that the eclipse generates or releases a burst of harmful radiation—sometimes described as cosmic radiation, gamma rays, ultraviolet radiation spikes, electromagnetic pulses, or vaguely characterized "space radiation"—that poses health risks to humans outdoors. In other variants, the alignment of the Sun, Moon, and Earth during an eclipse is claimed to intensify radiation passing through the atmosphere in ways that cause increased cancer risk, cellular damage, or acute radiation sickness. Some iterations assert that NASA has warned pregnant women or children to remain indoors, that immune-compromised individuals face elevated risk, or that the radiation can penetrate buildings through windows.

More specific iterations of the claim sometimes include fabricated NASA advisory text, complete with official-sounding language about "elevated ionizing radiation" or "disrupted magnetospheric shielding," alongside NASA logos or formatting designed to mimic authentic agency communications. Screenshots of these fabrications circulate widely on mobile messaging applications, particularly WhatsApp and Telegram, where they move beyond fact-checker visibility before the eclipse concludes.

A partial basis for the claim exists in genuine astronomical phenomena that bear some terminological relationship to the language used in the hoax. NASA does monitor solar activity, including solar flares and coronal mass ejections, which genuinely can affect radiation levels at orbital altitudes and in polar regions. Space weather events, particularly during elevated solar activity approaching solar maximum, can produce geomagnetic storms with downstream effects on satellites, power grids, and high-frequency radio communications. These genuine phenomena provide the conceptual raw material that is then stripped of context, inflated beyond recognition, and attached to eclipse events with which they have no relationship.

The "NASA warning" frame is specifically important to the claim's persuasive architecture. NASA represents scientific authority, government institutional credibility, and technical expertise about space in public imagination. A purported NASA warning bypasses lay skepticism about radiation claims by invoking an authority presumed to know—an authority that, in reality, issued no such warning and would not issue one, because no such hazard exists.


## 2. What's Actually True

Eclipses—solar or lunar—do not produce, release, increase, or focus radiation in ways that pose health risks to humans. This statement reflects fundamental physics rather than institutional policy. Understanding why requires briefly understanding what an eclipse is physically.

A solar eclipse occurs when the Moon moves between the Earth and the Sun, casting the Moon's shadow onto Earth's surface. The portion of Earth's surface within the Moon's umbra (the darkest central shadow) experiences totality—a brief period during which the Sun's visible disk is completely blocked by the Moon. The partial solar eclipse zone extends more broadly across regions within the Moon's penumbra. During a lunar eclipse, the Earth moves between the Sun and the Moon, casting Earth's shadow onto the lunar surface.

Neither event changes the radiation environment at Earth's surface in any meaningful way. The Sun's electromagnetic radiation output—across the spectrum from radio waves through visible light through ultraviolet through X-ray—is not increased, redirected, concentrated, or otherwise altered by the geometric alignment that produces an eclipse. The Moon blocks light from the Sun during a solar eclipse, reducing rather than increasing the radiation reaching Earth's surface in the eclipse zone. The Earth's atmosphere, magnetosphere, and Van Allen radiation belts continue performing normal radiation-shielding functions during eclipse events. No physical mechanism exists by which an eclipse could cause dangerous radiation.

The one genuine, well-established safety concern associated with solar eclipses is solar retinopathy—eye damage caused by looking directly at the Sun. The hazard is not eclipse-specific in the sense of the eclipse generating new risk; rather, solar eclipses attract more people to look at the Sun directly than do ordinary days, and during totality, the dimming of ambient light causes pupil dilation that leaves the retina more exposed when the bright photosphere reappears. Certified eclipse glasses with ISO 12312-2 optical filters, which reduce solar light transmission to roughly 1/100,000th, provide adequate protection. This is NASA's actual eclipse safety guidance, and it has been communicated consistently for decades by NASA, the American Astronomical Society, and ophthalmological organizations.

Viewing the eclipse itself through properly certified eclipse glasses, pinhole projectors, or during the brief totality phase (for total solar eclipses only) is completely safe. The radiation hazard from staring at the Sun is the same during an eclipse as on any ordinary day—the eclipse context simply creates circumstances where more people are motivated to stare, making the risk socially elevated even if physically unchanged.

NASA does issue genuine space weather alerts through its Space Weather Prediction Center (operated in partnership with NOAA) and through the Goddard Space Flight Center's heliophysics research programs. These alerts address solar flares, coronal mass ejections, geomagnetic storm indices, and predicted impacts on satellite operations, radio communications, and high-latitude power infrastructure. They are not issued in connection with eclipse events and do not use language about radiation dangers to ordinary civilians on Earth's surface during eclipses.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The eclipse radiation hoax persists and recurs partly because it exploits a genuine cognitive gap in public understanding of space and radiation. For most people, space is vaguely associated with danger—astronauts wear protective suits, solar panels on spacecraft are sensitive to radiation, and news coverage of space weather mentions radiation hazards. The conceptual boundary between "radiation that matters in space" and "radiation that affects you at home" is not intuitively obvious to non-scientists, creating a plausible surface for claims that collapse that distinction.

Eclipses occupy a peculiar psychological space. They are genuinely extraordinary natural events—rare, visually dramatic, and capable of producing awe and mild unease even in well-informed observers. The eerie dimming of daylight, the sudden visibility of stars at noon, the drop in temperature, and the appearance of Baily's beads and the corona during totality combine to create an experience that feels profoundly unusual. This phenomenological strangeness makes the atmosphere ripe for amplified caution and mild superstition about unusual physical conditions during the eclipse period, even among people who intellectually understand the physics.

The hoax's recurring format—"NASA says stay indoors tonight"—is specifically calibrated for mobile messaging environments. The claim is short, urgent, comes from a trusted-sounding authority, requests a simple precautionary action (stay home), and carries virtually no verification friction. Forwarding a safety warning costs nothing and carries social benefit if the warning turns out to be true; not forwarding a warning and being responsible for a neighbor's radiation injury carries imagined social cost. This asymmetry creates strong forwarding incentives even among people who have mild doubts about the claim's authenticity.

The hoax also exploits and conflates genuine NASA communication about eclipse eye safety with the fabricated radiation warning. NASA genuinely does publish eye safety guidance before major solar eclipses, reminding viewers to use certified eclipse glasses. People who have vague memories of seeing NASA eclipse safety communications may conflate this with the "dangerous radiation" fabrication, lending the false claim credibility by association with genuinely remembered official communications.

Health-protective caution is evolutionarily adaptive and socially reinforced, creating what behavioral scientists call an omission bias—the preference for precautionary inaction over risky action under uncertainty. Staying indoors during an eclipse costs relatively little compared to what would be lost if the radiation warning were true, activating precautionary logic even for individuals who are skeptical but not certain. This precautionary heuristic is exploited by safety hoaxes broadly, not just eclipse-related ones.


## 4. Verdict

**False on the central claim; partially true on the underlying eye-safety issue.** NASA has not issued and would not issue warnings about dangerous radiation associated with eclipses. No such radiation hazard exists. The physical mechanism described—eclipses producing, releasing, or concentrating harmful radiation—contradicts fundamental astrophysics and atmospheric science.

The claim is a recurring hoax that propagates predictably before every major eclipse event, drawing on fragmented public knowledge about space radiation and legitimate NASA eclipse communications to construct a plausible-seeming but entirely fabricated safety advisory.

The genuine safety concern associated with solar eclipses—retinal damage from unprotected direct viewing of the Sun's photosphere—is real, documented, and irrelevant to the "radiation" framing of the hoax. Eye protection guidance for solar eclipse viewing is accurate and important; it does not involve radiation beyond the ordinary hazard of staring at the Sun.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The eclipse radiation hoax belongs to a recognizable category of what information scientists call "recurring event-triggered misinformation"—false claims that are systematically generated, spread, debunked, and forgotten, only to re-emerge at the next opportunity when the triggering event recurs. Eclipses are particularly reliable triggers because they occur on predictable schedules, receive substantial advance media coverage that broadly familiarizes potential audiences with the upcoming event, and provide the "tonight" urgency that makes safety warnings feel immediately actionable.

Research on health-related misinformation by groups including First Draft News, the Reuters Institute for the Study of Journalism, and the Harvard Kennedy School's Shorenstein Center has documented that health safety hoaxes consistently outperform corrections on mobile messaging platforms. The combination of health stakes, plausible authority framing, brevity suited to mobile sharing, and easy precautionary action creates near-optimal conditions for rapid viral spread. Eclipse radiation warnings specifically circulate primarily through WhatsApp and Telegram rather than indexed social platforms, placing them substantially outside the reach of real-time fact-checker responses and algorithmic intervention.

The recurrence of the eclipse radiation hoax also reveals important features of how communities process scientific authority. NASA is trusted as an institutional authority, but that trust is available for exploitation by any sufficiently convincing impersonation of NASA communication. The fabrications that attach NASA's name to false radiation warnings do not require recipients to verify them against NASA's actual publications; the name alone transfers credibility to the false claim. This pattern—legitimate authority exploitation rather than transparent fabrication—is characteristic of health misinformation more broadly, and understanding it is central to developing effective media literacy.

A notable variant of the eclipse radiation claim involves electromagnetic sensitivity claims—the assertion that some individuals with "electrohypersensitivity" or similar conditions are particularly vulnerable to eclipse-related radiation. Scientific consensus does not recognize electrohypersensitivity as a diagnosable physiological condition; double-blind challenge studies find that individuals claiming this sensitivity perform at chance levels when asked to identify whether electromagnetic fields are present. Eclipse-specific variants of electromagnetic sensitivity claims extend the hoax into alternative health communities with different primary concerns while sharing the same core factual error about eclipse radiation.

It is worth acknowledging what NASA genuinely communicates about eclipses, because the hoax's power depends partly on the genuine NASA-eclipse communication relationship. NASA runs extensive public eclipse education programs, deploying citizen science networks to collect atmospheric data during eclipses, broadcasting totality paths, providing interactive maps, organizing school viewing programs, and coordinating with state and local governments on traffic and public safety management for major eclipses that draw millions of viewers to prime viewing corridors. This genuine institutional engagement with eclipse events provides the background credibility that the radiation hoax parasitizes.

The 2017 and 2024 total solar eclipses in the United States both generated substantial volumes of radiation-warning misinformation in their run-up periods, each time prompting responses from NASA's official social media accounts and from organizations like the American Astronomical Society and the Eclipse Megamovie Project. The pattern of debunking followed by the claim's re-emergence essentially unchanged suggests that one-time corrections have limited durability against hoaxes designed to recur with each triggering event.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The eclipse radiation warning has been addressed by fact-checkers before each major eclipse event in recent years, typically by Snopes, PolitiFact, AFP Fact Check, Reuters Fact Check, and numerous local science journalists embedded in communities preparing for eclipse viewing events. The fact-checking approach is relatively uniform: identify the specific fabricated NASA advisory circulating, confirm via NASA's actual communications that no such advisory was issued, explain the physics of why eclipses do not produce radiation hazards, and provide accurate information about the genuine eye-safety concern.

The challenge for fact-checkers is structural rather than evidentiary. The facts of the case are unambiguous and easily established; what is difficult is the timing and reach problem. Eclipse radiation warnings tend to peak in the 24 to 48 hours before an eclipse event, exactly when readers are most motivated to forward precautionary messages and least likely to pause for verification. Fact-check articles published in this window compete against messaging-app saturation of the false warning in an environment where fact-check reach is already limited by platform dynamics.

NASA and the American Astronomical Society have adopted proactive communication strategies for this reason, pre-publishing eclipse safety information well before major events specifically to establish the "NASA said X" narrative before hoaxes can colonize NASA's authority with "NASA said Y." The strategy has met with mixed success, as it requires users to have encountered the accurate information before they encounter the misinformation, which cannot be reliably engineered.

Linguist and media scholar Danah Boyd's framing of "context collapse" is useful here: in mobile messaging chains, NASA's actual carefully contextualized eclipse safety communications and a fabricated radiation warning share the same stripped, decontextualized form—a few sentences, perhaps an image—indistinguishable in format from the content that surrounds them. Media literacy interventions that focus on teaching audiences to check sources and verify claims are individually sound but face an inherent scale problem against message-chain propagation of health warnings, where the social trust relationship between sender and recipient is primary and source verification is not part of the forwarding culture of most messaging communities.

The eclipse radiation hoax, viewed longitudinally across multiple eclipse cycles, serves as an instructive case study in how the same false claim can be repeatedly and thoroughly debunked while remaining effectively impervious to correction at scale. The debunking record is extensive and authoritative; the hoax's recurrence suggests that individual claim falsification, however well-executed, is insufficient as a misinformation countermeasure against claims that exploit institutional authority, health anxiety, and mobile sharing dynamics simultaneously.
