## The Claim

Beginning in late 2020, advertisements began circulating on Facebook promoting a product called Powersave — a plug-in device claimed to reduce household electricity bills by as much as fifty percent. The advertisements centered on a compelling narrative: an eighteen-year-old genius named Nathan Gilroy, who had grown up in the poorest neighborhood of St. Louis, had invented a revolutionary energy-saving technology. Gilroy, according to the promotional materials, had subsequently graduated from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) at a precociously young age. His device, the story went, was now helping struggling poor families across America cope with crippling electricity costs.

A video accompanied the claims. In it, a young teenager appeared — silent throughout, his supposed words conveyed instead via text overlaid on the footage. Testimonials from alleged users appeared in the same format: talking heads on screen, their voices never heard, their words presented as text captions. Ancillary websites published what purported to be biographical facts about Gilroy, reinforcing the MIT graduation claim and elaborating on his rags-to-genius backstory.

By August 2023, the same product had resurfaced under new branding. Now it was called BoldHero. The footage appeared to be repurposed from the original Powersave campaign, with an entirely new fictional inventor: a man named Noah Watson, who this time had grown up poor in Jacksonville, Florida, raised by a single mother before attending Harvard. The device, the story, and the emotional architecture were identical. Only the names had changed.

The claim Snopes investigated: that Nathan Gilroy, an MIT-educated inventor from St. Louis, had created and sold a legitimate energy-saving device that could reduce home electricity consumption by half.

## What's Actually True

Snopes investigated the claim in January 2021, rating it **False**, and updated its findings in August 2023 to document the BoldHero rebrand.

The investigation had two distinct components: whether Nathan Gilroy exists, and whether the Powersave device works.

**On the existence of Nathan Gilroy:** Snopes contacted MIT directly. A representative from the university's media relations team explained that MIT's standard policy is not to definitively confirm that a given person did not attend, since individuals may have enrolled under different names or participated in special programs. In this case, however, MIT made an exception: the Registrar's Office specifically checked and found no record of any enrolled student by the name Nathan Gilroy. The educational credentials that formed the cornerstone of Gilroy's credibility were entirely fabricated.

Beyond the MIT issue, Gilroy has essentially no verifiable online presence outside the promotional materials themselves. A real person, particularly one alleged to have achieved the extraordinary distinction of graduating from one of the world's most competitive technical universities at a young age, would leave traces: admissions announcements, alumni records, local news coverage, academic publications, social media activity independent of the product being sold. Gilroy leaves none of these. There is no photograph of him not connected to the Powersave campaign. There are no interviews in which he speaks. There is no verifiable identification.

The video's production choices are in themselves revealing. The young man depicted never speaks. His purported statements are conveyed through text overlays. The testimonials are presented the same way — faces visible, voices absent. This is a deliberate technique: it allows the video to present a convincing visual impression of endorsement without any audio that could be tracked, verified, or attributed. If the young man in the video is not Nathan Gilroy — if he is a stock footage subject, an actor hired for a shoot, or a person whose footage was obtained without their knowledge — there is no audio recording that could prove it. The voicelessness is not a technical limitation; it is a legal and forensic precaution.

**On the device itself:** The Powersave product, described variously as a "smart device plugged into socket" and a "voltage stabilizer," has no credible technical mechanism for producing the savings it claims. Claims of fifty percent reductions in household electricity bills are extraordinary and would represent a fundamental departure from known electrical engineering principles. Domestic electricity billing is based on energy consumed over time (kilowatt-hours); a plug-in device cannot reduce the underlying energy draw of the appliances connected to the same electrical system unless it performs active power factor correction, and even genuine power factor correction devices produce only marginal savings for residential consumers whose billing is based on real power rather than apparent power.

Amazon reviews for the product — sold under the Powersave name at the time of the original investigation — were overwhelmingly negative, with numerous buyers describing it as useless or a scam. The consumer review channel Scamadvisor called it "a complete waste of money" and noted that the physical product, when ordered, looked substantially different from how it had been advertised. The product has cycled through multiple brand names — ElectroPro, Power Saver, Powersave, BoldHero — which is itself a red flag: legitimate products with established positive reputations have no incentive to rebrand. Serial rebranding is a standard defensive tactic for products that accumulate negative reviews and regulatory attention under one name, allowing them to reset their review profile and evade consumer complaints tied to the previous brand.

## Why People Believe This

The Powersave / Nathan Gilroy campaign is a carefully engineered persuasion apparatus, and understanding why it works illuminates both the specific techniques employed and the deeper emotional vulnerabilities being exploited.

**The aspirational underdog narrative is among the most emotionally compelling story structures in American culture.** A poor kid from a neglected neighborhood who is also a genius — who transcends poverty through intellect and invention — is a story that activates deeply held values: the belief in meritocracy, the resonance of the underdog, the fantasy that intelligence alone can overcome structural inequality. This narrative is not incidental decoration on the Powersave advertisement; it is the product's primary marketing asset. The choice of St. Louis' "poorest neighborhood" and the MIT credential are specific and deliberate. MIT functions as shorthand for the highest possible intellectual legitimacy. The combination says: this is not a grifter selling you something; this is a genius you should be honored to support.

**Economic anxiety is the product's target market.** The promise of cutting electricity bills by fifty percent is aimed at people for whom electricity costs are a real and pressing concern — families on tight budgets, households in high-energy-cost regions, people who have seen their utility bills rise and are looking for any way to reduce them. The claim resonates specifically with people who are already anxious about energy costs and who do not have the technical background to evaluate whether a plug-in device could plausibly achieve what is being claimed. The product is positioned as the solution to a real problem, which makes skepticism of the solution feel like passive acceptance of the problem.

**The voiceless video format exploits the gap between seeing and verifying.** We are habituated to trusting video — especially video that appears to show a real person in a real setting. The teenage inventor appears to be genuinely present and genuine; his silence reads, to most viewers, as shyness or editing rather than as the forensic precaution it actually represents. We tend to evaluate claimed identities in video by whether the person looks right, not by whether we can independently verify who they are. This is a cognitive shortcut that served well in a world where fabricating convincing video was difficult; it is exploited systematically in a world where it is not.

**The Facebook advertising ecosystem enables precision targeting.** Facebook's advertising platform allows advertisers to target users based on economic indicators, location, age, and behavioral signals. This means the Powersave ad did not reach the broad population indiscriminately; it was served disproportionately to users whose behavioral and demographic profile suggested they would be receptive to an energy-saving pitch. The audience that saw the ad had already been pre-selected for vulnerability.

**Social proof via testimonials is a standard persuasion technique employed here with extra insulation.** The voiceless testimonials — faces talking, words displayed as text — provide the visual impression of social proof (other real people endorsing the product) while avoiding any verification pathway. Real testimonial videos can be tracked; actors can be identified; false claims can be investigated. Text overlaid on mute video leaves no such trail.

**The MIT credential is precisely calibrated to overwhelm critical evaluation.** When someone claims MIT graduation, this triggers a strong halo effect. We associate MIT not only with intelligence but with rigor, evidence-based thinking, and technical legitimacy. The credential is designed to answer the question "but does it actually work?" before that question is consciously asked. If an MIT graduate invented it, the reasoning goes, surely it works — an MIT engineer would never put his name on a scam. The credential is doing the work of suppressing skepticism rather than satisfying it.

**Serial rebranding exploits platform memory limitations.** Each rebrand — to BoldHero, to ElectroPro, to whatever comes next — resets the negative review trail, confuses anyone who tries to research the product, and creates the impression of a new and different product. The core persuasion architecture is preserved precisely because it works; only the brand assets that have accumulated negative attention are changed.

## Verdict

The claim is **False** on both its central contentions. Nathan Gilroy does not exist as described: MIT has no record of him as a student, he has no verifiable online presence outside the promotional campaign, and the video presenting him is constructed in ways consistent with fabrication rather than documentation. The Powersave device has no credible mechanism for achieving the claimed fifty percent reduction in electricity bills, has accumulated overwhelmingly negative consumer reviews, has been condemned by consumer protection commentators, and has serially rebranded — all indicators of a fraudulent consumer product rather than a genuine invention.

Truth Vault classification: **Distorted But Grounded**. The underlying economic anxieties the campaign exploits — real energy costs, real concerns about electricity bills, real hope that technology might offer relief — are genuine. The product and its fictional inventor are fabricated responses to those genuine needs.

The Powersave / BoldHero case is an example of what might be called **persona-scaffolded consumer fraud**: a scam product that creates an elaborate invented human backstory to lend legitimacy, rather than relying solely on product claims. The human story is the vehicle; the product is the payload.

## The Wider Picture

The Powersave campaign belongs to a recognizable genus of energy-saving device scams that have circulated for decades. These products typically claim dramatic reductions in electricity bills through mechanisms they describe in vague technical language — "voltage stabilization," "power factor correction," "harmonics reduction" — that sounds plausible to non-specialists and is difficult to trivially disprove without either technical knowledge or a power meter to test the device. The vagueness is structural: specific, testable technical claims would be more easily investigated and debunked; fuzzy mechanistic language operates in the space of plausibility rather than falsifiability.

What distinguishes the Powersave campaign from earlier iterations of the energy-saving device scam is the sophistication of its persona construction. Older versions of the scam relied on celebrity endorsements (often fabricated), scientific jargon, or testimonials from unnamed satisfied customers. The Nathan Gilroy campaign built an entire biographical identity — including a social origin story specifically calibrated to emotional resonance — around a fictional inventor. This represents an escalation in the investment required to run the scam, which reflects both the increasing skepticism of consumers and the increasing ease with which persuasive video content can be produced.

The 2023 BoldHero rebrand, with its replacement fictional inventor Noah Watson, confirms that the underlying template is being run as a reusable scam architecture rather than a one-off operation. The substitution of Harvard for MIT, of Jacksonville for St. Louis, of Watson for Gilroy, is purely cosmetic. The emotional architecture — poor kid, elite institution, miraculous device, voiceless video — is identical. The operators appear to have determined that the persona-scaffolding model is effective enough to re-deploy with modified surface features when the previous iteration has accumulated too much negative attention.

This pattern — a modular scam framework populated with different brand identities — is increasingly common in the Facebook advertising ecosystem. The platform's targeting capabilities make it efficient to find audiences; the relatively low cost of video production makes it cheap to refresh brand assets; the review reset provided by rebranding makes it possible to evade consumer protection at scale. The business model relies on the transaction between the claimed benefits, the emotional resonance of the backstory, and the volume of impressions served to targeted audiences by automated advertising.

There is a secondary dimension worth examining: the specific choice of MIT (and later Harvard) as claimed credentials. These institutions function in the American imagination as the gold standard of intellectual legitimacy, and their names are recognized in contexts far beyond their academic communities. Scammers using them are effectively borrowing institutional credibility they have not earned. This is a form of reputational theft as well as consumer fraud. Legitimate MIT and Harvard graduates who do pursue invention and entrepreneurship operate in an ecosystem partly poisoned by the association of elite credentials with fraudulent claims.

The energy-saving device sector has been a target of Federal Trade Commission scrutiny in the United States, and the UK's Trading Standards has similarly pursued misleading energy-saving product claims. But regulatory action is inherently reactive — it responds to complaints that have already accumulated — and the serial rebranding strategy appears designed in part to stay a step ahead of complaint aggregation. By the time a product has accumulated enough negative reviews and FTC complaints to attract serious regulatory attention, the operators have already launched the next brand.

Consumer awareness of the specific red flags characterizing this class of scam is therefore an important complement to regulatory oversight.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes published its investigation in January 2021 and issued an update in August 2023 to document the BoldHero rebrand. The rating throughout was **False**. The investigation used two primary verification approaches: direct institutional verification (contacting MIT to check enrollment records) and technical product evaluation (assessing whether the device had a credible mechanism for its claimed effects, and examining consumer reviews).

The institutional verification approach — directly contacting MIT rather than accepting the credential at face value — is important methodologically. It would have been easy to note the absence of evidence for Gilroy's MIT attendance and leave it at that. By actively contacting the institution, Snopes obtained a direct negative: MIT checked and found no record. This is a meaningfully stronger finding than "we couldn't verify the credential." It shifts the burden significantly.

The August 2023 update illustrates a key aspect of ongoing fact-check responsibility: scams evolve, rebrand, and re-emerge, and a fact-check that rates a specific named product or persona as false does not automatically capture its successors. The update was triggered by the emergence of the BoldHero videos and correctly identified their relationship to the original Powersave campaign. For readers encountering BoldHero without knowing it was related to the previously debunked Powersave/Nathan Gilroy claim, the updated fact-check provides the connective tissue.

From a media literacy standpoint, the Nathan Gilroy / Powersave case illustrates a set of specific warning signs that should trigger caution when evaluating any consumer product claim:

**Voiceless video presentation of supposed human subjects.** When a promotional video purports to show a real inventor or real users but never allows those individuals to speak in their own voices, this is a significant red flag. Natural video production gives subjects voice; the systematic absence of audio from human subjects serving marketing functions is a technique for avoiding verification.

**Credentials that cannot be independently confirmed.** Elite university credentials (MIT, Harvard) are specific enough to be verifiable. Legitimate claimants can be confirmed through alumni directories, public announcements, faculty profiles, or the institutions themselves. Inability to confirm a credential through any pathway is informative; a direct negative from the institution itself is conclusive.

**Claims that significantly exceed known physical limitations.** A fifty percent reduction in household electricity bills from a plug-in device is not a conservative product claim — it is a claim that would represent a major engineering breakthrough. Such claims warrant proportionally high scrutiny and proportionally strong evidence before being accepted.

**Serial rebranding.** Multiple names for what appears to be the same device or same underlying product, across different websites and promotional campaigns, indicates either a very confused legitimate business or a scam cycling through brand identities to evade negative review accumulation. Legitimate products with good track records have no incentive to rebrand serially.

**Emotionally compelling backstories as the primary persuasive vehicle.** When most of the persuasive weight in a product advertisement is carried by a human story rather than by technical evidence of efficacy, this is worth noticing. The story is doing work that evidence should be doing. That does not automatically make the product fraudulent, but it does mean the consumer is being asked to evaluate a narrative rather than a technical claim.

**Negative consumer reviews at scale.** Amazon reviews for the Powersave device were overwhelmingly negative. Consumer review platforms are imperfect — reviews can be manipulated — but a strong negative signal across a large number of reviews is meaningful, especially when reviewers use consistent language ("scam," "useless," "nothing happened").

The Powersave case also raises broader questions about platform responsibility. Facebook's advertising ecosystem enabled the campaign to reach targeted audiences at scale for an extended period. The persona-scaffolding and voiceless video techniques are detectable patterns; they appear to be specifically designed to evade automated content moderation while still reaching vulnerable audiences. The gap between what platform policies say about misleading advertising and what is actually served to users represents one of the persistent structural vulnerabilities in the current information environment.
