# "Natural Disasters Are Increasing Because of Climate Change"


## 1. The Claim

The assertion that "natural disasters are increasing because of climate change" appears frequently in media coverage, political discourse, environmental advocacy materials, and public discussions about climate impacts. This broad claim encompasses multiple sub-assertions: that the overall frequency of natural disasters is rising, that their intensity or severity is increasing, and that these trends can be directly attributed to anthropogenic climate change rather than natural variability or other factors.

Variants of this claim include statements that extreme weather events are becoming more common and more destructive, that climate change is making hurricanes stronger and more frequent, that droughts and floods are intensifying due to altered precipitation patterns, that wildfire seasons are lengthening and becoming more severe, and that the rising economic costs of natural disasters reflect climate-driven increases in destructive potential rather than socioeconomic factors like population growth and infrastructure expansion.

The claim often accompanies news coverage of specific disasters, with implicit or explicit suggestions that the featured event—whether a hurricane, wildfire, flood, or heat wave—exemplifies broader climate-driven trends and represents "the new normal" or "what climate change looks like." This framing creates public impressions that individual events can serve as evidence for long-term climatic trends, even when attribution science shows more complex relationships between specific events and climate change.

The statement represents a complex intersection of meteorological science, statistical analysis, attribution research, risk assessment, and disaster management that requires careful disaggregation to evaluate accurately. The claim conflates several distinct scientific questions: whether extreme weather frequency is changing, whether intensity is increasing, whether human-caused climate change is responsible for observed changes, and whether disaster impacts are rising due to climatic rather than socioeconomic factors.

The complexity increases when considering that "natural disaster" encompasses phenomena with different physical mechanisms, temporal scales, and relationships to climate variables. Heat waves, hurricanes, tornadoes, floods, droughts, wildfires, and winter storms respond differently to climatic changes and operate across different spatial and temporal scales. Treating these diverse phenomena as a unified category obscures important differences in both scientific understanding and confidence levels.

The claim also operates within broader political and cultural contexts where climate change science intersects with policy debates, economic interests, and ideological frameworks. This context creates incentives for both oversimplification (to motivate action or support particular policies) and overcomplexification (to create doubt or delay response). The scientific nuances of extreme event attribution often get lost in simplified narratives that serve political or advocacy purposes rather than accurate understanding.

Media coverage patterns compound these complexities by emphasizing dramatic individual events while providing limited context about long-term trends, statistical significance, or attribution confidence. News cycles focus on immediate impacts rather than systematic analysis, creating public impressions based on memorable recent events rather than comprehensive data analysis.


## 2. What's Actually True

The relationship between climate change and natural disaster trends requires systematic examination across multiple types of evidence, with findings that vary significantly by event type, geographic region, and temporal scale.

**IPCC Assessment Reports**: The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) Sixth Assessment Report (AR6, 2021-2022) provides the most authoritative synthesis of climate change and extreme events research based on comprehensive review of peer-reviewed literature by hundreds of expert scientists:

**Heat Waves and Temperature Extremes**: The IPCC expresses "virtually certain" confidence (99-100% probability) that human influence has warmed the planet and that heat extremes have become more frequent and intense since the 1950s across most land regions. Attribution confidence is very high for heat wave intensification, with clear physical mechanisms linking greenhouse gas concentrations to increased temperatures and altered heat wave characteristics including duration, intensity, and spatial extent.

**Heavy Precipitation Events**: The IPCC reports "high confidence" that human influence has increased the likelihood of compound flooding events and the intensity of heavy precipitation events in most regions since the 1950s. This reflects thermodynamic relationships where warmer air holds more moisture (approximately 7% more per degree Celsius warming), leading to more intense precipitation when atmospheric conditions favor rainfall.

**Tropical Cyclones and Hurricanes**: The evidence shows "medium confidence" that the global proportion of Category 3-5 tropical cyclone activity has increased since 1979, with evidence for intensification of the strongest storms. However, total global tropical cyclone frequency has likely decreased or remained unchanged, showing that intensity and frequency trends can diverge. Regional patterns vary significantly, with North Atlantic showing increases while other basins show decreases or no clear trends.

**Drought Conditions**: Regional patterns show "low confidence" in human influence on meteorological drought trends globally, though some regions show medium confidence for specific drought types including agricultural and ecological drought. The complexity reflects competing effects: increased evaporation from warming versus changes in precipitation patterns, with regional variation in which effect dominates.

**Tornado Activity**: No clear climate change signal has been detected in tornado frequency or intensity trends in the United States, which has the most comprehensive tornado records. Annual U.S. tornado counts show high interannual variability with no statistically significant long-term trend, though some studies suggest potential shifts in seasonal timing and geographic distribution.

**Wildfire Activity**: The IPCC expresses "high confidence" that weather conditions conducive to wildfire (high temperatures, low humidity, strong winds, low precipitation) have become more frequent in some regions, though fire activity depends heavily on management practices, land use changes, and ignition sources both natural and human. Observed wildfire increases reflect complex interactions between climate factors and human activities.

**Winter Storms and Cold Extremes**: Evidence shows warming has reduced the frequency and intensity of extreme cold events in most regions, though some studies suggest potential for more intense winter storms in certain regions due to changes in atmospheric circulation patterns, with ongoing research into mechanisms and regional patterns.

**Disaster Database Analysis**: Multiple international disaster databases track event frequencies and impacts, but interpretation requires careful consideration of systematic biases and confounding factors:

**EM-DAT (Emergency Events Database)**: Maintained by the Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters, shows apparent substantial increases in disaster reporting since 1970, but critical analysis reveals that much of this reflects improved global communications, expanded media coverage, lower reporting thresholds, population growth in hazard-prone areas, and changing definitions of what constitutes a reportable disaster rather than actual frequency increases in underlying physical phenomena.

**Munich Re NatCatSERVICE**: Insurance industry database shows dramatic increases in economic losses from natural disasters over recent decades, but normalization studies demonstrate that this largely reflects growing wealth, population increases, and infrastructure expansion in hazard-prone areas rather than increases in event frequency or intensity. Coastal development, urban expansion into floodplains, and rising property values create larger pools of assets at risk.

**NOAA Storm Events Database**: The most comprehensive U.S. database shows apparent increases in many event types, but NOAA explicitly warns against using the database for trend analysis due to changes in reporting practices, technology improvements, and evolving criteria for event classification over the historical record period.

**Normalization Studies and Economic Analysis**: Research by Roger Pielke Jr. and others on disaster losses, when normalized for inflation, population growth, and wealth increases, finds no statistically significant trend in hurricane, tornado, flood, or earthquake damages in the United States over multi-decade periods. Similar normalization studies in other regions show mixed results, with some showing residual trends after socioeconomic adjustment while others show trends disappearing after proper normalization.

**Detection vs. Attribution Framework**: Climate science maintains critical distinctions between detecting changes in extreme events and attributing those changes to specific causes, particularly human influence:

**Detection**: Statistical identification of changes in climate or weather patterns that exceed the range of natural variability expected from internal climate system fluctuations, solar variations, and volcanic activity.

**Attribution**: Determining the physical causes of detected changes, particularly quantifying the role of human activities versus natural climate drivers, requiring understanding of mechanisms and ability to reproduce changes in climate models.

Many studies successfully detect changes in extreme event patterns but attribution to climate change requires additional evidence demonstrating causal mechanisms and ruling out alternative explanations including natural climate variability, changes in observing systems, and regional factors unrelated to global climate change.

**Regional and Temporal Complexity**: Climate change effects vary dramatically across geographic regions and temporal scales, with some areas experiencing intensification of certain extreme events while other regions see decreases or no significant changes. Arctic regions show dramatic warming and associated changes, while tropical regions show different patterns, and mid-latitude regions exhibit complex responses with seasonal and geographic variation.


The scientific evidence presents a nuanced picture that resists simple "increasing/not increasing" characterization, with conclusions that vary significantly by event type, region, confidence level, and the specific aspect of disasters being evaluated (frequency, intensity, or impacts):

**Strong Evidence for Specific Trends with High Confidence**:
- Heat wave frequency and intensity increases are very well-established across most land regions, with clear physical mechanisms, strong observational evidence, and high confidence in attribution to climate change
- Heavy precipitation event intensification in most regions has good evidence base with clear thermodynamic mechanisms and medium to high confidence in climate attribution
- Wildfire-conducive weather conditions are expanding in many regions with good physical understanding and medium confidence in attribution, though actual fire activity depends heavily on ignition sources and management practices
- Extreme cold event decreases show strong evidence with high confidence in climate attribution

**Moderate Evidence with Medium Confidence for Some Trends**:
- High-intensity hurricane proportions may be increasing globally with medium confidence, though total hurricane frequency shows no clear increase and regional patterns vary significantly
- Some types of drought show evidence for intensification in specific regions, though global patterns remain unclear and confidence varies by drought definition and region
- Compound extreme events (combinations of heat, drought, fire weather) show some evidence for increased frequency with medium confidence in many regions

**Weak or Conflicting Evidence for Other Phenomena**:
- Total hurricane/cyclone frequency globally shows no clear climate change signal with low confidence in any directional trend
- Tornado patterns show no detectable climate signal in the most comprehensive observational records (United States) with very low confidence in any climate-related trends
- Flood frequency trends remain confounded by land use changes, water management, and urbanization, making climate signal detection difficult despite evidence for increased heavy precipitation
- Some winter storm patterns may be changing due to Arctic warming effects, but evidence remains mixed and confidence is low

**Economic Loss and Impact Trends**: Rising disaster costs and damages primarily reflect socioeconomic factors rather than climatic changes in most analyses. When properly normalized for inflation, population growth, and wealth increases, most disaster loss trends disappear or become statistically insignificant, indicating that economic impacts are primarily driven by increased exposure and vulnerability rather than changes in hazard frequency or intensity. However, some studies suggest residual trends after normalization in certain regions or for certain event types.

**Attribution Confidence Framework**: The confidence in attributing specific trends to climate change varies substantially across event types and regions, following established frameworks from IPCC and national assessment reports:
- Very high confidence: Heat-related changes (temperature extremes, heat waves)
- High confidence: Heavy precipitation intensification in most regions
- Medium confidence: Some aspects of tropical cyclone activity, wildfire weather, some drought types in some regions 
- Low confidence: Tornado activity, total cyclone frequency, many flood and drought patterns
- Very low confidence: Most winter storm patterns, many regional-specific phenomena

**Temporal and Spatial Heterogeneity**: Climate change effects on extreme events show marked variation across time periods, seasons, and geographic regions. Some areas experience intensification of certain extreme events while other regions see decreases or no significant changes. Arctic and high-latitude regions generally show stronger and more consistent climate change signals, while tropical and some mid-latitude regions show more complex and variable responses.

**Emerging vs. Established Signals**: Some climate change signals in extreme events are well-established and have been detected for decades (especially temperature-related changes), while others are emerging more recently as longer data records become available and as climate change signals strengthen relative to natural variability. The temporal evolution of different signals affects confidence levels and attribution assessments.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The complexity of evaluating disaster trends stems from multiple methodological challenges and conceptual issues that affect how evidence should be interpreted and what confidence levels are appropriate for different types of conclusions:

**Observational Record Limitations**: Historical disaster records suffer from systematic biases that complicate trend analysis. Earlier periods show significant underreporting due to limited communications infrastructure, different recording standards, varying definitions of disaster severity thresholds, and sparse population in many hazard-prone regions. Apparent increases in disaster frequency may reflect improved documentation, expanded media coverage, and lower thresholds for what events get recorded rather than actual increases in underlying physical phenomena.

**Baseline and Reference Period Problems**: Detecting climate change signals requires comparison against appropriate baselines representing natural climate variability, but reliable instrumental meteorological records only extend back 150-200 years for most regions, and comprehensive global coverage is even more recent. Natural climate variability operates on multiple timescales including decadal and multidecadal oscillations (like El Niño/Southern Oscillation, Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation, Pacific Decadal Oscillation) that may not be adequately characterized in available observational records.

**Multiple Confounding Factors**: Trends in disaster impacts and reporting reflect multiple simultaneous changes that must be disentangled to isolate climate signals:
- Rapid population growth and urbanization in hazard-prone areas including coastal zones, floodplains, and wildland-urban interfaces
- Substantial economic development increasing total asset values at risk from natural hazards
- Major infrastructure expansion into previously undeveloped vulnerable locations
- Evolution in building standards, land use planning, and engineering practices affecting vulnerability
- Improvements in emergency response, early warning systems, and medical care reducing fatalities while impacts on property damage are mixed
- Changes in disaster definitions, reporting criteria, and administrative thresholds affecting what events get recorded in databases
- Shifts in insurance coverage, reporting incentives, and legal frameworks affecting damage reporting
- Technological changes affecting detection, measurement, and communication of extreme events

**Spatial and Temporal Scale Matching**: Climate change affects different regions differently due to varying geographic, topographic, and oceanic influences. Global averages may obscure important regional trends where climate signals are stronger or weaker, while regional case studies may not represent broader patterns or may reflect local factors unrelated to global climate change. Temporal scales also matter, as some climate signals emerge gradually over decades while others involve changes in the frequency of rare, high-impact events that require longer records to detect statistically significant trends.

**Statistical Power and Rare Event Challenges**: Many of the most impactful natural disasters are rare events (major hurricanes, extreme floods, severe droughts) that occur infrequently even under stable climate conditions. Detecting changes in the frequency or intensity of rare events requires very long data records or large changes to achieve statistical significance, creating challenges for attribution when observational records are relatively short and climate change signals may be moderate relative to natural variability.

**Physical Mechanism Complexity**: The relationships between global climate change and regional extreme events involve complex physical mechanisms including changes in atmospheric circulation patterns, ocean-atmosphere interactions, land surface feedbacks, and interactions between multiple climate variables. Some mechanisms are well understood (like thermodynamic relationships between temperature and precipitation intensity), while others remain areas of active research (like changes in storm tracks, blocking patterns, and jet stream behavior).

**Model Validation and Projection Challenges**: Climate models provide essential tools for understanding mechanisms and projecting future changes, but their ability to simulate extreme events varies by event type and region. Models generally perform well for temperature-related variables but show more uncertainty for precipitation patterns, tropical cyclone activity, and regional circulation features that influence extreme events. This affects confidence in both attribution of observed changes and projections of future trends.

**Interdisciplinary Integration Requirements**: Understanding disaster trends requires integration across multiple disciplines including climatology, meteorology, hydrology, engineering, economics, social sciences, and risk management. Different fields may use different methodologies, have different standards for evidence evaluation, and reach different conclusions about confidence levels, creating challenges for synthesis and communication.


## 4. Verdict

**ACCURATELY COMPLEX with Significant Framing Limitations**: The claim that natural disasters are increasing because of climate change contains important elements of scientific accuracy embedded within a framework that oversimplifies complex evidence and obscures crucial distinctions between different types of phenomena, levels of scientific confidence, and regional variation patterns.

**Event-Specific Accuracy Assessment**:
- **Heat-related events** (heat waves, extreme temperatures): **Strong supporting evidence** with very high confidence for increases in frequency and intensity, with clear attribution to climate change
- **Heavy precipitation events**: **Good supporting evidence** with high confidence for intensification in most regions, medium to high confidence in climate attribution 
- **High-intensity hurricanes**: **Mixed evidence** with medium confidence for some intensification trends but unclear or negative trends for total frequency, medium confidence in attribution for intensity changes
- **Droughts**: **Regionally variable evidence** with low to medium confidence depending on drought type, region, and definition, low confidence in global attribution
- **Tornadoes**: **No supporting evidence** for climate-driven changes with very low confidence in any trends or attribution
- **Floods**: **Conflicted evidence** due to heavy precipitation increases versus land use confounding factors, medium confidence for some types in some regions
- **Wildfires**: **Moderate supporting evidence** for increased fire-conducive weather with medium confidence, but actual fire activity heavily dependent on non-climate factors

**Methodological and Framing Concerns**: The claim's framing as a unified statement about "natural disasters" conflates multiple distinct phenomena with dramatically different levels of scientific support, physical mechanisms, and confidence in climate attribution. More scientifically accurate communication would specify event types, acknowledge varying confidence levels, and distinguish between frequency, intensity, and impact trends that may show different patterns.

**Statistical and Socioeconomic Complexity**: Apparent increases in disaster frequency and impacts often reflect improved reporting systems, population growth in hazard-prone areas, economic development increasing assets at risk, and infrastructure expansion into vulnerable locations rather than changes in underlying climate hazards. Economic loss trends, when properly normalized for socioeconomic factors, often show reduced or eliminated climate signals, indicating that impact trends primarily reflect exposure and vulnerability changes rather than hazard intensification.

**Regional and Temporal Heterogeneity**: Climate change effects vary dramatically across geographic regions, seasons, and timescales, with some areas experiencing intensification of certain extreme events while others show decreases or no significant changes. Global summary statements necessarily obscure important spatial and temporal patterns that affect both scientific understanding and policy relevance.

**Gap Category Justification**: This claim qualifies as "contested-consensus" because while there is strong scientific consensus that climate change affects some extreme weather phenomena, there is ongoing legitimate scientific debate about the magnitude, timing, regional patterns, and confidence levels appropriate for different types of attribution claims. The scientific community broadly agrees on the direction of many trends (especially temperature-related changes) while continuing to refine understanding of specific impacts, regional patterns, and appropriate uncertainty quantification.

**Communication and Policy Implications**: The case exemplifies fundamental challenges in communicating complex, probabilistic, and regionally variable scientific findings to public audiences who may prefer simple, definitive statements that can guide policy decisions and personal risk assessment. Effective communication must balance scientific accuracy with accessibility while avoiding both false certainty about uncertain phenomena and inappropriate relativism about well-established trends.

**Educational Value for Critical Thinking**: This claim serves as an excellent case study for understanding how complex scientific questions involving multiple types of evidence, different confidence levels, and ongoing research can be oversimplified in public discourse. It demonstrates the importance of distinguishing between different types of natural phenomena, understanding how scientific confidence varies across different types of claims, and recognizing how socioeconomic factors can confound apparent trends in disaster impacts.

**Recommendations for Improved Communication**: More effective communication would acknowledge the spectrum of confidence across different phenomena, celebrate scientific successes in understanding well-established trends like heat wave intensification while honestly acknowledging areas of ongoing uncertainty, distinguish between changes in physical hazards versus changes in societal impacts, and provide regional context relevant to specific audiences rather than relying on global generalizations that may not apply locally.

The broader lesson involves recognizing that scientifically accurate communication about complex topics like climate change and extreme events requires nuanced approaches that respect both scientific integrity and public information needs, avoiding both oversimplification that misrepresents scientific understanding and overcomplexification that obscures actionable insights about well-established risks and trends.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**Rapid Attribution Science**: A major development in climate science has been the emergence of rapid-response attribution studies that assess climate change's role in specific events within weeks of their occurrence using established methodologies and high-performance computing. These studies typically find that climate change substantially increased the likelihood or intensity of heat-related events (heat waves, extreme temperatures) while showing mixed results for other phenomena including some evidence for increased heavy precipitation, mixed results for drought depending on type and region, and generally limited evidence for hurricane or tornado attribution.

The World Weather Attribution initiative and similar groups have developed standardized methodologies for comparing the probability and intensity of observed events in the current climate versus counterfactual scenarios representing climate without human influence. Results consistently show strong climate change signals for temperature-related events while other phenomena show more variable results depending on specific event characteristics and regional context.

**Compound and Cascading Event Research**: Research increasingly focuses on compound extreme events—simultaneous or sequential hazards that produce greater impacts than individual events alone—and cascading failures where initial extreme events trigger secondary hazards or infrastructure failures. Examples include heat-drought combinations that intensify wildfire risk, hurricane-induced flooding followed by prolonged power outages during heat waves, or winter storm-induced power failures affecting heating systems during extreme cold. Climate change may increase compound event frequency even if trends in individual event types remain unclear, representing an emerging area of risk assessment.

**Infrastructure Vulnerability and Cascade Effects**: Modern society's interconnectedness and infrastructure complexity mean that modest increases in extreme event frequency or intensity can produce disproportionate impacts through infrastructure failures, supply chain disruptions, and cascading system breakdowns. Critical infrastructure including power grids, transportation networks, and communication systems often have design standards based on historical climate patterns that may be exceeded more frequently under changing climate conditions, even if changes in extreme events are relatively modest.

**Adaptation and Resilience Considerations**: Disaster impact trends reflect not only hazard changes but also societal adaptation efforts including improved building codes, enhanced early warning systems, better emergency management practices, ecosystem-based adaptation measures, and infrastructure hardening. These adaptation measures may offset some climate-driven risk increases, creating complex interactions between changing hazards and changing vulnerability that affect overall disaster impact trends.

**Future Projection Challenges**: Climate models generally agree on continued warming with implications for temperature-related extreme events, but translating temperature and precipitation changes into specific disaster frequency and intensity predictions remains challenging due to complex physical processes, regional variation, and interactions with human systems. Projections typically show increased confidence for temperature-related changes and decreased confidence for other phenomena, with substantial uncertainty about tipping points, non-linear responses, and regional patterns.

**Policy Communication Complexities**: The nuanced scientific picture creates challenges for policy communication and public engagement. Simple messages about "increasing disasters" may effectively motivate action and convey general direction of risk changes, but risk scientific credibility if they oversimplify complex evidence or overstate confidence levels. Conversely, detailed technical assessments that accurately reflect scientific uncertainty may fail to communicate urgency effectively or may be exploited by those seeking to delay climate action.

**Media Coverage and Public Perception Patterns**: News media coverage tends to emphasize dramatic individual events and may implicitly or explicitly link them to climate change regardless of attribution confidence or scientific consensus about specific event types. This coverage pattern can create public impressions that diverge from scientific consensus, either by overstating the strength of climate signals for some phenomena or by underestimating them for others. The temporal mismatch between immediate news cycles and longer-term scientific analysis compounds these communication challenges.

**Risk Management Industry Evolution**: Insurance, reinsurance, and broader risk management industries are increasingly incorporating climate projections and extreme event research into planning and pricing models while acknowledging substantial uncertainty about specific regional impacts, timing, and magnitude of changes. Industry practices vary from conservative approaches that assume historical patterns will continue to more aggressive approaches that anticipate significant changes based on climate projections, with implications for insurance availability and pricing in hazard-prone areas.

**International and Regional Variation**: Global climate change effects on extreme events show substantial variation between developed and developing countries, between different climate zones, and between regions with different levels of infrastructure, preparedness, and adaptive capacity. Developing countries often face greater vulnerability to climate-related disasters due to limited infrastructure, fewer resources for adaptation, and greater exposure of vulnerable populations to natural hazards.

**Legal and Liability Implications**: Attribution science is increasingly being used in legal contexts including climate litigation cases where plaintiffs seek to establish causal links between greenhouse gas emissions and specific damages from extreme events. The evidentiary standards for legal proceedings differ from scientific publication standards, creating tensions between scientific uncertainty acknowledgment and legal requirements for causal determination.

**Interdisciplinary Research Integration**: Understanding disaster-climate relationships requires ongoing integration across multiple research communities including climate scientists, disaster researchers, social scientists, economists, engineers, and policy analysts. Different disciplines often have different methodological approaches, evidentiary standards, and conceptual frameworks, creating both opportunities for synthesis and challenges for communication and consensus-building.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This specific claim has not received comprehensive treatment by Snopes as a discrete fact-check article, likely because it involves nuanced scientific assessment across multiple disciplines rather than straightforward fact-checking of discrete factual assertions that can be verified through accessible sources and expert consultation. The claim's complexity—encompassing multiple event types, different temporal and spatial scales, varying levels of scientific confidence, and complex causal relationships—makes it unsuitable for the simple "true/false" or "mostly accurate/mostly false" evaluation framework that characterizes much traditional fact-checking work.

However, related climate change claims have received Snopes attention across multiple articles dealing with specific aspects of climate science, extreme weather attribution, and climate policy debates. The platform's approach has generally supported mainstream scientific consensus on anthropogenic climate change while noting the complexity of attributing specific events to climate factors and acknowledging areas where scientific uncertainty remains significant.

The absence of dedicated coverage may reflect several factors beyond complexity: the claim operates more as a general scientific conclusion than as a specific factual assertion that can be definitively verified or debunked, it requires specialized expertise in multiple technical domains including climatology and disaster statistics, and it involves ongoing scientific research where conclusions continue to evolve as new evidence and methodologies develop.

Snopes' coverage of climate-related topics has typically focused on more straightforward claims about specific data points, policy positions, or clearly false assertions rather than attempting to adjudicate complex scientific questions where legitimate disagreement exists about interpretation of evidence or appropriate confidence levels. This approach reflects the practical limitations of fact-checking in addressing complex scientific questions that require synthesis of multiple lines of evidence and ongoing research.

The gap in coverage may also reflect the challenge of addressing claims that operate across different levels of scientific confidence and regional variation. While some aspects of extreme weather trends have high scientific confidence (like heat wave intensification), others remain uncertain or show mixed evidence, making comprehensive evaluation difficult within traditional fact-checking frameworks that seek clear conclusions.

Educational fact-checking represents an underexplored area where organizations like Snopes could contribute to public understanding by addressing how complex scientific questions are framed, understood, and communicated in public discourse, particularly when simplified statements about complex phenomena create misleading impressions about the state of scientific knowledge or consensus.
