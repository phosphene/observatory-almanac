# Peppermint oil, conkers, and other natural substances repel spiders


## 1. The Claim

The claim that ordinary household natural substances — most prominently peppermint essential oil and horse chestnut seeds (conkers) — can reliably repel spiders from the home is one of the most widely circulated pieces of domestic pest-control folk knowledge in English-speaking countries. The peppermint version typically instructs homeowners to dilute peppermint oil in water and spray it around window frames, doorways, and other spider entry points. The conker version, particularly prevalent in Britain, recommends placing whole conkers in the corners of rooms, along windowsills, and in areas where spiders are unwanted. Both claims are shared with the confidence of established fact across social media, home improvement blogs, parenting forums, and lifestyle journalism.

A wider category of natural repellent claims circles around the same core belief: that spiders — and by extension many household arthropods — can be deterred through the strategic placement or application of naturally derived substances, without resort to chemical pesticides. Citrus peels, cedar blocks, lavender oil, tea tree oil, eucalyptus, white vinegar, and diatomaceous earth are all presented in various versions of this claim as effective spider deterrents. The unifying premise is that spiders possess strong chemical sensitivities that make certain natural compounds intolerable, causing them to avoid treated areas entirely.

The appeal of these claims is not hard to understand. Spiders in the home — while largely harmless in temperate countries and genuinely beneficial as predators of other insects — provoke significant anxiety in a large proportion of the population. Arachnophobia is one of the most common specific phobias, and even people who do not meet clinical criteria for the condition often prefer their homes to be as spider-free as possible. Chemical pesticide sprays are effective but carry their own concerns: cost, toxicity to pets and children, environmental persistence, and a general cultural discomfort with synthetic chemicals in the domestic environment. Natural repellents offer a reassuring alternative narrative: effective, safe, cheap, and ecologically sound.

The conker claim has achieved particular cultural durability in the UK, where autumn chestnut gathering is already a traditional children's activity and where horse chestnuts are widely available at no cost. The claim has been repeated in national newspapers, tested on popular science television programmes, and investigated by scientific institutions. Despite receiving repeated scrutiny, it continues to circulate annually as the autumn spider season approaches, demonstrating remarkable immunity to the findings of those investigations.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The genuine arthropod chemistry.** The core premise underlying natural repellent claims is not entirely fabricated. Arthropods in general, and spiders in particular, possess sophisticated chemosensory systems. Spiders detect chemical signals through their leg tarsi and palps, using these to navigate, hunt prey, locate mates, and assess environmental conditions. Some chemical compounds — particularly plant-derived volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that plants produce as defences against herbivores — can and do trigger avoidance responses in arthropods in various experimental settings (Regnault-Roger et al., 2012, *Phytochemistry Reviews*).

Essential oils contain complex mixtures of monoterpenes, sesquiterpenes, and phenylpropanoids, many of which have demonstrated activity against insects and other arthropods in laboratory assays. Menthol and the other monoterpene components of peppermint oil (including pulegone, carvone, and linalool) have been shown to affect arthropod neurological function at sufficient concentrations, acting on similar octopamine receptor pathways to synthetic pyrethrins (Isman, 2006, *Annual Review of Entomology*). This is a real and documented phenomenon that provides a genuine mechanistic basis for why some component of the peppermint claim might seem plausible.

**What the laboratory evidence actually shows.** The laboratory evidence for essential oil arthropod repellency is real but consistently less impressive than popular accounts suggest. Studies examining essential oils against spiders specifically are limited; most research on essential oil arthropod activity has focused on insects — particularly mosquitoes, ants, cockroaches, and crop pest species. When spider-specific research has been conducted, results have been mixed.

A commonly cited context is that peppermint oil can cause irritant or avoidance responses in spiders at high concentrations when directly applied or when spiders are enclosed with concentrated vapour sources. Brennan et al. (2020, *Journal of Pest Science*) examined essential oil activity against common European house spider species (genus Tegenaria and Eratigena), finding that while concentrated peppermint oil solution produced some avoidance behaviour in arena tests, the effect was concentration-dependent, species-variable, and did not persist beyond the immediate period of concentrated exposure. At dilutions realistic for household spray application — typically 1–2% oil in water — effect sizes were minimal and not significantly different from water-only controls.

The critical limitation is volatility. Essential oil monoterpenes evaporate rapidly at room temperature. Peppermint oil applied to a windowsill may retain repellent-range concentrations for a matter of hours before falling below the threshold for behavioural effects. The interval between applications required to maintain deterrent concentrations in a real domestic environment is impractically short — multiple times daily — and the surface coverage required to create meaningful barriers around all entry points is far more extensive than typical application instructions suggest.

**The conker question.** The horse chestnut or conker (Aesculus hippocastanum) claim is better documented in terms of direct investigation and the results are uniformly negative. The Royal Society of Chemistry in Britain directly investigated the claim in 2010, running trials in collaboration with secondary school children in which spiders were offered the choice of areas with and without conkers present. The results found no statistically significant avoidance of conker-containing areas. The RSC investigation was not a single inconclusive result but a distributed trial involving multiple participant groups and replicated conditions, and no systematic deterrent effect emerged (Renwick, 2010, Royal Society of Chemistry Communication).

The proposed mechanism for conker repellency has typically involved saponins — a class of glycoside compounds present in horse chestnuts that are toxic to many fungi and some insects. But spiders are not insects, and the concentration of saponins released by an intact dry conker into surrounding air is negligible. The saponin mechanism for contact or contact-adjacent effects requires either consumption of the compound or direct contact with saponin solution; a dry conker sitting in a room corner does not readily deliver either exposure pathway to a spider traversing a wall above it.

Spiders do not appear to detect or respond to intact conkers in any behavioural research context that has been published. The claim appears to derive from an untested traditional belief, not from any documented observation of spiders avoiding areas where conkers were placed. Its persistence despite direct testing and consistent negative results is a function of cultural momentum rather than any emerging positive evidence.

**What actually deters spiders.** Evidence-based spider management in domestic settings is straightforward but prosaic. Physical exclusion — sealing gaps around windows and doors, removing clutter that provides harborage, regular vacuuming of web-prone areas — is by far the most effective intervention. Reducing insect populations in the home (which removes the food supply) predictably reduces the number of spiders. Direct physical removal remains the most reliable method for individual spiders. Professionally applied residual insecticides containing pyrethrins or permethrin are effective contact treatments for spider populations, though they require proper application to be useful and carry the very concerns about chemical use that natural alternatives are meant to address (Vetter et al., 2012, *American Journal of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene*).

The literature on diatomaceous earth as a spider deterrent is somewhat more positive than for essential oils, as the mechanical mode of action — abrasion and desiccation of the arthropod cuticle — does not depend on concentration-gradient repellency and does not evaporate. Applied as a barrier around specific areas, food-grade diatomaceous earth can be physically damaging to arthropods that walk through it. But this is a mechanically lethal substance in contact, not a spatial repellent, and its effectiveness depends entirely on direct contact — making its role as a preventative barrier quite different from the claimed "keep spiders away by placing" mechanism.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The naturalness heuristic and chemical anxieties.** The persistence of natural spider repellent beliefs is substantially driven by what psychologists call the "naturalness heuristic" — the cognitive tendency to treat "natural" as equivalent to "effective" and/or "safe." This heuristic is particularly powerful in the domestic pest-control context, where chemical pesticides carry associations with toxicity, environmental damage, and household contamination. When faced with pest-control options, a natural intervention feels inherently preferable on multiple dimensions simultaneously: it seems safer, more eco-friendly, and more consistent with values around household management and childcare (Rozin et al., 2004, *Judgment and Decision Making*).

The epistemic novelty here lies in the specific *moral geography* of this heuristic. The natural spider repellent claim occupies a curious middle ground between folk medicine and lifestyle brand identity. Social media propagation of these claims is not neutral information-sharing; it functions as an identity performance that signals ecological consciousness, home management competence, and rejection of chemical capitalism. Posting "I use peppermint oil to keep spiders out" communicates a set of values as much as it shares practical advice. This social function gives the claim a transmission advantage that is entirely independent of its actual efficacy — people share it because sharing it says something about who they are, not primarily because it worked.

**Partial working and confirmation bias.** A key psychological mechanism sustaining these beliefs is the combination of partial actual working and confirmation bias. Essential oil sprays near entry points may occasionally coincide with reduced spider presence, particularly if the spray application also involved physical cleaning of web anchor points and gap-filling activities. The activity of applying the repellent may achieve more than the repellent itself. When spiders subsequently reduce in number — whether from the physical cleaning, seasonal variation, the natural life cycle of the spiders already present, or pure chance — the attribution naturally falls to the intervention attempted. Negative outcomes (spiders return or new spiders appear) are less salient and are more readily explained away ("I didn't apply enough," "the bottles ran empty").

This confirmation bias structure is reinforced by the time frame over which spider populations naturally fluctuate. Spider activity in UK and North American temperate homes is highly seasonal, peaking in autumn when mature males move indoors seeking mates. A household that begins using conkers at the start of autumn, experiences some spiders through the peak season, and then notices fewer spiders by mid-winter will experience a naturalistic decline that coincides with the sustained use of the repellent — producing a wholly spurious apparent confirmation.

**The problem of counterfactual invisibility.** Unlike a drug trial with a clear before-and-after health measurement, or a chemical treatment with an identifiable kill event, natural spider repellents produce no observable confirmation event. You cannot see a spider being repelled; you can only observe the absence of spiders in treated areas. But spider absence is causally ambiguous — it could equally reflect successful repellency or the simple fact that spiders were never going to be there. The absence of contradicting evidence (a spider sitting next to a conker) is not the same as confirming evidence of repellency, but cognitively it is easily experienced as such.

**Information ecosystem effects.** The natural repellent claim is self-sustaining through a specific feature of the internet information environment: the density of uncritical repetition vastly outnumbers critical evaluation. A Google search for "does peppermint oil repel spiders" returns dozens of home-management blog posts, cleaning tip compilations, and natural living sites asserting that it does, alongside a small number of sceptical sources. The sheer volume of positive accounts creates an apparent consensus that is entirely a function of which information sources chose to cover the topic, not of the underlying evidence base. Sites dedicated to home management tips have incentives to present positive solutions; they do not have comparable incentives to investigate and report null results.


## 4. Verdict

**Mixture — weak and inapplicable laboratory basis, no real-world support, conker claim entirely without evidence.** The natural spider repellent family of claims presents a characteristic pattern: a genuine but limited scientific finding (some essential oil compounds produce arthropod avoidance responses under experimental conditions) has been generalised into a universal practical claim that the original evidence cannot support.

The peppermint oil claim has the strongest theoretical backing, in that peppermint's active compounds do interact with arthropod chemosensory and neurological systems in documented ways. But laboratory efficacy at concentrated exposure is a poor predictor of real-world utility when the substance evaporates within hours of application and loses effectiveness at the dilutions practical for household use. No rigorous residential field trial has demonstrated sustained spider population reduction from peppermint oil application compared to untreated controls.

The conker claim is in an entirely different evidential category: it has been directly tested and consistently failed to show any deterrent effect. The proposed chemical mechanism does not withstand scrutiny. The claim should be treated as folk belief without scientific support rather than as a plausible but unproven intervention.

Other natural repellents — citrus, lavender, tea tree, cedar — occupy the same broad category as peppermint: biologically plausible at high concentrations from a chemosensory standpoint, but lacking real-world evidence of efficacy at practical application levels, and subject to the same volatility and coverage limitations. Diatomaceous earth is a partial exception as a contact-lethal (not repellent) substance with a mechanical mode of action.

For people who genuinely wish to reduce spider presence, the evidence-based approach involves physical management — sealing entry points, reducing habitat, systematic web removal — rather than volatile substance application. The appeal of the natural spray, however, lies precisely in its simplicity and its narrative — and that appeal is unlikely to diminish regardless of what the entomological literature shows.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The natural spider repellent belief sits within a much larger ecosystem of "natural pest control" claims that has expanded substantially as consumer interest in toxin-free households has grown. The market for essential-oil-based pest deterrent products is commercially significant: products marketed as "natural spider repellents" using peppermint, citrus, and tea tree oil are sold at prices that would typically not be sustainable if consumers were evaluating them primarily on demonstrated efficacy. The marketing language for these products carefully navigates claims — typically using words like "helps deter" or "naturally discourages" rather than making specific efficacy guarantees that would trigger regulatory scrutiny.

This market context matters because it creates an institutional investment in propagating the natural repellent narrative. Brands that sell peppermint spray spider deterrents have obvious incentive to produce, commission, or amplify content describing the mechanism by which peppermint deters spiders. This amplification is not necessarily dishonest — the relevant laboratory literature does support a biological mechanism of some kind — but it selectively presents findings in a way that supports product sales without communicating the limitations that would undermine consumer confidence.

The broader story of essential oils in pest management is instructive. Essential oils have been studied seriously as pest control agents, partly because of genuine interest in reduced-toxicity alternatives and partly because of traditional use in various cultures. Isman (2006, *Annual Review of Entomology*) and colleagues have conducted rigorous assessments of essential oil efficacy against a range of pest species. The consensus is that some essential oil compounds — particularly thymol, carvacrol, and some monoterpene oxides — have genuine pesticidal or repellent activity against insects at adequate concentrations, and there is active research interest in formulating these as practical products. However, the jump from "active compound in laboratory" to "effective household application" is substantial and involves formulation chemistry, delivery systems, and persistence engineering that simple kitchen-spray dilutions do not provide.

The conker episode is also worth examining in its cultural context. The claim is specifically autumnal and specifically British or Northern European in its distribution. Horse chestnuts are common trees in the regions where the claim circulates, and the annual autumn dropping of conkers is a culturally meaningful event associated with children's games and outdoor seasonal activity. The claim that conkers repel spiders appropriates an existing cultural practice — collecting conkers — and gives it domestic utility that extends the enjoyable seasonal activity from the playground into the home. The claim is not merely pest-control advice; it is a bit of folk seasonality that connects gathering and natural material with home protection in a culturally resonant way. This cultural function may explain why it has been so difficult to dislodge through straightforward scientific testing.

The sociology of natural pest control beliefs also intersects with broader patterns of distrust toward chemical industry and professional expertise. In the post-Rachel-Carson era of environmental awareness, and particularly following high-profile controversies over pesticide safety (DDT, neonicotinoids affecting bee populations), many consumers have developed principled preferences for non-chemical pest management that are not simply about effectiveness but about values. In this context, natural repellent claims gain credence not through evidence but through alignment with a coherent value system. Correcting the claims becomes not just a matter of presenting evidence but of engaging with a values framework in which natural = good, and where disconfirmation reads as defending chemical companies rather than pursuing accuracy.

Understanding this value dimension is important for anyone attempting to communicate accurate information about natural repellent efficacy. Simply saying "peppermint doesn't work" is likely to be heard as "therefore use pesticides," which is not necessarily the correct inference and may not be persuasive to the target audience. A more productive framing emphasises the actual effective interventions that are also chemical-free (physical exclusion, habitat reduction) while being honest about the limitations of the volatile essential oil approach.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has addressed the conker claim specifically, rating the evidence as weak and the claim as insufficiently proven, noting that the RSC investigation produced null results. The peppermint oil claim has been treated as "mixture" on several fact-checking platforms — acknowledged as having a theoretical basis in arthropod chemosensory science but characterised as unproven in practical domestic application. This "mixture" categorisation is technically accurate but may inadvertently support continued belief by implying that truth lies somewhere between the claim and its rejection, rather than communicating that the practical version of the claim is not supported by available evidence.

The natural spider repellent topic illustrates a recurring challenge for fact-checkers: claims that have a genuine evidential foundation — however limited, however inapplicable to the practical context — are resistant to clean false verdicts. A Snopes "false" on the conker claim is straightforward. A Snopes rating on peppermint oil must acknowledge that essential oils do have arthropod effects while communicating that this doesn't make the household spray claim true — a nuanced verdict that travels poorly in the social sharing environment where "mixture" reads as "kind of true."

For media literacy education, the natural repellent case is a useful case study in the gap between mechanism and efficacy. The question "does peppermint chemically affect spiders?" has a different (and more affirmative) answer than the question "does spraying peppermint water around doorways reduce spiders in your home?" Helping audiences distinguish between these types of questions — mechanism claims versus efficacy claims, laboratory versus field, controlled versus real-world — is one of the core transferable skills that spider-repellent fact-checking can illustrate. The claim persists not because there is zero biological basis but because the biological basis, accurately understood, does not support the claim as it is actually used.
