## 1. The Claim

*"Nature (biology) determines gender identity."*

And its political mirror: *"Gender identity is entirely a social construct."*

These two claims bracket an intensely contested empirical and cultural debate. The "nature determines" claim appears in arguments that transgender identity reflects an innate, biological reality—that people with gender dysphoria have brains that do not match their birth sex, and that transition is therefore a medically appropriate response to an objective biological condition. It also appears in biological essentialist arguments that attempt to restrict gender identity to a binary fixed by chromosomes or reproductive anatomy.

The "social construct" claim appears in arguments that gender is culturally produced and variable, that the categories of "man" and "woman" are historical formations rather than natural kinds, and that gender nonconformity challenges rather than confirms biological categories.

Both strong versions of these claims misrepresent the current state of scientific knowledge. The evidence supports a more complicated picture in which biological factors contribute meaningfully to gender identity without fully determining it, and in which social, cultural, and developmental factors also play significant roles—in proportions that current research has not been able to specify with confidence.

## 2. What's Actually True

**Gender identity has partial biological determinants including genetic and prenatal hormonal contributions, but is not fully determined by any currently identified biological mechanism. Social and developmental environments also contribute in ways that research is beginning to characterize but cannot yet fully specify.**

### Twin studies and heritability

Twin studies consistently find evidence for a heritable component to gender identity. Studies of twins where one is transgender find higher concordance in identical (monozygotic) twins than in fraternal (dizygotic) twins—the pattern expected if genetics plays a role. A 2013 study by Heylens et al. found 39% concordance for gender identity disorder in monozygotic twins versus 0% in dizygotic twins. A larger survey study by Polderman et al. (2018) used data from over 2,600 identical twin pairs and found heritability estimates for childhood gender nonconformity in the range of 50–70%.

These findings support a genuine heritable component, but heritability below 100% in identical twins—who share 100% of their DNA—confirms that genetics alone does not determine gender identity. Environmental factors, including likely prenatal hormonal environments, must also contribute.

### Brain structure research: Zhou, Kruijver, and the debate

The most cited neurobiological evidence for a biological basis of gender identity comes from Dutch research on hypothalamic structures. Zhou et al. (1995), published in *Nature*, reported that the bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BSTc)—a region in the hypothalamus associated with sexual behavior—was female-sized in a sample of male-to-female transgender individuals and male-sized in a female-to-male transgender individual. Kruijver et al. (2000) replicated this finding using a different measure of the same region.

These studies were widely cited as evidence that transgender people have brains that do not match their birth sex. But the findings face substantial methodological challenges:

**Small sample sizes.** The original studies were based on postmortem tissue from very small samples (6–10 individuals in the transgender groups). Small samples are vulnerable to chance findings and do not support strong population-level claims.

**Confounding by hormone treatment.** All transgender participants in these studies had undergone long-term hormone therapy before death. Since the BSTc is known to be sensitive to sex hormones, it is impossible to determine whether the observed brain differences preceded gender identity or resulted from hormone treatment.

**Replication uncertainty.** More recent neuroimaging studies examining broader brain characteristics in transgender individuals have produced mixed results. Some studies find that transgender individuals' brains resemble those of their identified gender in certain metrics; others find patterns that are intermediate or not clearly attributable to either binary sex. No specific brain region or signature has been identified as a reliable, validated biomarker of gender identity.

The honest conclusion is that neurobiological factors *contribute* to gender identity, that the brain is a plausible site of relevant individual differences, but that no specific brain-based mechanism has been established with confidence.

### Prenatal hormone exposure

A separate line of evidence examines the effects of prenatal androgen exposure on gender identity and gender-typical behavior. Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) is a condition in which fetuses are exposed to elevated levels of androgens prenatally. Girls with CAH show higher rates of gender nonconformity, rough-and-tumble play, and (in some studies) gender dysphoria than girls without CAH. Boys with CAH show smaller effects. This is some of the clearest available evidence that prenatal hormone environments influence gender-related behavioral outcomes, including (less robustly) gender identity.

The mechanism is thought to involve organizational effects of androgens on brain development during sensitive periods—a process that is irreversible and distinct from the activational effects of hormones in adulthood. But CAH involves many other hormonal and physiological consequences, making it difficult to isolate the specific effects of androgen exposure on gender identity specifically.

### Social constructionism: what the evidence supports

Cross-cultural anthropological research establishes that gender categories vary significantly across societies. Many cultures have recognized gender categories beyond the binary—Two-Spirit identities in many Indigenous North American traditions, hijra in South Asia, fa'afafine in Samoa, muxe in Oaxacan Zapotec culture. The specific forms gender nonconformity takes, the social meanings attached to it, and the degree to which it is stigmatized or accommodated differ enormously.

This variation is consistent with a model in which biological factors create individual differences in gender-related traits and identities, while culture shapes how those differences are expressed, labeled, and socially organized. The social constructionist insight is real: gender as a social institution is indeed culturally formed and historically variable. But this is not the same as claiming that gender identity has no biological inputs—those are different claims that operate at different levels of analysis.

The radical social constructionist position—that gender identity is entirely culturally produced with no biological substrate—struggles to account for the twin data, the CAH findings, and the cross-cultural consistency of some forms of gender nonconformity. But the biological determinist position struggles equally with the cross-cultural variation and the inability to identify specific, validated biological mechanisms.

### The gene-environment interaction frontier

Current research increasingly treats gender identity development as a gene-environment interaction phenomenon. Genetic and prenatal hormonal factors likely create a range of dispositional tendencies. Development occurs within social environments that may amplify, suppress, or shape how those tendencies are expressed. Individual developmental histories—relationships, experiences of affirmation or stigma, access to language and community for understanding one's identity—interact with biological substrates.

This interactionist model is plausible and consistent with the available evidence, but it is not yet a specified mechanistic account. The field lacks the data to quantify how much of the variance in gender identity outcomes is attributable to genetic factors, prenatal environment, postnatal social environment, or specific developmental experiences. That quantification is the scientific frontier.

## 3. Why People Believe This

**Strong claims about biological determination or social construction both serve political and psychological needs that the messy scientific evidence does not.**

**The advocacy utility of biological essentialism.** For many transgender advocates and allies, biological accounts of gender identity serve a political purpose: they frame transgender identity as something discovered rather than chosen, innate rather than performed, and therefore deserving of the same respect and protection as other characteristics like race or sex. The implicit argument is: if it's biology, it's not a lifestyle choice, and therefore opposition to it is discrimination. This framing motivates emphasis on brain studies and genetic evidence.

**The advocacy utility of social constructionism.** Radical gender theorists and some feminist traditions emphasize social construction for different political purposes: to challenge the naturalizing of binary gender, to resist medicalization, to assert human freedom from biological constraint. If gender is fully socially constructed, it can be reconstructed; if it's fixed biology, it cannot. This framing motivates emphasis on cultural variation and critique of biological research.

**Motivated simplification.** The interaction model—biology and environment, in proportions not yet known—is accurate but difficult to communicate and not emotionally satisfying. Strong claims are politically useful in ways that qualified claims are not.

**The sex/gender conflation.** Popular discourse often conflates *sex* (biological characteristics including chromosomes, hormones, and anatomy), *gender identity* (one's internal sense of gender), and *gender expression* (how one presents gender socially). These are correlated but separable. Arguments about the biological determination of sex (which is robust) are frequently imported into arguments about gender identity (which is a different, less well-characterized phenomenon).

## 4. Verdict

**Contested consensus: biological factors contribute to gender identity—this is supported by heritability data and some neurobiological evidence—but no specific mechanism has been established, and social and developmental environments also play roles that cannot currently be quantified.**

Neither "nature determines gender identity" nor "gender identity is entirely socially constructed" accurately represents the evidence. The first overstates the specificity and completeness of biological accounts; the second ignores the heritability evidence and the cross-cultural consistency of some gender nonconforming identities.

The defensible empirical claim is that gender identity development involves gene-environment interactions—biological tendencies shaped by developmental and social environments—but that current science cannot specify the mechanisms or proportions with confidence.

This is not a "both sides" verdict that falsely equates strong claims. It is a recognition that an important and contested phenomenon is not yet fully explained, and that honest reporting requires acknowledging the limits of current knowledge.

## 5. The Wider Picture

This debate is exceptional in how tightly scientific claims are entangled with political and ethical commitments. The same empirical findings—evidence for heritability, neurobiological correlates, cross-cultural variation—are interpreted through radically different political frameworks, often simultaneously. This is not a sign of bad faith; it is a sign that the stakes are extremely high and that the evidence genuinely does not resolve the normative questions people care about.

What the science can and cannot tell us:

**Can tell us:** Biological factors contribute to gender identity. Gender nonconformity occurs across virtually all known cultures. No specific gene or brain mechanism for gender identity has been validated. Twin studies support partial heritability.

**Cannot yet tell us:** What specific mechanisms produce gender identity variance. How the proportions of genetic, hormonal, developmental, and social contributions compare. Whether gender dysphoria is best understood as a single condition or a heterogeneous group. What the full range of normal gender development looks like.

The politics of this topic regularly demand that scientists go beyond what the evidence supports. Biological essentialists want the brain-sex studies to be more definitive than they are. Social constructionists want the heritability evidence to be more dismissible than it is. The scientific community's responsibility is to resist both pressures and report what is actually known—which includes the uncertainty itself.

### On the ethical implications

It is worth noting that the ethical and political conclusions people draw from this science are not actually determined by the science. Even if gender identity were entirely socially constructed, that would not make transgender people's experiences less real or deserving of respect—social realities are real realities. Even if gender identity were entirely biologically determined, that would not settle questions about medical protocols, legal recognition, or social accommodation. The move from "is" to "ought" requires additional normative premises that the science cannot supply.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This topic is a challenging case for conventional fact-checking. The politically charged framing means that any assessment risks being read as taking a side. Specific claims related to this area have been fact-checked with varying quality:

- Claims that "sex is binary" have been addressed with generally accurate responses that distinguish biological sex (which shows bimodal rather than strictly binary distributions, with a small but real proportion of intersex conditions) from simplistic binary claims.
- Claims that "there is no scientific evidence for a biological basis of gender identity" have been addressed with appropriate corrections noting twin studies and neurobiological research, while also noting the methodological limitations.
- Claims that specific brain studies "prove" transgender people have wrong-sex brains have been appropriately qualified.

Snopes has addressed specific adjacent claims, generally with appropriate caution about overstating either biological determinist or social constructionist positions.

The honest fact-checking verdict on "nature determines gender identity" is: **misleadingly incomplete**. There is evidence for biological contributions; there is also evidence for social and developmental contributions; neither alone determines gender identity; the mechanisms are not established. Any fact-check that lands on a clean "true" or "false" is itself misrepresenting the evidence.

This is precisely the kind of contested scientific consensus where the Truth Vault format is most needed—because the binary fact-check verdict cannot adequately represent a domain where the honest answer is "we know less than both sides claim."
