## §1 — Claim and Verdict

The claim: a species of spider new to the United States was spreading across the country in the summer of 2018, and had already killed five people in seven days across South Carolina, West Virginia, Tennessee, and Mississippi. According to the post, its bite was invariably lethal, and the U.S. government was actively developing an antivenom.

Snopes rated this claim **False**.

Every specific assertion in the post is false:

1. The depicted spider (*Dysdera crocata*, the woodlouse spider) is not new to the United States — it is a long-established resident.
2. The woodlouse spider's bite is medically insignificant, not fatal.
3. No human deaths from any spider were documented in the stated states during that period.
4. There was no U.S. government antivenom research program targeting this species or any "new deadly spider."

The post was traced to a Facebook user, Gary Neaderhiser, who had a documented history of posting fabricated scare content. The same account had recently published a post headlined "HEADLESS MANNEQUIN STABS MAN IN MALL WITH KNIFE" — establishing a pattern of deliberate misinformative provocations rather than good-faith error.

## §2 — Origin and Viral Spread

The post went viral on 21 August 2018, formatted as an urgent all-caps "public service announcement" — a rhetorical template common to dangerous-animal hoaxes. The PSA format is deliberately chosen: by mimicking the register of official health communications, it elevates the claim's apparent credibility and triggers sharing behavior motivated by genuine concern for others' safety. People who share such posts are typically acting in good faith; the deception operates precisely on that good faith.

The post included close-up photographs of a spider that appears formidable: large, prominently fanged, reddish-brown. These photographs depict *Dysdera crocata* — the woodlouse spider, also known as the sow-bug killer, pill-bug hunter, or stiletto spider — but were presented without any identification, allowing viewers to project their spider-fear onto the image. The use of genuine, high-quality entomological photographs of a real (though harmless) species distinguishes this hoax from cruder fakes: the spider exists, the photographs are authentic, and the mismatch between appearance and toxicity enables the deception.

The specific geographic progression claimed — first seen in South Carolina in July, then deaths in West Virginia, Tennessee, and Mississippi — follows a narrative template common to invasive species scares. A point of origin, a progression through named states, a federal government response: this is the grammar of a legitimate Wildlife Alert, redeployed for fiction. The claim that "US Government working on anti-venom at this time" added an institutional texture that made the post feel like a leak from an official process rather than a fabrication.

By late August 2018, the post had accumulated hundreds of thousands of shares, with sincere but misplaced alarm spreading across regional Facebook communities, particularly in rural areas where spider encounters with large specimens (e.g., wolf spiders, garden spiders) are common and where entomological identification expertise is less accessible. The social context amplified the fear: summer pool season meant outdoor activity, arachnophobia is among the most common specific phobias in the general population (Fredrikson et al., 1996, *Journal of Psychiatric Research*), and "found in the house/yard/pool area" stories about spiders reliably attract engagement.

## §3 — Epistemic Analysis

The epistemic novelty of this case is the **photo-misidentification exploit**: using authentic photographs of a real species to generate false toxicological claims. This is a structurally distinct category of biological misinformation from either complete fabrication (which requires no factual anchor) or exaggeration of real risks (which at least begins from an accurate baseline).

The photo-misidentification exploit works because:

1. Most audiences cannot identify spider species by visual inspection. Even trained observers distinguish many spider taxa only under magnification with access to morphological keys.
2. The authentic photographs lend a documentary quality to the claim. The spider is *real* — you can see it. The gap between authenticity of image and falsity of caption is invisible to most viewers.
3. The spider depicted (*D. crocata*) has a genuinely alarming appearance: large (8–15 mm body length), with prominently developed chelicerae (fangs) adapted for penetrating the hard exoskeletons of pill bugs. It looks dangerous. This appearance-reality chasm is essential to the hoax's mechanics.

This exploit has significant epistemological implications beyond this single case. It demonstrates that **visual verification** — "I can see it, therefore it's real" — fails catastrophically when the claim is about the *properties* of a visible object rather than the *existence* of the object itself. A photograph can confirm that a spider exists and has a certain appearance; it cannot confirm toxicity, range, or fatality statistics. The hoax exploits the audience's conflation of these distinct epistemic categories.

There is also a structural feature of spider risk communication that makes this category of hoax particularly effective: legitimate spider advisories do exist. Several genuinely dangerous spider species exist in the U.S. (brown recluse, black widow, hobo spider in some accounts), and legitimate medical literature documents spider envenomations. The hoax borrows credibility from this legitimate background of real risk, inserting a false specific claim into a real landscape of spider concern.

Finally, the **antivenom fabrication** serves a specific rhetorical function: it implies an official response structure that would be verifiable in principle but requires resources (government database access, expert contact, media monitoring) that most sharing users don't have. The false institutional detail acts as a credibility prosthetic.

## §4 — Scientific and Biological Context

*Dysdera crocata* (Latreille, 1804) is a hunting spider in the family Dysderidae, native to the Mediterranean region but now broadly distributed across the Northern Hemisphere. In North America, it is well established from New England south to Georgia and west to California. In the British Isles, northern Europe, and Australia, it is also a commonly encountered species — it is among the most cosmopolitan of introduced spider species, having been transported inadvertently in horticultural and agricultural trade.

The species is morphologically distinctive: a robust body, a deep reddish-orange cephalothorax, grayish abdomen, and exceptionally large chelicerae relative to body size. The outsized chelicerae are a functional adaptation: *D. crocata* is a dietary specialist, preying almost exclusively on woodlice (pill bugs, sow bugs — terrestrial isopod crustaceans). Woodlice have chitinous armor that is resistant to most spider fangs; *D. crocata*'s enlarged chelicerae evolved to pierce this armor. The formidable appearance of the fangs is therefore a specialization for hunting armored invertebrates, not an indicator of potency against vertebrate tissue.

Vetter and Isbister's (2006) systematic review in *Toxicon* — "Verified Bites by the Woodlouse Spider, *Dysdera crocata*" — documented human bite cases and concluded that bites are "virtually innocuous." The dominant symptom is minor pain of short duration (typically under one hour), attributed primarily to mechanical puncture of skin by the large chelicerae rather than to any chemical envenomation effect. No systemic toxicity (neurotoxicity or cytotoxicity) was documented.

Pennsylvania State University's College of Agricultural Sciences (Jacobs, 2015) concurs: "*D. crocata* bites have been implicated in causing a localized, intensely itching erythema 4 to 5 millimeters in diameter. The bites apparently do not result in any systemic neurotoxicity or cytotoxicity."

The Natural History Museum of Bern's Araneae database (Nentwig et al., 2018) — one of Europe's foremost arachnological reference databases — characterizes the bite as potentially causing "moderate local itchiness and redness," with "median duration of 40 minutes." In approximately one-third of cases, pain is described by the patient as severe, but no fatalities or systemic effects are documented.

For comparative context: the only spider-attributable human fatalities documented in U.S. medical literature typically involve black widow (*Latrodectus* spp.) envenomations in vulnerable populations (very young children, elderly individuals, immunocompromised patients) or brown recluse (*Loxosceles reclusa*) bites causing necrotic skin lesions that become secondarily infected. Even these fatalities are rare: Boyer et al. (2001, *Annals of Emergency Medicine*) documented that antivenin for black widow bites was used in only a small fraction of the several thousand annual *Latrodectus* envenomation cases reported to poison control centers in the U.S.

No reputable toxicological literature attributes fatal bites to *D. crocata* at any point in recorded medical history.

## §5 — Evidence Assessment

**Toxicological evidence:**

- Vetter, Richard S. and Geoffrey K. Isbister. "Verified Bites by the Woodlouse Spider, *Dysdera crocata*." *Toxicon*, Vol. 47, Issue 7, 1 June 2006. — Systematic case review concluding that bites are virtually innocuous; no systemic toxicity documented.

- Jacobs, Steve. "Woodlouse Hunter Spider." College of Agricultural Sciences, Pennsylvania State University, 2015. — Institutional entomology factsheet confirming long-established presence in North America and absence of systemic toxic effects.

- Nentwig, Wolfgang et al. "Dysdera crocata." *Araneae — Spiders of Europe*, Natural History Museum Bern (Switzerland), 6 March 2018. — European arachnological database entry confirming localized, short-duration bite effects with no systemic or fatal outcomes.

**Epidemiological evidence:**

No death notice, coroner record, hospital report, local news account, or official public health bulletin from South Carolina, West Virginia, Tennessee, or Mississippi documented spider fatalities in the summer of 2018. Snopes consulted multiple federal and state-level authorities and found no corroboration. The absence of institutional trail is itself diagnostic: five deaths from an unknown arachnid threat in multiple states within a single week would generate intensive public health response, mandatory case reporting to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, and local news coverage — none of which existed.

**Source credibility:**

The post's author had a documented pattern of fabricated scare content. This shifts the charitable interpretation (innocent error) to a more parsimonious explanation: deliberate hoaxing. The all-caps formatting, urgent public-service register, and specific false institutional claim (government antivenom development) are consistent with intentional misinformation architecture rather than misremembered or misread information.

**Rating confidence:** Extremely high. Multiple independent lines of evidence — toxicology, epidemiology, source tracking — uniformly support the False rating.

## §6 — Broader Implications

This case is a near-perfect specimen of the **dangerous-animal hoax genre**, which Snopes tracks as one of the most persistent categories of biology-adjacent misinformation. The genre's persistence reflects structural features of human risk psychology: we evolved in environments where novel animal threats were genuine life-or-death information, and the neurological systems that prioritize and propagate such information are not well-calibrated for distinguishing genuine alerts from fabrications that merely pattern-match to genuine alerts.

Several features of this specific hoax have been replicated across multiple iterations of the animal-scare genre over the past decade:

1. **Specific geography**: Named states, a named point of origin, a progression narrative. This creates testable specificity without requiring verification.
2. **Urgent timeline**: Five deaths in one week implies rapid escalation requiring immediate action.
3. **Institutional authority invoked**: "US Government working on anti-venom" implies expert recognition.
4. **Real animal photographs**: Authentic images reduce the visual suspicion that would arise from digitally altered or cartoon representations.
5. **PSA format**: Mimics legitimate public health communications to borrow credibility.

Understanding this template is valuable for media literacy education: each element serves a specific function in the hoax's persuasion architecture, and being able to name those functions equips audiences to recognize them before sharing.

The societal cost of such hoaxes is not trivial. Legitimate entomology and public health communication compete for attention in a media environment populated by these fabrications. Arachnophobia, already one of the most prevalent specific phobias in industrial societies (affecting approximately 3.5–6.1% of the population; Fredrikson et al., 1996, *Journal of Psychiatric Research*), is amplified and reinforced by repeated exposure to such content. Pest management professionals report customer calls driven by misidentified spiders following viral scare posts. And the erosion of trust in institutional public health communications that results from a landscape of fabricated PSAs has downstream consequences for the reception of genuine urgent health advisories.

The knowledge gap identified here — accessible, systematic science communication about the actual risk profile of common household spiders calibrated to counter the specific claims that recur in scare-genre hoaxes — remains unmet in publicly available sources.
