## 1. The Claim

In September 2021, news outlets around the world reported that a newly discovered insect species had been named after RuPaul Charles, the iconic American drag queen, television personality, and host of *RuPaul's Drag Race*. The species, described as an iridescent soldier fly from Australia, was said to bear the formal taxonomic name *Opaluma rupaul*. The story spread through entertainment and science media alike, prompting the predictable question: was this real, or an elaborate joke? Snopes covered the story in a confirmed-true news article by Madison Dapcevich published on September 17, 2021, noting that the naming was genuine and the species had been formally described in the peer-reviewed journal *Austral Entomology*.

The claim, in summary, is straightforward: a real, previously undescribed insect species from Australia was formally named *Opaluma rupaul* in honour of RuPaul, and the naming passed through the standard channels of taxonomic peer review. The species was one of thirteen new soldier flies described in that publication, and the researcher behind the work — Bryan Lessard of CSIRO's National Research Collections Australia — stated publicly that extravagant names could help draw attention to species facing conservation pressures. This Truth Vault entry examines the claim in full, traces it to primary sources, and explores the wider scientific and epistemic context that makes the story both charming and instructive.

## 2. What's Actually True

Every substantive element of the reported claim is confirmed by the primary scientific literature. The species *Opaluma rupaul* was formally described in a paper authored by Bryan D. Lessard, Norman E. Woodley, and others, published in the September 2021 volume of *Austral Entomology* under the title "Opaluma Lessard & Woodley, Gen. Nov: A New Genus of Iridescent Soldier Flies (Diptera: Stratiomyidae) from Australia, Including Seven New Species" (Lessard, Woodley et al., 2020, *Austral Entomology*, vol. 59, no. 3, pp. 467–486). The paper erected the new genus *Opaluma*, a name derived from Latin roots: "opal" (*opulus*), referencing the opalescent iridescence of the flies' bodies, and "thorn" (*aculeus*), referencing a morphological feature of the group. The specific epithet *rupaul* explicitly honours RuPaul Charles. The genus *Opaluma* contains seven newly described species in this paper, and the broader publication described a total of thirteen new soldier fly species from the Australian continent.

The naming follows the rules of the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature (ICZN), which permits — and indeed has a long tradition of — honouring public figures through specific epithets. The paper was peer-reviewed and published in a journal of the Australian Entomological Society, a respected venue for Australasian insect taxonomy. There is no ambiguity about the species' validity: it is deposited in the Australian National Insect Collection at CSIRO, where type specimens are maintained under curatorial standards. Lessard, the lead author, is a postdoctoral research fellow at the National Research Collections Australia, a division of CSIRO (the Commonwealth Scientific and Industrial Research Organisation), Australia's national science agency.

*Opaluma rupaul* possesses a strikingly iridescent body — a feature shared across the genus — with structural coloration produced by microscale features of the cuticle rather than by pigments. This optical property is what inspired both the genus name and the association with RuPaul, whose public persona is defined by flamboyant visual aesthetics. Lessard stated in interviews accompanying the paper's release that the fly's rainbow shimmer and "fabulousness" made the association natural, and that using celebrity names was a deliberate strategy to generate media interest in species that might otherwise be ignored, particularly when those species face ecological threats.

This last point is not merely rhetorical. Two other species described in the same body of work — *Opaluma opulens* and *Antissella puprasina* — are now formally recognised by the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) as endangered. *Opaluma opulens* is known only from Lamington National Park in Queensland, a region that was significantly burned during Australia's catastrophic Black Summer bushfire season of 2019–2020. The fires destroyed or degraded habitat across millions of hectares, and species with extremely restricted ranges — so-called narrow endemics — faced disproportionate risk (Legge et al., 2020, *Nature Ecology & Evolution*). The IUCN listing of *O. opulens* and *A. puprasina* underscores that these are not merely taxonomic curiosities but organisms whose extinction risk is tangible and measurable.

The primary and secondary sources for the news coverage include a news release from CSIRO and reporting by *The Guardian* (September 15, 2021). Both sources are consistent with the primary scientific paper and with Lessard's public statements about the rationale for celebrity-inspired nomenclature.

## 3. Epistemic Novelty: Celebrity Nomenclature as a Conservation Strategy

The Snopes article, in keeping with its mission, confirmed the factual claims and moved on. What it did not explore — and what deserves analytical attention — is the deeper question of whether celebrity nomenclature actually works as a conservation attention strategy, and what the epistemic and institutional implications of that strategy might be. This is not a trivial question. It sits at the intersection of conservation biology, science communication, and what scholars of public engagement with science have called the "attention economy" of biodiversity.

The core problem is well established. Taxonomists describe approximately 18,000 new species per year (Costello, Wilson, and Houlding, 2012, *Systematic Biology*, vol. 61, no. 5), yet the vast majority of these descriptions attract no public attention whatsoever. The descriptions appear in specialist journals, are read by a handful of experts, and enter databases like the Catalogue of Life without ever registering in public consciousness. Meanwhile, conservation funding and policy attention are demonstrably skewed toward charismatic megafauna — large, photogenic vertebrates — at the expense of invertebrates, fungi, and plants, which together constitute the overwhelming majority of described biodiversity (Clark and May, 2002, *Science*, vol. 297, no. 5579). Invertebrates alone account for an estimated 80% of all multicellular animal species, yet they receive a fraction of conservation research funding relative to their diversity (Cardoso et al., 2011, *Biological Conservation*, vol. 144, no. 12).

Into this gap steps the practice of celebrity nomenclature. The strategy is simple: by naming a species after a famous person, researchers can exploit existing media networks and fan communities to generate coverage that would otherwise never materialise. The logic is that attention — even fleeting, entertainment-driven attention — can serve as a gateway to conservation awareness. This is not a new idea. Species have been named for celebrities for over a century; notable historical examples include the beetle *Agathidium bushi* named for US President George W. Bush (Miller and Wheeler, 2005, *Bulletin of the American Museum of Natural History*) and the spider *Aptostichus stephencolberti* named for comedian Stephen Colbert (Bond, 2008, *Zootaxa*). But the strategic deployment of this practice — with conservation messaging explicitly embedded in the accompanying media — is a more recent and self-conscious development.

The question of efficacy is where the analysis becomes genuinely interesting. A study by Enghoff (2018, *Contributions to Zoology*, vol. 87, no. 2) examined the relationship between naming events and media coverage at the time of a new species description and found that species named after celebrities received significantly more media hits than those given purely descriptive Latin names. However, Enghoff also noted that this effect was overwhelmingly transient: media interest spiked sharply at announcement and decayed to baseline within days. The critical question, then, is not whether celebrity nomenclature generates attention — it clearly does — but whether that ephemeral attention translates into durable conservation outcomes.

There are reasons to think it might, at least in indirect and non-obvious ways. First, media coverage of a named species enters the permanent digital record, creating searchable reference points that journalists, educators, and policy advocates can rediscover and re-cite at later dates. When *Opaluma opulens* was subsequently listed by the IUCN as endangered, the existing media trail from its initial description made the conservation story easier to tell and more likely to attract renewed coverage. Second, the naming event can create a parasocial relationship between a celebrity's fan base and a species. Research on parasocial interaction theory (Horton and Wohl, 1956, *Psychiatry*, vol. 19, no. 3; extended by Dibble, Hartmann, and Rosaen, 2016, *Communication Theory*, vol. 26, no. 1) suggests that fans experience psychological proximity to celebrities and may extend that sense of attachment — however tenuously — to entities associated with them. A RuPaul fan who learns about *Opaluma rupaul* may develop a trace of affective investment in the species' fate that would not have existed otherwise.

Third, and perhaps most significantly, celebrity nomenclature serves what might be called an epistemic bridging function. Public understanding of biodiversity loss is often abstract: people know that species are going extinct, but they cannot name the species, describe their ecologies, or locate them geographically. The act of naming a species after a specific person creates a narrative hook — a memorable, searchable, socially transmissible cognitive anchor — that grounds the abstraction in a concrete instance. The species ceases to be a nameless entry in a database and becomes a story. This is epistemically powerful because human cognition is narrative-driven: we retain, share, and act on stories far more readily than on statistics (Dahlstrom, 2014, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, vol. 111, supplement 4). Lessard's strategy, whether or not he would frame it in these terms, leverages narrative cognition to smuggle conservation information into public discourse under cover of entertainment.

There is a legitimate objection to celebrity nomenclature, and it should be acknowledged. Critics argue that it trivialises the naming process, degrades the dignity of taxonomic science, and may lead to perverse incentives in which researchers chase media attention rather than descriptive rigour. The International Commission on Zoological Nomenclature has periodically debated whether restrictions should be placed on the practice (ICZN, 1999, *International Code of Zoological Nomenclature*, 4th ed., Article 11). However, the ICZN has repeatedly declined to impose restrictions, recognising that the stability of the nomenclatural system depends on author discretion and that policing taste is not within its institutional mandate. The pragmatic counterargument is that in an era of mass extinction — what many biologists call the sixth mass extinction event (Ceballos et al., 2015, *Science Advances*, vol. 1, no. 5) — any strategy that increases public awareness of undescribed and endangered species is worth tolerating, even if it occasionally makes traditionalists uncomfortable.

The case of *Opaluma rupaul* is, in this light, a near-ideal instance of the strategy. The species is visually spectacular, the celebrity association is thematically coherent (iridescence, flamboyance, self-presentation), and the accompanying media coverage included substantive conservation messaging about the endangered status of congeners. It did not merely announce a novelty; it used the novelty as a vehicle for ecological information. Whether the attention translates into funding, policy change, or habitat protection for the Lamington National Park populations of *Opaluma opulens* remains an empirical question that future research will need to address. But as a case study in science communication strategy, it is remarkably well constructed.

## 4. The Biology of Soldier Flies (Stratiomyidae) and Australian Ecosystem Services

The family Stratiomyidae, commonly known as soldier flies, is a large and globally distributed group within the order Diptera (true flies). The family contains over 2,800 described species in approximately 400 genera, making it one of the more species-rich families of lower Brachycera (Woodley, 2001, in *Manual of Nearctic Diptera*, vol. 1; updated estimates in Lessard and Woodley, 2020, *Austral Entomology*). Despite their diversity, soldier flies are poorly known to non-specialists and rarely figure in public discussions of insect ecology. This is unfortunate, because their ecological roles are substantial and, in some ecosystems, disproportionately important.

The larval ecology of soldier flies is characterised by saprophagous and detritivorous feeding — that is, the larvae feed on decaying organic matter, including dead plant material, decomposing animal tissue, and organic-rich sediments. In doing so, they perform a critical nutrient recycling function, accelerating decomposition and returning nutrients to soil and aquatic systems. The best-known species in this regard is the black soldier fly, *Hermetia illucens*, which has attracted global attention as a bioconversion agent: its larvae are used commercially to process organic waste, producing protein-rich biomass that can be fed to livestock (Tomberlin, Sheppard, and Joyce, 2002, *Florida Entomologist*, vol. 85, no. 3). While the Australian *Opaluma* species are not used in commercial bioconversion, they play analogous ecological roles in native forest ecosystems, cycling nutrients through leaf litter and contributing to soil formation.

Adult soldier flies occupy a different ecological niche. Many species are flower visitors, and while they are not as well studied as bees or butterflies in the pollination literature, they contribute to the pollination of certain Australian plant species, particularly those with open, accessible floral architectures. The contribution of non-bee pollinators — including flies, beetles, and wasps — to global pollination services has been increasingly recognised in recent years (Rader et al., 2016, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, vol. 113, no. 1), and Diptera are estimated to account for a significant fraction of pollination visits in certain biomes, including Australian heathlands and tropical forests. The ecological services provided by soldier flies are therefore twofold: nutrient recycling in the larval stage and pollination in the adult stage.

The Australian continent is a centre of diversity for Stratiomyidae, as it is for many insect lineages that diversified during Gondwanan isolation. The erection of the new genus *Opaluma* by Lessard and Woodley (2020) reflects the ongoing taxonomic description of this continental fauna, much of which remains undescribed. The genus is defined by the striking structural iridescence of the adult body, which produces vivid opalescent coloration through the interaction of light with microstructures in the cuticle — a phenomenon known as structural coloration. This form of coloration, which is also responsible for the iridescence of morpho butterflies, peacock feathers, and many beetle elytra, is produced not by chemical pigments but by nanostructured layers that selectively reflect certain wavelengths of light (Kinoshita, Yoshioka, and Miyazaki, 2008, *Reports on Progress in Physics*, vol. 71, no. 7). The specific cuticular structures responsible for *Opaluma* iridescence have not yet been characterised at the nanoscale, but the optical effect is visually dramatic and immediately distinguishes the genus from other Australian Stratiomyidae.

The conservation context is sobering. Australia's insect fauna is under multifactorial pressure from habitat loss, climate change, pesticide exposure, altered fire regimes, and invasive species. The Black Summer bushfires of 2019–2020 burned approximately 18.6 million hectares across eastern and southern Australia (Boer, Resco de Dios, and Bradstock, 2020, *Nature Climate Change*, vol. 10), destroying habitat for countless invertebrate species, many of which had never been formally described. For narrow-range endemics like *Opaluma opulens*, known only from Lamington National Park in the Gold Coast hinterland of Queensland, a single catastrophic fire event can represent an existential threat to the entire species. The subsequent IUCN listing of *O. opulens* and *Antissella puprasina* as endangered reflects a grim reality: some of the species described in Lessard and Woodley's paper may face extinction before their biology is fully understood. This compression of the timeline — between discovery, description, and potential extirpation — is a defining feature of what entomologists have termed the "taxonomic emergency" facing global insect diversity (Engel et al., 2021, *Insect Conservation and Diversity*, vol. 14, no. 6).

## 5. The Broader Batch: Other Species Named in the Same Period

The naming of *Opaluma rupaul* did not occur in isolation. The same period saw a burst of taxonomic activity across multiple groups, with several naming decisions that attracted significant media interest. Among the most widely reported was the naming of three new beetle species from Australia after the legendary Pokémon characters Articuno, Zapdos, and Moltres. The species — *Binburrum articuno*, *Binburrum zapdos*, and *Binburrum moltres* — were described from specimens in Australian entomological collections and named by their describer in a deliberate echo of the celebrity-nomenclature approach, albeit drawing on fictional rather than real public figures. The Pokémon beetle trio attracted comparable media coverage to the RuPaul fly, demonstrating that the attention-generation mechanism operates across cultural referents rather than being specific to any single celebrity domain.

Beyond invertebrates, new species descriptions from the same approximate period included a smoothhound shark, *Mustelus andamanensis*, described from the Andaman Sea; four new species of rocky reef fishes, contributing to the increasingly detailed picture of tropical marine biodiversity; a new species of gecko; and twenty-eight new species of *Syzygium*, a vast genus of flowering plants in the myrtle family (Myrtaceae) described from New Guinea. The *Syzygium* descriptions are particularly notable for their sheer number and for what they reveal about the pace of plant taxonomic discovery in Melanesian rainforests, one of the least botanically explored regions on Earth relative to its species richness.

This context is important because it counteracts a common public misconception: that the natural world has been thoroughly catalogued and that new species descriptions are rare curiosities. In fact, the global rate of new species description has remained remarkably stable over recent decades, averaging approximately 18,000 new species per year across all kingdoms of life (Costello, Wilson, and Houlding, 2012, *Systematic Biology*). The overwhelming majority of these are invertebrates and plants. The backlog of undescribed species in museum collections worldwide is enormous; by some estimates, natural history museums contain millions of specimens representing species that have been collected but never formally described (Bebber et al., 2010, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*, vol. 277, no. 1680). Every one of the species mentioned in this section — from the RuPaul fly to the Pokémon beetles to the New Guinean plants — represents a specimen that sat in a drawer or jar, sometimes for decades, before a taxonomist had the time, funding, and expertise to examine it, compare it to known species, and publish a formal description.

The parallel between these naming events and the broader crisis in taxonomic workforce capacity is worth underscoring. The number of active taxonomists has been declining in many countries even as the number of undescribed species remains vast (Paknia, Rajaei, and Koch, 2015, *PLoS ONE*, vol. 10, no. 3). Taxonomic positions have been cut or unfilled in universities and museums across the developed world, and the remaining specialists are often retirement-age. The work of Bryan Lessard and colleagues at CSIRO, describing thirteen new soldier fly species in a single paper, represents a significant contribution not only to entomological knowledge but to the institutional capacity of Australian taxonomy — a national science infrastructure that sustains all downstream ecological research, from conservation status assessments to biosecurity monitoring.

## 6. Verdict and Key Takeaways

The claim is entirely true. *Opaluma rupaul* is a valid, peer-reviewed, formally described species of iridescent soldier fly from Australia, named in honour of RuPaul Charles. The naming was published in *Austral Entomology* by Bryan D. Lessard and colleagues at CSIRO (Lessard, Woodley et al., 2020, *Austral Entomology*, vol. 59, no. 3, pp. 467–486), and the species is deposited in the Australian National Insect Collection. There is no ambiguity, no hoax, and no qualification required.

Key takeaways from this analysis:

- **The species is real and the naming is valid.** *Opaluma rupaul* was described under the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature and published in a peer-reviewed journal of the Australian Entomological Society. The genus name *Opaluma* derives from Latin for "opal" and "thorn," and the specific epithet *rupaul* explicitly honours RuPaul Charles.

- **Celebrity nomenclature is a deliberate conservation communication strategy.** Researcher Bryan Lessard's stated rationale — that extravagant names attract attention to otherwise overlooked species — reflects a documented and increasingly self-conscious practice in contemporary taxonomy. Evidence suggests that celebrity-named species receive significantly more media coverage than descriptively named ones, though the durability of that attention remains an open empirical question.

- **The conservation stakes are genuine.** Two congeners described in the same body of work, *Opaluma opulens* and *Antissella puprasina*, are now IUCN-listed as endangered. *Opaluma opulens* is known only from Lamington National Park in Queensland, which was severely affected by the 2019–2020 Black Summer bushfires. These are not abstract conservation risks; they are immediate and measurable threats to species survival.

- **Soldier flies perform critical ecosystem services.** Stratiomyidae larvae recycle nutrients from dead organic matter, while adults contribute to pollination of certain Australian plant species. The ecological importance of the family is disproportionate to its public profile.

- **The broader context is one of ongoing taxonomic discovery.** The RuPaul fly was one of thirteen new soldier flies described in a single paper, and its naming coincided with the description of Pokémon-named beetles, a new shark species, reef fishes, a gecko, and twenty-eight new plant species from New Guinea. New species descriptions are not rare events but a continuous, large-scale scientific enterprise, constrained primarily by workforce capacity and funding.

- **The epistemic function of naming deserves more attention.** Naming is not merely a bureaucratic act of cataloguing. It creates cognitive anchors, narrative hooks, and searchable reference points that determine whether a species enters public awareness or remains invisible. In an era of mass biodiversity loss, the politics and strategy of naming are conservation-relevant in ways that traditional taxonomy has only recently begun to grapple with.
