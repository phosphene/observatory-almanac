# New law bars employers from asking Californians about criminal history on job applications


## 1. The Claim

A widely circulated claim, reported frequently in the popular press and shared across social media in the months following its January 2018 implementation, holds that a new California law bars employers from asking job applicants about their criminal history. The claim is typically presented in one of several formulations: that employers can no longer ask about criminal records on job applications, that "the box" asking whether applicants have a criminal background has been made illegal in California, or more broadly that California has banned employers from considering criminal history in hiring decisions. Some versions of the claim extend it further, asserting that employers cannot run background checks or that criminal history is now wholly off-limits as an employment factor in the state.

The headline that generated the most circulation was straightforwardly declarative in tone: a new law bars employers from asking Californians about criminal history. This framing appeared in news summaries, policy advocacy materials, legal explainers, and social media posts, and it was accurate up to a point — but only up to a point. The portion of the claim that is true is significant, and the underlying law represents a genuine and consequential expansion of employment protections for Californians with criminal records. The portions that are overstated, however, matter considerably: they determine whether a given applicant, employer, or HR practitioner actually understands what the law requires and what it permits.

The political context of the claim's circulation is important. California's Fair Chance Act emerged from a sustained national campaign to reform employment practices that advocates, researchers, and returning-citizen organisations argued amounted to a de facto permanent employment bar for people with prior convictions. The "ban the box" movement — named after the checkbox appearing on many job applications asking whether the applicant had ever been convicted of a crime — had successfully advocated for similar measures in dozens of cities and states before California adopted a statewide standard. By 2018 California's law was among the most comprehensive state-level fair chance hiring statutes in the country, and it attracted substantial coverage, much of it from advocates with strong interests in communicating a clear, emphatic message about new legal protections.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The law, accurately stated.** California Assembly Bill 1008, signed by Governor Jerry Brown in October 2017 and effective January 1, 2018, amends the California Fair Employment and Housing Act to restrict when and how employers may inquire about job applicants' criminal conviction history. The law applies to employers with five or more employees. Its core provisions are as follows: employers may not ask about criminal conviction history — on written applications, during initial interviews, or by any other means — until after they have extended a conditional offer of employment to the applicant (California Legislature, 2017, AB 1008, amending Government Code §12952).

The conditional offer threshold is the operative mechanism. Before extending a conditional offer, the employer is entirely prohibited from soliciting or considering criminal conviction history. After the conditional offer, the employer may conduct a background investigation including a check of criminal records. If the background check reveals prior convictions, the employer may not automatically rescind the offer on that basis. Instead, the law requires an individualised assessment: the employer must consider the nature and gravity of the offence; the time elapsed since the offence and the sentence completion; and the nature of the job sought and the relationship between the offence and the specific duties of the position (Government Code §12952(c)). This three-factor test is drawn from the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission's 2012 guidance on the use of arrest and conviction records in employment decisions (Equal Employment Opportunity Commission, 2012, *Consideration of Arrest and Conviction Records in Employment Decisions Under Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964*, EEOC Enforcement Guidance No. 915.002).

If, after completing the individualised assessment, the employer intends to rescind the conditional offer, it must provide written notice to the applicant, specifying the disqualifying conviction and a copy of the background report. The applicant then has five business days to respond with evidence of inaccuracy in the record or evidence of rehabilitation or mitigating circumstances. The employer must consider any such response before making a final decision.

**What the law does not do.** The law does not prohibit background checks. It does not prevent employers from learning about criminal history — it restricts *when* in the hiring process that inquiry can occur and what process must follow it. Employers with fewer than five employees are not covered. The law explicitly exempts certain employment categories where criminal history is a legally required or highly relevant consideration: positions where a criminal background check is required by another law, positions with criminal justice agencies, farm labour contractors, in-home supportive service providers, and positions working with children or other specified vulnerable populations (Government Code §12952(a)(2)). These are not marginal carve-outs; they affect substantial portions of California's workforce in healthcare, childcare, education, and law enforcement.

The law also does not prevent rescission of conditional employment offers based on criminal history — it requires a process before that rescission can occur. Employers who conduct the required individualised assessment and give applicants the prescribed notice and response opportunity may still decline to hire applicants whose conviction history is directly relevant to the position. The law raises the procedural bar; it does not eliminate the disqualification entirely.

**Enforcement and compliance.** The California Department of Fair Employment and Housing (now the Civil Rights Department) has enforcement authority and has issued regulations and guidance for compliance. Applicants who believe an employer has violated the law may file a complaint and seek remedies including back pay, compensatory damages, and attorneys' fees. Legal enforcement actions in the early years of the law's operation focused primarily on employer application forms that still contained criminal history questions, and on rescissions of conditional offers made without providing the required individualised assessment notice (California Civil Rights Department, various enforcement guidance documents, 2018–2022).

**The research landscape.** The empirical research on "ban the box" laws at the city, state, and federal employer level substantially predates California's statewide action, providing context for evaluating the law's likely effects. The most cited research is that of Amanda Agan and Sonja Starr, whose field experiment examining callback rates to fictitious job applications before and after the adoption of ban-the-box policies in New Jersey and New York City found that the laws did reduce the callback penalty associated with disclosed criminal records — but also documented a significant and troubling countervailing effect, discussed in section three (Agan & Starr, 2018, *Ban the Box, Criminal Records, and Racial Discrimination: A Field Experiment*, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 133(1), 191–235).


## 3. Why People Believe the Stronger Version

**Advocacy framing and motivated simplification.** The organisations and coalitions that campaigned for the Fair Chance Act had strong communicative incentives to state the law's protections in clear, emphatic terms. "Employers can no longer ask about your criminal past on the job application" is a message that is easily understood, emotionally resonant for affected communities, and translatable into direct action (informing applicants of their rights, supporting complaints). The more accurate formulation — "employers cannot ask until after a conditional offer, then must conduct an individualised assessment with notice procedures" — is accurate but requires legal comprehension to act on. Simplified messaging is not dishonest intent; it is rational communication strategy for organisations serving populations with limited access to legal counsel.

**News headline compression.** Legal stories involving new employment rights are subject to the same headline compression that affects all complex regulatory reporting. A statute that amends the California Fair Employment and Housing Act by adding a conditional-offer threshold and a notice-and-assessment procedure before employment decisions based on criminal history is not a headline — it is a subheading in a legal brief. "New law bars employers from asking about criminal history" is a headline. The second sentence of most news stories added qualifications; the shareable version was the headline.

**The epistemic novelty of the ban-the-box paradox.** The research finding that requires genuine pause in assessing this law concerns a counterintuitive mechanism that has received substantial scholarly attention but very little popular coverage. Agan and Starr's 2018 Quarterly Journal of Economics study found that while ban-the-box policies did reduce callback rates for white applicants with disclosed criminal records, they simultaneously increased racial callback disparities. In jurisdictions without ban the box, white applicants without disclosed records received approximately 7 percentage points more callbacks than Black applicants without disclosed records. After ban-the-box implementation, this gap widened to approximately 45 percentage points. The authors interpret this as evidence of statistical discrimination: when employers cannot ask about criminal history early, some update their beliefs about applicant criminal history based on observable demographic characteristics, using race as a proxy for the criminal history information they can no longer directly solicit (Agan & Starr, 2018, *Quarterly Journal of Economics*).

Jennifer Doleac and Benjamin Hansen extended this analysis and found consistent patterns across a broader set of policy adoptions. Their study, examining 33 ban-the-box policies across jurisdictions from 2004 to 2014, found significant reductions in employment for young, low-skilled Black and Hispanic men following ban-the-box adoption, consistent with increased statistical discrimination against these groups (Doleac & Hansen, 2020, *The Unintended Consequences of "Ban the Box": Statistical Discrimination and Employment Outcomes When Criminal Histories Are Hidden*, Journal of Labor Economics, 38(2), 321–374). This is precisely the demographic that ban-the-box policies are most intended to help.

The paradox is real, documented, replicated to some degree, and philosophically significant. It does not mean the law is counterproductive on net, or that the approach is wrong — the research also reflects that information-based discrimination is a real phenomenon operators can address through complementary interventions. But it means the popular account of the law as a straightforward civil rights win misses a genuine empirical complication. People who believe the stronger version of the claim — that criminal history is simply barred from consideration — are not just confused about the law's provisions; they are also insulated from engaging with a genuine policy tension in the underlying research.

**Normalisation from city-level predecessors.** California's 2018 law came after decades of city and county ban-the-box measures in jurisdictions including San Francisco (2014), Los Angeles (2016), and dozens of municipalities outside California going back to Hawaii's 1998 law. By 2018, the policy concept was sufficiently normalised in progressive policy circles that coverage focused on the expansion of the principle rather than examination of its mechanisms. Familiarity with the concept made it easier to skip the specifics.


## 4. Verdict

**True in substance, significantly oversimplified in popular circulation.** California's Fair Chance Act does bar employers from asking about criminal conviction history at the job application stage for employers with five or more employees, and this accurately reflects the claim's core assertion. The law is real, enforceable, and has changed employment practices across California. Employers who ask about criminal history on initial applications, or who conduct pre-offer background checks, or who rescind conditional offers without the required individualised assessment and notice procedures, are violating state law and subject to enforcement action.

The overstatement lies in what the claim implies: that criminal history is altogether removed from the employment calculus, that background checks are prohibited, or that the law covers all employers and all employment categories. None of these is correct. The law restructures the process of criminal history inquiry and adds procedural protections for applicants; it does not eliminate employer access to that history or prohibit all hiring decisions influenced by it.

The underlying empirical situation is more complicated still. The research on ban-the-box laws suggests that the relationship between criminal history disclosure restrictions and employment outcomes for the most affected populations is not straightforwardly positive, and that statistical discrimination mechanisms can partially offset the intended benefits. This does not undermine support for the law's procedural goals, but it matters for accurate understanding of what the law achieves.


## 5. The Wider Picture

California's Fair Chance Act sits within a broader national policy movement that has reframed employment discrimination based on criminal history as a civil rights issue. The EEOC's 2012 guidance, which the California law's individualised assessment requirement closely tracks, represented a significant federal-level articulation of the view that blanket employment bars based on criminal records disproportionately affect Black and Latino workers who are disproportionately represented in the criminal justice system owing to a complex of historical and structural factors. The civil rights framing has been influential and contentious.

The tension between the civil rights case for removing early criminal history inquiry and the empirical finding that removal can increase statistical discrimination by race reflects a deeper structural problem: employer discrimination operates through multiple mechanisms, and constraining one mechanism without addressing underlying biases may redirect discrimination rather than reduce it. Kyle Rozema and Marc Rosen examined this in the context of "ban the box" and found heterogeneous effects depending on employer characteristics, including business sector and workforce composition (Rozema & Rosen, 2018, *Do Ban-the-Box Policies Work? A Survey of the Recent Research*, American Economic Review Papers and Proceedings, 108, 206–211).

The policy response to this tension has moved in at least two directions. Some jurisdictions have paired ban-the-box requirements with affirmative fair-chance hiring initiatives, tax incentives for hiring people with records, and bonding programmes that reduce employer risk. Others have moved toward occupational licensing reform, recognising that the employment barriers created by criminal records are not limited to initial hiring but extend through professional credentialing systems. California's AB 2138 (2018), passed the same year as the Fair Chance Act, significantly restructured the state's approach to criminal history in occupational licensing, limiting the ability of licensing boards to deny licences based on prior convictions that are not substantially related to the licensed occupation.

The claim that "employers are barred from asking about criminal history" also has significant misapplication risk in the other direction: applicants who believe they cannot legally be asked about criminal history at any point in the process — rather than only at the application stage — may be surprised to find conditional offers rescinded following background checks that revealed undisclosed convictions. Understanding where the restriction actually falls is practically important for the population the law most affects.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Fair Chance Act has not been the subject of high-profile fact-check investigations on major platforms because the core claim is substantially accurate and the nuances are legal-procedural rather than involving contested empirical facts. The claim is not false; it is oversimplified. This places it in a category that conventional fact-check workflows struggle with: the claim scores as "true" under headline-level assessment but would score as "partially true" or "needs context" under more granular analysis.

The most useful fact-checking intervention for this type of claim is not a true/false verdict but a precision audit: does the claim accurately represent the scope of the law (five or more employees, not all employers), the mechanism (ban at application stage, not throughout), the process (individualised assessment requirement, not blanket protection), and the exemptions (healthcare, law enforcement, positions with vulnerable populations)? Applicants relying on the strong version of the claim who encounter a post-offer background check or a conditional offer rescission may believe they are being illegally treated when they are not, and may fail to invoke their actual legal rights — the individualised assessment notification process — because they were primed by an inaccurate summary.

Media literacy educators using this case should focus on the specific error type: overgeneralistation from a genuine legal provision. The law is real, significant, and worth knowing about. The error is extrapolating from the genuine core to a broader claim that the law does not support and that the legal text does not establish. Teaching readers to ask "what exactly does the provision cover, and what does it leave untouched?" is the precision habit the Fair Chance Act's coverage most illustrates. The Doleac-Hansen finding about statistical discrimination is also a valuable media literacy case study in what empirical complications can look like for well-intentioned civil rights policy, and it deserves more popular coverage than it has received.
