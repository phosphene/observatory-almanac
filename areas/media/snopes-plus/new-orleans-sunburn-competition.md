# Real Photos of 'Sunburn Competition in New Orleans'?


## 1. The Claim

In early June 2025, a collage of four photographs circulated on social media platforms with the caption identifying them as authentic images of participants in a "sunburn competition" held in New Orleans, with a prize of a $50 gift card to Target. The images showed people with extremely severe, bright-red sunburned skin in poses suggesting competitive display or formal judging. One Facebook post alone received over 64,000 reactions, 49,300 comments, and 24,000 shares before being analyzed by fact-checkers. The same or equivalent images spread across X (formerly Twitter), Facebook, Instagram, TikTok, Reddit, and Threads.

The claim was presented as straightforwardly documentary: these were real people who had deliberately subjected themselves to extreme sun exposure as part of an organized competition with a small monetary prize, and the photographs captured that competition. The caption's specificity about the prize—$50 gift card to Target—was presumably intended to add an element of deadpan authenticity to the absurdist premise, suggesting a community event with mundane institutional validation.

The images were strikingly and deliberately shocking: bodies with skin tones associated with the most severe end of sunburn pathology, presented in a context of voluntary, competitive self-exposure to solar radiation. The emotional response they generated was complex—revulsion mixed with vicarious curiosity, concern for the depicted individuals, disbelief at the premise, and humor at the absurdity—a mixture that reliably drives social media sharing regardless of the content's authenticity.

What makes the claim analytically interesting beyond its immediate falsity is what it reveals about the social mechanics of viral synthetic-media spread: the specific content formula that makes AI-generated "extreme competition" images reliable viral vehicles, the two-year gap between the images' creation and their second major viral circulation, the geographic relabeling that transformed "Florida sunburn competition" into "New Orleans sunburn competition" without changing any visual content, and the systematic features of AI-generated imagery that allow trained analysts to identify synthetic origin while ordinary viewers—even skeptical ones—may find the images plausible.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes correctly rates the claim as Fake on multiple independent grounds, and their investigation is methodologically exemplary. The fact-check by Aleksandra Wrona establishes the images' AI origin through three convergent lines of evidence: visual anomaly analysis, computational AI-detection tools, and identification of the images' original creator.

Visual anomaly analysis identified several telltale markers of AI-generated imagery present in the collage: unnaturally smooth and plastic-like skin texture inconsistent with actual sunburn pathology; distorted features including deformed feet and facial anomalies; body parts appearing to dissolve into the background; hair blending into faces; and skin displaying shapes and textures physically impossible for actual human bodies. These markers are consistent with the characteristic failure modes of diffusion-model image generation as of the 2023 creation date, when models running on Bing Image Creator (DALL-E backbone) produced imagery with recognizable artifacts in fine detail rendering, particularly at the boundaries between skin surfaces and environmental backgrounds.

Computational detection using the Hive AI-detection tool determined with 98% certainty that the images were "likely to be AI-generated." Hive, a commercial AI content-moderation platform, employs ensemble classification models trained on large datasets of known AI-generated and human-photographed imagery. The 98% confidence level reported by the Snopes investigation represents an unusually high confidence score, reflecting the relatively clear visual signature of early DALL-E-derived imagery.

Crucially, Snopes also traced the images' origin through prior fact-check reporting. Reuters and Logically Facts had both investigated the same images in 2023, when they circulated online with the caption "sunburn competition in Florida." Logically Facts traced the images to the Facebook group "Cursed AI," specifically to a post by a member named Will Cartwright, who confirmed directly to the publication that he had created the images using Bing Image Creator with a prompt along the lines of "people competing in an extreme sunburn competition in Florida." A partially archived version of the original August 2023 post exists via the Wayback Machine.

The chain of evidence is therefore complete: the images were created in August 2023 by a named individual using a named AI platform for a named online community, with the original poster explicitly confirming their synthetic origin. The geographic relabeling from "Florida" to "New Orleans" in the 2025 recirculation was the only modification—no new images were generated, no new claims were supported.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The epistemic novelty in this case is not in the detection methodology—Snopes's approach is entirely correct and their chain of evidence is sound—but in the sociology of recirculation: why did images that were publicly debunked in 2023 achieve substantially greater viral spread when recirculated with a geographic label change in 2025, and what does this tell us about the relationship between AI image detection, media literacy interventions, and the lifecycle of synthetic misinformation?

The "two-year lag" recirculation pattern visible here is documented in the misinformation research literature as a characteristic feature of synthetic media longevity. Images and videos that are debunked during an initial viral cycle often retain latent viral potential and recirculate years later in contexts where the debunking is not attached to the recirculated version. The causal mechanism proposed by Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018, *Science*, "The spread of true and false news online") applies here: corrections spread less virally than the original false claims, leaving a population of people who encountered the debunking smaller than the population who encountered the original claim, and a population of people who encountered neither even larger. When the images recirculate with the geographic change, they function as novel content for most new viewers who did not encounter the 2023 cycle.

The geographic relabeling from "Florida" to "New Orleans" is analytically interesting because it suggests a deliberate or semi-deliberate attempt to refresh the images' viral potential by attaching them to a new cultural context. New Orleans carries strong cultural associations with extreme behavior, carnival excess, public nudity (associated with Mardi Gras), and competitive public exhibitions—associations that make a "sunburn competition" seem culturally plausible in New Orleans in a way it might seem slightly less plausible in generic Florida. The geographic update is not arbitrary metadata; it represents an attempt to improve the claim's cultural fit, making the story more believable by attaching it to a location where the behavior described would seem contextually coherent.

This represents a second-order manipulation: not simply recirculating false images but recirculating them with edited framing that increases their apparent plausibility. This pattern—editing caption metadata rather than the images themselves to improve a false claim's believability—is characteristic of what researchers call "context manipulation" or "out-of-context sharing" (Lim, 2020, *Journalism Practice*, "Checking Fake News: How Effective is Content-Based Fact-Checking?"). The images themselves are not new; the manipulated element is the contextual framing that makes them seem more plausible to a new audience.

The specific content type—extreme body modification or injury framed competitively—also warrants analysis. Content featuring bodies in states of extreme physical distress or modification generates a reliably high emotional response characterized by the interaction of disgust, curiosity, and moral concern that psychologists Haidt, McCauley, and Rozin (1994, *Psychological Assessment*, "Individual Differences in Sensitivity to Disgust: A Scale Sampling Seven Domains") identified as constituting the evolved disgust response in humans. This response is not simply negative—it involves compulsive attention and approach as well as revulsion, explaining why people share content that disturbs them. AI image generation tools optimized for viral content would, if used with this psychology in mind, reliably produce content in this register.

Will Cartwright's stated motivation—that "'extreme competition' posts have been very common in the group, often of situations that would be very uncomfortable, which is what gave me the idea"—reflects an accurate understanding of what generates engagement within the Cursed AI community and, as it turned out, beyond it. The group's content formula is built around generating images of plausible-seeming but deeply unpleasant activities presented as ordinary competitive events, exploiting the gap between the mundane competition framing (prizes, judging, participants) and the extreme physical content.


## 4. Verdict

**Propositional Status:** False / Fake
**Confidence:** Very High (explicit source confirmation + AI detection + prior fact-checks)
**Epistemic Basis:** Multi-source triangulation including original creator confirmation
**Phenomenological Status:** Synthetic
**Mechanistic Status:** Known AI generation (Bing Image Creator, DALL-E backbone, August 2023)
**Folk Wisdom Value:** None (pure disinformation vector)

The images are AI-generated, their origin is definitively established, and no "sunburn competition" took place in New Orleans or anywhere else in 2025 (or in Florida in 2023). The verdict is unambiguous.

The more nuanced verdict concerns the implications of this case for synthetic media literacy and detection. The Snopes investigation demonstrates that AI-generated images from 2023 can be identified with high confidence through a combination of visual anomaly analysis and AI-detection tools—but this detection requires trained analysts with access to purpose-built tools. Ordinary social media users encountering the images in their feeds, without the investigation that Snopes conducted, face a content item that is not immediately recognizable as synthetic to an untrained eye. The 64,000 reactions on a single Facebook post suggest that many of those who saw the images did not identify them as AI-generated with sufficient confidence to refrain from engaging with and amplifying them.

The case illustrates an asymmetry in AI-generated content detection that has significant implications for synthetic media governance: detection is feasible but not democratically distributed. Tools like Hive are available to investigators but not integrated into social media feed displays. Visual anomaly analysis requires training that most users have not received. The debunking infrastructure—fact-checking organizations, AI-detection tools—operates downstream from initial viral spread, meaning detection typically happens after substantial propagation has already occurred.

Closing this detection asymmetry gap is a genuine and unresolved challenge in platform governance and AI media literacy. The National Academies of Sciences report on deepfakes (Balwit et al., 2020, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, "AI and Deepfakes: Threats to Democracy and Personal Privacy") identified content provenance infrastructure—cryptographic signing of authentic images at the point of camera capture—as a promising technical solution, and the Content Authenticity Initiative led by Adobe has made progress in developing such standards. But adoption is far from universal and the architecture required for meaningful protection at scale remains nascent.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The "Cursed AI" sunburn competition images are not an anomaly but a representative case study in the current media ecology of AI-generated content, reflecting broader patterns in synthetic image production, community dynamics, and platform amplification that have significant implications for information quality across multiple domains.

**The "Cursed AI" Content Ecosystem**: The Facebook group "Cursed AI" from which these images originated represents a specific and productive genre within AI image creation communities. The genre combines two elements: the deliberate courting of extreme or transgressive content (which is inherently engaging), and the exploitation of AI image generation's characteristic ability to produce superficially plausible scenarios that would be physically difficult or legally impossible to photograph. The "cursed" framing names the content's essential quality—these are images that disturb because they should not exist, because the scenarios they depict are implausible or deeply unpleasant, but that are visually presented with a photographic realism that triggers cognitive uncertainty about whether they might be real.

This genre has significant overlap with existing "shock image" communities that long predated AI image generation—communities like those that shared real photographs of medical conditions, accidents, or extreme human activities with captions misrepresenting their context. AI image generation has dramatically lowered the cost of producing content for this genre by eliminating the need for real images of real events: the AI can generate convincing imagery of scenarios that would require real harm to photograph, making the production of extreme content both easier and ethically cleaner for the creator (since no real bodies are involved).

**Temporal Dynamics of Synthetic Media Recirculation**: The two-year recirculation lag documented in this case is broadly consistent with what is known about false content lifecycle dynamics. Vosoughi et al.'s 2018 Science study demonstrated that false news stories spread faster, farther, and more broadly than true stories across all categories of false content measured; the same asymmetry in initial spread extends to the relationship between correction and recirculation. Wardle and Derakhshan (2017, "Information Disorder: Toward an Interdisciplinary Framework for Research and Policy," Council of Europe) introduced the framework of "information disorder" encompassing misinformation, disinformation, and malinformation, noting that synthetic imagery can function as any of the three depending on the intent and context of the sharer.

The recirculation pattern with geographic label changes represents a specific adaptation strategy for synthetic media with limited original viral shelf life. False content that has been widely debunked in one context retains viral potential in new contexts if the debunking is not widely encountered by new audiences, and geographic relabeling provides a simple low-effort method of refreshing the apparent novelty of content while requiring no modification of the images themselves. This pattern suggests that fact-checking organizations might benefit from maintaining searchable databases of previously debunked synthetic images that could be matched against recirculating content using perceptual hashing or similar image-fingerprinting technologies.

**The Detection Technology Gap**: The 98% AI-detection confidence score from Hive moderation represents a high-precision detection capability in 2025—but the technical landscape of AI-detection is changing rapidly as both generative models and detection systems improve. Research on AI image detection suggests that detection accuracy degrades as generative models improve, creating an ongoing arms race between generation and detection capabilities (Rossler et al., 2019, *Proceedings of the IEEE International Conference on Computer Vision*, "FaceForensics++: Learning to Detect Manipulated Facial Images"). Models trained to detect the artifact signatures of one generation of image generators may be less effective against subsequent generations, meaning that the 98% confidence achievable for 2023-vintage DALL-E imagery may not be replicable for 2025-vintage image generation.

The deeper governance problem is that AI-detection tools are currently deployed primarily by professional fact-checkers and content moderation teams, not integrated into the feed presentation layer where most users encounter content. Even if AI-detection confidence were 99.9%, the protection this affords depends on detection happening before rather than after viral spread—and the current deployment architecture prioritizes post-hoc investigation over preventive flagging.

**Sunburn Biology and the Specific Content Choice**: It is worth noting that the specific content type—extreme sunburn—also has a plausible biological anchoring that contributes to the images' credibility. Severe sunburn pathology is real; erythema and blistering from ultraviolet radiation exposure can produce dramatic and distressing visual changes in skin appearance, and medical photography of severe sunburn cases does exist. The AI-generated content exploits this by producing images that visually resemble real pathological photography, creating plausibility through visual category resemblance even when specific features reveal AI origin.

Understanding this mechanism is important for media literacy education. AI-generated images are most difficult to identify when they fall into visual categories that have real documentary examples—extreme bodies, medical conditions, disasters, accidents—because viewers' uncertainty about whether realistically detailed images of rare events are "real or fake" is itself a genuine epistemic challenge. Teaching media literacy requires not just "AI images often have distorted fingers" but a more sophisticated category-based awareness of when content is visually exploiting real documentary categories to leverage their credibility.

**The Role of Competitive Framing**: Research in social psychology suggests that framing unpleasant activities as competitions reduces moral condemnation and increases engagement among observers. Bruins, Miedema, and Veltman (1998, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, "The Effects of Competition on Motivation") document how competitive framing focuses attention on performance metrics rather than on the activity itself, creating a different moral psychology around the observed behavior. Extreme sunburn framed as a voluntary competition with a small prize activates a different response than extreme sunburn framed as an accident or violent assault: the competitive framing implies consent, agency, and humor, making the content more shareable and less morally distressing than its underlying subject matter would otherwise be.

This mechanism—using competitive framing to rehabilitate extreme content into shareable form—is potentially systematic and applicable across content categories. The "Cursed AI" group's documented pattern of using "extreme competition" prompts for generating disturbing content suggests an implicit understanding of this psychological effect. Content creators in disinformation contexts who use similar framing are potentially leveraging the same psychological insight.

**Platform Responsibility and Labeling Architecture**: The mechanics of how this content spread across multiple platforms without synthetic-content labels raises questions about platform responsibility for content provenance and labeling. Meta, X, TikTok, and Reddit have each made various commitments to labeling AI-generated content, but these commitments have been implemented unevenly and with different technical architectures.

The EU AI Act, which entered into force in 2024, includes provisions requiring disclosure of AI-generated content and mandating that providers of foundation models implement technical measures to ensure that AI-generated content is detectable (European Parliament, 2024, Regulation EU 2024/1689). The practical implementation of these provisions—what technical measures are required, how "detectable" is defined, and what platform obligations are triggered—remains under development through delegated regulation, and the 2025 recirculation of AI-generated images without platform-level disclosure labels suggests that even where legal obligations exist, technical implementation has not fully caught up.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates this claim as Fake, published June 11, 2025. The investigation by Aleksandra Wrona is methodologically strong and exemplary in its use of multiple independent evidence streams. The fact-check deploys reverse image search, Google search for corroborating news coverage, AI-detection tools (Hive), visual anomaly analysis with annotated screenshots, discovery of prior fact-checks by Reuters (2023) and Logically Facts (2023), and identification of the original creator through the secondary fact-check record.

The prior fact-check discovery is particularly valuable: Snopes essentially benefits from the investigative work done by Reuters and Logically Facts in 2023, which had already established the images' AI origin and traced them to Will Cartwright and the Cursed AI Facebook group. By citing this prior work and connecting the 2025 circulation to the 2023 original, Snopes provides a more complete picture of the content's lifecycle than a from-scratch investigation would have allowed.

The treatment of the Hive AI-detection result is appropriately calibrated—Snopes presents the 98% confidence figure as supporting evidence rather than as the sole proof, which is correct given that AI-detection tools have known limitations and that a single computational confidence score should not be treated as independently conclusive without corroborating evidence. The convergence of visual analysis, prior fact-checks, and computational detection provides a more robust epistemic foundation than any single line of evidence could supply.

The analytical gap in the Snopes treatment is in the domain of platform and governance analysis. The fact-check establishes what the images are and where they came from but does not substantially engage with why they spread as far as they did despite being clearly identifiable as AI-generated to trained analysts, what the two-year recirculation lag tells us about the lifecycle of synthetic media misinformation, or what technical or governance interventions might reduce the viral potential of content with known synthetic origins. These questions are beyond the scope of a standard fact-check but are precisely the domain where additional analytical depth would add value for readers seeking to understand not just whether a specific claim is false but why false claims of this type reliably spread and what structural responses might be appropriate.

This case is an unusually clean example of the category of claims Snopes is well-positioned to handle: false, definitively verifiable, with a traceable origin and a clear chain of evidence. The methodological template it demonstrates—visual analysis, AI-detection tools, reverse image search, prior fact-check review, original-creator identification—could usefully serve as a model for AI-generated image investigations across the broader fact-checking ecosystem.
