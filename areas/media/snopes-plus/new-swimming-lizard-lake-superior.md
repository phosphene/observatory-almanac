# New Swimming Lizard Was Not Discovered in Lake Superior


## 1. The Claim

In October 2024, a 59-second TikTok video described as showing footage recorded by scuba divers in Lake Superior, Michigan began circulating on the platform and subsequently spread to Instagram. The video, posted to the account @nunchakusdragon, claimed to show "a whole pack" of previously undiscovered swimming lizards dwelling "super deep in the water." The video's narrator acknowledged that the animal "looks like a marine iguana" — which is exactly what the animal is, and exactly what the animal is not doing anywhere near Lake Superior. The video accumulated significant engagement on both TikTok and Instagram, and the claim was widely repeated in comments speculating about undiscovered freshwater reptile species in the Great Lakes.

The claim belongs to a well-documented pattern of viral wildlife misidentification: in this case, the combination of genuinely spectacular footage (marine iguanas are visually arresting, and footage of groups of them in and around water is inherently compelling) with completely fabricated geographic attribution. The narrator's own acknowledgment that the animal "looks like a marine iguana" is an instance of the subject performing its own partial debunking within the claim — and the audience's willingness to nevertheless accept the Lake Superior attribution illustrates how strongly the desire for a surprising discovery can override the evidence of direct observation.

The claim connects to a broader cultural fascination with undiscovered creatures in familiar environments — a variant of the cryptid-belief tradition, in which the sense of wonder at finding something monstrous or unknown in a known location is the desired emotional experience. Lake Superior is the largest of the Great Lakes by surface area, famously cold and deep, and has a popular mythology of its own, including the persistence of local legends about unusual creatures in its depths. The Lake Superior setting primes the audience to believe that something extraordinary might plausibly be found there.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The animal in the video is definitively identified.** A reverse image search on the TikTok video led to the same footage — and many substantially similar videos — posted on other platforms identifying the animals as marine iguanas (*Amblyrhynchus cristatus*), the only sea-going lizards in the world. The identification is unambiguous: marine iguanas are distinctive in appearance, with dark coloration (adults are predominantly black for most of the year, as shown in the video), laterally flattened tails adapted for swimming, and a characteristic blunt-snouted profile. There is no other species that could be confused with marine iguanas by anyone who has seen footage of them; the TikTok narrator's own observation that the animals "look like" marine iguanas represents essentially a correct identification.

**Marine iguanas are exclusively Galápagos residents.** According to the Galápagos Conservation Trust, *Amblyrhynchus cristatus* is endemic to the Galápagos Islands — meaning it evolved there and is found naturally nowhere else in the world. The Galápagos archipelago consists of 13 major islands and numerous smaller islets and rocks belonging to Ecuador, located approximately 900 kilometres west of the South American mainland near the equator. The islands' geographic isolation, which drove the high degree of endemic speciation that made them famous as the site of Charles Darwin's observations during the voyage of HMS *Beagle*, also means that marine iguanas have no natural range outside the archipelago. They are adapted to the specific temperature regimes, food sources (primarily marine algae), and ecological conditions of the Galápagos marine environment.

The IUCN Iguana Specialist Group lists 46 living iguana species and one extinct species. None have natural habitats associated with Lake Superior or any of the surrounding geographic region.

**Lake Superior's actual reptile fauna.** Lake Superior is bordered by the U.S. states of Michigan, Minnesota, and Wisconsin, and the Canadian province of Ontario. None of the reptile species documented in these jurisdictions bear any resemblance to marine iguanas:

- The Wisconsin Department of Natural Resources lists four lizard species: the common five-lined skink (*Plestiodon fasciatus*), prairie skink (*Plestiodon septentrionalis*), six-lined racerunner (*Aspidoscelis sexlineata*), and slender glass lizard (*Ophisaurus attenuatus*). None exceed a few dozen centimetres in total length, and none are aquatic.
- The Michigan Department of Natural Resources lists two lizard species: the five-lined skink and six-lined racerunner.
- The Minnesota Department of Natural Resources lists the same three: common five-lined skink, prairie skink, and six-lined racerunner.

All of these species are small terrestrial or semi-fossorial lizards. The largest among them — the slender glass lizard — can reach approximately 60 to 107 centimetres but is legless and resembles a snake rather than the robust, swimming, dark-coloured animal in the video.

**The physical impossibility of the claim.** Lake Superior's water temperature effectively rules out marine iguana survival even if individuals were somehow transported there. Marine iguanas, as ectotherms (cold-blooded reptiles), regulate their body temperature through behaviour — they bask in the Galápagos sun to warm themselves after feeding in cold ocean waters, which deplete their body heat. They are adapted to the Galápagos' equatorial climate and have no physiological mechanisms to tolerate the temperatures of Lake Superior, where surface temperatures range from approximately 4°C (39°F) in winter to approximately 22°C (72°F) at peak summer in shallow areas, with deep-water temperatures remaining near 4°C year-round. The Galápagos ocean temperatures around which marine iguana physiology has evolved range from approximately 18°C to 28°C. An individual marine iguana introduced to Lake Superior would suffer fatal hypothermia with near certainty.

Furthermore, marine iguanas feed almost exclusively on marine algae, particularly the intertidal and subtidal algae of the Galápagos rocky coastline. Lake Superior, as a freshwater lake, contains none of the marine algal species on which marine iguanas depend, and the freshwater algae of the lake would not substitute for their dietary requirements.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The documentary aesthetics of the footage.** Wildlife footage — particularly underwater or diving footage of animals in their natural environment — carries strong documentary credibility signals. Audiences are conditioned by decades of nature documentary viewing to identify this type of footage as authentic evidence of real events. The footage in the video is indeed authentic; it is genuine footage of genuine marine iguanas in the Galápagos Islands. The authenticity of the footage provides a credibility transfer to the false caption in a way that a digital fabrication would not. Acknowledging to oneself that the footage is real is incompatible, for many viewers, with simultaneously questioning whether the location and context are accurately described. The cognitive shortcut — authentic footage must mean authentic description — is exploited by the false framing.

**Discovery narrative appeal.** The claim that a new species has been discovered in a familiar environment activates a powerful and recurrent social media content pattern: the unexpected creature found where it should not be. This pattern draws on the emotional experience of scientific discovery — the sense that the world is larger and stranger than maps suggest — and applies it to familiar geographic contexts that the audience has personal connection to. Lake Superior is a real, named, known lake in the United States and Canada; it is not a remote Amazon tributary or a deep Pacific trench. The claim that something extraordinary was found there, by scuba divers, "super deep in the water," is optimally tuned to provoke the "this can't be real — wait, could it be real?" response that drives high engagement.

**Partial credibility from the narrator's hedging.** The TikTok narrator's statement that the animal "looks like a marine iguana" is a form of epistemic inoculation against debunking: by naming the species the animal resembles, the narrator demonstrates awareness of marine iguanas and implicitly suggests that the comparison was considered and rejected in favour of the "new species in Lake Superior" explanation. This makes the claim seem less naive and more researched than a flat assertion would be. Audiences may read the narrator as having done due diligence and concluded that despite resembling a marine iguana, the animal is something different. In fact, the animal is exactly what it appears to be, and the narrator's claimed consideration of the marine iguana comparison functions as misdirection rather than evidence of genuine investigation.

**Epistemic novelty: the endemic species concept as fact-check tool.** The concept of endemism — species that are found only in a specific geographic location and nowhere else in the world due to evolutionary isolation — is not universally understood by general audiences. Marine iguanas are a paradigm case of endemic species: they evolved in isolation on the Galápagos Islands over millions of years, and there is no wild population anywhere else. Understanding endemism immediately resolves the question of whether marine iguanas could be "discovered" in Lake Superior: the answer is categorically no, because their natural range is as precisely defined as any geographic boundary in natural science, and that range does not include any part of North America. Endemism is perhaps the single concept whose absence most efficiently explains why a factually impossible claim can nonetheless attract credulous engagement; general audiences who have not internalised the concept of restricted native range lack the intellectual tool to immediately identify the geographic impossibility of the claim.


## 4. The Verified Facts

**Miscaptioned — the video is authentic footage of marine iguanas in the Galápagos Islands, not a new species found in Lake Superior.** The footage is real and shows real animals. The animals are *Amblyrhynchus cristatus*, the marine iguana, which is endemic exclusively to the Galápagos Islands of Ecuador. No species identified in the states bordering Lake Superior resembles the animals in the video. Marine iguanas could not survive in Lake Superior due to temperature, dietary, and physiological incompatibility. The "new species discovered in Lake Superior" framing is entirely fabricated; the most probable explanation is that the footage was deliberately mislabelled to generate engagement.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The marine iguana / Lake Superior case is a clean example of the "authentic footage, false context" category of viral misinformation, which has become an increasingly significant subset of image and video-based false claims. The category is distinct from digitally fabricated imagery (deepfakes, AI-generated images) and from miscontextualised historical photographs. It involves real footage of real things with completely invented captions about where, when, or what the footage shows. The challenge this category poses for fact-checkers and media literacy educators is that traditional suspicion of "that doesn't look real" is misplaced — the footage does look real because it is real. The question is not authenticity but attribution.

Reverse image search and reverse video search have become the primary methodological tools for addressing this category. When the same footage appears on other platforms under correct identification — as was the case here, where the same video appeared elsewhere correctly identified as marine iguana footage — the attribution error can be rapidly resolved. But the accessibility of these tools is uneven; many social media users who encounter mislabelled wildlife footage do not have the habit of verifying video provenance before sharing, and the engagement dynamics of social media reward immediate sharing over deliberate verification.

The case also illustrates the particular vulnerability of wildlife content to fabricated captions. Wildlife videos are a reliably high-engagement content category; dramatic animal footage generates strong emotional responses and high share rates. This makes wildlife footage commercially attractive as raw material for misleading frame-stories. The pattern is not limited to marine iguanas in Lake Superior: similar mislabelled wildlife videos have attributed Galápagos animals to locations across the U.S., attributed deep-sea footage to familiar bodies of water, and attributed rare African or Asian species to North American locations. The underlying mechanism is consistent: find compelling authentic footage of a visually striking animal, apply a geographic caption that activates local connection and discovery-narrative appeal, and let the engagement dynamics of social media do the rest.

**The Galápagos as a recurring source.** Marine iguanas and other Galápagos species appear with particular frequency in mislabelled wildlife videos, for a straightforward reason: the Galápagos Islands have extremely unusual wildlife that is visually remarkable and photographed extensively by ecotourists. Animals that evolved in the absence of significant predator pressure are often habituated to human presence and can be photographed at close range, producing footage that is both professionally composed-looking and genuinely spectacular. Marine iguanas swimming in groups, diving for algae, and basking on rocks produce outstanding footage that circulates widely in legitimate natural history contexts. That circulation makes mislabelled clips both easy to find and easy to repost as "discovered in" a context that would generate more local engagement than "filmed in the Galápagos" — a location most viewers associate with documentaries rather than their own experience.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated this claim "Miscaptioned" in its December 2024 analysis by Madison Dapcevich, identifying the animals in the video as marine iguanas through reverse-image and reverse-video search, confirming the animals' Galápagos endemism through the Galápagos Conservation Trust, cataloguing the actual lizard species documented in the U.S. states bordering Lake Superior, and establishing the IUCN Iguana Specialist Group's record of 46 living iguana species — none of which have natural habitats near Lake Superior.

The "Miscaptioned" verdict is a useful and increasingly important fact-check category that distinguishes between false footage (the common understanding of "fake video") and authentic footage with fabricated context. For media literacy educators, the distinction is methodologically important: the skill of evaluating whether footage is real is different from the skill of evaluating whether the claimed context of real footage is accurate. Both skills are necessary in the current media environment.

For fact-checkers encountering mislabelled wildlife videos, the core methodological steps are: (1) perform reverse image/video searches to identify prior appearances of the footage with different captions; (2) consult authoritative sources for the species' geographic range — species distribution databases, conservation organisations, and government natural resources agencies all maintain authoritative range data; (3) check state/provincial natural resources databases for the species documented in the claimed location; (4) assess the physical plausibility of the claimed environment for the observed species; and (5) consider the incentive structure — if the correct location would have generated much less engagement than the claimed location, that asymmetry is a red flag for deliberate mislabelling rather than innocent error. The discipline of asking "what is this actually, and where does it actually live?" before accepting a wildlife video's stated context is becoming an essential component of everyday media literacy in an era when spectacular authentic footage is available for repurposing at essentially zero cost.
