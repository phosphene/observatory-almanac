# "No Two Snowflakes Are Exactly Alike"


## 1. The Claim

"No two snowflakes are exactly alike" ranks among the most culturally ubiquitous statements in popular science. It appears on winter decorations, in children's books, in motivational posters deployed by educators who use it as a metaphor for human individuality. It circulates through science classes as both a genuine meteorological fact and a lesson in uniqueness. It is invoked so frequently in non-scientific contexts that it has acquired the character of a folk axiom — something "everyone knows" about the natural world.

The claim is interesting because it inhabits an unusual epistemological position: unlike most claims about nature, it is essentially impossible to verify directly. Nobody has compared all the snowflakes ever formed; nobody ever will. The claim is therefore not an empirical finding in the conventional sense but a probabilistic inference — a statement about overwhelming likelihood rather than verified observation. This does not make it false, but it does make it more interesting and more nuanced than its casual deployment suggests.

It is also complicated by scale: at what level of physical description must two snowflakes be similar to count as "alike"? Two snowflakes can look virtually identical to the naked eye while being distinguishable at the molecular level. Two snowflakes made of molecules that are in every chemically resolvable way identical could still differ in their distribution of rare water isotopes. The claim's truth value shifts depending on what "exactly alike" means — and most people who repeat it have never thought carefully about that question.

The cultural weight of the claim extends far beyond meteorology. "No two snowflakes are alike" is the most quoted fact in favor of natural uniqueness; it is deployed as evidence that the universe generates difference without bound, and that individuality is a fundamental feature of complex natural processes rather than an exception. This makes accuracy in discussing it particularly important.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim is almost certainly true for complex natural snowflakes, but "almost certainly" is load-bearing in that sentence in ways casual usage ignores.

**The physical formation process:** A snowflake forms when a water droplet freezes around a microscopic nucleus — typically a dust particle or pollen grain — high in the atmosphere. As the growing ice crystal descends through varying temperature and humidity zones, water vapor deposits onto its surface via a process called diffusion-limited aggregation. The crystal's shape at any moment in its development depends on the precise temperature and humidity of the air immediately surrounding it. Because temperature and humidity vary continuously through the atmosphere, and because a crystal's trajectory through the atmosphere is approximately unique, each crystal experiences a distinct sequence of environmental conditions during its formation. This unique developmental history produces a unique structural outcome.

The molecular scale of snowflake complexity is extraordinary. A single snowflake of typical size (roughly 5 mm diameter, a few micrometers thick) contains approximately 10^18 water molecules. The arrangement of these molecules encodes the entire developmental history of the snowflake: every shift in temperature or humidity during the crystal's descent is recorded in the branching and layering structure of the crystal arms. Two snowflakes following different trajectories through the same storm will have experienced different developmental environments and will therefore differ at some structural level.

**The probability argument:** Atmospheric physicist Kenneth Libbrecht at Caltech has estimated the number of distinct physical states available to a snowflake of typical complexity (Libbrecht, 2006, *Scientific American*, "The Formation of Snow Crystals," December). His analysis, considering the number of possible molecular configurations across the crystal's structure, yields numbers so astronomically large — far exceeding the total number of atoms in the observable universe — that the probability of two natural complex snowflakes being identical is effectively zero for any practical calculation. This is the rigorous basis for the "no two alike" claim: not that identical snowflakes are physically impossible, but that the state space of possible snowflakes is incomprehensibly vast relative to the number of snowflakes ever formed.

Estimates of total snowflakes fallen in Earth's history run to approximately 10^35 (a rough figure derived from annual snowfall estimates over geological timescales). The number of distinguishable molecular configurations for a typical snowflake is estimated to enormously exceed this figure by many orders of magnitude. The probability of exact duplication is therefore vanishingly small — functionally zero under any conditions likely to be encountered in nature.

**The laboratory exception:** Physicist Nancy Knight at the National Center for Atmospheric Research reported in 1988 an observation of two visually identical simple columnar snow crystals collected over Wisconsin during a research flight (Knight, 1988, *Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society*, 69(5), 496-497). These were not complex stellar dendrites — the elaborate six-armed snowflakes seen in photographs — but simple hollow columns at an extremely early stage of crystalline development. Their visual similarity at photographic resolution did not mean they were identical at the molecular level; Knight herself noted that they could well differ in subtle structural details invisible to her instrumentation. Nevertheless, this case established that even the observational claim — no two snowflakes look alike — can be challenged for simple crystal forms.

Subsequently, Kenneth Libbrecht and colleagues have grown visually identical simple snow crystals deliberately under controlled laboratory conditions (Libbrecht & Tanusheva, 1998, *Physical Review E*, 58(1), 75-85). By maintaining precise temperature and humidity, they produced simple prismatic crystals that were indistinguishable by optical microscopy. Again, these were simple early-stage crystals, not the complex dendrites the popular claim envisions.

**The resolution:** The claim is therefore true in modern practice: for the complex, dendritic, full-sized snowflakes most people visualize when they hear "snowflake," the probability of two being molecularly identical is so small as to be effectively nil. For very small, simple snow crystals at early stages of development, visual identity is achievable both in nature and in the lab. The accuracy of the claim depends entirely on which snow crystals are under consideration and at what level of description "identical" is defined.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The claim is well-grounded — but as probabilistic inference, not empirical observation:** One revealing feature of this claim's epistemology is that most people who repeat it believe it as an observational fact when it is actually a theoretical inference. No one has systematically compared snowflakes looking for identical pairs. The claim's truth rests on probability calculations about state spaces, not on exhaustive observation. This matters because it distinguishes "no two snowflakes are alike" from other empirical claims: belief in it, if it is rational, should rest on accepting mathematical arguments about combinatorial complexity, not on trusting that someone has looked.

Wilson Alwyn "Snowflake" Bentley, the Vermont farmer and amateur photographer who spent decades (1885–1931) photographing snow crystals and never found two alike, is often cited as evidence for the claim (Bentley & Humphreys, 1931, *Snow Crystals*, McGraw-Hill). Bentley photographed approximately 5,381 crystals over his career. This is an admirable body of work, but 5,381 is a negligible sample from the 10^35 total. The appropriate reading of Bentley's work is at best "among the crystals Bentley photographed, no two appeared identical," which is consistent with the probabilistic argument but is not independent confirmation of it.

**The cultural metaphor creates motivated belief:** "No two snowflakes are alike" is one of the most useful metaphors in popular culture for expressing the idea of infinite natural variation and the uniqueness of individual identity. Parents who tell children "you're as unique as a snowflake" are not citing crystallography; they are deploying a metaphor that derives its rhetorical force from the supposed physical fact. This creates a motivated interest in the factual claim being true: if it turned out that many snowflakes were identical, the metaphor would be weakened. Cultural investment in the truth of a claim is one of the most reliable predictors of that claim's persistence regardless of evidential status.

The identical direction runs: snowflakes are unique, humans are unique, therefore human uniqueness is a general natural law. This is a category error (human genetic and developmental specificity rests on different mechanisms than crystal formation), but it is emotionally compelling. The factual claim about snowflakes carries the weight of the metaphorical argument about human individuality, which encourages its uncritical acceptance.

**Difficulty of falsification inflates confidence:** The impossibility of comparing all snowflakes ever formed means the claim cannot be directly falsified. People who believe unfalsifiable claims often experience greater confidence in them than is epistemically warranted, precisely because they are never confronted with counter-evidence. The claim is empirically safe in the way that any claim about an infinite or effectively infinite sample is empirically safe: there is always more sample space to hide a potential falsifier.

This is distinct from the claim being irrational or unjustified — the probability argument for the claim's truth is sound. But the psychological experience of "this can never be proven wrong" can be mistaken for "this is proven right," and the two are not equivalent.

**Simple vs. complex crystals are not distinguished by most people:** The visual archetype of "snowflake" in popular culture is the complex stellar dendrite: the elaborate six-armed, branching crystal seen on December greetings from at least the 19th century. The conditions that produce these spectacularly complex crystals — specific temperature ranges, appropriate humidity, sufficient time — are not universal. Many snowflakes that actually fall from the sky are simpler: needles, columns, plates, irregularly aggregated polycrystalline masses. The "no two alike" claim is robustly true for complex dendrites but is potentially false for simple early-stage crystals. Because people imagine dendrites when they hear "snowflake," they accept the overall claim without registering that it is less secure for the simpler crystal types that make up a substantial fraction of actual snowfall.

**The claim was introduced by someone credible doing empirical work:** Bentley's photographic project gave the "no two alike" observation genuine empirical roots. A Vermont farmer who spent 46 winters laboriously photographing individual snow crystals and never found duplicates carries real authority. His historical credibility launders the claim even when the reasoning behind accepting it should be purely probabilistic. "Bentley looked at thousands and never found duplicates" is a weaker argument than the combinatorial mathematics, but it is more accessible and more memorable.


## 4. Verdict

**MOSTLY TRUE WITH IMPORTANT QUALIFICATIONS** — The claim is overwhelmingly likely to be true for complex natural snowflakes, but its truth rests on probabilistic inference rather than direct observation, and at small scales or under controlled conditions, visually identical simple snow crystals have been documented.

**For complex dendritic snowflakes:** The claim is robustly supported. The number of possible molecular configurations for a full-sized complex snowflake is so vast that the probability of any two natural snowflakes being identical is for all practical purposes zero. This conclusion follows from rigorous analysis of the state space available to ice crystals of typical complexity (Libbrecht, 2006, *Scientific American*).

**For simple early-stage snow crystals:** The claim is less secure. Nancy Knight (1988, *Bulletin of the American Meteorological Society*) collected apparently identical hollow columnar crystals in 1988, and Kenneth Libbrecht has deliberately produced visually identical simple crystals under controlled laboratory conditions (Libbrecht & Tanusheva, 1998, *Physical Review E*). These cases do not refute the claim for complex snowflakes but establish that the generalization does not apply universally to all snow crystal forms.

**The epistemological point:** The claim is justified by probability theory and combinatorial analysis, not by exhaustive observation. Its truth cannot be directly verified and should be understood as a statement about overwhelming likelihood rather than a verified empirical finding. This is a legitimate basis for the claim — many well-established scientific facts rest on theoretical inference rather than direct observation — but the epistemological status should be acknowledged rather than elided.

**The cultural use:** The deployment of this claim as a metaphor for human uniqueness, while emotionally resonant, reflects a category error. Human uniqueness rests on genetic variability, developmental individuality, and biographical specificity — mechanisms that are not relevantly similar to the chaotic trajectory of an ice crystal through the atmosphere.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**Ice crystal physics and morphology:** The morphology of ice crystals — the shapes they adopt as they grow — is one of the more beautiful problems in applied physics. The macroscopic shape of a snow crystal is determined by the micro-scale growth rates of each crystal face, which are themselves determined by temperature and supersaturation (the degree to which the air is oversaturated with water vapor relative to ice). Libbrecht's comprehensive research program at Caltech, spanning several decades, has produced detailed phase diagrams mapping ice crystal morphology as a function of temperature and supersaturation (Libbrecht, 2005, *Reports on Progress in Physics*, 68(4), 855-895). These diagrams reveal complex and non-monotonic relationships: at around -2°C, crystals tend to be thin plates; at -5°C, hollow columns or needles; at -15°C, the thin flat plates associated with the stellar dendrites of popular imagination; at -30°C, simple hollow columns again.

This temperature dependence means that a snowflake's shape is a record of the temperature history it has traversed during its descent, and because temperature profiles vary continuously across storm cells, each crystal acquires a structurally unique record. This is the physical mechanism underlying the uniqueness claim: a snowflake is, in a meaningful sense, an integration of the atmospheric conditions it passed through, encoded in crystal structure.

**Water isotope variation:** Another source of variation is the isotopic composition of the water molecules forming the crystal. Natural water contains several oxygen isotopes (oxygen-16, oxygen-17, oxygen-18) and hydrogen isotopes (protium, deuterium, and trace tritium). The distribution of these isotopes in a given snowflake is determined by the isotopic composition of the water vapor in the air at each point during its formation. Isotopic ratios vary across different atmospheric masses, and their specific distribution in a crystal provides another layer of molecular information distinguishing it from any other crystal.

Isotope ratio analysis of ice cores is a major tool in paleoclimatology, used to reconstruct historical temperature records from ice preserved over thousands of years (Dansgaard, 1964, *Tellus*, 16(4), 436-468). The same principle that makes ice cores informationally rich — each layer's isotopic signature encodes the temperature conditions of the year it formed — amplifies the "no two alike" argument by adding isotopic variation to geometrical variation as a distinguishing criterion.

**The information-theoretic argument:** From an information-theoretic standpoint, a complex snowflake can be thought of as storing an enormous amount of information about its formation history. The number of bits required to specify the complete molecular arrangement of a complex snowflake vastly exceeds the information capacity that would be needed to generate a duplicate from the same atmospheric initial conditions, because atmospheric conditions themselves vary at spatial and temporal scales far below those accessible to macroscopic measurement. This suggests that exact duplication would require not just similar atmospheric conditions but literally identical microscopic states of the atmosphere — which thermodynamic principles make effectively impossible over any macroscopic volume.

**Historical development of the claim:** The association of snowflake observation with uniqueness has deeper historical roots than Bentley. Johannes Kepler's 1611 essay *Strena Seu de Nive Sexangula* (A New Year's Gift of Hexagonal Snow) analyzed snow crystal symmetry, noting the six-fold symmetry of snowflakes and speculating about its origin. Kepler was not specifically arguing for uniqueness, but his foundational analysis of snow crystal morphology set the stage for later detailed study. René Descartes sketched snow crystal shapes in his 1637 *Les Météores*, producing what are probably the first European illustrations of snowflake morphology. The claim was eventually formalized through Bentley's photographic project and later through the mathematical analyses of Libbrecht and colleagues.

**Philosophical implications for identity:** The snowflake uniqueness claim is frequently pressed into service as evidence for a philosophical position about natural individuality: that the universe generates distinct individuals without bound, that identity is a fundamental feature of physical processes rather than a human projection. This is a substantive philosophical claim — often associated with Leibniz's principle of the identity of indiscernibles — and it should be evaluated on its philosophical merits rather than treated as empirically settled by snowflake physics.

Whether two physically distinct objects are "identical" depends entirely on what level of physical description is considered relevant. Two hydrogen atoms at absolute zero are identical in every quantum mechanically meaningful sense (they are fermions subject to quantum statistics that makes them inherently indistinguishable). Whether snowflakes can be identical at any practically relevant level of description depends on whether the relevant level is macroscopic shape, optical appearance, X-ray diffraction pattern, isotopic composition, or full quantum mechanical state. The claim's truth value cannot be evaluated without specifying this. The philosophical deployment of the snowflake claim usually ignores this dependency, treating physical distinctness as a single unambiguous category rather than a level-relative one.

**Environmental science and snowpack:** The uniqueness of snow crystals has practical implications in atmospheric chemistry and snow science. The specific surface area of snowpack — which determines reaction rates for atmospheric chemical processes and meltwater production rates — depends on the distribution of crystal shapes, which is itself determined by the temperature and humidity profile experienced during snowfall (Legagneux, Cabanes, & Domin, 2002, *Journal of Geophysical Research — Atmospheres*, 107(D17), 4335). Understanding snow crystal morphology distribution is therefore directly relevant to snowpack chemistry, avalanche science, and hydrology. The variability of snowflake structures is not merely a charming curiosity but an operationally significant physical reality in Earth systems science.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not formally addressed the "no two snowflakes are alike" claim, perhaps because it is broadly accurate and therefore less urgently in need of debunking than straightforwardly false myths. When science communication organizations have addressed it — including *Scientific American*, *Popular Science*, and various science education institutions — they typically rate it as true with qualifications: true for complex natural snowflakes, false or uncertain for simple crystal forms, and justified by probability rather than observation.

The claim occupies an unusual position in the myth-busting landscape: it is a popular claim that is largely accurate but tends to be stated without its epistemological foundations, resulting in uncritical acceptance rather than informed assent. It is also a case where the cultural use of the claim (as a metaphor for uniqueness) slightly outstrips its evidential basis — not by much, and not in ways that cause harm, but in ways that obscure the genuinely interesting probability theory and crystal physics that give it real grounding.

The most scientifically honest version of the claim is neither the confident assertion familiar from winter decorations nor the skeptical challenge that laboratory production of similar simple crystals seems to imply. It is something more precise: for the complex dendritic crystals that form under the conditions responsible for most photogenic snowfall, the probability of any two being identical across all of Earth's snowfall history is negligibly small, for reasons rooted in the extraordinary combinatorial complexity of large-scale crystalline structures recording chaotic atmospheric trajectories. That statement is less catchy, more accurate, and more illuminating than either its popular version or its debunking.
