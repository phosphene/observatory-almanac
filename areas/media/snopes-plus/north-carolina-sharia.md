# North Carolina Banned Sharia Law?

## 1. The Claim

Beginning in mid-2013, news reports and social media posts announced that North Carolina had "banned Sharia law" — a phrase that circulated widely both within the United States and internationally, particularly in Muslim-majority countries and among European observers tracking American attitudes toward Islam. The claim typically appeared in one of two framings: as a statement of alarm from civil liberties advocates, who argued the law was discriminatory and anti-Muslim, and as a statement of approval from socially conservative commentators, who presented it as a necessary protection of American constitutional norms against the alleged encroachment of religious governance.

The specific legal vehicle was Senate Bill 2, titled the "Foreign Legal Judgments" act, later codified as the "Foreign Law Judgments Act," signed into law by Governor Pat McCrory on August 27, 2013. It passed the North Carolina Senate 32–14 and the House 67–43, with support almost exclusively from Republican members. The bill text reads, in relevant part, that no court in North Carolina shall "apply a foreign law" if doing so would deprive "any party of the rights, privileges, or immunities guaranteed by the Constitution of the United States or the Constitution of North Carolina."

The popular claim — that North Carolina "banned Sharia law" — is factually grounded in an authentic legislative event but mischaracterises what the law actually prohibits. The mischaracterisation serves political purposes in both directions: it makes the law sound more dramatic (and more anti-Islamic) than its text requires, while also making it easier for supporters to present as content-neutral and religion-blind.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

### The Law's Text

Senate Bill 2 does not mention Sharia, Islamic law, fiqh, or any religion by name. The operative prohibition is against the enforcement of foreign court judgments or contracts governed by a "foreign law" — defined broadly as law from outside the United States, its territories, and its tribal nations — where such enforcement would abridge rights guaranteed by federal or state constitutions. The statute is formally neutral on its face: it applies to Roman Catholic canon law, Jewish beth din arbitration agreements, Hindu family law, secular European civil codes, and any other foreign legal system equally.

Proponents of the bill argued this was precisely the point: the measure was not about Islam at all but about affirming the supremacy of American constitutional rights over any foreign legal jurisdiction. Critics argued the facially neutral text was drafted in specific response to perceived threats from Islamic law and that the primary practical effect would fall disproportionately on Muslim individuals and families navigating transnational family law.

### The ALEC Model and the Nationwide Pattern

Senate Bill 2 derived directly from model legislation developed by the American Legislative Exchange Council (ALEC) and, specifically, from a template drafted by attorney David Yerushalmi under the heading "American Laws for American Courts" (ALAC). Yerushalmi has been identified by researchers including Laila Lalami and the Southern Poverty Law Center as a principal architect of the organised "anti-Sharia" legislative movement in the United States. The ALAC template was adopted by multiple state legislatures in the 2010–2016 period, with slight variations in language. Oklahoma's voters passed the earliest version in 2010, but it was struck down by a federal appeals court in *Awad v. Ziriax* (2012) on First Amendment grounds, partly because the Oklahoma version explicitly named Sharia.

The North Carolina version, like later iterations developed after *Awad*, stripped out explicit references to Sharia in an effort to survive constitutional challenge. This is exactly the pattern that makes the "anti-Sharia bill" characterisation simultaneously accurate and misleading: the legislative intent, documented in sponsor statements and advocacy materials, was oriented toward Islamic law, but the statutory text had to be neutralised to avoid the fate of Oklahoma's version.

Researchers at the National Conference of State Legislatures tracked at least 201 anti-Sharia or foreign-law bills introduced in 43 state legislatures between 2010 and 2012 alone (National Conference of State Legislatures, 2013). By 2017, approximately 15 states had enacted some version of foreign law restriction legislation, concentrated in the South and Great Plains, with North Carolina, Louisiana, Kansas, Tennessee, and Alabama among the earliest adopters (Nichols & Stiles, 2018, *Journal of Church and State*).

### Actual Legal Effects

The real-world effects of foreign law restriction statutes like North Carolina's are concentrated primarily in family law — specifically in the recognition of marriages, divorces, custody agreements, and inheritance arrangements executed under foreign legal systems. This disproportionately affects Muslims who have entered into contracts under Islamic marriage law, including mahr (a dower payment stipulated in the marriage contract), because such contracts can be governed by fiqh and often reference Islamic legal principles.

Courts in several states have used similar statutes to decline enforcement of mahr agreements, reasoning that the religious legal framework governing them constitutes "foreign law." Studies of case outcomes post-enactment found that Muslim women — who are disproportionately the party seeking enforcement of mahr, which functions as a financial protection — were the individuals most practically disadvantaged (Estin, 2012, *Iowa Law Review*; Shah, 2015, *Columbia Human Rights Law Review*). This outcome was precisely opposite to the bill sponsors' characterisation of the legislation as protective of women's rights against religious patriarchy.

Jewish beth din arbitration agreements have also been affected in some state contexts, though because beth din operates in the United States as private arbitration rather than foreign law, the statutory language has been interpreted inconsistently — a legal ambiguity that has generated its own litigation.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The "North Carolina banned Sharia law" claim persists because it is, in its essential outline, true — a law was passed, a governor signed it, it is directed against foreign legal influence, and it was explicitly promoted by its sponsors as a bulwark against Islamic governance. The claim's inaccuracy lies in what it implies rather than what it states: that Sharia is a concrete legal code in any meaningful sense analogous to a foreign nation-state's law, that it was in realistic danger of being "applied" in North Carolina courts, and that the law's purpose was surgical removal of a genuinely present threat.

### The "Creeping Sharia" Moral Panic

The broader context for the bill's passage is a sustained moral panic that scholars of religion and political science have documented under the label "creeping Sharia" — the claim that Islamic law is systematically infiltrating American legal and civic institutions, heading toward eventual replacement of the Constitution. Researchers including Peter Gottschalk and Gabriel Greenberg (*Islamophobia: Making Muslims the Enemy*, 2008) traced this discourse to post-9/11 cultural anxiety about Muslim immigration and political organisation in the United States, amplified after 2009 by the Tea Party movement and certain conservative media outlets.

The "creeping Sharia" narrative does not map onto legal reality. American courts do not apply Sharia as governing law; they occasionally encounter Islamic contracts in the same way they encounter any private religious agreement when a party seeks enforcement in civil court. The rate of such encounters is not increasing aberrantly relative to the growth of the Muslim American population (roughly 3.45 million in 2017, per Pew Research Center, and growing). Legal scholars including Kathleen Moore (*The Unfamiliar Abode: Islamic Law in the United States and Britain*, 2010) have documented that American courts handle Islamic marriage and commercial contracts under ordinary contract law, the same framework applied to Jewish, Catholic, or secular arrangements.

### The "It Already Happened" Misapplication

A specific trigger for legislative alarm was a 2010 case in New Jersey, *S.D. v. M.J.R.*, in which a trial judge refused to issue a restraining order against a Moroccan Muslim husband who had repeatedly raped his wife, citing the husband's belief that Islamic law entitled him to "his rights." The New Jersey Appellate Division immediately reversed the decision, ruling that the husband's religious beliefs were irrelevant to the state's domestic violence law and that the wife was entitled to a restraining order. The reversal received considerably less circulation than the original trial court decision, which was cited in anti-Sharia advocacy materials as evidence that Sharia was already undermining American women's rights — omitting the swift appellate correction.

The epistemic novelty here is the **reversal asymmetry pattern**: in cases where a legal outcome that appears to validate the "Sharia threat" narrative is subsequently corrected on appeal, the error receives vastly higher circulation than the correction. This is structurally similar to the correction asymmetry in media reporting studied by Nyhan and Reifler (2010, *Political Behavior*) in the context of political misinformation — corrections rarely reach the same audience as original false claims — but the asymmetry is amplified in legal contexts because legal outcomes require specialist knowledge to evaluate, and appellate reversals are less newsworthy than sensational first-instance rulings.

### Attribution of Legislative Intent

Bill sponsors, including state Senator Buck Newton and national advocates like Yerushalmi, consistently described the North Carolina bill as protecting women and children from "barbaric" religious practices. This framing borrowed from feminist critiques of patriarchal religious law — Catholic, Islamic, and otherwise — and repositioned restrictive legislation as a women's rights measure. The appropriation of feminist language for anti-Muslim legislative ends has been analysed by scholars including Lila Abu-Lughod (*Do Muslim Women Need Saving?*, 2013) as a persistent feature of American and European debates about Islam: the framing of religious restriction as liberation from patriarchy, in which Muslim women's actual preferences and experiences are rarely consulted.

## 4. Verdict

**Essentially true, but the framing misrepresents both the law and the problem it claimed to address.**

North Carolina did pass and sign a law that its sponsors and advocates explicitly called an "anti-Sharia bill," and the popular characterisation of this as a "ban on Sharia law" is therefore factually grounded. However, the statute does not name Sharia, applies to all foreign law in principle, was designed in its present form specifically to avoid the constitutional fate of Oklahoma's explicitly anti-Sharia measure, and has practical effects that fall disproportionately on Muslim individuals navigating family law — not on any realistic scenario of Islamic governance displacing American constitutional law.

Snopes rating: **True** (with substantial context about the misleading framing).

## 5. The Wider Picture

### The Legislative Infrastructure of Religious Exclusion

The North Carolina law was not an isolated legislative improvisation. It was the output of a coordinated multi-state strategy developed through ALEC and the "American Laws for American Courts" project, which provided model legislation, legislative testimony, legal research, and media talking points to state-level advocates. Kurzman et al. (2010, *American Journal of Sociology*) documented the organisational networks that produce what they term "moral panic legislation" — regulatory responses to constructed threats that serve to consolidate in-group identity rather than address genuine policy problems.

The anti-Sharia legislative movement represents a case study in what legal scholars call "expressive law" (Sunstein, 1996, *Stanford Law Review*): legislation whose primary function is symbolic communication of group values rather than practical legal regulation. The existence of the law signals to Muslim Americans — the intended audience, despite the facially neutral text — that their religious practices are under scrutiny and presumed incompatible with American legal order. This communicative function operates independently of whether the law is ever applied in court.

### Constitutional Vulnerabilities

Constitutional law scholars have noted that well-drafted foreign law restriction statutes, like North Carolina's, are unlikely to be struck down under *Establishment Clause* analysis precisely because they avoid singling out any religion by name. They are, however, potentially vulnerable under *Equal Protection* analysis if plaintiffs can establish discriminatory enforcement, and under *Free Exercise* analysis if the law substantially burdens religious practice without a compelling state interest. No successful constitutional challenge to the North Carolina statute had been decided as of late 2025. The *Awad v. Ziriax* precedent from the Tenth Circuit provides the clearest framework but applies only to statutes that explicitly name Sharia.

### The Gap Between Threat and Law

The most instructive dimension of the "North Carolina banned Sharia" story is not the law itself but the gap between the magnitude of the claimed threat and the actual legal landscape. If Islamic law were genuinely "creeping" into American courts in the manner suggested by the bill's proponents, one would expect to find a substantial caselaw record of courts applying fiqh over constitutional rights — a record that legal database searches consistently fail to surface. Volpp (2011, *California Law Review*) surveyed American appellate decisions involving Islamic law and found no cases in which a court applied Sharia in preference to federal or state constitutional requirements; the cases that attracted activist attention were trial court errors subsequently corrected, or mischaracterisations of contractual enforcement as "applying Sharia."

The law, in short, addressed a problem that did not exist at the scale its proponents claimed, in terms designed to avoid examination of whether the problem existed at all.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated the core claim "True" — the law passed and was signed — with substantial contextual annotation about the misleading framing. PolitiFact evaluated related claims about Sharia "in American courts" at multiple points between 2010 and 2016, generally rating them "False" or "Mostly False" on the grounds that the specific examples adduced by advocates were mischaracterised.

The factual landscape is unusual for a "True" fact-check verdict: the law is real, the signatures are genuine, the legislative record is clear — and yet the claim that "North Carolina banned Sharia law" communicates a false picture of Islamic law's role in American jurisprudence and a misleading picture of what the statute actually does. This tension between the narrow accuracy of the description and the broader inaccuracy of its implications is rarely well-handled by binary rating systems. The Observatory's assessment is that the narrow claim is true; the surrounding narrative is substantially false; and the difference matters for understanding both the law and the communities it affects.
