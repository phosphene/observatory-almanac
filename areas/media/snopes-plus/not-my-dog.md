## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

An anecdote circulating in various forms since at least 1924 describes a social visit in which a large, badly behaved dog follows one party into the home of a more socially prominent host. Both parties maintain polite silence throughout the visit while the dog tracks mud, sniffs food, and generally demolishes the scene — each privately assuming the offensive animal belongs to the other. Only at the point of departure is the misattribution revealed, in the punchline: "My dog? I thought he was yours!" The story has been told in print, attached to celebrated names including mathematician John von Neumann and film star Burt Reynolds, collected as an urban legend, and adapted to non-canine variants including a version in which an unexpected elderly woman silently occupies a young couple's home for a month before each partner realizes neither invited her.

Snopes investigator Barbara Mikkelson (2002) assigned this claim the rating **Legend**. The earliest documented version of the story appears not in an anecdote recounted by a real person but in a 1924 novel by Lucy Maud Montgomery — author of the *Anne of Green Gables* series — where it is presented as a fictional social set-piece. From that fictional origin, the story has migrated into the repertoire of dozens of real-world tellers who claim it as personal experience, and to dates ranging from the 1940s to the present. This entry examines the story's literary origins, its attachment to the von Neumann persona as a specific case study in legend accretion, the social mechanics of class anxiety and formality paralysis the story encodes, and the broader cultural meaning of the legend as a critique of etiquette culture.

## §2 Literary Origin and Legend Accretion

Barbara Mikkelson (2002) traced the earliest documented version of this story to Chapter 12 of Lucy Maud Montgomery's 1925 novel *Emily Climbs* (the second volume of the Emily of New Moon trilogy, published by Frederick A. Stokes Company in 1925). In that episode, Emily calls upon the socially superior Miss Janet Royal; a large, very muddy dog follows Emily into the elegant parlor, where it proceeds to dirty everything while the two women maintain careful social decorum. The revelation that neither woman owns the dog comes only as Emily is leaving. Montgomery's novel predates any anecdotal claim of a real-life occurrence by decades.

This literary origin does not mean that no version of this exact scenario has ever occurred in real life — in a world with millions of dogs and millions of social visits, it almost certainly has — but it establishes that the *story as a frequently told anecdote* is culturally downstream of a fictional text. The claim that the event happened to a specific identifiable person is, in each documented case, unverifiable.

The most sociologically interesting documented version is the one attached to John von Neumann (1903–1957), the Hungarian-American mathematician whose contributions span game theory, quantum mechanics, computer architecture, and nuclear weapons design. In the version recorded by Edward Regis in *Who Got Einstein's Office? Eccentric and Brilliant Men of the Institute* (Addison-Wesley, 1987, p. 110), Julian Bigelow describes arriving at von Neumann's home on Westcott Road in Princeton in January 1946 for a job interview. A large Great Dane accompanies Bigelow inside; the entire 40-minute interview proceeds while the dog wanders the house; and only at the end does von Neumann ask whether Bigelow "always traveled with the dog." Neither the dog nor its actual owner is ever established. Bigelow's account has the signature feature of first-person testimony, which gives it substantially more surface credibility than a "friend of a friend" provenance — but Mikkelson (2002) correctly identifies that this is precisely the mechanism by which such legends achieve renewed credibility. The story has been told so many times with so many claimants that one of those claimants, at some point, may simply be recounting from memory what they heard as if it happened to them.

A third version, by Patrick Mahoney in *Barbed Wit & Malicious Humor* (Citadel Press, 1956, p. 100), places the incident at the home of Field Marshal Lord Alexander in London. Mahoney claims to have been the embarrassed visitor; a large stray dog follows him in, settles at his feet during lunch, and Lady Alexander finally requests that he remove his dog. The 1956 publication date slightly predates Regis — but both postdate Montgomery by three decades.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Legend as Social Critique and the Von Neumann Elaboration's Function

The "Not My Dog" legend endures not because anyone believes it literally but because it encodes a genuine and widely felt social truth about the paralysis induced by formal etiquette norms. Barbara Mikkelson (2002) articulates this reading directly: both parties maintain formality even as the situation spirals, because etiquette prescribes not drawing attention to awkward intrusions. The story dramatizes the consequence — extended, escalating social disaster — and punches its punchline at the exact moment the viewer has been made by the narrative to feel the absurdity of the entire situation.

The epistemic novelty in this case is the story's use of social class anxiety as both the engine of the comedy and the mechanism of its memetic propagation. Every version features a status asymmetry: the caller is positioned as socially inferior to the host — less wealthy, less celebrated, or (as in the von Neumann case) seeking employment from a revered intellect. This status differential creates the condition for formality paralysis: the inferior party would never embarrass the superior by complaining about the dog; the superior party maintains dignity by doing the same. The result is mutual hostage-taking by etiquette.

Erving Goffman, in *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life* (Anchor Books, 1959, pp. 208–237), developed the theoretical vocabulary for understanding why such paralysis occurs. Face-threatening acts — any action or utterance that damages the social standing of either party in an interaction — are avoided at high cost in high-stakes encounters. Pointing out a badly behaved animal that (one believes) belongs to the host is a doubly face-threatening act: it implicitly criticizes the host's pet management and assumes an implied superiority by bringing it up. In an encounter structured by power asymmetry, the inferior party faces a near-zero rational incentive to raise the issue, regardless of the objective cost.

This analysis illuminates why the legend's von Neumann variant is the one that achieves the widest literary circulation: von Neumann's intellectual celebrity is so extreme that Bigelow, a talented engineer presenting himself as a job candidate, would face maximal formality pressure. The story's specific humor mechanism — two extremely intelligent men, one of whom could design a computer and model atomic bomb detonations, failing to address a dog in a living room — relies on the gap between the protagonists' intellectual powers and their social paralysis. The comedy scales precisely with the prestige of the encounter. This is why the legend, over nearly a century of telling, consistently attaches itself to celebrated names rather than ordinary people.

The non-canine variant that Mikkelson (2002) includes — the Indo-Asian couple who each believe a strange silent woman in their home is the other's mother — extends the formula to a different register of social anxiety: the pressure not to criticize a presumed family elder in a context structured by filial obligation. The punchline ("My mother? I thought she was yours!") achieves its effect through the same mechanism but applied to a radically different social constraint, demonstrating the story's structural generativity as a cultural form.

## §4 The Etiquette Critique and Its Social Functions

The legend functions not only as entertainment but as a perennial cultural critique of formality as a social technology. Etiquette, as a coordinating mechanism, exists to reduce friction and enable smooth interaction between parties of disparate status. But as the legend dramatizes, etiquette can become a coordination failure when both parties follow the rules so assiduously that neither corrects a shared misperception that is visibly worsening. The story makes this visible and thereby licenses its audience to recognize the absurdity without having to articulate a direct critique of social forms they also value.

This dual function — critique and appreciation — is characteristic of what Alan Dundes (1965) called the "anti-proverb" or counter-traditional narrative in *The Study of Folklore* (Prentice-Hall, 1965, pp. 143–146, building on Propp's framework). Dundes argued that folkloric narratives often encode both the rule and its violation, serving simultaneously as prescriptive and descriptive guides to behavior. The "Not My Dog" legend prescribes by negative example: the audience understands that a more direct approach ("Is this your dog?") would have resolved the problem immediately, and are thereby instructed in the virtues of directness — while simultaneously recognizing that they themselves, in the same scenario, might have remained silent.

The legend's class commentary is further sharpened by its consistent setting in the homes of the socially elevated. It is never told as a story of two equals; it is always about a visitor who feels outclassed. This means it functions as what Bourdieu (1984), in *Distinction: A Social Critique of the Judgement of Taste* (Harvard University Press, 1984, pp. 66–69), would call a field-revealing narrative — one that makes the normally invisible rules of a social field legible by staging their catastrophic application.

As Mikkelson (2002) notes, Burt Reynolds reportedly told this story — starring himself as the embarrassed visitor — to Johnny Carson on *The Tonight Show* in the 1980s. Reynolds, despite his public persona of easy confidence, choosing the caller's role (the embarrassed inferior) rather than the host's role is itself a form of class performance: the celebrity who tells a self-deprecating anecdote about formality paralysis presents as relatable to an audience for whom the status asymmetry that paralyzed him would have been overwhelming.

## §5 The Non-Canine Variant and the Legend's Operating Range

The version Mikkelson (2002) collected from the internet in 2002, in which an unidentified elderly woman silently inhabits a young couple's home for a month, extends the legend into a darker register. Where the dog story produces comedic embarrassment and then resolves cleanly (the dog leaves, the visit ends), the human variant produces a sustained domestic siege. The intruder is not an animal but a person with full cognitive agency who has, apparently, also assumed a misattribution — she is in the wrong house, or perhaps the right house under a different misunderstanding. The couple, unable to address the situation because each believes the other invited this presumably important elder, descends into spousal hostility before the reveal.

The escalation from one embarrassing visit to one fractured marital relationship demonstrates the legend's capacity for structural amplification. The time period can expand (the dog is present for an afternoon; the woman for a month), the relationship stakes can escalate (a social call versus a marriage), and the harm can compound accordingly — but the structural mechanism remains identical. This scalability is part of what makes the legend generative: storytellers can tune the duration and relationship stakes to their audience without altering the core mechanism.

This non-canine variant also demonstrates the legend's operating range beyond the species-specific. The thing that walks in uninvited need only be: (1) large enough to be impossible to ignore, (2) affiliated visually or by circumstance with the visitor, (3) behaving in ways that impose ongoing social cost, and (4) impossible for both parties to address without violating the etiquette norms that structure the encounter. The form is essentially a slot-filler comedy pattern with a stable structure.

## §6 Epistemic Summary and Lessons for Verification Practice

The "Mistaken Dog" legend presents an instructive case for verification because it is uniquely resistant to confident refutation. Unlike hoaxes built on physical impossibility (bonsai kittens) or implausible concealment (penguin in a backpack), this story could have happened: dogs do follow people into houses, and polite visitors do sometimes maintain awkward silence about social disasters. The legend's implausibility is not physical but epidemiological: the same story attached to dozens of different protagonists across a century of telling is overwhelmingly more likely to be a circulating narrative than independent occurrence of the same event dozens of times.

The key verification move Mikkelson (2002) executes is backward provenance tracing to a fictional origin. Montgomery's *Emily Climbs* predates every claimed real-world occurrence in the published record. This does not rule out independent invention of the scenario, but it establishes a prior that any new claimed occurrence is more probably a retelling of the legend than a fresh incident.

The lesson for contemporary verification: when an anecdote bears a celebrity's name and involves an exactly patterned social comedy, check whether the pattern has a prior literary home. Many celebrity anecdotes that circulate as genuine recollections are structurally identical to pre-existing folkloric scripts, and the correspondence is rarely coincidental.

The claim is **Legend** — a structurally well-formed narrative with a documented fictional origin in 1924 that has accumulated a century of false-authenticity claims.
