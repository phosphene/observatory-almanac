# Did an NYC Restaurant Get a License To Serve Human Flesh?

**Verdict: Labeled Satire — the story originated as explicit fiction.**

There is no restaurant called SKIN. There is no government license to serve human flesh. There is no chef named Mario Dorcy. What there is, however, is an instructive case study in how satirical content sheds its labels as it travels, how cumulative republication launders fictional claims into something that reads like news, and why the human appetite for transgressive stories — even implausible ones — makes this particular kind of misinformation surprisingly durable.

---

## 1. The Claim

In various iterations circulating between 2016 and 2019, the claim held that a newly opened restaurant in New York City had received an unprecedented government license to serve human flesh as food. The restaurant was named "SKIN" and its owner, a supposed four-star Michelin chef named Mario Dorcy, was quoted as saying: "As a species, we are at the top of the food chain, and the only meat left to tackle was other humans. We battled long and hard to be able to serve human meat in our restaurant, and the government finally conceded that we were in the right."

A particularly widely circulated version of the story surfaced in November 2019 on a website called Elite News Press, which published a near-verbatim copy of the original article under the title "Restaurant in New York gets Government approval to sell Human Flesh as Meat." Unlike the original source, Elite News Press did not carry a prominent disclaimer, and this omission transformed the story for many readers into something that looked like journalism.

The claim is false. It is a work of deliberate fiction — satire — that migrated from its original satirical context and was received as literal reporting by a segment of the public.

---

## 2. What's Actually True

### The origin: Empire News

The story was first published on March 15, 2016, by a website called Empire News at the URL `empirenews.net`. Empire News was a so-called "satire" or "entertainment" website — one of many that proliferated during the mid-2010s in a period when the social media economics of outrage-sharing made clickbait of any stripe highly profitable.

In its own terms and conditions, Empire News stated: *"Empire News is intended for entertainment purposes only. Our website and social media content uses only fictional names, except in cases of public figure and celebrity parody or satirization."* This self-description as a satirical outlet was explicit, though it appeared in fine print rather than adjacent to each article.

The article was constructed with enough surface plausibility to be entertaining but not enough to survive any serious scrutiny. No Michelin chef named "Mario Dorcy" exists in any culinary record. No state or federal law permitting the sale of human flesh for consumption exists in the United States. The FDA, USDA, and individual state health departments exercise comprehensive jurisdiction over what may legally be sold as food, and human flesh is not on any approved list.

### The legal reality

The consumption of human flesh — cannibalism — is not explicitly criminalised under a single federal statute in the United States. This legal lacuna is a real curiosity that has received genuine scholarly attention. However, the *sale* of human flesh as food would encounter prohibitive regulatory barriers long before it reached any restaurant table. Under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (21 U.S.C. § 301 et seq.), any article "intended for use as food" for humans is subject to FDA oversight and must be free of adulteration and contamination hazards. Human flesh, lacking any approved food safety pathway, would be considered adulterated per se.

Furthermore, the acquisition of human flesh for commercial purposes would implicate laws on homicide, trafficking in human remains, abuse of a corpse, and the Uniform Anatomical Gift Act, any one of which would present insurmountable legal obstacles. The notion that a state or federal government "conceded" the right to serve human flesh — as the satirical article claimed — reflects no actual legal procedure and no real regulatory framework.

### Why NYC specifically?

The choice of New York City as the fictional setting is not accidental. NYC is reliably deployed in viral misinformation as a shorthand for cosmopolitan excess, elite liberal permissiveness, and transgressive urbanism. Stories about New York restaurants doing outrageous things play into a pre-existing cultural narrative about the city as a place where normal rules do not apply. This framing serves the satirical purpose well but also makes the claim more believable to readers who already distrust urban institutions.

## 3. The Epistemic Puzzle: Why Satire Stops Working

The sociological and cognitive mechanisms by which satire "fails" — that is, by which it is received as literal reporting — have been the subject of sustained academic inquiry in the years since social media platforms became primary news distribution infrastructure.

The central mechanism identified in the literature is **label detachment**: satirical articles carry their labels in colophons, "About" pages, and fine-print disclaimers rather than adjacent to the content itself. When such an article is shared on Facebook or WhatsApp, the social metadata (the name of the satirical site, the disclaimer language) rarely travels with it. What travels is the headline, an image, and perhaps an excerpt. The recipient sees a claim, not a source context.

Pennycook and Rand (2019), writing in *Cognition*, found that mere familiarity with a claim — even a false one — increased participants' tendency to regard it as accurate, a phenomenon they termed the "illusory truth effect." Applied to satire: each time the SKIN restaurant story was shared, its apparent credibility increased marginally for readers who had already encountered a version of it, regardless of whether they had registered it as fiction the first time.

Roozenbeek and Van der Linden (2019), writing in *Palgrave Communications*, documented how "prebunking" — exposing people to weakened forms of misinformation before they encounter the real thing — could significantly reduce susceptibility to false viral stories. The methodological implication for media platforms is clear, but implementation remains inconsistent.

A separate dynamic concerns **moral shock as epistemological bypass**. Research by Brady et al. (2017) in *PNAS* demonstrated that content containing moral-emotional language spreads reliably further and faster on social media than content that is merely informative. A headline claiming that a restaurant has received permission to *serve human flesh* is maximally morally shocking. It triggers a fight-or-flight information-sharing impulse that bypasses the slower evaluative processes associated with source-checking and plausibility assessment. The share happens before the question "but is this real?" is even formed.

The SKIN restaurant story also benefits from a third cognitive dynamic: **plausibility by incremental strangeness**. If someone were told that a restaurant obtained a license to serve dog meat in New York City — which, while unusual in the US, is not biologically transgressive — they might be more likely to check the source. Human flesh is so far outside the ordinary that it triggers a kind of stunned acceptance: "I can barely believe this is possible, therefore I don't know how to evaluate it, therefore I'll share it and let others respond." Extreme implausibility paradoxically disables normal scepticism.

The 2019 republication on Elite News Press illustrates a final mechanism: **downstream laundering**. A satirical claim published by a self-disclosed satire site acquires nothing resembling journalistic authority. But republished — even verbatim — by a site without a satire label, it inherits a new epistemic status. The republishing site functions as a credibility launderer, stripping the "entertainment only" label and presenting the content as de facto reportage. This pattern was extensively documented by Hunt Allcott and Matthew Gentzkow (2017) in their landmark *Journal of Economic Perspectives* study on fake news in the 2016 US election, which found that many false stories had identifiable origin points in satirical or fictional content that had been decontextualised and redistributed by downstream sites.

## 4. The Deeper Context: Cannibalism as Cultural Flashpoint

The persistence of this particular satirical topic — human flesh as restaurant food — reflects something genuine about cannibal anxiety as a cultural phenomenon. Anthropologists have long noted that cannibalism functions as an absolute moral boundary in most human societies, and violations of that boundary (real or fictional) generate intense, visceral responses.

Arens (1979), in his controversial *The Man-Eating Myth: Anthropology and Anthropophagy*, argued — provocatively but with substantial documentation — that anthropological and historical accounts of cannibalism as a routine cultural practice have been systematically exaggerated, serving as mechanisms of de-humanisation that justified colonial violence against peoples accused of it. While Arens' thesis was critiqued by later scholars who documented specific historical instances of institutionalised cannibalism (Petrinovich, 2000, in *The Cannibal Within*), his core insight — that cannibal accusations function as boundary-marking devices in inter-group conflict — remains analytically durable.

The SKIN restaurant story is, in a sense, a contemporary cannibal accusation directed at an abstraction: "New York City elites" or "the government" or "permissive modernity." It scripts the same anxieties that historically surrounded genuinely false cannibal accusations — otherness, transgression, elite impunity — and deploys them in a satirical register. That the satirical frame was lost in transmission is, from this perspective, not entirely surprising; the story was doing the same cultural work as a genuine accusation.

---

## 5. Verdict

**Labeled Satire** — wholly fictional in every particular.

There is no restaurant called SKIN. There is no Michelin chef named Mario Dorcy. No state or federal government in the United States has issued, could issue, or has considered issuing a license to serve human flesh as food. The story originated as explicit satire published by Empire News in March 2016, was republished multiple times without its satirical label, and briefly recirculated in November 2019 after appearing on Elite News Press without a disclaimer.

The claim has no factual foundation of any kind and does not represent a distorted version of any real event. It is, from a truth-evaluation standpoint, among the cleanest cases in the Truth Vault: pure fiction mistaken for fact due to label detachment and iterative republication.

What the case does reveal, at a meta-level, is the vulnerability of digital information ecosystems to the satire-to-misinformation pipeline — a pipeline well-documented academically and largely unsolved at the platform level.
