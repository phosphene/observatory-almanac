## 1. The Claim

In March 2025, a photograph that had been circulating online for at least fourteen years resurfaced across social media platforms, this time gaining renewed viral attention. The image showed a view of the New York City skyline as seen from orbit, with a distinct plume of smoke rising from the southern tip of Manhattan. The claim accompanying the image was that the photograph had been taken from the International Space Station (ISS) by NASA astronaut Frank L. Culbertson Jr. on September 11, 2001, in the hours immediately following the terrorist attacks on the World Trade Center. The caption stated that Culbertson "was the only American in space" at the time, and that as the ISS passed over the East Coast, "he captured this haunting image of smoke billowing from Lower Manhattan."

The claim was shared on Instagram, Reddit, and other platforms, often accompanied by emotionally charged language. One Reddit post titled the image with the simple but poignant observation that "there was one American astronaut aboard the ISS on September 11, 2001, the only American not on Earth on that day." The posts attracted hundreds of thousands of views, thousands of comments, and widespread engagement across platforms. In an era when manipulated images and AI-generated content are increasingly common, many viewers approached the photograph with skepticism, wondering whether it was a genuine historical document or a digital fabrication.

The Snopes fact-check, published March 23, 2025 and written by Joey Esposito, investigated the claim and rated it "True." Snopes confirmed that the photograph was authentic, that it had been taken by Culbertson from the ISS on September 11, 2001, that NASA had published the image on its website alongside Culbertson's contemporaneous journal entries, and that additional video footage of the event existed and was publicly available. In an information environment where claims about 9/11 are frequently contested and surrounded by conspiracy theories, this confirmation was not trivial. The image is real, and the story behind it adds depth and human dimension to one of the most documented days in modern history.

## 2. What's Actually True

The photograph is genuine, and nearly every detail in the viral posts is accurate. Frank L. Culbertson Jr. was indeed the commander of Expedition 3 aboard the International Space Station on September 11, 2001. He was the only American crew member on the station; his fellow crew members were Russian cosmonauts Mikhail Tyurin and Vladimir Dezhurov. When news of the attacks reached the ISS, Culbertson was informed by ground control. He described the moment in his journal, dated September 12, 2001: "The flight surgeon told me they were having a very bad day on the ground. I had no idea… He described the situation to me as best he knew it at ~0900 CDT. I was flabbergasted, then horrified. My first thought was that this wasn't a real conversation, that I was still listening to one of my Tom Clancy tapes."

Culbertson's response was immediate. As the ISS passed over the East Coast, he "zipped around the station until I found a window that would give me a view of NYC and grabbed the nearest camera." The camera he grabbed happened to be a video camera rather than a still camera, but he managed to capture both video footage and still images. The still image that has circulated on social media is one of those frames. The orbital outpost was flying at an altitude of approximately 250 miles, and the image shows a smoke plume rising from the Manhattan area. Culbertson's journal entry continues: "The smoke seemed to have an odd bloom to it at the base of the column that was streaming south of the city. After reading one of the news articles we just received, I believe we were looking at NY around the time of, or shortly after, the collapse of the second tower. How horrible…"

NASA has maintained a dedicated page for Culbertson's letter and photographs on its official website since at least 2011. The agency's caption for the image reads: "The image shows a smoke plume rising from the Manhattan area. The orbital outpost was flying at an altitude of approximately 250 miles. The image was recorded with a digital still camera." The NASA Image and Video Library hosts a gallery of pictures from the space station on September 11, 2001, and surrounding dates. The video footage Culbertson shot was posted by NASA to YouTube on September 7, 2011, ahead of the ten-year anniversary of the attacks.

The authenticity of the image is not in serious doubt. Reverse image searches on TinEye return more than 1,400 results, many of which date back more than a decade. The image was featured in news stories marking both the tenth and the twentieth anniversaries of the attacks. NBC News ran a story about the image in September 2011. The image has been cited in books, documentaries, and museum exhibits. The metadata, the chain of custody, and the corroborating documentation all confirm that the photograph is exactly what the viral posts claim it to be.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's fact-check of this claim is an exemplary piece of verification journalism. The article traces the image's circulation history, identifies its NASA provenance, reproduces Culbertson's journal entries extensively, and links to the original NASA sources and video footage. The rating of "True" is unambiguous and well-supported. The article does exactly what a good fact-check should do: it answers a straightforward factual question with clear evidence and authoritative sourcing.

Where the Snopes article is less strong is in its treatment of the claim's broader significance. The fact that the photograph is authentic is not, in itself, surprising. NASA has been open about the image's provenance for over a decade. The viral posts in March 2025 were not the first time the image had circulated, nor were they accompanied by any novel claims or disputed details. The Snopes article provides an accurate verification but does not particularly enrich the reader's understanding of the image's historical or emotional meaning.

The article also does not engage with the question of why this particular image resurfaces periodically and why it attracts attention. There is a genuine story here about public memory, collective trauma, and the way iconic images function as sites of emotional processing. The photograph of smoke rising from Manhattan as seen from space is not merely a piece of evidence; it is a meditation on perspective, distance, and helplessness in the face of catastrophe. The Snopes article, by focusing narrowly on the factual question of authenticity, misses the opportunity to explore what the image means and why it matters beyond its evidentiary status.

There is also a missed opportunity to address the broader landscape of 9/11 conspiracy theories. While many such theories are patently false—claims about controlled demolition, missiles, or the absence of planes—they continue to circulate widely and find new audiences on social media. The Snopes article does not situate this particular claim within that landscape. A reader encountering the image for the first time might not know that 9/11 is a domain in which even basic factual claims are frequently contested. By not providing that context, the article leaves the reader without a framework for understanding why a simple verification of an authentic photograph is worth doing at all.

## 4. Why This Matters

This photograph matters for reasons that go well beyond the narrow question of authenticity. The image is a primary document of a world-historical event, captured from an extraordinary vantage point. It is one of the few visual records of the 9/11 attacks taken from outside the Earth's atmosphere, and it provides a perspective that no other photograph from that day can offer. When Culbertson looked down at the smoke rising from Manhattan from 250 miles above, he was seeing the attacks from a position that forced a profound reflection on scale, distance, and human vulnerability.

The image also matters because of Culbertson's unique position as the only American not on Earth that day. His journal entries, which are preserved alongside the photograph, convey a sense of isolation and helplessness that is deeply moving. "The most overwhelming feeling being where I am is one of isolation," he wrote. This is not the isolation of a distant observer but the isolation of someone who wants to help and cannot, who wants to share in a collective experience and cannot. The image and the journal together form a record not just of what happened in New York but of what it felt like to be disconnected from one's own country at its moment of greatest modern crisis.

The periodic resurgence of the image online also says something about how collective memory operates in the digital age. The photograph was not "rediscovered" in 2025; it has been publicly available for over a decade. Each time it circulates, it finds a new audience—people who were too young to remember 9/11, people who encounter the image for the first time, people who need to be reminded of what was lost. The fact that the image needs to be verified anew each time it goes viral is a symptom of a broader erosion of trust in the visual record. In an age of generative AI and deepfakes, even authentic historical photographs cannot simply be assumed to be real. The Snopes fact-check, in this context, is not just a verification service but an act of historical preservation.

Finally, the photograph matters because it provides an antidote to the most corrosive forms of 9/11 conspiracy theorizing. In a discursive space cluttered with claims of controlled demolition and inside jobs, a real photograph taken by a real American astronaut from the International Space Station, preserved by NASA, corroborated by video footage, and described in Culbertson's own words provides a solid anchor of documented reality. It cannot single-handedly refute every conspiracy theory, but it can serve as a reminder that some things about that day are known, documented, and true.

## 5. Research Notes

The primary sources for the photograph and Culbertson's account are maintained by NASA. The agency's dedicated page, "Astronaut Frank Culbertson Letter from September 11, 2001," is hosted on the NASA website at nasa.gov and contains the photograph with an official NASA caption, a transcript of Culbertson's journal entries, and links to related materials. The journal entries were written on September 12, 2001, the day after the attacks, and represent a contemporaneous primary account of unprecedented emotional and historical value.

The NASA Image and Video Library provides a searchable gallery of imagery from the ISS on and around September 11, 2001. This gallery includes both still photographs and video footage. The video footage that Culbertson shot on the day of the attacks is available on NASA's official YouTube channel, posted on September 7, 2011, and is one of the most direct visual records of the event from orbit. It shows Culbertson narrating in real time as the station passes over the East Coast and he begins to understand the scale of what has happened.

NBC News published a story on the image on September 8, 2011, as part of coverage leading up to the tenth anniversary of the attacks. That story, which includes an interview with Culbertson, provides additional context about his experience and his reflections on watching the attacks from orbit. The Space.com article by Denise Chow, published the same week, provides a broader account of NASA's response to the attacks and the implications for space security.

One area where additional research would be valuable is in tracking the exact circulation history of the image. TinEye returns more than 1,400 results, but a systematic analysis of how and when the image has been shared across platforms over the past fourteen years would provide useful insight into the dynamics of viral memory online. It would be valuable to know which platforms have been most responsible for the image's periodic resurgences, what captions and framing have accompanied it in different contexts, and whether the image has been deployed in misleading or deceptive ways (for example, as part of a false claim about what the smoke represents).

The question of why the image resurfaces in 2025 is also worth examining. The March 2025 resurgence does not appear to have been triggered by any specific news event or anniversary. It may simply be a case of algorithmic amplification—the image was shared by one account with a large following, and the platform's recommendation systems did the rest. Understanding these amplification dynamics would help fact-checkers anticipate future resurgences and prepare verification resources in advance.

## 6. Verdict

The Snopes fact-check is correct: the photograph of New York City taken from the International Space Station on September 11, 2001, by astronaut Frank Culbertson, is authentic. The claim is True. The image is preserved by NASA, corroborated by Culbertson's journal entries and video footage, and independently verified by news organizations. There is no doubt about its provenance or its accuracy as a historical record.

The Snopes article serves its primary function effectively. It answers the factual question clearly and provides authoritative documentation. There is a deeper story here that the article only gestures at: the emotional experience of being the only American off the planet during the nation's greatest modern crisis. Culbertson's journal captures a feeling that no satellite image or government report can convey. "It was difficult to think about work after that," he wrote. "The most overwhelming feeling being where I am is one of isolation." That isolation was not just physical distance from the attacks but a deeper existential separation from the collective experience of grief and shock that united his fellow citizens on the ground. The photograph is a document of that separation, and its enduring power comes from the way it collapses the distance between the cosmic and the intimate—a smoke plume from a burning city, seen from a quarter of a million miles away, by a man who could not help, could not comfort, could not even touch the ground of his own country.

For the Truth Vault's purposes, this entry confirms the Snopes verdict with a supplementary treatment of the image's broader significance, its role in collective memory, and its function as an anchor of documented reality in a sea of speculation. The rating is True, and the analysis is sound.
