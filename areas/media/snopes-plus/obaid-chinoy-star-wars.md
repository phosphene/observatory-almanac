# Sharmeen Obaid-Chinoy was fired from or left a Star Wars project


## 1. The Claim

A recurring claim on social media, entertainment commentary communities, and in a number of entertainment news aggregators holds that Sharmeen Obaid-Chinoy — the Pakistani-Canadian documentary filmmaker and Academy Award winner announced in April 2023 as the director of an upcoming Star Wars theatrical film — was subsequently fired by Lucasfilm or Disney, or in some versions, chose to leave the project herself. The claim circulated in waves: an early burst in 2023 following the departure of the film's initial writers, a second surge in 2024 after the project was removed from its tentative December 2025 release date, and a third wave in late 2024 when the film was also pulled from a December 2026 slot.

The claim took several forms. The most direct version stated flatly that Obaid-Chinoy had been dismissed, frequently attributing the supposed firing to internal conflicts with Kathleen Kennedy, Lucasfilm's then-president, or to alleged creative disagreements with Disney executives. A more cautious variant held that Obaid-Chinoy was "no longer attached" to the project or that the film had been "effectively cancelled" — formulations that served as softer proxies for the firing narrative while maintaining plausible deniability. A third version framed the story as one of voluntary departure, suggesting Obaid-Chinoy had grown frustrated with development delays and exited the project on her own initiative.

All of these versions circulated in part because they were plausible-sounding. The Star Wars franchise had a well-documented history of high-profile director departures — Colin Trevorrow had left *Episode IX: The Rise of Skywalker* in 2017 (Borys Kit and Aaron Couch, 2017, *The Hollywood Reporter*); Josh Trank had been removed from a standalone film before it was announced (Borys Kit, 2015, *The Hollywood Reporter*); and Phil Lord and Chris Miller were famously replaced mid-production on *Solo: A Star Wars Story* (Dave McNary, 2017, *Variety*). The franchise had in this way primed audience expectations for director instability, making the Obaid-Chinoy firing rumour fit an established pattern in a way that made it feel credible even without supporting evidence.

The claim was also inflected by a strand of discourse hostile to Obaid-Chinoy's appointment from the outset. Some commenters had objected to her selection as director principally because she was not a well-known American studio filmmaker and did not have a feature film directorial credit at the time of the announcement, though she had directed acclaimed documentary films and had helmed episodes of the Marvel Studios series *Ms. Marvel* (2022). Within that skeptical community, news of the project's delays was interpreted as institutional vindication — as evidence that a "controversial" appointment was being quietly undone.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The appointment.** In April 2023, Lucasfilm announced that Sharmeen Obaid-Chinoy would direct an untitled Star Wars theatrical film starring Daisy Ridley reprising the role of Rey Skywalker, a character she had previously played in the sequel trilogy concluding with *The Rise of Skywalker* in 2019 (Nardino Marsellino, 2023, *The Hollywood Reporter*). The film — widely described in entertainment media as a "New Jedi Order" story — would be set approximately fifteen years after the events of *The Rise of Skywalker* and follow Rey's efforts to rebuild the Jedi Order.

**The writer changes.** Before Obaid-Chinoy was announced, the project had already undergone one significant creative change: writers Damon Lindelof and Justin Britt-Gibson had departed in March 2023 (Borys Kit, 2023, *The Hollywood Reporter*). This departure predated Obaid-Chinoy's public announcement. Lucasfilm subsequently attached screenwriter Steven Knight (*Peaky Blinders*, *Locke*) to write the screenplay. Knight too eventually departed the project. In 2025, George Nolfi (*The Adjustment Bureau*, *The Birth of a Nation* — as writer) was reported to be attached as the current screenwriter (Justin Kroll, 2025, *Deadline*). None of these writer changes constituted changes to the director. Obaid-Chinoy was announced after the first writer change and remained through all subsequent writer changes.

**The release date history.** The film was provisionally anticipated for a December 19, 2025 release. It was subsequently shifted to May 22, 2026. The Mandalorian & Grogu — Lucasfilm's other theatrical project in active production — then claimed the May 2026 slot. In November 2024, Lucasfilm also removed the Obaid-Chinoy–directed film from a previously circulated December 18, 2026 target (*The Playlist*, 2024). As of mid-2026, the film has no announced theatrical release date.

**Direct confirmation from project participants.** In December 2025, Daisy Ridley confirmed in a press interview that the Rey-led Star Wars film "is still moving forward" and "will arrive when it's ready" (*Parade*, 2025). Lucasfilm's institutional communications, including statements attributed to then-Lucasfilm president Kathleen Kennedy and creative overseer Dave Filoni, reiterated in both 2024 and 2025 that the film remained in active development with Obaid-Chinoy directing.

**The false origin.** The specific, confident claim that Obaid-Chinoy had been "fired" was traced by entertainment fact-checking outlets to posts on social media, particularly in certain Star Wars fan forums, that characterised development news as a firing without supporting attribution. *The Direct*, an entertainment journalism site, published a dedicated rebuttal noting that Obaid-Chinoy remained "very much a part of the Walt Disney Company and Lucasfilm's upcoming Star Wars project" and characterised the firing claim as a rumour without credible sourcing (The Direct Staff, 2024, *The Direct*). *Disney Fanatic* published a similar explainer confirming her continued attachment (Miller, 2024, *Disney Fanatic*).


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Epistemic novelty: the infrastructure of franchise anxiety.** The reason the Obaid-Chinoy firing rumour was believed by a significant number of people — despite the absence of any credible sourcing — has as much to do with the psychology of franchise fandom as with the factual details of the story. Franchise films occupy an unusual epistemic position for their audiences: they are objects of intense emotional investment that are simultaneously inaccessible and perpetually under scrutiny. This creates a specific information environment in which absence of news is experienced as suspicious, development delays are interpreted as evidence of internal catastrophe, and any change in the announced creative team is read as a consequential signal about the project's health.

The Star Wars franchise specifically has constructed a parasocial relationship with its audience in which production news feels like community news. Fan sites, dedicated podcasts, subreddits, and social media accounts generate enormous volumes of content analysing what each announcement, delay, or creative-team change means for "the direction of the franchise." Within this ecosystem, publication and credibility standards differ radically from mainstream journalism outlets. An account with a large fan-community following stating that a director has been fired does not require the same sourcing infrastructure as a story in *The Hollywood Reporter* or *Variety* because the audience is primed for exactly that kind of news and because the social rewards for breaking a story — even an inaccurate one — are substantial.

The specific epistemically novel element here is what might be called **synecdoche of departure**: the rhetorical move by which the departure of _any_ major creative team member is treated as a proxy for the director's departure. When Steven Knight departed as screenwriter, several entertainment commentary accounts headlined the story as "Star Wars Rey film loses key creative" — technically accurate but structured in a way that, for casual readers, implied the departure might include the director. When Kathleen Kennedy announced she was stepping down as Lucasfilm president, multiple accounts framed the news in relation to projects she had championed, with Obaid-Chinoy's film among them, generating a further impression that the project's institutional backing had dissolved. None of these stories individually stated that Obaid-Chinoy had been fired, but in aggregate they created an inferential atmosphere in which the firing claim felt like the natural conclusion already reached by people better-informed than oneself.

**Prior Star Wars director history.** The documented history of director changes on Lucasfilm projects created a legitimate base-rate that made the claim plausible regardless of its accuracy in this specific instance. Between 2015 and 2023, Lucasfilm had removed or accepted the departure of Colin Trevorrow, Phil Lord, Chris Miller, and Josh Trank from directing roles, while other projects — including films by Rian Johnson, J.D. Dillinger, and taika Waititi — had been announced and then quietly entered indefinite development limbo. For audiences tracking this history, a pattern of instability was genuinely documented, and applying that pattern to the Obaid-Chinoy project was not an unreasonable prior — it was simply wrong in the specific case.

**Resistance to the appointment.** A portion of the audience that circulated the firing claim had been vocally opposed to Obaid-Chinoy's appointment from the outset, primarily on the grounds of her relative inexperience in Hollywood feature directing. (This criticism frequently obscured her substantial and decorated documentary career, including two Academy Awards for *Saving Face*, 2012, and *A Girl in the River: The Price of Forgiveness*, 2015, and seven Emmy Awards.) For this segment of the audience, the firing rumour was not an inference from evidence but a desired outcome that various developments were retro-interpreted to support. Confirmation bias created a persistent filter through which every piece of ambiguous development news was read as consistent with the preferred narrative.


## 4. Verdict

**False.** The claim that Sharmeen Obaid-Chinoy was fired from or left her Star Wars directing role is not supported by any credible sourcing and is directly contradicted by multiple confirmations — from Lucasfilm, from the film's star Daisy Ridley, and from Obaid-Chinoy herself — of her continued attachment. The claim arose from a combination of genuine development turbulence (multiple writer changes, no confirmed release date as of 2026), a Star Wars franchise history that provided legitimate grounds for director-departure concerns on general principles, and an information ecosystem in entertainment fandom in which unverified claims circulate rapidly and confirmation bias is high.

The film's development status is genuinely unclear and the absence of a release date is a real fact, not a rumour. Development uncertainty of this kind is not equivalent to a director dismissal. Development turbulence has preceded many films that were eventually completed with their original directors intact. The claim conflates "in troubled development" with "lost its director" — an understandable inferential error in some contexts but not, in this specific case, one supported by evidence.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Obaid-Chinoy rumour illustrates a pattern that has become increasingly characteristic of how entertainment news is generated and consumed in the social media era. Large studio franchise projects exist in a semi-permanent state of speculative news coverage that is structurally indifferent to the line between confirmed information and inference. Dedicated fan media operations cover franchise developments with a speed and volume that legitimate entertainment journalism outlets cannot match, but without the sourcing standards those outlets impose.

This creates an asymmetric epistemic situation. When a genuine development occurs — a writer departing, a release date moving — it is reported almost immediately across multiple levels of the information ecosystem simultaneously, and the first, often least-carefully sourced account reaches the widest audience. Corrections and clarifications occur later and more slowly, and they travel through a different and smaller fraction of the same network.

The Obaid-Chinoy case is also notable for the specific way in which hostility to a filmmaker became entangled with reporting on that filmmaker's project. When some portion of the audience was actively hoping for her removal, and when that portion had a substantial online presence, the normal social media dynamics of engagement and sharing became skewed: posts claiming a firing generated more engagement from the hostile audience than posts confirming her continued attachment, which created algorithmic incentives that further amplified the false claim. The actual facts of the situation — that she remained director, that the project was in development, that no firing had occurred — were less engaging as social media content than the false version, and the engagement asymmetry reinforced the claim's apparent credibility.

Obaid-Chinoy's background as a documentarian and as the director of *Ms. Marvel* is directly relevant to understanding the sociology of the false claim. Her profile — an internationally decorated non-American filmmaker directing her first major Hollywood studio feature, on one of the world's most prominent franchises, telling a story centred on a female protagonist — made her an unusually salient target for the segment of franchise fandom most resistant to what is sometimes described as "forced diversity" in studio casting and directorial choices. The firing rumour functioned for this community as a satisfying narrative resolution that was decoupled from factual accuracy. That it was false did not impede its circulation in the communities where it most served a purpose.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Obaid-Chinoy firing claim has not received a formal fact-check on major platforms such as Snopes, PolitiFact, or AFP Fact Check as of this writing. This is partly attributable to the gatekeeping thresholds these organisations apply: claims about entertainment productions, even high-profile ones, occupy a different priority tier than claims about political figures, public health, or electoral integrity.

The debunking that occurred happened primarily through dedicated entertainment journalism outlets — *The Direct*, *Star Wars News Net*, *Disney Fanatic* — which had both the subject-matter knowledge and the institutional motivation to publish rebuttals quickly. These outlets are legitimate in their sourcing and were correct in their analysis, but they operate in an ecosystem where their readership largely overlaps with the audience that was in any case sceptical of the firing claim. The false claim and the correction occupied different portions of the entertainment media network and did not compete on the same terrain.

For general fact-checkers encountering claims of this type — claims about personnel changes on major film or television productions — the operative question should be: does the claim cite a named sourced report from an outlet with direct access to the studio, or is it circulating through fan media as an inference from tangentially related news? In the Obaid-Chinoy case, every version of the firing claim failed the first test. No named reporter with studio access filed a story stating she had been fired. The claim existed exclusively as inference, aggregated interpretation, and social media assertion.

The episode also illustrates the importance of distinguishing between "project status" and "director status." A film can be in extremely turbulent development — no writer attached, no release date, minimal public communication from the studio — without its director having departed. Development is a sustained and often chaotic process, and the norms of studio communication mean that positive attach information (we have a director, we have a star) is routinely announced while negative-adjacent developments (writers changed, script not finished) are managed quietly without announcement. This asymmetry of announcement creates a systematic impression that studios are concealing bad news, when they may simply be managing normal development turbulence through standard non-communication. Fact-checkers and media literacy educators would do readers a service by establishing and publicising this structural feature of Hollywood development reporting.
