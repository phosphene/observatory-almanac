# Is This a Real Video of Octopus Aggression?


## 1. The Claim

**Core assertion**: On March 18, 2021, author Lance Karlson posted a video to Instagram — taken in shallow water at Dunsborough, Western Australia — that showed an octopus apparently lunging at him with what appeared to be aggressive intent. The video spread virally across social media, received coverage in The New York Times, and prompted expert commentary from researchers in both cephalopod biology and cognitive neuroscience. The claim at issue is simply that the video is genuine — that a real octopus, in a real coastal environment, did in fact lunge at a real human in what can be described as aggressive, defensive, or territorial behavior.

**The context of the encounter**: Karlson described the initial encounter as taking place in extremely shallow water. His reaction — captured on the audio of the video — was a composed "Oh. Golly." The video's combination of genuine biological spectacle and Karlson's understated Antipodean response contributed substantially to its virality. But the biological content was the real news: here was a small, solitary, invertebrate animal actively moving toward a large vertebrate in what appeared to be a challenge display. This is not how most people conceptualize octopus behavior.

**The extended encounter**: The video documents only the initial lunge. What followed, as reported in The New York Times, is also scientifically significant: Karlson returned to the same area approximately 20 minutes later, swam about 30 meters out, noticed a pile of crab shells arranged neatly together, and dove to investigate. He had recalled watching a documentary about octopus feeding behavior — octopuses accumulate crab shell middens near their dens. At that point, an octopus — presumably the same individual — struck him on his arm and back with its arms, causing some pain. The behavioral sequence, from warning lunge to territory defense near a food cache, forms a coherent ethological narrative.

**Previous viral cephalopod from the same region**: This was not the first time a Western Australian octopus had achieved international internet fame. In 2015, a different octopus from nearby Yallingup, Western Australia — just a few kilometers from Dunsborough — was filmed pursuing a crab across a terrestrial surface, capturing it on land. The geographical clustering of viral Western Australian octopus behavior suggests both that the species is genuinely behaviorally remarkable and that Western Australian coastal recreational culture involves the kind of casual human-wildlife interaction that generates such footage.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The video is genuine**: There is no credible reason to doubt the authenticity of the video. Lance Karlson is a named, identifiable Australian author with a public Instagram account and a traceable identity. The video is internally consistent with the coastal environment of Dunsborough, Western Australia, which can be verified through satellite imagery and is consistent with the known habitat of the Western Australian common octopus. No evidence of digital manipulation has been identified. The video was published to Instagram contemporaneously with the encounter (the post date is March 18, 2021), before it became a viral phenomenon that might incentivize fabrication.

**The species is correctly identified**: The Waters West Diver guide cited in the Snopes fact-check identifies the species as the Western Australian common octopus — a species reclassified as distinct from the closely related Sydney common octopus (*Octopus cf. tetricus*) based on genetic and morphological analysis. Research by Amor, Norman, Ortiz & Hochberg (2017, *ICES Journal of Marine Science*) clarified the taxonomic separation of Western Australian and eastern Australian octopus populations, confirming that the Dunsborough population represents a distinct species closely related to but not identical with *Octopus tetricus*. This species is, as described in the relevant taxonomy, typically encountered in and around inshore reef systems and sandy benthos in nearshore and continental shelf waters — which is precisely the environment of the Dunsborough video.

**The behavior is biologically consistent**: Peter Ulric Tse, a professor of cognitive neuroscience at Dartmouth College who studies octopus cognition, characterized the behavior in The New York Times: octopuses "can express what we would call aggression when they feel threatened or when they feel their territory is under threat." He specifically interpreted the lunge in Karlson's video as likely a warning display — "pre-emptive aggression, meant to signal 'don't mess with me'" — rather than a genuine attempt to injure Karlson. This interpretation is consistent with documented octopus behavioral repertoire: warning arm displays, color and texture changes, and lunging toward perceived threats are established components of cephalopod agonistic behavior.

**The crab midden context is corroborated**: The subsequent arm-strike Karlson experienced after finding the crab shell pile near the den is consistent with documented den-defending behavior in octopods. Octopuses are highly territorial around their dens and associated food caches. The behavioral sequence — warning display during initial encounter → defensive strike near den site — is ethologically coherent and consistent with published accounts of octopus territorial behavior.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What This Case Reveals Beyond the Verdict

The verdict here is straightforward: the video is real, the behavior is consistent with known octopus biology, and the expert characterization is well-supported. The more interesting dimensions of this fact-check lie in what it reveals about octopus cognition, individual personality, and the evolving scientific understanding of cephalopod mental life.

**Octopus aggression and the problem of attribution**: When a human displays an arm toward another human in a threatening way, we readily attribute intent — a mental state that motivates the act. When an octopus extends an arm toward a human in a similar context, the question of whether we are observing behavior driven by something that deserves to be called intent, fear, threat-assessment, or territorial motivation is contested. Tse's characterization as "pre-emptive aggression" is itself an attribution of something like strategic behavior — the octopus is not merely reflexively retreating but actively signaling. But the neural architecture underlying octopus decision-making is radically different from vertebrate cognition: the octopus has a distributed nervous system with approximately two-thirds of its neurons in its arms, raising genuine questions about the locus and nature of its decision-making (Hochner, 2012, *Current Biology*).

**Individual behavioral variation in octopuses**: Research on octopus temperament and individual variation provides context for interpreting the Dunsborough encounter. Anderson, Mather, Monette & Zimsen (2010, *Journal of Comparative Psychology*) documented consistent individual differences in boldness, curiosity, and activity in captive octopuses — what they characterized as rudimentary "personality." The octopus in Karlson's video behaved in a notably bold and approach-oriented rather than retreat-oriented manner. Whether this reflects species-level tendencies in *O. cf. tetricus*, individual temperament, or situational factors (shallow water, perceived threat to territory) is not determinable from a single video.

**Octopus cognition and the limits of anthropomorphism**: Viral octopus videos — including both the 2015 land-crab-hunt video and Karlson's 2021 encounter — are frequently narrated in anthropomorphic terms: the octopus is "angry," "protective," "territorial." These framings are communicatively effective but epistemologically problematic. The science of cephalopod cognition has advanced substantially in the twenty-first century, with documented short-term and long-term memory (Mather & Anderson, 1993, *Journal of Comparative Psychology*), play behavior, and tool use (Finn, Tregenza & Norman, 2009, *Current Biology*), yet researchers remain cautious about the application of human mental state vocabulary to octopods. The appropriate scientific language is behavioral: the octopus produces a lunge response in the presence of a perceived threat or intruder. Whether there is something it is like to be that octopus having that experience is an open question.

**The geographic clustering of Western Australian viral octopus encounters**: The fact that two internationally viral octopus videos emerged from a ~10 km coastal zone in Western Australia (Dunsborough and Yallingup) within six years is notable. It is unlikely to reflect simply a regional difference in actual octopus behavior — octopuses across their global range exhibit comparable behavioral complexity. More probably it reflects a combination of factors: high coastal recreational participation rates in southwestern Australia, good underwater visibility in the Indian Ocean, smartphone adoption among beachgoers, and perhaps a culture of paying close attention to marine wildlife among Western Australian coastal communities. The videos are data about human observational behavior as much as they are data about octopus behavior.

**The role of viral wildlife media in species recognition**: Before the 2015 land-crab-hunt video and the 2021 Karlson video, the Western Australian common octopus had essentially no public profile outside specialist taxonomic literature. The viral circulation of these videos created a global awareness of a species that taxonomists had only recently distinguished from its eastern Australian relative. This represents an unusual case of public scientific interest — and, implicitly, conservation awareness — generated through entertainment media rather than through formal science communication channels.


## 4. Evidence Assessment and Source Quality

**Primary source — the video itself**: The Instagram post by Lance Karlson (@lancekarlson) is a primary source. Its authenticity rests on: (1) the demonstrable identity and continuity of the Instagram account over time; (2) the consistency of the setting with Dunsborough's known coastal environment; (3) the biological plausibility of the behavior depicted; and (4) the absence of any technical indicators of digital manipulation. Snopes found no reason to doubt its authenticity.

**Expert commentary — Peter Ulric Tse**: Tse's characterization, delivered to The New York Times, constitutes expert opinion from a named researcher with relevant credentials (cognitive neuroscience, Dartmouth College, documented publications on octopus cognition). His interpretation — warning display rather than attempted attack — is consistent with the scientific literature on cephalopod agonistic behavior and represents the most parsimonious interpretation of the events depicted.

**Taxonomic literature**: Amor, M.D., Norman, M.D., Ortiz, N. & Hochberg, F.G. (2017). Worming through the taxonomic labyrinth: Unravelling a cryptic species complex in the cosmopolitan octopus, *Octopus vulgaris*. *ICES Journal of Marine Science*, 76(7), 2205–2212. This work established the Western Australian common octopus as distinct from *O. tetricus* and from *O. vulgaris*, providing the species identification framework used in reporting on the video.

**Behavioral ecology context**: Documented den-defending behavior, arm threat displays, and territorial aggression against perceived intruders in octopods are discussed in Hanlon, R.T. & Messenger, J.B. (1996). *Cephalopod Behaviour*. Cambridge University Press. The behavioral elements depicted in the Karlson video are consistent with descriptions in this standard reference.

**Gap assigned**: **R (Research Gap)** — The specific behavioral ecology of *O. cf. tetricus* in nearshore Western Australian habitats is understudied relative to *O. vulgaris* populations in better-studied European waters. Individual-level behavioral variation — including the specific triggers and thresholds for approach vs. retreat responses in wild-caught individuals — remains poorly characterized for this species. The video provides a single anecdotal data point; systematic study of human-octopus encounter dynamics in Western Australian recreational waters has not been published.


## 5. Broader Context — Octopus Behavior, Cephalopod Science, and the Viral Wildlife Nature Video

The Karlson video exists at the intersection of several scientific and cultural currents. At the empirical level, it is a behavioral observation: a documented instance of a Western Australian octopus performing a lunge display directed at a human in a context consistent with territorial defense near a den site. At the cultural level, it is the latest in a series of cephalopod viral moments that have contributed substantially to public awareness of octopus behavioral complexity.

The early twenty-first century has been, in many respects, a golden age of octopus science communication. Sy Montgomery's popular book *The Soul of an Octopus* (2015) reached a wide non-specialist audience. Peter Godfrey-Smith's *Other Minds: The Octopus, the Sea, and the Deep Origins of Consciousness* (2016) introduced readers to the evolutionary biology of cephalopod cognition and its implications for philosophy of mind. The Netflix documentary *My Octopus Teacher* (2020) won an Academy Award. Into this prepared public consciousness, the Karlson video landed as evidence: here was an octopus doing something recognizably behavioral — asserting itself, defending its space — in a way that was viscerally comprehensible to a lay audience.

This cultural context shapes how the video was received and why it required fact-checking at all. The growing popular appetite for narratives of octopus behavioral complexity creates both genuine scientific opportunity — public interest drives research funding and attention — and distortion risk, because that appetite is often served by anthropomorphic narratives that overstate what is known. The claim that an octopus "attacked" Karlson is somewhat misleading if, as Tse suggests, the lunge was a warning display; and the subsequent arm-strike near the den is more accurately characterized as a defensive territorial response than an "attack" in any sense that implies predatory intent.

The Western Australian common octopus (*O. cf. tetricus*) occupies the southwestern Australian coastal zone in relatively dense populations, and interactions with recreational water users are not unusual. The species is not commercially targeted in Western Australia to the same degree as some other octopus species in other fisheries, and its conservation status is not of immediate concern. However, growing interest in cephalopod fisheries globally, combined with the species' short lifespan (1–2 years) and strong seasonal population dynamics, means that monitoring of population trends in Western Australian waters has practical management relevance.


## 6. Verdict Summary and Takeaways

**Snopes Verdict: True**

The video is genuine. A Western Australian common octopus (*O. cf. tetricus*) in shallow water at Dunsborough, Western Australia, performed a lunge display toward Lance Karlson on March 18, 2021. The behavior is consistent with documented octopus territorial and defensive behavioral repertoire. Expert interpretation characterizes it as a warning display rather than a predatory attack. The encounter subsequently escalated when Karlson returned to the vicinity of the octopus's den, where he was struck with octopus arms — a coherent behavioral sequence consistent with den-site defense.

**Key takeaways**:

1. **The video is authentic**: No evidence of manipulation; identifiable setting, identifiable individual, biologically consistent behavior. The claim is supported by expert commentary from a named cognitive neuroscientist.

2. **"Aggression" requires qualification**: The behavioral display is best characterized as a warning or threat display — pre-emptive territorial signaling — rather than an attempt to injure. The subsequent physical contact near the crab midden is more accurately described as den defense.

3. **Western Australian octopus taxonomy**: The species is *Octopus cf. tetricus* — a recently taxonomically distinguished species specific to southwestern Australian coastal waters, whose behavioral ecology is less well-studied than its eastern Australian relative.

4. **Cephalopod cognition context**: Individual behavioral variation in octopuses is documented, and the particular boldness displayed by this individual may reflect either species-level tendencies or individual temperament. The distributed nervous system of octopods makes the cognitive substrate of such behavior genuinely distinct from vertebrate behavioral neuroscience.

5. **Research gap**: The specific behavioral ecology of human-octopus encounters in Western Australian nearshore coastal habitats, including the frequency and context of threat display and physical contact, has not been systematically studied.
