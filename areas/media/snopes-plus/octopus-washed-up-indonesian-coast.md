## The Claim

An image began circulating in mid-2024 and resurged dramatically in January 2025: a sprawling, monumental octopus lying beached on what was described as an Indonesian coastline — sometimes specifically called Bali. The creature's body was enormous, spanning what appeared to be several metres, with five large tentacles visible. Dozens of people stood near it on the beach, with a larger crowd visible in the background. Some posts claimed this was a "real size" specimen; others framed it as evidence of previously unknown ocean megafauna, or simply as astonishing photographic evidence of something never before documented. Posts appeared on X (formerly Twitter), Instagram, Facebook, and other platforms, accumulating hundreds of thousands of interactions.

The claim is false. The image was not taken in Indonesia or anywhere else. It was not a photograph. It was generated by an AI image system and originally posted as explicitly fictional creative content on Instagram, where it was labelled as part of "a fictional story" and titled "The Mourning of the Abyssal Titans." The creator never presented it as real; human audiences, or accounts that deliberately stripped context, made it real through the process of social sharing. By the time the image reached widespread viral circulation on X in January 2025, the fictional caption was gone and the fictitious octopus had acquired geographic precision — Bali, Indonesia — and a growing body of commenting users who treated it as documentary evidence of something extraordinary.

## What's Actually True

**The image is AI-generated.** Snopes fact-checker Nur Ibrahim used Google's reverse-image search tool to trace the image to its earliest documented appearance online. The search identified an Instagram post by an account called *best_of_ai_*, a self-described "Digital Creator" who "experiments with different AI engines, themes and styles" and regularly publishes AI-generated imagery. The post predated the viral circulation on X by four days. The image appeared within a video presenting the fictional octopus from multiple simulated angles, and the post's caption identified it unambiguously as fictional: "This is a fictional story." The caption also included the full creative text of "The Mourning of the Abyssal Titans," a piece of dramatic creative writing describing the octopus as a mythological creature emerging from the deep.

**No news organisation reported the event.** If a giant cephalopod of the size depicted in the image — a creature with tentacles appearing to span many metres, with the body proportions of an organism that would represent a new maximum size record by a significant margin — had actually washed up on a populated Indonesian beach, it would have generated immediate, extensive coverage from Indonesian news media, marine biology institutions, tourism organisations, and international news agencies. It would have been photographed by hundreds of people on site with smartphones. It would have been immediately investigated by marine biologists and government officials. None of this happened. No Indonesian news outlet, no government agency, no marine research institution, no professional photographer produced any documentation of the event. The absence of this corroborating evidence-trail is, itself, strong evidence that the event did not occur.

**Visual artefacts are consistent with AI generation.** Although Snopes's confirmation came through source-tracing rather than technical artefact analysis, the image contains characteristics that have become recognisable signatures of AI image generation. The octopus depicted has five arms visible — real octopuses have eight. The human figures surrounding the creature display inconsistencies in scale relative to one another and to the animal. Skin textures, crowd composition, and background elements contain the kinds of spatial inconsistencies that large-scale AI image models still produce when rendering complex multi-subject scenes with unusual spatial relationships. These artefacts do not constitute proof of AI generation independently — they can be explained by image compression, camera limitations, or perspective effects — but they are consistent with the provenance established by source-tracing.

**Real giant cephalopods exist but not at the depicted scale.** The giant squid (*Architeuthis dux*) is genuinely enormous — confirmed specimens have reached approximately 13 metres in total length including tentacles, making it one of the largest invertebrates on Earth (Roper & Boss, 1982, *Scientific American*). The colossal squid (*Mesonychoteuthis hamiltoni*) has a larger mantle and greater mass, though shorter total length. However, these are squid, not octopus. The largest documented octopus species is the giant Pacific octopus (*Enteroctopus dofleini*), which can reach arm spans of up to approximately 4.3 metres, with a mantle length typically below 0.6 metres and an individual weight record of 71 kg (Mather, 1993, *Journal of Zoology*). The octopus depicted in the viral image appears to dwarf the human figures around it in ways that would imply a creature many times larger than any documented octopus specimen. Such a creature would be a revolutionary zoological discovery; the absence of any serious scientific reaction is itself decisive.

**The image's fictional context was clearly communicated by its creator.** The *best_of_ai_* account from which the image originated consistently labels its work as AI-generated creative fiction. The account displays a variety of clearly fantastical AI-generated images — scenes that no reasonable viewer would interpret as documentary photography. The original post included an elaborate piece of creative writing explicitly framed as fiction. The spread of the image as purportedly real represents a failure of mediation — of the chains of sharing, captioning, and re-posting that progressively stripped the image of its original context — rather than any deceptive intent by the original creator. This is a common pattern in AI-image misinformation: the creator acts in good faith; stripping and re-sharing strips context; viral audiences interpret the decontextualised image based on its visual content alone.

## Why People Believe This

The Indonesian beach octopus image succeeded as a piece of viral misinformation not because of unusual technological sophistication but because it activated a rich combination of cultural and cognitive factors that make certain false images reliably shareable.

**Deep-sea mystery as permanent cultural appetite.** Humans have a persistent, documented fascination with the possibility that the ocean contains creatures far larger than any encountered so far — creatures that explain legends of sea monsters, krakens, and leviathans. This cultural appetite is partly grounded in reality: the deep ocean genuinely remains understudied, and discoveries of giant squid and colossal squid in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries confirmed that very large cephalopods do exist and are rarely encountered alive. The history of cryptozoology — the study of animal species whose existence has not been scientifically confirmed — is substantially a history of oceanic mystery: sea serpents, giant oarfish misidentifications, unusual whale beachings. An image of a genuinely enormous octopus on a populated beach taps directly into this appetite, offering what feels like confirmation of something long suspected. This is a desire that has been epistemically licensed, in a partial way, by genuine scientific discovery.

**The cognitive difficulty of AI image detection.** In 2024 and 2025, AI image generation systems had improved to the point where casual inspection of individual images was frequently insufficient to distinguish AI-generated from real photography, particularly for audiences without specific training in artefact detection. The octopus image was plausible enough in its basic visual structure to defeat quick inspection by most viewers. Standard AI artefact signals — hand distortions, background inconsistencies — are less reliable guides when the subject is unfamiliar (most viewers have limited reference for what a massive beached octopus should look like). The image's plausibility was aided by the genuine existence of octopus beachings; octopuses do occasionally wash ashore or beach themselves in various circumstances. The image was not entirely dissociated from biological reality; it was a scaled-up version of something that actually happens. Köbis & Mossink (2021, *iScience*) conducted studies showing that human ability to detect AI-generated imagery was only slightly better than chance under typical viewing conditions, even as AI image quality improved substantially.

**Context stripping as the core mechanism of this type of misinformation.** The octopus image was not created as disinformation. It was created as explicitly fictional creative content, clearly labelled, by a creator with a transparent track record of AI art. The mechanism by which it became misinformation was not fabrication but context stripping: the image was detached from its caption, re-uploaded without attribution, assigned a new geographic claim (Bali, Indonesia), and shared by accounts with varying levels of awareness that they were distributing false information. Some of these secondary accounts may have genuinely believed the image was real and were themselves deceived. Others may have known it was false and shared it for engagement. Most were probably somewhere in between — uncertain about the image's provenance, sharing it as notable or interesting without having performed the verification that would have revealed it to be AI-generated.

This is the dominant workflow by which AI-generated false images circulate in 2024-2025. The technical creation of the false image is separable from — and often precedes by days or weeks — the act of social amplification that makes it misinformation. The original creator is often innocent; the amplifiers are often more negligent than malicious; and the audience encounters the image as apparently credible because it arrives from accounts they follow rather than from obscure sources they might distrust. This diffuse responsibility structure makes AI image misinformation harder to address through simple "stop lying" public messaging than through platform-level technical interventions and media literacy education.

**Geographic specificity and the Indonesia-Bali heuristic.** The image's attachment to Indonesia, and specifically Bali, is not random. Indonesia is a specific, real place with extensive coastline, warm tropical waters, and well-known diving culture associated with unusual marine biodiversity. The Indonesian archipelago includes the Coral Triangle — one of the most biodiverse marine regions on Earth — and is adjacent to deep-sea trenches and abyssal environments where large organisms are plausibly encountered. "Washed up on a Bali beach" is, in other words, more geographically plausible as a setting for an unusual marine sighting than, say, "washed up on a North Sea beach." Geographic specificity lends credibility to unverified claims; it suggests someone knows where this happened, which implies someone was there, which implies documentary authenticity. The choice — or the evolution through sharing — of Indonesia as the claimed location was, probably without conscious calculation, well-calibrated to maximise credibility.

**Social proof dynamics in viral sharing.** By the time the image reached wide circulation on X in January 2025, it carried social proof from extensive prior sharing: many thousands of reposts, interactions, and comments that, in aggregate, create an epistemic signal that can override individual scepticism. Cialdini's foundational work on influence (Cialdini, 1984, *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*) identified social proof as one of the most powerful drivers of belief and behaviour. A viewer encountering the octopus image for the first time and seeing thousands of shares and many commenting users expressing belief is receiving social evidence that the image is real, regardless of the actual photographic evidence. Social proof dynamics are amplified by platform architectures that surface high-engagement content algorithmically, meaning images that receive rapid initial sharing are shown to progressively larger audiences regardless of their accuracy.

## Verdict

**The claim is Fake.** The image of a giant octopus on an Indonesian beach is not a photograph. It is an AI-generated piece of creative fiction, originally published with an explicit fictional label by an AI art account, that was stripped of its context through secondary sharing and circulated as purportedly real documentary photography. No such event occurred. No Indonesian news outlet, marine research institution, or government agency documented any such event. The creature depicted is inconsistent with any documented species of octopus by a substantial margin of size, and the absence of professional scientific coverage of a discovery of this significance is decisive confirmation.

The original creator published the image in good faith as fiction; the misinformation arose through the ordinary mechanics of context-stripping and re-sharing that characterise AI image misinformation in social media environments. This does not excuse the amplifiers who contributed to its spread, but it does locate the primary problem as a media literacy and platform architecture failure rather than deliberate fraud.

## The Wider Picture

The fake octopus image fits within a rapidly developing and genuinely concerning landscape of AI-generated visual misinformation, with implications well beyond any individual viral image.

**AI image generation and the collapse of photographic authority.** Photography has served, for roughly 180 years, as the most authoritative form of visual evidence in public discourse. The cultural assumption that photographs show things that happened — that there was a scene before a camera, and the camera documented it — is so deeply embedded that it has survived multiple waves of photographic manipulation technology, from darkroom compositing to Photoshop. AI image generation represents a qualitatively different challenge because it enables the creation of photorealistic images without any physical referent — scenes that were never in front of any camera, of events that never occurred, of creatures that do not exist — at low cost and without requiring the specialised skills that photographic manipulation previously required. This is not a marginal capability development; it is a structural change in the epistemology of visual evidence (Chesney & Citron, 2019, *Harvard Law Review*). The octopus image is a low-stakes example — fabricated marine life is amusing rather than dangerous — but the same technology and the same social distribution mechanisms apply to politically consequential fabrications.

**Real cephalopod biology in context.** The viral image raises implicit questions about what genuinely large cephalopods look like and how large they actually are — questions worth answering on their own terms. The giant squid (*Architeuthis dux*) remained almost entirely known from beachings, specimens in whale stomachs, and suckers embedded in sperm whale skin until the early 21st century. The first confirmed photographs of a living giant squid in its natural habitat were obtained in 2004 by Tsunemi Kubodera and Kyoichi Mori (2005, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B*) — a scientific landmark that attracted enormous global attention precisely because the giant squid was simultaneously real, genuinely enormous, and almost entirely unknown in life. In 2019, the first footage of a giant squid in United States waters was captured in the Gulf of Mexico by researchers using the Medusa camera system (McClain et al., 2020). These real discoveries of genuinely large cephalopods are scientifically documented, immediately covered by multiple independent news organisations, confirmed by named researchers, and integrated into peer-reviewed literature. The contrast with the octopus image's complete absence from any of these channels is instructive: real discoveries leave evidence-trails; fabricated ones leave only the image.

**AI content labelling and platform responsibility.** The failure of context to travel with the octopus image reflects a systemic absence of technical infrastructure for AI content provenance. Initiatives including the Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity (C2PA), backed by Adobe, Microsoft, and others, aim to embed cryptographic provenance metadata in media files at the point of creation, allowing consumption platforms and viewers to verify where content came from and whether it was AI-generated. As of 2025, adoption of C2PA standards was growing but incomplete; most AI image generation platforms did not embed recognisable provenance metadata, and most social platforms did not surface that metadata even when present. The current environment therefore relies heavily on human fact-checking after the fact — a reactive approach that struggles to keep pace with the volume and velocity of AI-generated content. Proactive technical interventions at the point of creation and distribution represent a more tractable long-term solution, though they face significant challenges around adoption, standardisation, and circumvention.

**Reverse-image search as a basic media literacy tool.** The primary verification tool Snopes used to debunk the octopus image was Google's reverse-image search — a freely available, straightforward tool that can be used by any person with internet access. Had any significant fraction of the millions of people who shared the image performed a reverse-image search before doing so, the viral spread would have been curtailed at its earliest stages. Reverse-image search is not sophisticated; it requires no technical skills and is available on both desktop and mobile platforms. The fact that such a simple verification tool exists, is freely available, and is not routinely used by social media users before sharing striking images reflects the current state of media literacy. Teaching reverse-image search as a basic information hygiene practice — alongside reading URLs, checking dates, and identifying original sources — is among the most tractable near-term media literacy interventions. Snopes publishes explicit guidance on performing reverse-image searches, and this resource is cited in the octopus fact-check article itself.

**The creative economy of AI art and the misinformation risk.** Accounts like *best_of_ai_* represent a legitimate creative practice: generating AI imagery, often in fantastical or speculative styles, and sharing it as creative content. The account did nothing wrong in the octopus case; the fictional label was present, the creative intent was transparent, and the account's overall portfolio of clearly fantastical AI images provides unambiguous context. The misinformation problem arose in the interaction between this creative practice and a social media ecosystem where content moves across platforms rapidly, captions are detachable, and visual appeal can drive sharing independent of accuracy. This creates a structural risk that legitimate AI art creators impose on the information environment without intending to, simply through existence. Whether this risk warrants proactive labelling requirements for AI-generated content, platform-level provenance verification, or simply audience education is a policy debate actively underway in multiple jurisdictions. The octopus image is a useful low-stakes test case for thinking through those policy questions: what interventions would have prevented or limited its spread, and would those interventions have imposed acceptable costs on legitimate creative expression?

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check on the Indonesian octopus image, written by Nur Ibrahim and published January 25, 2025, is a clean example of the reverse-image search workflow applied to AI-generated viral content.

**Source-tracing as the core methodology.** Ibrahim's primary investigative move was reverse-image search, which identified the original Instagram post by *best_of_ai_*. This is exemplary practice: rather than making a judgment call about whether the image looked AI-generated, the fact-checker traced the image to its earliest documented online source and let that source speak for itself. The *best_of_ai_* account's label of "fictional story" and clear AI art identity are independently decisive; no technical artefact analysis is needed once the source is identified. This methodological priority — source-tracing before technical analysis — is sound because sources are more reliable than technical artefact detection, which can be circumvented and remains imperfect.

**The absence of confirmatory evidence.** Ibrahim correctly noted that no news outlets reported the event and that no photographer claimed the image. This argument-from-absence is particularly powerful when the event would, if real, necessarily have generated extensive documentation. A giant octopus beaching in Bali is an event that would produce dozens of independent photographs, news stories in Indonesian and international media, government responses, and scientific investigation. The total absence of this infrastructure is not mere absence of confirmation; it is strong positive evidence of non-occurrence.

**Transparency about update plans.** The article noted that Snopes contacted the *best_of_ai_* Instagram account to determine if they were the original creator and committed to updating the post if more information emerged. This transparency about the limitations of the current investigation and the commitment to follow-up is good practice. In this case, the source identification was sufficient for a definitive verdict — the fictional label on the original post is unambiguous — but the acknowledgment that additional confirmation has been sought reflects appropriate epistemic humility.

**The "Fake" verdict and AI-generated content.** Snopes's rating of "Fake" with the contextual note "The image was generated by artificial intelligence" is precisely correct and precisely calibrated. "Fake" as a verdict category is distinct from "False" — the latter applies to claims that are inaccurate, while the former applies to media artefacts that have been fabricated. An AI-generated image presented as a real photograph is Fake in a specific sense: the photographic claim (that someone took this photograph of something that happened) is false because no photograph was taken and the depicted scene did not occur. The "Fake" verdict is appropriately strong for this case and communicates the relevant information clearly. The contextual addendum about AI generation is important because it distinguishes this case from, say, an old photograph falsely labelled as recent — the mode of fabrication matters for public understanding of what kind of visual claims to distrust and how.

The octopus case is, in some respects, the simplest kind of AI image fact-check: clear provenance trail, explicit original labelling, complete absence of corroborating real-world evidence. Not all AI image misinformation cases will be this tractable. As AI generation systems improve and context-stripping techniques become more sophisticated, the evidential chain from AI creation to viral image will become harder to follow. The octopus is therefore worth understanding now, in its relative legibility, as a template for a class of misinformation claims that will become both more common and more difficult to debunk as the decade progresses.
