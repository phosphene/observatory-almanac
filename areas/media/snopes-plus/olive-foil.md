# Is 69% of All Olive Oil Fake?


## 1. The Claim

In early March 2015, a viral article titled "Fake Olive Oil: What You Need to Know [Now]" began circulating widely across social media, particularly in health-oriented communities. The article's central assertion was arresting in its precision: independent tests at the University of California had found that 69% of all store-bought extra virgin olive oils in the United States were "probably fake." The claim did not hedge — it presented the figure with statistical confidence, listed a roster of well-known brands said to have failed, and implied that the oil in your kitchen cupboard was not actually olive oil at all, or was substantially adulterated with cheaper oils.

The claim resonated strongly with health-conscious consumers because extra virgin olive oil holds a distinctive cultural status as a "superfood." The Mediterranean diet literature had for decades enthusiastically attributed cardiovascular benefits, anti-inflammatory properties, and longevity associations to olive oil consumption (Estruch et al., 2013, *New England Journal of Medicine*). The prospect that most commercially available olive oil was counterfeit struck at some deeply held assumptions about trustworthy food labelling, and it spread accordingly. The specific 69% figure lent the claim an air of empirical precision that vague assertions of "widespread fraud" would have lacked, and the named brands — Bertolli, Colavita, Newman's Own, Whole Foods, Safeway, and others — were staples of middle-class pantries across the country.

The story had a compelling narrative arc: a prestigious university had run the tests, the results were damning, major brands had failed, and yet somehow this information had been suppressed or ignored by mainstream media. Embedded links in the viral article pointed to the UC Davis Olive Center, lending the claim institutional credibility. The article's headline — calling the oils "fake" — was, however, a substantial leap from what the UC Davis research actually measured and concluded.

The Snopes fact-check, published 3 March 2015 and updated 19 February 2017 with a revised verdict of "Mixture," rates the claim as neither wholly true nor wholly false. To understand why, it is necessary to understand both what the UC Davis research actually found and what the word "fake" does and does not mean in the context of olive oil quality standards.


## 2. What's Actually True

The 69% figure is real. It originates in a July 2010 report from the UC Davis Olive Center, "Evaluation of Extra-Virgin Olive Oil Sold in California," and was further detailed in an April 2011 follow-up report. The UC Davis research was a genuine scientific inquiry conducted between 2008 and 2010, examining samples of popular imported olive oil brands sold in California retailers.

What the research found was that 69% of the imported olive oil samples tested failed the International Olive Council (IOC) sensory evaluation standards required for the "extra virgin" designation. The IOC defines extra virgin olive oil by both chemistry criteria (acidity, peroxide values, and specific spectrophotometric measurements) and sensory criteria: the oil must have zero defects and measurable positive fruitiness when evaluated by a trained tasting panel. The oils that failed these tests were not olive oil frauds in the sense of being made from other ingredients — they were, by all available measures, still olive oil. They failed because they exhibited one or more sensory defects: rancidity, mustiness, fusty notes, or simply diminished fruitiness that fell below the "extra virgin" threshold.

The UC Davis Olive Center itself was careful to distinguish between substandard grading and outright fraud. The research report stated explicitly that "quality level of the largest imported brand names is inconsistent at best, and that most of the top selling olive oils we examined regularly failed to meet international standards for extra virgin olive oil." The word "fake" appeared nowhere in the original research. The Olive Center's own framing was about grading consistency and consumer protection, not about contamination or substitution.

The distinction matters enormously. "Fake" in ordinary usage implies substitution — that the bottle labelled "extra virgin olive oil" contains something other than olive oil. What the research actually identified was *mis-labelled grade* — an oil that is genuinely derived from olives, processed in ways that involve genuine olive pressing, but which has degraded or been processed in such a way that it no longer meets the sensory standards for the premium "extra virgin" tier. These oils would typically be classed as "virgin" or "refined" olive oil under the IOC grading scheme, not as fraudulent products.

The 2015 viral article transformed "failed to meet extra virgin standards" into "probably fake," a transformation that overstates the actual finding by such a margin as to constitute significant misinformation. The health risk calculus is entirely different: an oil that is merely a lower grade of olive oil is not a health hazard. An oil adulterated with hazelnut oil, soybean oil, or industrial-grade refined oils would be.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty

The olive oil story is an unusually instructive case study in what we might call *precision laundering* — the process by which a genuine quantitative finding from a legitimate scientific source gets subtly reframed at each sharing event until the specific number (69%) acquires a meaning entirely different from what it originally measured. The number is preserved precisely — it does not become "about 70%" or "nearly three-quarters" — but the noun it modifies transforms entirely. UC Davis measured the percentage of oils failing sensory standards for one particular grade label; the 2015 viral article measured nothing and simply repurposed the percentage to answer a different question entirely ("how much olive oil is fake?").

This transformation exploits a genuine information asymmetry. Most consumers do not know that olive oil has a multi-tier grading system (extra virgin, virgin, refined, pomace), that sensory defects are distinct from adulteration, or that the IOC's sensory panel evaluations require trained tasters applying specific methodologies. Without this background knowledge, the translation from "failed extra virgin standards" to "fake" seems intuitive, even reasonable. The claim achieves its traction not through any single lie but through the exploitation of genuine consumer ignorance about food chemistry and commodity grading.

There is also an important literature on the social psychology of food fear that is relevant here. Rozin et al. (1999, *Judgment and Decision Making*) demonstrated that consumers respond asymmetrically to contamination cues — small amounts of "bad" substances dominate judgements about food safety far more powerfully than large amounts of "good" substances. The same cognitive machinery that produces disgust responses to genetic contamination of "pure" food also makes the "fake oil" frame viscerally compelling. Once the category of "fake" is activated, consumers find it difficult to update to the more mundane reality of "inadequately labelled grade." The 69% figure, coupled with the "fake" framing, essentially immunises the claim against correction at the level of affective response even when the intellectual correction is understood and accepted.

A separate and genuine question — whether the global olive oil trade involves substantial adulteration with non-olive oils — is not answered by the 2010 UC Davis study at all. There is a real and ongoing scientific literature on olive oil fraud involving non-olive adulterants. Aparicio et al. (2013, *Journal of Agricultural and Food Chemistry*) and Tres et al. (2015, *Analytical Chemistry*) have documented methodologies for detecting hazelnut, sunflower, and other vegetable oil adulteration in commercial olive oil. This literature suggests that adulteration with non-olive oils does occur in the global supply chain, though the scale and frequency varies substantially by country of origin, market, and regulatory enforcement context. The UC Davis 2010 study was not designed to measure this kind of adulteration and its 69% figure tells us nothing about it.


## 4. The Scientific and Regulatory Record

The International Olive Council's quality standards for olive oil are codified in detailed trade standard documents that specify both chemical parameters and organoleptic (sensory) requirements. Under the IOC's Trade Standard Applying to Olive Oil and Olive-Pomace Oil, extra virgin olive oil must have a free fatty acid content of no more than 0.8%, a peroxide value no greater than 20 milliequivalents of active oxygen per kilogram, specific absorbance values (K232 ≤ 2.50, K270 ≤ 0.22), and must pass a panel test with a median defect score of zero and positive fruity median score greater than zero (IOC, 2019, Trade Standard COI/T.15/NC No 3/Rev. 14).

The sensory panel test — the source of the 69% failure rate — is particularly susceptible to degradation through improper storage, light exposure, heat, and extended shelf time. Kalua et al. (2007, *Food Chemistry*) documented that extra virgin olive oil exposed to fluorescent light at room temperature through transparent glass can develop detectable rancidity defects within weeks, transforming a genuinely compliant oil at point of production into a non-compliant one at point of retail sale. This degradation pathway is entirely consistent with a scenario in which oils are produced to specification in the country of origin but fail sensory standards by the time they reach American store shelves after international shipping, warehousing, and retail display — none of which involves any fraudulent intent by producers.

The UC Davis Olive Center's findings fit naturally within this supply chain degradation framework. The Olive Center's Bob Berger and Dan Flynn documented in their 2011 report that the most commonly observed defects in failed samples were rancidity and fusty characteristics — both indicators of oxidation and fermentation that develop after harvest and processing (Flynn & Berger, 2011, "Report: Evaluation of Extra-Virgin Olive Oil Sold in California," UC Davis Olive Center). These findings suggest that the primary quality failure is in distribution and retail rather than in fraudulent production.

Spanish food safety authorities examining their own domestic olive oil sector found similar patterns (Agencia Española de Seguridad Alimentaria y Nutrición, 2012), as did the Olive Center's own 2011 report which noted that "similar quality problems have been found in Andalusia, the world's most productive olive oil region, by Spanish authorities." This international corroboration reinforces the interpretation of a systemic supply-chain quality management failure rather than organised fraud involving substitution with non-olive oils.

The USDA established its own olive oil standards in 2010, which substantially align with the IOC standards, and the FDA has authority to act on mislabelled olive oil products. However, routine testing for grade compliance has historically been limited. Mueller (2011, *Extra Virginity: The Sublime and Scandalous World of Olive Oil*) surveys the history of olive oil fraud comprehensively and notes that while genuine adulteration with non-olive oils has occurred historically — most dramatically in the 1981 toxic oil syndrome in Spain, which involved denatured rapeseed oil sold fraudulently as olive oil and caused approximately 2,500 deaths — the modern US retail market faces a different and less dangerous problem of grading inaccuracy rather than toxic contamination.


## 5. Verdict Analysis

Snopes' "Mixture" verdict, assigned in the 2017 update, reflects a careful and appropriate weighing of what the claim gets right and what it distorts. The "What's True" component acknowledges that the 2010 UC Davis testing did find widespread failure to meet extra virgin standards among major import brands, and that this represents a genuine quality and consumer protection problem. The "What's False" component correctly identifies that the tested oils were not "fake" in the sense of being made from something other than olives.

The evolution of the verdict from presumably a simpler assessment to the more nuanced "Mixture" designation reflects an important principle in fact-checking practice: claims that contain a genuine empirical nucleus embedded within a distorted or overstated frame cannot be simply rated true or false without doing further damage to the record. Rating it "False" entirely would falsely imply that the UC Davis research was fabricated or that olive oil quality compliance is unproblematic. Rating it "True" would validate the dangerous "fake" framing that invites consumers to treat their olive oil as a health risk. The "Mixture" designation with its careful distinction does more epistemic justice to the complexity.

The gap between what the research found and how it was reported in the viral article is an instance of a broader pattern in the communication of food science findings to the public. Studies showing that certain foods "fail" standardised tests are routinely translated into "dangerous," "fake," or "toxic" claims that bear little relationship to the actual risk profile identified. Nestle (2002, *Food Politics: How the Food Industry Influences Nutrition and Health*) documents extensively how food science findings undergo systematic distortion as they pass through media intermediaries, each of whom has incentives to amplify risk perception.


## 6. Implications

The olive oil case raises several issues that extend beyond the specific claim. First, it demonstrates that numeric precision in viral health claims is not evidence of accuracy — it may in fact be a red flag, since invented claims often rely on spurious precision to simulate scientific authority. When a figure like 69% appears in a health claim, the appropriate question is not just "is this number accurate?" but "what exactly did this number measure in its original context, and is that measurement relevant to this claim?"

Second, the case illustrates the regulatory gap in olive oil grading enforcement. The UC Davis findings represented a genuine failure of the distribution and retail system to maintain grade quality from production to sale. Whether or not the specific oils were labelled fraudulently in the narrow legal sense, consumers paying premium prices for "extra virgin" olive oil were systematically receiving lower-grade products. This consumer protection problem persists and is genuinely worthy of regulatory attention — it simply has nothing to do with whether the oil contains olives.

Third, the case highlights the ongoing international challenge of olive oil adulteration surveillance. The genuine adulteration literature — Tres et al. (2015); García-González et al. (2010, *Food Chemistry*) — identifies real methodological tools for detecting non-olive adulterants, and legitimate concerns about supply chain integrity at global scale. This real concern is not well served by the viral article's inaccurate framing, which conflates the manageable and mundane problem of grade degradation with the distinct and more serious problem of intentional adulteration.

For consumers, the practical upshot is straightforward: buying from reputable producers, checking harvest dates rather than best-before dates, choosing dark glass over transparent containers, storing oil away from heat and light, and preferring certified olive oils from recognised industry quality programs (such as the California Olive Oil Council certification or the North American Olive Oil Association's testing program) are all evidence-based strategies for obtaining genuinely high-quality extra virgin olive oil. The 69% figure, correctly understood, is a call for better supply chain management and consumer education, not a reason to treat olive oil as a fraudulent or toxic product.
