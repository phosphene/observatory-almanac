# The Omicron variant of COVID-19 caused fewer deaths than previous variants


## 1. The Claim

From late 2021 onward, as the Omicron variant (B.1.1.529 and its sublineages) spread globally, a widespread claim emerged and consolidated: that Omicron was less dangerous than previous variants, caused milder illness, and resulted in fewer deaths. This framing appeared in media commentary, political statements, public health communications, and social media discourse, and it carried significant public health implications — it was used to support the position that Omicron represented a transition toward endemicity, that pandemic restrictions could be lifted, and that the population need no longer be significantly alarmed by COVID-19 spread.

The specific formulation "Omicron causes fewer deaths than previous variants" has appeared in diverse forms with different levels of precision: sometimes as a statement about individual case fatality rate (the proportion of confirmed infected people who die, which is lower for Omicron than for earlier variants in most studied populations); sometimes as an implicit or explicit claim about aggregate death tolls (suggesting that Omicron killed fewer people overall than, say, the Delta wave); and sometimes as a narrative about disease severity trajectory more broadly (implying that COVID-19 was becoming progressively less dangerous as it evolved toward endemicity).

The claim's epidemiological accuracy depends entirely on which framing is meant — and in public discourse, the crucial distinction between individual case severity and population-level mortality impact was frequently collapsed or ignored. The case for the claim is strong on the individual level: Omicron does appear to cause less severe acute illness per infection, particularly lower rates of progression to respiratory failure and intensive care admission, in comparable populations. The case becomes much more complicated at the population level, where infection volumes, pre-existing immunity, and healthcare system capacity interact in ways the simple "fewer deaths" framing does not capture.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Omicron's reduced per-infection severity is well-established.** Multiple peer-reviewed studies confirmed that Omicron subvariants produce lower case fatality rates (CFR) and infection fatality rates (IFR) than Delta and earlier variants in comparable populations with comparable access to medical care. Ferguson et al. (2021, preprint subsequently revised, Imperial College London) found early evidence of reduced severity in South African data. Wolter et al. (2022, *The Lancet*) analysed hospitalisation risk among South African patients in the BA.1 wave compared to prior waves and found a significantly lower risk of severe disease and ICU admission. UK data from the Health Security Agency (UKHSA, 2021 technical briefings) similarly found that Omicron carried a substantially lower risk of hospitalisation per confirmed infection compared to Delta. Danish surveillance data showed reduced ICU admission rates (Lund et al., 2022, *The Lancet Regional Health — Europe*).

Mechanistically, the reduced severity appears attributable to two factors: first, Omicron's documented preferential replication in the upper respiratory tract rather than the lower respiratory tract and lung parenchyma (Hui et al., 2022, *Nature*, which found Omicron replicated approximately 70 times faster in the bronchus than Delta but less efficiently in the lungs), which reduces the direct pathological damage associated with severe COVID-19 pneumonia; and second, the effect of population immunity — either from vaccination or prior infection — which by the Omicron period was broader and had specifically adapted to recognise and limit progression even for novel variants. Distinguishing these two contributions is methodologically difficult and remains an active area of research; real-world outcomes are a product of both simultaneously.

**The population-level picture is substantially different.** The critical data point that the "Omicron caused fewer deaths" framing frequently obscures is the scale of Omicron's transmission compared to prior variants. Omicron was dramatically more transmissible than Delta, which was itself substantially more transmissible than the original Wuhan strain. The effective reproductive number (R₀) of Omicron BA.1 was estimated in multiple analyses at approximately 8–15 in naive populations, compared to estimates of 5–7 for Delta and 2–3 for the original strain (Liu & Rocklöv, 2022, *Journal of Travel Medicine*). This was not a modest difference; it represented a qualitative step-change in transmission dynamics.

The consequence is that even as the per-infection fatality probability declined, the total number of infections in the Omicron wave vastly outnumbered prior waves. In England, for example, the ONS Infection Survey (Office for National Statistics, 2022) estimated approximately 1 in 15 of the population was infected during the peak week of the BA.2 wave — an incidence rate without any precedent in the prior pandemic period. When a per-infection risk that is substantially lower than Delta's is applied to an infected population that is an order of magnitude larger, the relationship between per-infection risk reduction and aggregate deaths becomes highly non-linear.

Actual mortality data from the Omicron period in several countries illustrates this: the United States recorded more COVID-19 deaths in January 2022 (during the BA.1 wave peak) than in any prior month of the pandemic (US CDC, 2022 surveillance data). The UK's Omicron BA.1 wave produced weekly death counts approaching the peak Delta levels, despite substantially lower per-infection severity. The period from December 2021 through early 2022 was among the highest total death-count periods of the pandemic in several European and North American populations, not among the lowest.

To be fair, the picture varies across national populations and time periods. Countries with high vaccination coverage and early boosters did see substantially reduced death-to-case ratios compared to unvaccinated populations, and in settings with strong vaccination campaigns the Omicron waves were markedly less deadly per hospitalisation than earlier waves. The aggregate outcomes varied accordingly. But the headline claim — "Omicron caused fewer deaths" — if interpreted as a statement about total population deaths rather than individual case prognosis, does not accurately characterise the mortality record in the period during which Omicron was dominant.

**The severity-of-illness dimension.** Beyond mortality, Omicron did produce a genuine reduction in the proportion of infected individuals developing severe acute illness requiring hospitalisation or intensive care: this is robustly established. This reduction was clinically and practically significant, both because it reduced individual suffering and because it affected healthcare system demand per infected person. The load placed on intensive care by the Omicron wave in vaccinated, boosted populations was lower per hospitalisation than prior waves. These are real differences that matter for clinical practice, healthcare capacity planning, and individual risk assessment.

The complications again emerge at the population level: hospitalisation counts during Omicron waves were, in several settings, still extremely high in absolute terms because so many people were infected simultaneously. The pressure on healthcare systems, while reduced in per-infection terms, remained substantial in aggregate because of the volume multiplier.

**Long COVID under Omicron.** An important and frequently omitted dimension of comparative harm is the incidence of post-acute sequelae ("long COVID"). Earlier studies on long COVID from the Alpha and Delta period found that a significant proportion of infected individuals experienced persistent symptoms for months beyond acute illness (Deer et al., 2021, *Annals of Internal Medicine*; ONS, 2022, *Prevalence of ongoing symptoms following coronavirus (COVID-19) infection in the UK*). Early evidence suggested that the Omicron variants produced a lower rate of long COVID per infection compared to Delta, though the absolute incidence remained substantial given infection volumes (Antonelli et al., 2022, *The Lancet*). The long COVID dimension illustrates that total morbidity burden — the aggregate of suffering caused by an outbreak — cannot be captured by CFR alone, and that population-level comparisons of variant outcomes need to account for the long consequences of infection, not only acute mortality.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Accurate partial information presented without population-level context.** The specific mechanism driving widespread acceptance of the "Omicron caused fewer deaths" claim is that the claim is straightforwardly and demonstrably true in one important dimension — per-infection case fatality rate — and that this accurate partial information was communicated widely by credible sources including public health authorities, clinicians, and scientists, who were emphasising it for valid reasons. The individual risk reduction per infection is real, clinically important, and warranted emphasis for individual decision-making purposes. But when a factual statement about individual risk is heard by audiences processing it as a population-level claim, the inferential error is cognitive rather than a failure of the original communication: the listener generalises from "lower risk per person" to "fewer total deaths" in a way that is intuitively appealing but mathematically wrong given the volume difference.

This is a form of the base-rate neglect error documented comprehensively in the judgment and decision-making literature by Kahneman and Tversky. When both the per-infection risk (lower for Omicron) and the infection base rate (vastly higher for Omicron) are relevant, intuitive reasoning tends to anchor on the vivid, specific, and directly communicated dimension (per-infection risk) and underweight or ignore the base rate (number infected). The result is confident but incorrect inference about overall outcomes.

**Pandemic fatigue and motivated reasoning.** By the time Omicron emerged in late 2021, a significant proportion of populations in many countries had experienced approximately two years of pandemic-related disruptions including lockdowns, economic losses, social restrictions, and extended fear and uncertainty. There was a powerful motivational environment in which good news — particularly news that COVID severity was declining — was psychologically welcome and in which information that challenged that narrative was unwelcome. Motivated reasoning has been shown to influence both belief acquisition and belief retention under conditions where emotional stakes are elevated (Kunda, 1990, *Psychological Bulletin*). The claim that Omicron caused fewer deaths resonated with an existing desire for the pandemic to be ending, and this desire created a cognitive environment less disposed to examine the population-level caveat carefully.

**Political normalisation narratives.** The "Omicron as mild" narrative was actively amplified by political actors in multiple countries who were seeking to end pandemic-response policies including mask mandates, testing requirements, and vaccination programmes. In the United Kingdom, the Conservative government's January 2022 "freedom day" messaging was substantially built around the framing that Omicron represented a less dangerous phase of the pandemic that warranted lifting restrictions. Similar narratives appeared in United States conservative media and in Australian and Canadian political commentary. These narratives selected the true partial information (lower per-infection severity) and deployed it in contexts that invited the false total-deaths inference, without providing the total infection volume and aggregate mortality data that would have complicated the picture. The selective deployment of accurate partial data by politically motivated actors is a distinct mechanism from ordinary inadvertent misunderstanding, and it contributed to the durability and geographic spread of the oversimplified version.

**Evolutionary expectations and folk epidemiology.** A widespread and incorrect folk model of pathogen evolution holds that viruses necessarily evolve toward greater transmissibility and reduced virulence over time, because killing hosts is "bad for the virus." This model — sometimes attributed to a misreading of evolutionary biology — is not supported by contemporary evolutionary theory, which predicts no uniform tendency toward reduced virulence (Alizon et al., 2009, *PLOS Pathogens*). However, the folk model is intuitively appealing and is frequently invoked in lay discussion of pandemic trajectory. The Omicron "less deadly" evidence was readily assimilated into this folk evolutionary narrative as confirmation that the virus was behaving as populations expected it to. The prior belief in inevitable attenuating virulence primed audiences to accept and amplify evidence for reduced severity, and to be less sceptical about applying it universally, because it confirmed an existing explanatory framework.


## 4. Verdict

**True-but-incomplete, tending toward misleading in typical use.** The claim that Omicron caused fewer deaths than previous variants is accurate as a statement about individual case fatality risk in comparable immunological contexts: Omicron does appear to produce lower per-infection mortality than Delta in matched populations, and the mechanistic reasons for this are reasonably well characterised. This is a meaningful and important finding for individual risk assessment and clinical practice.

The claim is misleading — and in many of the contexts in which it was deployed, functionally false — as a statement about aggregate population mortality outcomes. The Omicron waves in several countries with high absolute case volumes produced death totals comparable to or exceeding prior pandemic peaks, despite lower per-infection severity, because the vastly higher transmissibility multiplied the base population of infected people far beyond any prior phase. The simple statement "Omicron caused fewer deaths" does not convey this relationship; it implies that Omicron was less deadly in total terms as well as individual terms, which was not consistently true.

The appropriate formulation of the accurate claim is something like: "Omicron produced lower individual case fatality rates than prior variants in comparable populations, driven by both intrinsic biological differences and accumulated population immunity, though the aggregate mortality burden during Omicron waves remained substantial because of dramatically higher infection volumes."


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Omicron severity debate illustrates a structural problem in pandemic risk communication that has no clean solution: the epidemiologically precise and policy-relevant information (individual risk per infection versus aggregate population burden) are distinct dimensions that require different modes of communication and respond to different audience needs, but they are routinely conflated in both media reporting and policy messaging.

Individual risk communication is important for autonomous decision-making: a person deciding whether to attend an event, visit a vulnerable relative, or seek booster vaccination needs information about their own likely outcome if infected. Aggregate burden communication is important for policy: a government deciding whether to maintain hospital surge capacity, maintain testing infrastructure, or lift population-wide public health measures needs information about total deaths and healthcare system demand, not only per-infection severity. Conflating these two communicative registers produces systematically misleading output regardless of whether any individual statement in the communication is factually inaccurate.

The Omicron period also revealed the inadequacy of case fatality rate as a primary public health severity metric. CFR is highly sensitive to the denominator (confirmed cases, which varies enormously with testing rates and access) and to baseline population immunity. The infection fatality rate (IFR) is methodologically superior but harder to estimate in real time. Neither captures aggregate burden directly. Excess mortality data — the comparison of observed deaths to expected deaths absent the pandemic — provides a cleaner measure of total population impact, and excess mortality analyses of the Omicron period have provided a less reassuring picture than CFR comparisons alone.

Post-pandemic epidemiological work has established that the interpretation of cross-variant comparisons requires careful adjustment for population immunity, vaccination status, healthcare access, and testing practices. The Omicron period, specifically, coincided with a period of widely variable booster coverage across populations, making raw comparative mortality data between the Alpha/Delta period and the Omicron period difficult to interpret without controlling for these confounders (Mefsin et al., 2022, *Emerging Infectious Diseases*). The "Omicron was milder" conclusion drawn from raw CFR comparisons combines the intrinsic biological properties of the variant with the protective effect of accumulated population immunity in proportions that cannot easily be separated without counterfactual modelling.

This has implications beyond COVID-19: as future respiratory virus pandemics inevitably occur, the communicative template for describing variant severity comparisons needs to specify clearly which dimension of severity is being described, in which population, under what immunological assumptions, and whether the claim concerns individual or aggregate outcomes. The Omicron period provided a clear worked example of what happens when this specification is absent.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Omicron severity claim is not addressed in the Snopes format — it was processed primarily as real-time public health communication rather than as a misinformation claim subject to formal fact-checking, which partly reflects the fact that the core statement is true in one dimension and thus does not meet most fact-check platforms' threshold for "false." The nuance required to address it — explaining why a true per-infection claim does not translate into a true aggregate-mortality claim — exceeds what most standard fact-check formats handle well.

More systematic engagement appeared in epidemiology and public health journals and commentary through 2022 and 2023, as scholars reviewed the actual mortality record of the Omicron period against the "milder wave" predictions. Science journalists including those at *Nature News* and *The BMJ* published more careful analyses that distinguished the dimensions of severity and explicitly noted the volume-multiplier effect. Academic public health commentators including Carl Bergstrom and others known for science communication work on COVID-19 statistics noted consistently through the Omicron period that the "less deadly" framing required careful contextualisation.

For fact-checkers approaching claims about variant severity comparisons, the recommended methodology involves: (1) specifying which metric the claim is making — CFR, IFR, hospitalisation rate per infection, or total aggregate deaths; (2) examining excess mortality data from the variant period as an aggregate check on per-infection claims; (3) noting the immunological confounders that affect any cross-wave comparison; (4) examining whether absolute death and hospitalisation counts during the wave in question were in fact lower or higher than prior waves; and (5) distinguishing between claims about intrinsic variant biology and claims about the period's actual outcomes, which are the product of both variant biology and population immunity context.

The lasting media literacy principle is one of dimensional specification: severity, risk, and harm can be measured on multiple orthogonal dimensions, and a claim that is true on one dimension (per-infection severity) can be false on another (aggregate deaths) without any single statement being factually incorrect. Claims that omit dimensional specification in high-stakes public health contexts should be understood as communicatively incomplete, and the completion they invite — the audience inference from individual risk to aggregate outcome — is typically the more policy-relevant dimension. Precision in public health risk communication is not merely a technical nicety but a variable that demonstrably affects policy decisions, individual behaviour, and ultimately outcomes.
