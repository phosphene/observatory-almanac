# "Open-Plan Offices Boost Collaboration and Productivity"


## 1. The Claim

Open-plan offices—environments designed with minimal or no walls between workers—increase employee collaboration, creativity, and overall workplace productivity by facilitating spontaneous interaction, democratizing access to leadership, and fostering a culture of transparency and shared purpose.

The claim is not merely a folk belief but a corporate ideology codified in design briefs, management literature, and the physical architecture of the modern knowledge economy. From the 1960s Bürolandschaft (office landscape) movement in Germany to the 1990s tech campus revolution to the post-2008 austerity-driven open-plan conversion of legacy office stock, the premise has been consistent: break down the walls, break down the hierarchies, and productivity will follow. The open-plan office is estimated to house approximately 70% of U.S. workers today, a figure that represents a fundamental transformation of the physical environment in which knowledge work happens.

The most influential recent instantiation of this claim appeared in the tech sector. Google's sprawling campuses, Facebook's (now Meta's) Frank Gehry–designed MPK20 building—billed as the world's largest open-plan office—and Apple's Steve Jobs Theater–era redesign all signaled that the most economically productive organizations in human history had validated open-plan design. Fortune 500 companies followed, stripping out offices and cubicle farms to replace them with hot-desking arrangements, collaborative "neighborhoods," and "activity-based working" configurations. The architects of these spaces, and the management consultants who endorsed them, described the transition as an evidence-based optimization of human collaboration.


## 2. What's Actually True

The empirical record is considerably more complicated—and, in places, directly contradicts the claim.

**Bernstein and Turban (2018)**, the most rigorous study to date, produced findings that directly invert the prediction. Ethan Bernstein and Stephen Turban of Harvard Business School used sociometric badges and electronic data to measure actual face-to-face interaction in two large U.S. corporate headquarters before and after major open-plan renovations. Their findings, published in *Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society B*, were stark: open-plan transitions reduced face-to-face interaction by approximately 70%, while electronic communication (email and messaging) increased by roughly 20–50%. Workers did not respond to architectural openness by talking to each other more. They responded by doing the opposite—withdrawing into headphones, burying themselves in screens, and routing communication through digital channels rather than risk the cognitive and social cost of unmediated face-to-face contact.

The mechanism Bernstein and Turban identify is behavioral adaptation. Humans in open environments develop protective strategies. Headphones signal unavailability. Strategic positioning relative to walkways and high-traffic areas becomes a form of territorial management. Workers schedule high-concentration tasks during off-peak hours or work from home to escape interruption. The collaborative affordances built into the architecture are actively circumvented by the people living inside it.

**Perham and colleagues** at Cardiff Metropolitan University documented the concentration cost empirically. Workers in open-plan environments are more frequently interrupted and take longer to recover from interruptions than those in private offices or enclosed spaces. The "attention residue" phenomenon documented by Sophie Leroy (2009)—the mental carryover from an interrupted task that degrades performance on the subsequent task—is significantly more common in open environments. Complex cognitive work, writing, analysis, and programming require sustained attention; open environments systematically degrade the conditions for it.

**Kim and de Dear (2013)**, analyzing data from 42,764 respondents across 303 U.S. office buildings in the BOSTI (Buffalo Organization for Social and Technological Innovation) dataset, found that open-plan workers reported significantly worse outcomes across multiple dimensions: noise disturbance, privacy, proximity to colleagues, and temperature regulation. Critically, the claimed collaboration and communication benefits did not appear in sufficient magnitude to offset the concentration and privacy costs. Satisfaction was lowest in fully open plans compared to private offices, shared private offices, and partitioned open plans.

**Productivity measurement** is harder, and here the evidence genuinely is mixed rather than straightforwardly negative. Some studies find modest collaboration benefits in work where spontaneous interaction is the primary value driver—certain creative tasks, rapid cross-functional problem-solving, early-stage ideation. The issue is that these benefits accrue in specific task types while the concentration costs are broadly distributed across all task types. For a software engineer, analyst, or writer whose work requires deep concentration for extended periods, the collaboration benefits are likely insufficient compensation for the interruption costs.

**Post-pandemic evidence** has complicated the picture further. The mass forced experiment of remote work during COVID-19 demonstrated that many workers were dramatically more productive outside the open-plan office, and the "great resignation" and hybrid work trends that followed partly reflect workers refusing to return to environments they experienced as productivity-hostile. Microsoft's research on its own workforce found that remote workers maintained or improved output metrics while reporting higher concentration and job satisfaction.

The genuine complexity is this: the claim that open plans boost collaboration is not entirely false—there is evidence of collaboration benefits in certain contexts. But those benefits are narrower, more conditional, and more task-dependent than the claim implies, and the concentration costs are broader and less discussed. The claim presents a one-sided accounting that ignores costs documented by the same researchers who identified benefits.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The open-plan ideology has multiple interlocking sources of support, not all of them epistemic.

**The intuitive appeal of physical metaphor.** The idea that removing walls removes barriers is architecturally coherent as a metaphor. If walls keep people apart, their absence should bring people together. This mapping from physical to social space feels compelling without requiring empirical verification. Management literature of the 1990s and 2000s was saturated with the vocabulary of breaking down silos, flattening hierarchies, and enabling the free flow of ideas—and open-plan architecture literalized these metaphors in concrete and steel.

**Observation bias and selection effects.** When a company transitions to open plan and its subsequent years are productive, the architecture gets partial credit. The confound—that company performance depends on product decisions, market conditions, leadership, hiring, and a dozen other factors—is invisible to the people living through it. Companies that attribute their successes to architectural choice become case studies in management literature, which propagates the association without controlling for the confounds.

**The role of tech sector prestige.** Google, Facebook, and Apple built extraordinary enterprises and open-plan offices. The associative inference—these companies are successful and they build open offices, therefore open offices contribute to success—is logically invalid but psychologically compelling. It is the survivorship bias of architectural determinism: we see the successful open-plan companies, not the unsuccessful ones.

**Cost and real estate incentives.** Open-plan offices are dramatically cheaper to build and maintain than private office configurations. A company that wants to reduce its real estate footprint has a strong financial incentive to endorse the collaborative benefits of open-plan arrangements, regardless of what the evidence says. The economic interest and the architectural ideology aligned conveniently. Critics of open-plan offices have noted that management enthusiasm for collaboration as a workplace value intensified precisely as private office space was eliminated—the causal direction is plausibly inverted.

**The innovation narrative.** The mythology of serendipitous corridor encounters, of the breakthrough that happened because two people bumped into each other at the espresso machine, became canonical in the early twenty-first century through books like Steven Johnson's *Where Good Ideas Come From* (2010) and Walter Isaacson's biography of Steve Jobs. These narratives locate innovation in physical proximity and accidental interaction. The literature on deliberate practice, deep work, and the concentration requirements of complex problem-solving did not gain comparable cultural traction until Cal Newport's *Deep Work* (2016) and similar volumes began pushing back.

**Anchoring the prior literature.** The early research on open-plan offices was substantially less rigorous than Bernstein and Turban's study and tended to rely on self-report surveys, brief observation periods, or measures of stated intent to collaborate rather than actual collaborative interaction. This allowed proponents to point to peer-reviewed literature without acknowledging that more sophisticated measurement consistently produced less favorable findings.


## 4. Verdict

**Classification: Contested Consensus**

The claim is not simply false, but the evidence runs substantially against its strongest form—that open offices improve productivity and collaboration—while genuinely supporting only weaker and more conditional versions.

**What the evidence supports:**
- Open environments can increase *incidental* exposure to colleagues, which may benefit coordination in some task types
- For very early-stage creative work where ideation is the primary goal, proximity has some documented benefits
- Certain management objectives—monitoring, visibility, reduced real estate costs—are served by open plans, even if productivity is not
- Workers adapted to open environments report fewer complaints over time (though this may reflect normalization rather than actual improvement)

**What the evidence contradicts:**
- The strong claim that removing walls increases face-to-face collaboration—Bernstein and Turban found a 70% *decrease*
- That open plans improve productivity broadly—the evidence for concentration costs is substantial and consistent
- That the trade-off favors knowledge workers whose work requires sustained attention—the concentration costs dominate in that population
- That the tech sector's success validates open-plan design—the observed correlation confounds dozens of unrelated factors

**The honest middle ground:** Open-plan environments represent a genuine trade-off that different organizations will navigate differently depending on their task structure, workforce type, and management culture. A trading floor, a newsroom, or an early-stage startup may benefit from the density and visibility that open environments provide. A software development shop, a research organization, or a financial analysis team likely does not. The ideology was applied universally where the evidence supports selective and conditional use.

The architectural ideology outran its evidence base by roughly two decades. The management consulting and commercial real estate industries, which had financial interests in the open-plan transition, provided the momentum; the peer-reviewed organizational behavior literature, when it caught up, returned a substantially more mixed verdict.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The open-plan office controversy sits within a larger body of work on the conditions that enable—and disable—knowledge work.

**Cal Newport's "deep work" framework** (2016) synthesized research on concentration, deliberate practice, and cognitive performance to argue that high-value knowledge work requires extended periods of uninterrupted focus that open environments systematically preclude. Newport's account drew on Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's research on flow states, which require approximately fifteen to twenty minutes of uninterrupted engagement before they are achieved, and are collapsed by interruption. An environment in which workers are interrupted every three to five minutes—documented in multiple open-plan studies—makes flow states structurally impossible for most workers most of the day.

**Herbert Simon's concept of attention as a scarce resource** provides the theoretical foundation. In an information-rich environment, the limiting factor is not information but the attention to process it. Open-plan environments maximize information density—more people, more conversation, more movement, more ambient noise—and thereby maximize attention demand. For workers whose primary input is information, this is a direct degradation of the productive resource they depend on.

**Activity-based working (ABW)**, the most sophisticated modern evolution of open-plan thinking, attempts to address these critiques by providing multiple distinct work environment types—focus zones, collaboration spaces, informal meeting areas, phone booths—that workers can select based on their current task. The research on ABW is more favorable than on raw open-plan, precisely because it acknowledges the concentration-collaboration trade-off and attempts to manage it. The failure mode is organizational culture: companies that install ABW infrastructure but maintain unspoken expectations that workers remain visible at their desks recreate the worst of both worlds.

**Remote and hybrid work** has functionally answered one question: when given the choice, knowledge workers with concentration-intensive jobs prefer not to be in open-plan offices. The pandemic-era productivity data, while methodologically imperfect, consistently showed that remote workers in individual roles maintained or improved measured output. The resistance to full return-to-office policies is, among other things, a revealed-preference verdict on the open-plan environment.

**Acoustic research** provides one of the cleaner causal pathways. Irrelevant background speech—the kind prevalent in open offices—specifically degrades performance on tasks requiring verbal working memory, reading comprehension, and serial recall. The "irrelevant speech effect" documented by Salamé and Baddeley (1982) and extensively replicated shows that intelligible background conversation impairs verbal cognitive task performance even when the listener is not consciously attending to it. This is not a preference effect; it is a cognitive architecture effect. The workplace has been engineered into a form that is neurologically hostile to one of the primary forms of work that happens inside it.

The field of organizational behavior continues to produce nuanced accounts of when and for whom open environments are beneficial. The answer is consistently more conditional than the ideology assumed—and the conditions are not well-matched to the ways the ideology was actually deployed.

**Psychoacoustic measurement** has refined the noise picture considerably. Open-plan workers typically experience ambient noise levels between 50–65 dB(A), with periodic spikes during phone calls, group conversations, and foot traffic. The World Health Organization's guidelines for cognitive work recommend noise levels below 35 dB(A) for tasks requiring concentration—a threshold that essentially no operational open-plan environment meets. The gap between WHO guidance and actual open-plan conditions is not marginal; it is categorical. Every worker performing concentration-intensive work in a standard open-plan office is, by WHO standards, working in a cognitively impaired acoustic environment.

**The gender dimension** of open-plan research has received less attention than it warrants. Several studies have found that women in open-plan offices report higher levels of distraction, stress, and privacy intrusion than men. Possible explanatory factors include differential socialization around responding to ambient social cues, higher rates of task-interruption from colleagues, and the additional emotional labor of managing constant visibility. If the collaboration benefits of open-plan environments are more equally distributed while the concentration costs are asymmetrically borne, the aggregate productivity calculation differs by worker type in ways that aggregate statistics may obscure.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not examined the open-plan office claim despite its pervasive influence on workplace design and the substantial body of research that contests it. The claim circulates primarily in management literature, corporate communications, and architectural advocacy rather than in the forms—viral Facebook posts, chain emails—that Snopes typically tracks. This format mismatch means consequential ideological claims that operate through professional and institutional channels rather than popular media largely escape systematic fact-checking scrutiny.

The gap matters because the open-plan office is not a minor lifestyle preference but a structural decision affecting the working conditions of tens of millions of people. When that decision is made on the basis of an empirically contested claim presented as settled—"open plans improve collaboration and productivity"—workers have no mechanism to contest it, and the research that contradicts it is distributed across organizational behavior journals that neither their managers nor their HR departments typically read. The fact-checking infrastructure does not yet reliably reach the management ideology layer, where some of the most consequential empirical claims about human behavior are made and propagated without systematic scrutiny.
