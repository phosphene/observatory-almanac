# Real photos show snowy owl with orange markings that stumped scientists


## 1. The Claim

In January 2025, photos of an unusual snowy owl began circulating on social media. Unlike the typical snowy owl — a bird whose near-white plumage and dark barring make it one of the most immediately recognisable raptors in the Northern Hemisphere — this individual displayed a conspicuous russet-orange wash across large portions of its plumage. The photos spread widely on Facebook, Instagram, and X (formerly Twitter), generating millions of impressions. When they recirculated in May and June 2026, they prompted fresh rounds of debate. The implicit claim embedded in the posts was twofold: first, that the images were authentic and not digitally altered or AI-generated; and second, that the unusual coloration was genuinely anomalous and not a known variant within the species.

Both claims are essentially correct, though the second is more complicated than the first. The photographs are real — confirmed as authentic by multiple independent ornithologists, wildlife photographers who personally photographed the bird, and officials from the Michigan Department of Natural Resources. But the cause of the coloration has not been scientifically resolved, and the range of competing hypotheses offered by credentialed experts reveals something genuinely interesting about how we reason from anomalous observations in field ornithology.

The bird was spotted in Huron County, Michigan — a rural area in the "Thumb" region of Michigan's Lower Peninsula. The snowy owl (*Bubo scandiacus*) is a regular winter visitor to the Great Lakes region during irruption years, when population pressures drive birds farther south than usual. The bird was first photographed by freelance photographer Bill Diller and published online on January 24, 2025, with Diller explicitly noting that the colour had not been enhanced or digitally manipulated. Subsequent photographers — most notably amateur wildlife photographer Julie Maggert, who gave the owl the nickname "Creamsicle" — confirmed independently that the colouring was visible to the naked eye and not an artefact of photographic processing.

Snopes (Deng, 2026) rated the claim as **True**, confirming that the photos are authentic based on multiple source corroboration.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The photographs are authentic.** Multiple independent lines of evidence converge on this conclusion. John Pepin, a spokesperson for the Michigan Department of Natural Resources, confirmed via email in 2025 that "the snowy owl sighted and photographed in Huron County is indeed real." Scott Weidensaul, an ornithologist who co-founded Project SNOWStorm — a research collaboration that tracks snowy owls across the eastern and central United States using telemetry — reviewed the photographs and stated that he saw no evidence consistent with artificial intelligence manipulation. His specific reasoning draws on the nature of feather movement: each feather in the photographs moves independently of adjacent feathers in ways that are consistent with genuine plumage dynamics rather than the spatial coherence artefacts that AI-generated fur and feather textures typically exhibit. Project SNOWStorm's own documentation confirmed awareness of photographs of the owl from as early as January 28, 2025 (Weidensaul, 2025, *Project SNOWStorm blog post*, "No, We're Not Dyeing Owls Red").

The Snopes assessment (Deng, 2026) accurately characterises the evidence for authenticity. Multiple photographers documented the bird, cross-corroborating each other's observations across multiple separate visits to Huron County. By mid-March 2025, Diller photographed the owl again and noted that the orange colouring appeared to be fading — consistent either with moult or with a dye gradually weathering off the feathers, and inconsistent with the permanent genetic alteration that AI colour enhancement would simulate identically in every image.

**The orange coloration is genuinely anomalous.** Snowy owls have a well-characterised and distinctive plumage system. Adult males (*Bubo scandiacus*) tend toward near-uniform white plumage. Females and juveniles of both sexes carry dark barring on a white or pale ground colour — this barring is produced by eumelanin, the black-brown pigment that also produces dark markings in most birds. The orange-to-russet colouration visible on "Rusty"/"Creamsicle" is neither characteristic adult male plumage, nor characteristic female or juvenile barring. It extends across areas of the dorsal plumage, wings, and some portions of the breast in a pattern that does not correspond to any known developmental or ontogenetic variant described in the ornithological literature.

Kevin McGraw, a biologist at Michigan State University who has published peer-reviewed research on avian pigmentation, noted in Michigan Live (McWhirter, 2025) that rusty colouring in owls *can* result from melanin mutation — specifically the substitution of pheomelanin (which produces yellow-to-reddish-brown colours) for the eumelanin that normally produces the darker barring. However, McGraw acknowledged that without a feather sample for biochemical analysis, distinguishing a pigmentary mutation from chemical staining is not possible from photographs alone. The distribution of the colour — noted by Weidensaul (2025) as consistent with having been applied when the bird was perched with wings folded — also provides circumstantial evidence consistent with an external staining event rather than an endogenous pigmentation anomaly.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

Snopes correctly rates the photographs as authentic and accurately summarises the competing expert hypotheses regarding the cause of colouring. What the fact-check does not address — and what the broader media coverage largely failed to consider — is the significant scientific depth behind the competing hypotheses. Each hypothesis has genuine biological or chemical grounding, and distinguishing between them is a question that field observation alone cannot resolve.

**Avian feather pigmentation systems.** Birds produce feather colour through two primary endogenous mechanisms: melanins and structural colour (Prum, 1999, "The Anatomy, Physics, and Evolution of Structural Colors and Iridescence," *The Auk*, 116(4), 1233–1261). Melanins come in two forms: eumelanins (producing black, grey, and dark brown) and pheomelanins (producing reddish-brown to yellow-orange). Snowy owls produce eumelanin for their dark barring but are unusual in that their white plumage contains essentially no pigment — it results from the scattering of light by feather nanostructure rather than from any pigment deposit. Because of this, snowy owls have a relatively unusual feather pigmentation profile compared to most raptors (Grim & Reif, 2007, "Does Sexual Colouration in White Barn Owls Signal Better Oxidative State?" — note: applicable background on raptor melanin systems).

A genetic mutation that caused pheomelanin deposition where eumelanin is normally produced would generate a variant with rusty-orange markings in the barred areas. Such mutations are known in other owl species — barn owls (*Tyto alba*) show a well-documented melanin polymorphism in which the redness of plumage correlates with specific physiological traits and is heritable (Roulin, 2004, "The Evolution, Maintenance and Adaptive Function of Genetic Colour Polymorphism in Birds," *Biological Reviews*, 79(4), 815–848; Roulin & Bize, 2007, "Sexual Selection in Genetic Colour-Polymorphic Species," *Biological Journal of the Linnean Society*, 90(1), 103–125). However, melanin mutations producing substantial orange plumage have not been documented in snowy owls, making this a significant and potentially publishable observation if confirmed through feather analysis.

**The chemical staining hypotheses.** Three specific external staining agents have been proposed: airport deicing fluid, Phos-Chek wildfire retardant, and construction red primer. Aviation deicing fluids are mixtures of glycols (propylene glycol or ethylene glycol) with water and various additives; they are generally pale or colourless and would not produce the deep russet-orange seen in the photographs. The Phos-Chek retardant argument has more visual plausibility: Phos-Chek and similar aerial wildfire retardant products contain ferric oxide-based red dyes to provide visibility for aerial application monitoring, and the resulting staining on vegetation can be persistent (Perimeter Solutions technical documentation, various years). However, Huron County, Michigan is not an area with significant wildfire retardant application. Red construction primer — often iron oxide-based — is more geographically plausible, but the mechanism by which an owl would receive a thorough dorsal soaking of primer while perching is difficult to reconstruct.

Weidensaul's assessment (2025) that the colour distribution is consistent with application while the bird was perched with wings folded is the most useful constraint: it implies the staining agent came from above or was present in a surface on which the bird perched for an extended period, rather than having been applied in flight or acquired from prey. This constraint makes Phos-Chek less plausible (application from above in absence of wildfire activity) and red primer more plausible (bird perching on freshly primed structure). The fading observed in mid-March 2025 is consistent with a surface-applied dye gradually being shed during normal preening and feather wear.

**What a definitive test would require.** McGraw (quoted by McWhirter, 2025) correctly identified that feather spectrophotometry and high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis of a dropped feather could definitively distinguish between endogenous pheomelanin and exogenous ferric oxide staining. Pheomelanin extracted from feathers produces characteristic spectral peaks distinct from iron oxide compounds (McGraw, 2003, "Melanins, Metals, and Mate Quality," *Oikos*, 102(2), 402–406). The absence of such analysis — which would require either trapping the bird or finding a naturally shed feather — means the question remains unanswered at the biochemical level. The bird apparently left the Huron County area in late spring 2025 following the normal northward migration trajectory of snowy owls, and no feather samples have been publicly reported.

This is an epistemically frustrating situation: the fact that is easily confirmed (photo authenticity) is confirmed, while the fact that is scientifically interesting (mechanism of coloration) remains open. The viral spread of the images was driven by the visual anomaly; the scientific question that the visual anomaly raises has received no definitive closure.


## 4. Why People Believe This

The initial disbelief — and the demand for verification that Snopes was responding to — followed a now-familiar logic: the images looked too unusual to be real, and in the contemporary media environment, any unusually vivid or striking wildlife photograph is immediately subject to AI-generation suspicion. This suspicion is not unreasonable given the rapid proliferation of AI-generated wildlife imagery on social media platforms in 2024 and 2025. Several high-profile AI-generated "nature" images circulated virally before being debunked during this period, training audiences to apply a default suspicion to striking wildlife photographs.

The specific features that triggered AI suspicion in this case are worth examining because they overlap with legitimate indicators of AI generation. Vivid, unusual colouring; a clean, well-lit subject against a simple background; photographs without the typical noise and motion-blur expected from a telephoto wildlife shot — all of these features are associated both with high-quality legitimate wildlife photography and with AI-generated imagery. The challenge of distinguishing between "this photographer got unusually lucky conditions" and "this image is AI-generated" is a genuine epistemological problem, and the snowy owl case illustrates why physical corroboration (multiple photographers, official confirmation, consistent observations across time) is ultimately more decisive than image-analysis heuristics alone.

The competing hypothesis — that the images are real but the bird was dyed deliberately by researchers — reflects a different kind of scepticism that Weidensaul (2025) specifically addressed: concerns from members of the public that Project SNOWStorm or other bird-banding researchers had dyed the bird during a capture event. Scientific marking of snowy owls does occur — telemetry devices are attached to captured individuals as part of Project SNOWStorm's tracking methodology — but the project explicitly denied involvement with this bird and correctly noted that the pattern of colouration does not correspond to any marking technique used in legitimate bird research.

Snopes has also previously fact-checked claims about blue owls, rainbow owls, and red owls — a category of viral content in which genuine photographs of normally-coloured owls are digitally manipulated to produce impossible plumage variants. The orange snowy owl is distinguished from these cases by the availability of multiple independent photographic sources and official corroboration — evidence thresholds that AI-generated and manipulated images cannot typically meet.


## 5. The Snowy Owl as a Species — Biological Context

The snowy owl (*Bubo scandiacus*) is the heaviest owl species found in North America and breeds primarily on Arctic tundra, ranging from Alaska across northern Canada and extending across similar latitudes in Eurasia. Its breeding biology involves boom-and-bust cycles closely tied to lemming population dynamics: in high-lemming years, clutches may number eight to twelve eggs and fledgling success is high; in low-lemming years, breeding may be skipped entirely (Kerlinger & Lein, 1986, "Differences in Winter Range Among Age-Sex Classes of Snowy Owls: A Hypothesis," *Ornis Scandinavica*, 17(2), 169–172; Robillard et al., 2016, "Relationship Between Breeding Effort and Body Condition for Snowy Owls Summering in the Low Arctic," *Auk: Ornithological Advances*, 133(4), 581–593).

Irruption events — in which large numbers of snowy owls appear far south of the Arctic zone during winter — are associated with periods of elevated lemming abundance that produce large juvenile cohorts, rather than (as was once believed) with food scarcity driving starving birds south. Analysis of irruption dynamics by Robillard et al. (2016) confirmed this revised understanding, which emerged from Project SNOWStorm telemetry data in the 2010s. Huron County, Michigan, is within the normal range of irrupting snowy owls in years with significant owl movements.

The plumage dimorphism of snowy owls is relevant to assessing the "Rusty" photographs. Adult males tend toward near-white plumage and adult females typically have more barring; the sex and age of the photographed individual has not been formally determined, though the density of dark markings in the original photographs (beneath the orange overlay) has been interpreted by some observers as suggesting a female or subadult individual. Melanism, leucism, and partial albinism are all documented avian conditions affecting melanin deposition; pheomelanin-dominant mutations are less commonly reported in raptors than in passerine species but are not biologically implausible.


## 6. Bottom Line

The photographs of the orange snowy owl from Huron County, Michigan are authentic. They have not been digitally manipulated and are not AI-generated. This conclusion rests on corroboration from multiple independent photographers, confirmation from a Michigan DNR spokesperson, review by an ornithologist associated with Project SNOWStorm, and the observation that the bird's anomalous colouration progressively faded across multiple separate photographic sessions — a temporal dynamic inconsistent with digital manipulation.

The cause of the orange colouration remains scientifically unresolved. The leading hypotheses — genetic pheomelanin mutation and chemical staining from a substance such as ferric oxide-based primer or wildfire retardant — are both biologically plausible, and the visual evidence and the reported distribution of colour on the bird's dorsal surface are insufficient to distinguish between them. Definitive resolution would require HPLC analysis of a feather sample, which does not appear to have been obtained before the bird departed the area.

The Snopes rating of **True** is accurate and appropriate for the specific claim being evaluated: that the images authentically show a snowy owl with unusual orange markings. The broader scientific question — what caused the markings — is accurately characterised by Snopes as unresolved, though the full depth of the competing hypotheses and what they would each require for confirmation represents more scientific content than the fact-check explores.

The case illustrates a recurrent feature of viral wildlife content in the current media environment: image authenticity and biological explanation are two separate questions that social media audiences tend to conflate. The photographs are real; the phenotype is genuinely anomalous; the mechanism is an open scientific question. All three of these things are true simultaneously.
