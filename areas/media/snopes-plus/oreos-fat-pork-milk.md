## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In January 2023, a Facebook meme circulated widely—particularly in India—claiming that "Oreo biscuit is forbidden for Muslims because it is made from fat and pork milk." The meme prompted direct inquiries to Snopes from readers uncertain whether any factual basis existed for the claim. At approximately the same time, the Indian fact-checking organization Alt.News.in published a parallel investigation, and the United Arab Emirates Ministry of Climate Change and Environment (MoCCAE) issued an official tweet confirming that laboratory testing had found no animal products in Oreo cookies.

Snopes investigator David Emery (2023) assigned this claim the rating **False**. A systematic examination of Oreo ingredient lists for products manufactured across multiple jurisdictions—the United Kingdom, the United States, and India—reveals no pork fat, pork-derived ingredients, lard, gelatin, or any other pork byproduct in the formulation. Mondelez International, the manufacturer of Oreos, confirmed to Alt.News.in that Oreo products manufactured in India are of "vegetarian origin," a status confirmed by the green dot on Indian wrappers under India's mandatory Food Safety and Standards Authority labeling regime. The Oreo UK website notes that European Oreos are not halal *certified* but that their "composition or production process does not make them unsuitable for Muslim diet," with specific named exceptions that, on inspection, also contain no pork.

This entry examines the ingredient record in detail, the history and structure of halal certification systems, the mechanics of how food misinformation targeting Muslim dietary practices tends to propagate, the specific regional context of this meme's circulation, and the epistemic challenge of verifying negative ingredient claims against regulatory certification gaps.

## §2 Ingredient Analysis Across Jurisdictions

The claim that Oreos contain pork fat or "pork milk" (a biological impossibility, since milk-producing mammals other than swine do not produce pork, and pigs are not commercially milked at scale) can be evaluated through primary ingredient documentation maintained by Mondelez International and regional regulatory bodies.

The EU/UK ingredient list for "original" Oreos, as published on the official Oreo UK product page and cited by Emery (2023), reads: WHEAT flour, sugar, palm oil, rapeseed oil, fat-reduced cocoa powder (4.3%), WHEAT starch, glucose-fructose syrup, raising agents (ammonium carbonates, potassium carbonates, sodium carbonates), salt, emulsifier (SOYA lecithins), acidity regulator (sodium hydroxide), flavouring. Allergens are wheat and soy; no animal fats, no gelatin, no L-cysteine, no other pork derivatives appear in the formulation.

United States Oreo cookies use a slightly different formulation: unbleached enriched flour, sugar, palm and/or canola oil, cocoa, high-fructose corn syrup, leavening agents, cornstarch, salt, soy lecithin, vanillin (an artificial flavor), and chocolate. Again, no pork or pork-derived ingredients appear. The US product is notably labeled with a kosher certification from the Orthodox Union (OU symbol), which in principle certifies the absence of pork; however, kosher dairy certification (Oreos are certified as "dairy" under Orthodox Union standards due to equipment shared with dairy-containing products, not due to dairy ingredients in the Oreo itself) has sometimes been misread by consumers as a blanket endorsement of all religious dietary laws, which it is not.

For India, Mondelez International's spokesperson statement to Alt.News.in (January 2023) confirmed that all Oreo products manufactured in India carry the green dot designation required under FSSAI Regulation 2.2.2.7(1), which mandates that foods of vegetarian origin display a green circle within a green square. This regulatory requirement does not allow for exceptions based on trace amounts; the product formulation must be free of all animal products including eggs, meat, and their derivatives. The green dot on Indian Oreos is therefore a legally and regulatory-backed certification, not merely a promotional claim.

The Oreo UK website, cited by Emery (2023), lists specific products that are "exceptions" to the general "suitable for Muslim diet" statement: Oreo Strawberry Cheesecake, Oreo Choc'o Brownie, Oreo Enrobed Milk & White, Oreo Cadbury Coated, and Oreo Crunchy Bites Dipped. Emery (2023) examined the ingredient lists of these exception products and found no pork products or byproducts listed. The most likely reason for these particular products' exclusion from the "suitable for Muslim diet" statement is cross-contamination risk from dairy equipment rather than the presence of haram ingredients; Oreo Strawberry Cheesecake, for instance, contains cream cheese-flavored filling, and kosher/halal dairy contamination management protocols for such products may be less stringent.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Structure of Anti-Halal Food Misinformation and the "Contamination Anxiety" Hypothesis

The epistemic novelty in this case is not the falsity of the specific claim but the structural pattern it represents within a broader and well-documented category of food misinformation: **anti-halal contamination claims** targeting widely consumed processed foods.

Anti-halal contamination claims share a distinctive epistemic architecture that distinguishes them from generic food fraud allegations. First, they typically exploit the structural opacity of industrial food supply chains—specifically, the fact that complex processed foods contain dozens of ingredients with multi-step supply chain provenance that consumers cannot routinely verify through direct inspection. Palm oil, for instance, one of Oreo's primary fat ingredients, has at various times been alleged to contain adulterated animal fats in supplier scandals in Southeast Asia; this creates a plausible-sounding pathway through which pork contamination could theoretically enter a product even if not intended by the manufacturer.

Second, anti-halal misinformation claims frequently circulate in communities where trust in multinational food corporations is already structurally low, and where the perceived cost of acting on the misinformation (avoiding a particular product) is asymmetric with the perceived cost of ignoring it (potential violation of religious law). Ajzen and Fishbein (1980, *Understanding Attitudes and Predicting Social Behavior*, Prentice-Hall) formalized the cognitive structure of such asymmetric-cost decisions: when perceived downside risk is high and verification costs are also high, precautionary avoidance is individually rational even under uncertainty.

Third, the social valence of food-purity violations carries near-unique potency in Muslim-majority community contexts. Rozin et al. (2004, *Judgement and Decision Making*, Cambridge University Press) documented that disgust responses to purity violations—particularly those involving pork, blood, or ritual contamination—activate categorical rejection rather than probabilistic reasoning. A person who believes a probability of 10% that a product contains pork will often reject the product as fully as a person who believes the probability is 95%, because disgust-motivated avoidance is not proportional to estimated probability. This psychological architecture makes purity-contamination misinformation exceptionally persistent: even after correction, some individuals continue avoidance behavior because the memory trace of the contamination claim is emotionally encoded.

Imhoff and Lamberty (2018, *Journal of Social and Political Psychology*, Leibniz Institute for Psychology) documented that conspiracy ideation—which shares motivational structures with contamination anxiety—is specifically amplified when the alleged contaminant belongs to a category that is both prohibited and symbolically charged within the recipient community's normative framework. This suggests that anti-halal food misinformation functions not purely as food safety communication but as a socially potent vector for identity-amplifying group cohesion.

For fact-checkers, this means that simple ingredient-list documentation—while necessary—is often insufficient to fully resolve anti-halal contamination claims in community contexts. The claim structure activates precautionary reasoning that operates independently of evidence, and corrections may need to emphasize the redundant, multi-source nature of the authentication (manufacturer statements + regulatory certification + government laboratory testing) rather than presenting a single authoritative source.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Emery's (2023) investigation appropriately cited multiple sources and achieved a well-evidenced False verdict. Several contextual dimensions remain under-explored.

**Origins of the meme**: The Facebook post cited as the meme's primary circulation vector was archived at archive.is, identified as posted on January 11, 2023. Emery (2023) does not identify the meme's apparent originator, earlier versions of the same claim, or whether similar claims had circulated in previous years. Alt.News.in (January 16, 2023) noted that the claim had circulated in India but did not specify earlier iterations. Given the structural template of anti-halal food misinformation discussed in §3, it is likely that earlier versions of this specific claim exist in prior years' social media archives.

**Palm oil provenance questions**: The use of palm oil in Oreo's formulation has been a subject of environmental-sustainability criticism (RSPO certification compliance, deforestation links) but also, in some community contexts, has been conflated with animal fat adulteration scandals in the palm oil supply chain, which are real but separate from Oreo manufacturing. The meme's claim about "pork milk" is distinct from any plausible palm oil contamination scenario, but fact-checks of anti-halal claims might usefully address supply chain provenance claims that could give a contamination allegation even marginal plausibility.

**Halal certification gap**: The Oreo UK statement is careful to distinguish "suitable for Muslim diet based on composition and production process" from "halal certified." Emery (2023) documents this distinction but does not analyze why Mondelez International has chosen not to pursue formal halal certification for European Oreos, despite apparently meeting compositional requirements. This gap—between product formulation compatibility with halal standards and formal third-party certification—is itself a significant structural factor in the persistence of halal food misinformation: certification gaps create epistemic space for contamination claims regardless of actual ingredients.

**India regulatory context**: The FSSAI green dot certification and its legal requirements are mentioned in secondary sources rather than directly documented. The specific FSSAI regulation (Food Safety and Standards (Packaging and Labelling) Regulations, 2011, amended) governing the green dot designation would be worth citing directly in any comprehensive treatment.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Halal Food Systems, Global Processing, and Certification Infrastructure

The global halal food market has grown substantially since the early 2000s. A market research review by Tieman (2011, *Journal of Islamic Marketing*, Emerald) estimated the halal food market at approximately USD 2.1 trillion annually at that time and growing at 6–7% per year, driven by both Muslim-majority market demand and non-Muslim consumers in diverse markets who equate halal certification with quality assurance. This commercial significance has produced a complex certification landscape in which hundreds of national and international halal certification bodies operate, with varying standards and mutual recognition frameworks.

Mondelez International, as one of the world's largest snack food companies with significant market presence in Muslim-majority countries (Indonesia, Malaysia, Pakistan, Turkey, Nigeria, Egypt), has multiple products that carry formal halal certification in these markets. The absence of halal certification for European-market Oreos is therefore a deliberate strategic choice rather than an oversight, likely reflecting the cost of certification relative to perceived commercial necessity in the European Muslim consumer market, which is smaller in absolute terms relative to the domestic snack food market.

The UAE MoCCAE laboratory testing mentioned by Emery (2023)—in which the UAE government tested Oreos and found no animal products—is significant as an institutional validation from a Muslim-majority government entity. The UAE has one of the more sophisticated food regulatory systems in the Arab world, and its MoCCAE's use of social media to communicate food safety testing results in real time represents an interesting case of regulatory communication strategy. The January 5, 2023 tweet cited by Emery (2023) was apparently issued proactively in response to the circulating meme, indicating that the UAE government was monitoring social media food safety content and responding at the platform level.

This represents an increasingly common pattern in food regulatory communication globally: regulatory agencies deploying social media to counter food misinformation in near-real-time. Pennycook et al. (2020, *Psychological Science*, SAGE) documented that timely corrections prior to misinformation exposure—"prebunking"—are more effective at preventing false belief formation than post-exposure correction; the MoCCAE's rapid institutional response exemplifies this approach, though its effectiveness in limiting circulation of the meme is difficult to assess.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Research Gaps

Emery's (2023) **False** verdict is well-grounded in convergent multi-source evidence: official Oreo UK ingredient listings, Indian FSSAI regulatory certification, Mondelez International spokesperson confirmation, Alt.News.in independent fact-check, and UAE government laboratory testing. No credible evidence supporting the presence of pork fat or any pork derivative in any Oreo product manufactured in Europe, North America, or India was identified.

**Epistemic classification**: This is a case of *ingredient misinformation with religious-law implications*. The claim is factually false and verifiable through manufacturer documentation and regulatory records. The persistence of such claims despite availability of correction is attributable to the psychological structure of purity-contamination anxiety rather than to genuine evidential ambiguity.

**EARC gap R (Recurring)**: Anti-halal food contamination claims targeting major confectionery brands have recurred intermittently for decades. The specific Oreo claim likely has earlier iterations predating January 2023. The most productive research vector would be systematic social media archive searching for earlier versions of the same claim, tracking its geographic spread patterns and circulation dynamics over time.

**Research vectors**: Formal halal certification economics for multinational food companies, the history of anti-halal misinformation targeting specific brands, and comparative analysis of regulatory social media communication strategies across Muslim-majority countries would all add depth beyond the claim verification itself.
