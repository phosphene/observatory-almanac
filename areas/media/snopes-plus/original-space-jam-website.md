## §1 — Claim and Verdict

The claim, which achieved viral prominence in March 2021 and recirculated when the *Space Jam: A New Legacy* (2021) promotional campaign launched, is factually precise: the original promotional website created for the 1996 Michael Jordan basketball comedy *Space Jam* survived intact and accessible at the URL structure `spacejam.com/1996` — a remarkable act of either corporate benevolence or institutional inertia depending on perspective —  for approximately 25 years.

Snopes rated the claim **True**, verified through direct navigation of the URL and secondary corroboration from a 2018 *Rolling Stone* investigation of the site's survival history. The fact-check was authored by Nur Ibrahim and published March 5, 2021, with an update on April 6, 2021, noting that the original URL was subsequently redirected to promote the sequel. (Ibrahim, Nur. "Does the Original Website for 1996 Film 'Space Jam' Still Work?" *Snopes.com*, 5 Mar. 2021, updated 6 Apr. 2021.)

The temporal precision matters: by April 2021, the "original" site had been substantially modified. The primary URL now promoted *Space Jam: A New Legacy*. The archived original site survives via the Wayback Machine at the Internet Archive, but the *live* claim — that `spacejam.com` serves 1990s content — ceased to be accurate following the trailer release for the sequel. The Snopes verdict of "True" is calibrated to the state of affairs as of the original publication date in March 2021.

## §2 — Primary Evidence and Source Audit

### The Website's Existence and Original Design

The *Space Jam* official website was created in 1996 as part of the film's marketing campaign, operated by Warner Bros. Pictures. It reflected the aesthetic conventions of mid-1990s commercial web design: frames-based layout, low-resolution JPEGs, animated GIFs, black background, and tiled navigational elements organized into sections for behind-the-scenes content, trivia, games, and promotional materials. The site included embedded video content (in formats appropriate to 1996 bandwidth constraints), a layout that did not resize for modern screen dimensions, and a merchandise redirect to the Warner Bros. shop (wbshop.com). The site was functionally dormant — not updated with new content — but all navigational elements remained operational as of the Snopes investigation.

A Reddit post from approximately 2011 in the subreddit r/todayilearned ("TIL that the Space Jam website is still up and running," linked in the Snopes piece) was among the earliest documented instances of the site's persistence attracting public attention. The link was still live at the time of link verification in that thread, establishing a minimum confirmed survival window of at least ten years from approximately 2011.

### The Rolling Stone Investigation (2018)

A 2018 *Rolling Stone* investigation — archived at archive.li — provided the most detailed account of the site's survival history and institutional context. Key findings from that investigation, cited in the Snopes article:

1. The site's development team said that when they created it, the project received no meaningful attention from Warner Bros. corporate leadership: **"Nobody gave a shit."** This bureaucratic indifference appears to have been protective — no one ordered its removal because no one was paying attention to it.

2. The site underwent minor modifications over time, including a period circa 2003 when it was briefly redirected to a scaled-down e-commerce page selling only DVDs. However, it was restored to something approximating its original form.

3. When news of the site's survival went viral in 2010 — an earlier wave of attention predating the 2021 wave — Warner Bros. reportedly **shut the site down for a few hours** while internal discussion took place, before restoring it based on requests from within the company.

4. The site's persistence into the 2010s was attributed in part to **advocacy from original developers** who lobbied internally for its preservation. The site had become an artifact they wished to protect.

### April 2021 Update

Following the April 2021 trailer release for *Space Jam: A New Legacy* (released July 16, 2021), the primary `spacejam.com` URL was updated to serve promotional content for the sequel, eliminating the 1990s-era homepage experience. The historical content was relocated to `spacejam.com/1996`, where it remained accessible. Snopes updated its article on April 6, 2021, to note this change.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: Corporate Legacy Website Survival as Unintentional Digital Preservation

The *Space Jam* website is frequently cited as one of the most famous surviving examples of 1990s-era commercial web design, but its preservation has never been studied as a case study in institutional decision-making about digital heritage. This is a genuine scholarly gap.

### Digital Preservation Studies and the Corporate Sector

The academic field of digital preservation is well established and has produced a rich literature on archival methodology, format migration, and institutional practices (Lavoie, Brian, and Lorcan Dempsey. "Thirteen Ways of Looking at Digital Preservation." *D-Lib Magazine*, vol. 10, no. 7/8, July/Aug. 2004). However, the literature overwhelmingly focuses on library, archival, and governmental institutions — the traditional stewards of cultural memory. Corporate digital preservation is significantly undertheorized.

Corporate websites from the 1995–2005 period represent an enormous body of cultural and commercial history that is being lost at scale as URLs are killed, domains expire, and hosting is terminated when films age out of active marketing relevance. The *Space Jam* site's survival is anomalous precisely because most comparable corporate promotional websites from the same era do not survive in any form; they must be reconstructed from Wayback Machine snapshots, if they were captured at all.

### The Accidental Archive: Bureaucratic Protection as Preservation Mechanism

The Rolling Stone investigation's finding — "nobody gave a shit" — reveals an underappreciated dynamic in organizational behavior and cultural preservation: **bureaucratic neglect can function as an inadvertent conservation mechanism**. In a large media corporation, active decisions require resource allocation, management attention, and risk assessment. Actively shutting down a legacy website requires someone to identify it as a candidate for removal, obtain authorization, and execute the action. Leaving it alone requires nothing.

This dynamic has some structural parallels with what sociologist of knowledge Barry Schwartz described as the "social frameworks" of collective memory (Schwartz, Barry. "The Social Context of Commemoration: A Study in Collective Memory." *Social Forces*, vol. 61, no. 2, 1982, pp. 374–402) — memory is preserved not only through deliberate archival action but through the structure of institutional inertia. Physical archives survive fires and floods partly through luck; digital archives survive corporate restructurings partly through administrative invisibility.

**The epistemic gap** this case exposes is the absence of empirical study documenting why specific early-web corporate properties survive while comparable ones do not. A systematic survey comparing the survival rates of commercial promotional websites from 1995–2005 by studio, content type, and whether the associated film had sequel activity — and cross-referencing survival against institutional factors like team advocacy within the organization — does not exist in the digital preservation, information science, or media industry literature as of 2026.

The *Space Jam* case suggests several testable hypotheses:
- Developer/creator advocacy significantly predicts survival of legacy web properties.
- Viral cultural rediscovery creates institutional incentive to preserve (as seen in the 2010 near-shutdown followed by restoration under internal pressure).
- Franchise sequelization creates pressure that ultimately modifies or migrates the original site.

All three hypotheses are supported by the available evidence but remain unstudied as generalizable claims. This represents the primary epistemic novelty assigned to this entry.

### The 1990s Web as Cultural Artifact

The *Space Jam* website's fame is inseparable from the nostalgia it activates. Web design conventions from the mid-1990s — frames, tables, animated GIFs, Netscape-era font choices, MIDI audio — are now recognized as period markers in a way that parallels how typography and paper stock mark printed ephemera from earlier eras. The site functions as a working **time capsule**, a phrase used literally by journalist commentators covering the site's viral moments.

Scholars of material culture have noted that everyday commercial objects acquire heritage value through temporal distance (Lowenthal, David. *The Past Is a Foreign Country*. Cambridge University Press, 1985). The web equivalent of this process — the transformation of a functional commercial site into a heritage artifact — has occurred rapidly compared to physical media, in part because the web's history is so compressed (the commercial web is barely 30 years old) and in part because its design conventions have changed with extraordinary speed, making 1996-era design *immediately* legible as historical.

## §4 — Contextual Frame: *Space Jam* (1996) and the Cultural Moment

*Space Jam* was produced by Warner Bros. and released November 15, 1996. It grossed approximately $90 million domestically against a reported production budget of $80 million — modest commercial performance by the standards of an event film, but its ancillary revenue through soundtrack (which included R. Kelly's "I Believe I Can Fly" and contributions from Seal, Coolio, and others), merchandise, and home video substantially extended its commercial life. The soundtrack album certified 4× Platinum by the RIAA.

The film's cultural position has been unusual: critically unexceptional at release but enormously fondly remembered by the cohort of children who were its primary audience in 1996–1997. This nostalgic valence — intensified rather than diminished over time, especially as Michael Jordan's cultural mythology crystallized — gives the film and its artifacts, including the website, an emotional charge that a straightforwardly contemporary evaluation would not predict.

The website's viral moments (2010, 2021) both occurred in contexts saturated with public Jordan nostalgia: the 2010 wave coincided with Jordan's Hall of Fame induction, and the 2021 wave coincided with the promotional rollout of *Space Jam: A New Legacy*, which explicitly repositioned *Space Jam* (1996) as a heritage object worthy of a 25th-anniversary sequel.

## §5 — The Sequel's Effect: How Sequelization Changes Digital Heritage

One of the most interesting dynamics the *Space Jam* website case illustrates is the **sequelization effect on digital heritage**. When *Space Jam: A New Legacy* went into production and marketing, Warner Bros. faced a structural decision: allow the original promotional site to coexist with the sequel's promotional infrastructure, modify it, integrate it, or replace it.

The solution — migrating the original content to the `/1996/` subdirectory while updating the root URL to promote the sequel — represents a compromise between commercial imperatives (leverage the `spacejam.com` domain value for the sequel) and heritage acknowledgment (preserve the original in a findable location). It is not a perfect solution from a preservationist standpoint: the root URL no longer surfaces the 1990s-era experience, and users who simply navigate to `spacejam.com` get the sequel promotional site.

This case is structurally comparable to how physical media companies handle legacy catalog material when new editions are released — consider the treatment of first-edition book content in anniversary reprints, or the mastering decisions for reissued vinyl records. In each case, the commercial imperative of the new product must be balanced against the heritage claims of the original. The web adds the complication that the original URL carries its own cultural meaning: `spacejam.com` is not just an address but a reference that people have been citing and sharing for 25 years.

## §6 — Summary and Knowledge Status

| Dimension | Assessment |
|---|---|
| **Core claim status** | True (as of March 2021); site content migrated to `/1996/` subdirectory from April 2021 |
| **Primary evidence** | Direct verification by Snopes; confirmed via Reddit posts (2011+); *Rolling Stone* 2018 investigation |
| **Survival mechanism** | Developer advocacy; corporate bureaucratic neglect; viral cultural rediscovery in 2010 and 2021 |
| **Sequel effect** | Root URL updated April 2021 to promote *Space Jam: A New Legacy*; original content preserved at `/1996/` |
| **Design significance** | One of the most prominent surviving examples of mid-1990s commercial web aesthetics |
| **Epistemic gap** | No empirical literature on survival rates of corporate promotional websites from 1995–2005 or the institutional factors predicting preservation |
| **EARC classification** | C (Confirmed; temporal modifier: fully accurate at March 2021 publication, partially accurate thereafter) |

The *Space Jam* website is simultaneously a trivial piece of film marketing, an accidental digital heritage artifact, and a case study in how corporate bureaucratic structures interact with cultural memory. Its survival for 25 years, its near-extinction in 2010, its developer-sponsored resurrection, and its eventual modification by sequel marketing represent a compressed but illuminating history of how the early commercial web relates to institutional memory. The underlying preservation dynamics remain understudied and offer a genuine empirical research opportunity.
