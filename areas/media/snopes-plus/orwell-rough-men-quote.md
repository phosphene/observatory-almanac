# Did Orwell write, 'People sleep peaceably' because 'rough men stand ready to do violence on their behalf'?


## 1. The Claim

"We sleep safely in our beds because rough men stand ready in the night to visit violence on those who would do us harm." Or the variant "People sleep peaceably in their beds at night only because rough men stand ready to do violence on their behalf." In either form, this quotation is one of the most frequently invoked justifications for state-sanctioned violence and the armed professions. It is routinely attributed to George Orwell, author of "1984" and "Animal Farm," two of the most influential political novels of the 20th century.

The claim appears in multiple contexts. Police officers, military personnel, and border security advocates have used the quote to frame their work as a necessary, often thankless protection of civilian peace. Politicians quote it when arguing for increased defense spending or tougher law enforcement. Social media users share it as a pithy rebuke to pacifism. It has appeared on posters in police stations, in political speeches from across the ideological spectrum, and in countless online arguments about the legitimate use of force.

The quote's association with Orwell is not accidental. Orwell wrote extensively about politics, violence, nationalism, and the state's monopoly on force. His essay "Notes on Nationalism" (1945) contains the line: "Those who 'abjure' violence can only do so because others are committing violence on their behalf." This genuine Orwell statement is thematically related to the "rough men" quote, which helps explain why the misattribution has been so persistent. The misattributed line sounds like something Orwell could have written, even though he did not write it.

The claim received renewed attention in March 2026, when then-U.S. Department of Homeland Security Secretary Kristi Noem quoted it during a public address. Noem correctly noted that the quote is "often attributed to George Orwell," but her mention of the author's name in the context of a speech at a lectern flanked by American flags nonetheless reinforced the connection for many viewers. The timing was notable: Noem had been fired by President Trump just before that speech and was delivering it on what she described as her last days as DHS secretary.

## 2. What's Actually True

The "rough men" quote does not appear in any of George Orwell's published works. Despite extensive searching by quotation researchers, including the highly credible Garson O'Toole of Quote Investigator, no one has located the phrase in Orwell's essays, novels, letters, journalism, or diaries. The earliest known appearance of the exact quote in print does not occur until 1993—43 years after Orwell's death.

The 1993 source was a Washington Times article by Richard Grenier, titled "Perils of Passive Sex." In this article, Grenier wrote: "As George Orwell pointed out, people sleep peacefully in their beds at night only because rough men stand ready to do violence on their behalf." Grenier was not claiming to quote Orwell directly; he was offering a compact summary of what he understood to be Orwell's position. Crucially, as O'Toole noted, "There were no quotation marks in this passage because Grenier was not quoting Orwell. Instead, he was presenting a compact statement of the stance that he assigned to Orwell."

The transition from paraphrase to direct quote happened through the familiar mechanism of online citation decay. Readers of Grenier's article, or subsequent references to it, assumed that the line was a direct quotation rather than a summary. They republished it with quotation marks, attributed it to Orwell, and the misattribution became self-reinforcing.

A variant version of the quote—"We sleep soundly in our beds because rough men stand ready in the night to visit violence on those who would do us harm"—first appeared in print in 2003, in The New York Times Magazine. That publication attributed it to Orwell, further cementing the connection in the public mind.

An even earlier appearance, from 1998, in the Flint Journal of Michigan, ran: "It's been said that we 'civilized' types can sleep soundly in our beds because rough men stand ready to do violence on our behalf." Significantly, this version did not attribute the quote to Orwell, suggesting that the quote was circulated as anonymous folk wisdom before it was attached to a famous name.

What Orwell actually wrote on the subject is genuinely relevant. In "Notes on Nationalism," he wrote: "Those who 'abjure' violence can only do so because others are committing violence on their behalf." This is the closest Orwell came to expressing the idea contained in the "rough men" quote. He also wrote in his essay "The Prevention of Literature": "The people who have 'abolished' war are those who have abandoned the will to defend themselves, and are therefore at the mercy of their enemies." These genuine Orwell statements show that he was thinking along similar lines, which is why the misattribution is plausible but also why it matters to get it right.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes's fact-check, published March 10, 2026, by senior reporter Jordan Liles, is a careful and well-sourced investigation. The article correctly rates the claim as "Incorrect Attribution" and provides both the historical context of the quote's emergence and the specific details of Kristi Noem's speech that prompted the renewed interest.

The Snopes article draws on O'Toole's Quote Investigator research, which is the definitive treatment of this quotation. O'Toole's 2011 investigation traced the quote to the 1993 Washington Times article and explained the paraphrase-to-direct-quote transition clearly. Snopes corroborates this through newspaper archive searches and notes the 1998 unattributed appearance and the 2003 New York Times Magazine appearance.

The article also helpfully provides the genuine Orwell quotation from "Notes on Nationalism," allowing readers to compare the actual with the attributed. This is a valuable service: it addresses the likely objection that "Orwell said something like that, so what's the harm?" by showing what Orwell actually wrote and why the difference matters.

Where Snopes's treatment could be strengthened is in several areas. First, the political context of the quote's use deserves deeper analysis. The "rough men" quote is not a neutral observation; it is a political argument, deployed to justify state violence and to delegitimize pacifism. Understanding why people want to attribute it to Orwell—and what it would mean if he had actually written it—would enrich the analysis. Orwell's actual political positions, including his evolving views on pacifism during and after World War II, are more complex than the "rough men" quote suggests. Orwell was both a participant in armed struggle (fighting with the POUM militia in the Spanish Civil War) and a fierce critic of state violence and imperialism. He was not the uncomplicated defender of establishment force that the "rough men" quote would imply.

Moreover, the quote's appeal across the political spectrum deserves comment. While it is most commonly used by conservative hawks and law-and-order advocates, it has also been adopted by libertarians, anarchists, and even some progressive gun rights advocates. The quote is ideologically promiscuous because it validates a general principle—that legitimate violence undergirds peaceful society—that can be applied to any number of specific political projects. A quote that works equally well for a border patrol agent, a police union advocate, and a Second Amendment absolutist is a quote that has been stripped of any particular political content.

Second, the Snopes article does not explore the quote's function as a rhetorical trump card in online debates. The quote is often used to end discussions rather than advance them, serving as a conversation stopper that invokes Orwell's authority to silence dissent. This rhetorical function is part of why the misattribution persists: the quote is useful precisely because it sounds authoritative.

Third, the article's treatment of the Noem context is mostly descriptive rather than analytical. It notes that Noem correctly said the quote was "often attributed" to Orwell, but does not examine whether a public official has a responsibility to go further than that. When a person in a position of authority invokes a misattributed quotation in support of policy positions, the correction has real political stakes.

## 4. Why This Matters

The "rough men" quote matters because it is not merely an error of attribution—it is an error that shapes how people think about violence, security, and the state. The quote's political content makes its misattribution more consequential than the average misquotation.

First, the quote simplifies and distorts Orwell's actual political thought. Orwell was a complex figure who fought in the Spanish Civil War, was wounded by a fascist sniper, and wrote with brutal honesty about violence and its costs. But he was also a critic of imperial violence, a supporter of democratic socialism, and a writer who understood that the relationship between security and freedom is fraught. The "rough men" quote, with its uncomplicated endorsement of protective violence, flattens Orwell's thought into a bumper sticker. Readers who believe Orwell endorsed this view may miss the nuance of his actual positions.

Second, the quote functions as a rhetorical shield. When invoked, it implies that questioning the use of force is naive or ungrateful—that the person raising questions is a beneficiary of violence who refuses to acknowledge it. This rhetorical move is powerful precisely because it forecloses debate. The misattribution gives it the force of Orwell's cultural authority.

Third, the quote illustrates how misattributions can track political need. The quote became prominent in the 1990s and has resurged periodically when debates about state violence, policing, or military action are in the headlines. Each resurgence reflects a political moment when the argument that "violence is necessary" needs cultural reinforcement. The quote is useful to those making that argument, and its usefulness drives its spread.

Fourth, the Noem incident is a reminder that misattributed quotations have real public policy implications. When a high-ranking official invokes Orwell's authority to justify immigration enforcement or homeland security operations, the misattribution lends false weight to potentially contested policies. The correction matters not as pedantry but as a check on the unauthorized use of cultural authority.

For the Truth Vault, this entry participates in a larger project of documenting how quotations are weaponized in political discourse. The "rough men" quote is one of a family of misattributed political quotations—along with versions of Orwell's "Big Brother" and "thought police"—that shape public understanding of his work.

A fifth dimension is the quote's relationship to the broader American culture of violence and the mythology of the "good guy with a gun." The "rough men" quote resonates so deeply in American political discourse because it gives intellectual cover to the belief that violence, when wielded by the right people (the "rough men" who stand ready), is not only necessary but noble. This belief manifests in support for expanded policing, militarized border enforcement, permissive gun laws, and aggressive foreign policy. The quote provides a shortcut past the difficult moral questions about when violence is justified, who gets to wield it, and how to hold its wielders accountable.

Sixth, the quote's persistence illuminates the relationship between misattribution and cultural authority. Orwell is invoked not because his actual views on violence are widely known but because his name carries a specific cultural weight: he is the thinker who warned about totalitarianism, the defender of plain language against political manipulation, the intellectual who fought fascism in Spain. By attaching the "rough men" quote to Orwell, users of the quote borrow all of this cultural capital while avoiding engagement with what Orwell actually believed. This is misattribution as a form of intellectual appropriation.

## 5. Research Notes

The definitive treatment of this quotation is Garson O'Toole's 2011 article for Quote Investigator, updated periodically since. O'Toole's method—systematic searching of newspaper archives, book databases, and periodical indexes—is the gold standard for quotation investigation.

The 1993 Washington Times article by Richard Grenier is identified in O'Toole's research but is not freely available online. Snopes was also unable to locate a publicly accessible version. This is a limitation of the available evidence, though O'Toole's citation is credible and consistent with the chain of transmission he documents.

The 2003 New York Times Magazine appearance is confirmed through Google Books. The magazine, in a context O'Toole describes as an article about "reasons for the invasion of Iraq," published the variant version of the quote and attributed it to Orwell. This is significant because the New York Times Magazine is an influential publication, and its attribution added a layer of authority to the misquotation.

The 1998 Flint Journal article is on file at Newspapers.com. Its phrasing "It's been said that we 'civilized' types can sleep soundly" is notable for its lack of attribution to any specific source, suggesting the quote was already circulating as unattributed folk wisdom before it became attached to Orwell.

Orwell's "Notes on Nationalism" (1945) is available through the Orwell Foundation website and in collected editions of Orwell's essays. The essay is primarily concerned with how nationalist thinking distorts political judgment, and the line about those who "abjure" violence is made in the context of a broader argument about intellectual dishonesty.

For those interested in the broader phenomenon, the Quote Investigator website contains investigations of hundreds of misattributed quotations. O'Toole's book "Hemingway Didn't Say That" collects the most notable investigations and explains the methodology.

A note on methodology: tracing the provenance of a political quotation requires different techniques than tracing a scientific or literary one. Political quotations often circulate through speeches, congressional records, political memos, and advocacy materials before appearing in mainstream media. The 1993 Grenier article in the Washington Times is a conservative opinion outlet, and the quote's appearance there is consistent with its political valence. Future researchers might search the archives of the Heritage Foundation, Cato Institute, and other think tanks that produce the kind of policy advocacy where such quotes often first appear in political discourse.

The New York Times Magazine's 2003 appearance of the quote deserves additional scrutiny. Published during the buildup to the Iraq War, the article that included the Orwell attribution was part of a broader media ecosystem debating the legitimacy of the invasion. The quote's reappearance in this context is revealing: it provided a tidy justification for military action, framed not as a contemporary political argument but as timeless wisdom from a canonical author. The war context is not incidental to the quote's function.

## 6. Verdict

The claim that George Orwell wrote "People sleep peaceably in their beds at night only because rough men stand ready to do violence on their behalf" is **incorrectly attributed**. Extensive research has failed to locate this phrase in any of Orwell's published works. The earliest known appearance of the exact quote in print was a 1993 newspaper column that presented it as a summary of Orwell's views, not a direct quotation.

The confusion is understandable. Orwell did write in "Notes on Nationalism" that "those who 'abjure' violence can only do so because others are committing violence on their behalf," which expresses a similar idea with different words. This genuine quotation has been conflated with the "rough men" formulation over decades of citation decay.

The quote's ongoing circulation serves identifiable political purposes: it provides a succinct, authoritative-sounding justification for state violence and a rhetorical weapon against pacifism. Understanding why the quote is so persistently attributed to Orwell requires attention not just to the historical record but to the political work the quote performs. The correction matters because the stakes of the argument are high—and because Orwell's actual thoughts on violence, the state, and security are more complex and more valuable than the slogan attributed to him.
