## The Claim

Beginning in the spring of 2024 and accelerating through the second half of the year, social media users spread a claim that Ovia — a brand producing a suite of reproductive health tracking apps — requires users to provide their country and state of residence before they can access the app's services. The claim appeared on multiple platforms: a September 2024 Reddit post in r/WitchesVsPatriarchy titled "My ovulation tracker suddenly asked what state I live in" received 14,000 upvotes; an August 2024 Reddit post in r/BabyBumps titled "Ovia app required my country/state" generated substantial discussion; an X post from December 2024 by the user @NineInchClawz attracted significant attention; and multiple Facebook posts in the same period warned followers about the requirement.

There are three distinct apps under the Ovia brand, produced by Ovia Health (operating under Ovuline, Inc.): Ovia Fertility, Cycle & Health (a fertility, ovulation, and menstruation tracker); Ovia Pregnancy & Baby Tracker; and Ovia Parenting & Baby Tracker (an infant care app). Not all social media posts specified which of the three apps required location input; some posts referred to a single app, others implied the requirement was common to all Ovia products.

The specific motivation prompting the claims was the post-Dobbs environment: the Supreme Court's June 2022 decision in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* eliminated the federal constitutional right to abortion established by *Roe v. Wade* (1973), returning abortion regulation to individual states. In the resulting environment, a number of states enacted strict abortion restrictions or outright bans. Users collecting reproductive health data on apps located in or near these states expressed concern that their geolocation data could be subpoenaed as evidence in prosecutions related to abortion, miscarriage investigation, or related reproductive health events.

## What's Actually True

**The location requirement is real and applies to all three apps.** Snopes journalist Rae Deng downloaded all three Ovia apps and confirmed through direct testing that none of the three can be used past the basic information page without providing country of residence, and — if the user selects the United States — state of residence as well. These fields are marked as required; they cannot be skipped. This is straightforward reporter verification: the claim was tested by directly attempting to use the product.

**The privacy policy explicitly states data collection.** Ovia Health's privacy policy, last updated June 28, 2024, states that the company collects geolocation data from users, described as "approximate location by country, state, city, zipcode and regional area." This is explicit, not buried: it is the type of data disclosure the privacy policy format exists to communicate, and it is reasonably accessible to any user who reviews the policy before downloading or using the app.

**Ovia's stated justification.** The app's interface explains to users that it collects country and state information to ensure compliance with local privacy laws and to "further protect your data." The reference here is to the expansion of state-level data privacy legislation: as of 2024, a significant number of states have passed or were actively considering consumer data privacy laws with varying requirements. An app with users in multiple jurisdictions faces genuinely complex compliance obligations that differ by state, and collecting location data is one operational method by which to segment users for jurisdiction-specific compliance treatment. This explanation is plausible and probably accurate as one motivation for the requirement.

**The broader privacy risk is genuine and expert-confirmed.** The concern that prompted most of the social media activity — that Ovia's location data could be subpoenaed in abortion-related prosecutions in restrictive states — is not speculation or paranoia. It reflects a realistic assessment of legal risk that privacy experts and civil liberties organisations have analysed carefully in the post-Dobbs environment. Jake Laperruque, deputy director of The Center For Democracy and Technology's Security and Surveillance Project, confirmed to Snopes that his organisation is "unaware of any publicly reported instances to date in which period tracker data has been subpoenaed." However, he explicitly noted it is possible that instances exist that have not received public attention or that are subject to nondisclosure orders. NPR's 2022 coverage following the *Dobbs* decision raised the same risk in general terms, as did a July 2024 Stateline report on data privacy in the post-Dobbs environment, which noted that the absence of reported subpoenas was "probably due to the slow speed of which cases proceed through the court system."

The legal architecture for such subpoenas exists. State prosecutors investigating abortion-related conduct could, in principle, seek subpoenas for app data through conventional legal process. App companies that collect geolocation data — particularly those that operate under terms of service acknowledging data may be shared with law enforcement pursuant to legal process — cannot guarantee protection from this mechanism. The Electronic Frontier Foundation, the ACLU, and other digital civil liberties organisations have consistently recommended that users in states with abortion restrictions minimise the reproductive health data they store in cloud-connected apps for exactly this reason.

## Why People Believe This

The rapid spread of this claim — and the intensity of user concern it generated — reflects several converging factors specific to the post-Dobbs reproductive rights environment and to broader patterns of digital privacy awareness.

**The Dobbs shift and heightened reproductive data sensitivity.** Before *Dobbs*, concerns about reproductive health app data were real but relatively low-stakes for most users: the primary risks were data commercialisation, targeted advertising, and employer data access. After *Dobbs*, state-level criminalisation of abortion introduced a qualitatively different category of risk — criminal jeopardy. This transformed the salience of reproductive data collection for millions of users who had previously been comfortable using period and fertility tracking apps without particular concern. The shift from privacy-as-commercial-risk to privacy-as-criminal-risk is not merely quantitative; it changes the meaning of the data being collected and the significance of the entities that might access it.

**Digital privacy awareness and app literacy.** The claims about Ovia emerged from an environment of increasing public literacy about app data collection practices. The exposure of Facebook's data-sharing with Cambridge Analytica (2018), the broader attention to GDPR compliance in the EU, and the expansion of state-level privacy legislation in the US had all contributed to a more suspicious and attentive user base for app data practices. Users who encountered the Ovia location requirement in 2024 were more likely than users in 2020 would have been to flag it as noteworthy and potentially concerning, to share it as a public warning, and to frame it as a privacy risk rather than a neutral onboarding step.

**Epistemic novelty: The distinction between "true" and "sufficient reason for concern."** The most epistemically significant feature of this case is that the factual claim is confirmed true, and the concern motivating its spread is confirmed by expert assessment as a genuine risk — yet as of December 2024, no harm had materialised in the form of a confirmed subpoena or prosecution. The fact-check therefore confirms both the truth of the underlying claim and the reality of the risk driving concern about it, while also noting the absence of confirmed harmful incidents. This creates a specific epistemic structure: the claim is true, the concern is legitimate, but the anticipated harm remains in the future-tense. Users sharing the claim are not misinformed — they are correctly identifying a risk — but the intensity of alarm may be calibrated to a harm that has not yet occurred and may not occur in its most feared form.

This epistemic structure has important implications for how the public should interpret verified reproductive privacy concerns in the post-Dobbs environment. Confirmed true claims about data collection practices (Ovia collects location data) and genuinely informed regulatory risk assessments (this data could theoretically be subpoenaed) are distinct from confirmed harms (this data has been subpoenaed and used to prosecute someone). All three levels of information are relevant to user decision-making, and collapsing them — treating the presence of data collection as equivalent to confirmed prosecutorial use — would be an overreach. Maintaining all three levels of clarity simultaneously is what responsible communication about digital reproductive privacy requires.

**The app's user base and the specific prompt timing.** The Reddit post that received 14,000 upvotes framed the issue as a surprise: "My ovulation tracker suddenly asked what state I live in." This framing of sudden, unexpected appearance is revealing: either the user had been using the app before this requirement was added (suggesting an update that introduced the location requirement) or the user encountered the requirement on a reinstallation or new device setup (where the onboarding flow is encountered fresh). In either case, the framing of surprise suggests a prior experience without the requirement. If Ovia updated its apps around mid-2024 to make location a required field, this would explain both the timing of the viral claims and the surprise framing: established users encountered a new requirement that had not been present during their prior use.

---

## Verdict

**True.** All three Ovia apps — fertility and cycle tracking, pregnancy tracking, and infant care tracking — do require users to provide their country of residence, and if US is selected, their state of residence, as mandatory fields before accessing the apps' services. Ovia Health's privacy policy explicitly states collection of approximate geolocation data. The concern that this data could be sought through legal process in abortion-restrictive states is a genuine risk endorsed by expert privacy assessment, though no confirmed instances of menstrual tracking app data being subpoenaed had been publicly reported as of December 2024. Snopes rated this claim **True**.

## The Wider Picture

The Ovia location requirement is a specific instance of a much wider set of intersecting questions about reproductive health data, state-level legal frameworks, app commercial practices, and the political environment created by the Supreme Court's *Dobbs* decision. Each of these contexts is substantive.

**The legal landscape post-Dobbs.** The *Dobbs* decision in June 2022 overturned the constitutional abortion rights framework that had been in place since *Roe v. Wade* (1973) and *Planned Parenthood v. Casey* (1992). The decision returned abortion regulation to individual states, producing an immediate and rapidly evolving patchwork of state laws ranging from full bans (effective the day of the decision in trigger-law states) to relatively permissive frameworks. As of 2024, over a dozen states have complete or near-complete abortion bans in effect. The prosecutorial and legal risks these laws create extend beyond abortion providers to anyone who aids, abets, or assists in obtaining an abortion — a broad category that in principle could include those who share information about accessing abortion services out of state or who document their own reproductive health history in ways that could be interpreted as relevant evidence.

The specific legal theories by which period-tracker app data could be relevant to prosecution include: establishing pregnancy at a specific time (for gestational age calculations in abortion prohibition contexts); establishing location (connecting a user to a state where an abortion occurred); and documenting reproductive health status for miscarriage investigations in states where self-managed abortion and miscarriage may be subject to prosecutorial scrutiny. Laws on the books in several states on concepts like "chemical endangerment of a child" have been applied in ways that privacy and reproductive rights advocates argue create risk for anyone documenting pregnancy and reproductive health.

**The period tracker privacy response.** Following *Dobbs*, several period-tracking app companies made public commitments about user data protection. Some announced deletion of stored user data; others changed data retention policies; others introduced features designed to minimise stored information. Ovia's decision to require location data — and to store it by their own disclosure — has thus been interpreted by many users as a policy choice that runs counter to the data minimisation approach some user communities have advocated for in the post-Dobbs environment. Whether this is a reasonable reading of Ovia's approach depends on the full context of their privacy architecture, which includes statements about not selling personal health information for commercial purposes (per their privacy policy), but Ovia's choices sit within a crowded field of competing approaches that users must evaluate and compare.

**US health data law gaps.** The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), the primary US federal framework for health data privacy, does not apply to health apps unless they handle data covered under a specific covered entity or business associate relationship. Consumer-facing apps like Ovia are generally not HIPAA-covered entities; their data is governed by their own privacy policies, app store terms of service, and applicable state law. This creates a significant protection gap: the data that users intuitively believe to be protected by HIPAA — detailed reproductive health history stored in a health category app — is not, in fact, subject to HIPAA's legal protections. The Federal Trade Commission has pursued enforcement actions related to health app data under its unfair and deceptive practices authority, but this is narrower protection. The structural gap between HIPAA coverage and consumer health app reality is a documented legislative issue that researchers in health law and privacy have identified as requiring legislative attention (Cohen, Mello, 2018, *JAMA*).

**State privacy law patchwork.** The National Conference of State Legislatures has tracked a wave of consumer data privacy legislation at the state level, with multiple states passing their own comprehensive privacy laws. The Ovia app's statement that it collects state and country information partly to comply with local privacy laws reflects the compliance burden of operating under this patchwork. Different states have different definitions of sensitive health information, different opt-out rights, different breach notification requirements, and different restrictions on data sharing. An app serving users in California (with the California Consumer Privacy Act), Virginia (Consumer Data Protection Act), Texas, Florida, and states without comprehensive privacy laws faces materially different obligations in each jurisdiction. Collecting state information is operationally necessary for any app attempting to implement differentiated privacy protections.

**Reproductive health data and the commercial ecosystem.** Beyond prosecutorial risk, reproductive health data has commercial value that operates independently of any legal risk. Fertility tracking data, pregnancy status data, and related reproductive health information are commercially valuable to insurers, employers, marketers, and healthcare providers. Research by the Future of Privacy Forum and EFF on health app data monetisation has documented a range of practices by which health data collected by consumer apps flows through data brokers and into commercial uses. Ovia Health's privacy policy states a commitment not to sell personal health information, but the broader commercial ecosystem in which reproductive health apps operate includes companies that do monetise this data. Users concerned about the combination of location data and reproductive health status are responding not only to prosecutorial risk but to the full commercial surveillance context.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**The direct testing method.** The most straightforward and definitive element of this fact-check is Rae Deng's direct download and testing of all three Ovia apps. This approach converts a claim that could in principle be contested (maybe the requirement is optional, maybe only one app requires it, maybe it varies by device or operating system) into a directly verified finding. The testing established the requirement unambiguously across all three apps.

**The privacy policy citation.** The fact-check cites Ovia Health's privacy policy directly, including the specific language about geolocation data collection. This documentation approach is valuable both for transparency (the reader can verify the same language) and for durability (the policy language becomes the documented fact, not merely the journalist's assertion).

**The expert source on risk.** The inclusion of Jake Laperruque's assessment from the Center For Democracy and Technology provides expert endorsement of the risk framing while also qualifying it: the risk is real and not yet materialised in publicly confirmed harmful incidents. This dual framing — risk is confirmed, harm is not yet confirmed — is epistemically accurate and avoids both the error of dismissing the concern (the harm is real, even if not yet documented) and the error of overstating it (there are no confirmed prosecutions driven by this data as of the fact-check's writing).

**The scope of the verdict.** The Snopes rating of "True" covers the specific claim (location data is required) rather than the broader concern (this creates prosecutorial risk). The contextual sections of the article extend into the risk analysis, but the verdict is appropriately scoped to what has been directly confirmed. This is correct fact-checking practice: confirm what can be confirmed, contextualise what requires analysis and expert judgment. The distinction between "this is confirmed true" and "this risk is analytically credible" is maintained.
