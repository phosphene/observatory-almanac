# Is This an Authentic Photo of a Beach in Palestine Before Israel Was Founded?

## 1. The Claim

In late 2023 and early 2024, as the Israel-Hamas war raged following the October 7, 2023, attacks and the subsequent Israeli military campaign in Gaza, a vintage aerial photograph began circulating widely on social media platforms. The image showed a beachfront lined with orderly white buildings, with the Mediterranean Sea stretching into the distance. The posts claimed that this was a photograph of Palestine taken before the foundation of Israel—a visual testament to a Palestinian landscape that predated the Zionist project and the 1948 establishment of the state of Israel.

One post on X (formerly Twitter) from user @HumaZhr gained 5.7 million views, 17,600 likes, and more than 6,000 shares. Another post on Reddit in the subreddit LateStageCapitalism carried the caption "This was a Palestinian beach" tagged with "imperialism" and received 5,100 upvotes. The image appeared in multiple iterations, with varying claims about its date—some said it showed Palestine "before Zionism," others specified "before Israel" without further elaboration. The emotional resonance of the image was clear: it was being used as visual evidence that Palestine had existed as a thriving, developed society before the Zionist movement and the establishment of Israel sought to displace it.

Snopes published its analysis on April 22, 2024, with an update on April 24, authored by Anna Rascouët-Paz. The article concluded that the photograph was authentic in the sense that it was a real historical photograph, but that it did not show "Palestine before Israel" as claimed. Instead, it showed Bugrashov Beach in central Tel Aviv, specifically the Tel Aviv Promenade, featuring the Bauhaus architecture of the "White City." The article dated the photograph between 1942 and 1953 based on reader-provided evidence that London Garden, visible in the photo, was inaugurated in 1942, and a press caption from April 1953.

## 2. What's Actually True

The Snopes article's core factual findings are correct. The photograph is of Tel Aviv's beachfront, specifically Bugrashov Beach, with the buildings of the White City visible behind it. Tel Aviv was founded in 1906 as a Jewish neighborhood adjacent to the ancient port city of Jaffa. Its growth accelerated significantly in the 1930s with the arrival of Jewish architects and intellectuals fleeing Nazi Germany, who brought the Bauhaus architectural style that would define the city's distinctive aesthetic. The buildings visible in the photograph are part of what UNESCO designated the White City World Heritage Site in 2003, recognizing over 4,000 Bauhaus-style buildings constructed in the 1930s and 1940s.

The article's identification of the specific location as Tel Aviv is supported by multiple independent sources: a catalog from a 2013 Hermitage Museum exhibit, a 2013 blog post referencing a 2002 book, and an eBay listing for a press photograph with a caption dated April 3, 1953. The reader-provided tip about London Garden, inaugurated in 1942, provides a terminus post quem for the image, narrowing the date range to between 1942 and 1953.

The article is also correct that the photograph was taken within the British Mandate of Palestine, the political entity that existed from 1923 to 1948 under British administration. Technically, a photograph taken in 1942—seven years after the Nuremberg Laws, six years into the Arab Revolt, and at the height of the Holocaust—is a photograph of Mandatory Palestine, though the context in which this matters is far more complex than the simple geographic correction implies.

The article's historical context about Bauhaus architecture and Jewish emigration from Nazi Germany is factually accurate. The closure of the Bauhaus school by the Nazis in 1933, the persecution of Jewish architects and intellectuals, and their emigration to Palestine are well-documented historical events. Tel Aviv's White City is indeed a remarkable architectural legacy of this diaspora, and the city's rapid construction in the 1930s is correctly tied to the influx of Jewish refugees and immigrants.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

The Snopes article succeeds in its primary task: determining what the photograph actually depicts. The identification of Tel Aviv's Bugrashov Beach is well-supported by evidence. The dating of the photograph between 1942 and 1953 is careful and acknowledges the limitations of historical documentation. The article's discussion of Bauhaus architecture is informative and adds value. The inclusion of a reader's tip about London Garden is a nice example of participatory fact-checking.

**Where Snopes gets it right:**

The most important contribution of the article is its refusal to accept the viral posts' framing at face value. The posts claimed the image showed "Palestine before Israel," implying a pre-1948 Palestinian Arab landscape. Snopes correctly identified the location as Tel Aviv, a Jewish city. This matters because the distinction has real political and historical weight in the context of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict.

The article's investigation into the photograph's provenance—tracing it through museum catalogs, a blog post, a book, and an eBay listing for press photographs—is diligent. The discovery of the 1953 press caption provides independent verification of the image's source. The acknowledgment that the photograph may be older than the caption, and the incorporation of reader feedback about London Garden, shows intellectual honesty.

The article's explanation of why Tel Aviv became a center of Bauhaus architecture is historically sound and adds meaningful context. The connection between Nazi persecution and the architectural development of Tel Aviv is a genuine historical link that helps readers understand why a Middle Eastern city looks like a European modernist enclave.

**Where Snopes falls short:**

Despite its factual accuracy, the article's framing is significantly incomplete in ways that are consequential for readers trying to understand a deeply contested historical and political issue.

**The "Palestine before Israel" framing problem.** The article treats the viral claim as a straightforward factual error: the photograph is of Tel Aviv, not of a generic "Palestinian" beach. But this framing elides a critical complexity. Tel Aviv was built on land that was part of Mandatory Palestine, and before that, the Ottoman Empire. The land was purchased in 1906 by Jewish immigrants, including banker Jacobus Kann, who used his Dutch citizenship to circumvent Ottoman restrictions on land sales to Jewish subjects. The neighborhood of Ahuzat Bayit, which became Tel Aviv, was established on sand dunes outside Jaffa. While Tel Aviv was a Jewish city, it existed within the political entity of Palestine. The claim that the photograph shows "Palestine before Israel" is technically correct in the narrow geographical sense—it was a photograph of a place within the borders of what was then Mandatory Palestine. The viral posts' claim was misleading not because Palestine did not exist (it did, as a political entity under British administration), but because the image shows a specifically Jewish-developed city rather than a generic Palestinian Arab landscape. The article does not acknowledge this distinction with sufficient nuance.

**The developmental narrative.** The article presents Tel Aviv's construction in terms that implicitly valorize the Zionist project: a thriving city built by refugees fleeing persecution, a triumph of modernist architecture, a UNESCO-recognized cultural treasure. This narrative is not false, but it is incomplete. It omits the Arab population of Palestine, the displacement that accompanied Zionist settlement, and the fact that Tel Aviv's growth occurred in the context of a national conflict in which two peoples claimed the same land. The article's framing of the photo as evidence of "Zionist" development rather than "Palestinian" heritage is accurate, but it presents this as a simple factual correction without engaging with the political implications of that framing.

**The missing Palestinian perspective.** The article traces the photograph through Israeli, European, and American sources—a museum catalog, a blog post, a book by an Israeli architect (Nahoum Cohen), an eBay listing for a U.S. press photo. There is no engagement with Palestinian sources, Palestinian historiography, or the Palestinian understanding of this landscape. The article implicitly treats the Zionist narrative as authoritative and neutral, which in the context of the Israeli-Palestinian conflict is itself a political choice.

**The question of authenticity beyond location.** The article confirms that the photograph is real and identifies its location, but it does not address the broader question of what the photograph means. Was this beach accessible to Palestinians? Was it part of a segregated landscape? What was Jaffa—the Arab city adjacent to Tel Aviv—like at the same time? These questions are relevant to the viral posts' claim that the photograph shows "Palestine" in some meaningful historical sense. By identifying the location and stopping there, the article leaves readers with the impression that the viral posts were purely wrong, when in reality the truth is more entangled.

**The update was insufficient.** The April 24 update added information from a reader about London Garden dating the photograph to 1942 or later. While useful, this update did not address any of the framing issues raised above. It treated the article's work as purely technical—dating the image—rather than considering whether the original analysis might have benefited from a more contextualized approach.

## 4. Why This Matters

This fact-check matters because it sits at the intersection of historical memory, national identity, and the politics of visual evidence in one of the world's most contested conflicts.

First, the photograph and its viral spread demonstrate how historical images are weaponized in contemporary political debates. The shared image was not circulated out of antiquarian interest; it was circulated to make a political point about the legitimacy of Palestinian claims to the land. The claim "This was Palestine before Israel" is a claim about historical precedence, about which people have a rightful connection to the land. The photograph was meant to counter the Israeli/Zionist narrative that Palestine was an empty or underdeveloped land before Jewish settlement. By showing a developed beachfront, the viral posts were implicitly arguing: "Look, Palestine was not a desert. It was a thriving place before Zionism disrupted it."

Second, the Snopes article's response to this claim has political implications that extend beyond its factual corrections. By identifying the photograph as Tel Aviv and framing it within a Zionist developmental narrative, the article implicitly validates a particular historical framing: that Tel Aviv represents Jewish achievement and modernity, while its identification as "Palestine" is a factual error. This is accurate in a narrow sense, but it does not engage with the deeper question of whether the land was "Palestinian" in a meaningful national sense before 1948. The article's framing is consistent with the Israeli national narrative, and while it does not endorse that narrative explicitly, the absence of alternative framings gives it the appearance of neutrality.

Third, the article illustrates the limits of fact-checking in deeply contested historical contexts. For many readers, the question "Is this a photograph of Palestine before Israel?" is not a simple question of geographic identification that can be resolved by reverse-image search. It is a question about national identity, historical continuity, and the meaning of "Palestine" as a political and cultural entity. A fact-check that answers this question as if it were a simple matter of verifying a street address is providing a response that is simultaneously accurate and inadequate.

Fourth, this case matters for understanding the role of visual evidence in historical disputes. Photographs are powerful because they appear to offer unmediated access to the past. But photographs are always framed, captioned, selected, and contextualized. The same photograph of the Tel Aviv beachfront can be used to support entirely different historical narratives: one that celebrates Jewish achievement in the face of persecution, another that laments the displacement of Palestine. The photograph itself is neutral; the narrative context is not. Snopes' article, by providing only one narrative context, inadvertently participated in the framing contest rather than neutrally analyzing it.

Fifth, the article's treatment of this topic matters for trust. Snopes is read by audiences across the political spectrum, and its credibility depends on being perceived as objective. In a case like this, where the factual correction (the photograph is of Tel Aviv) cannot be separated from the political context (what it means for Tel Aviv to be "Palestine"), the article's framing choices are under intense scrutiny. Readers sympathetic to the Palestinian narrative will see the article as biased; readers sympathetic to the Israeli narrative will see it as accurate. This is not a failure of fact-checking per se, but it is a limitation that Snopes should acknowledge more explicitly.

## 5. Research Notes

The Snopes article was published on April 22, 2024, and updated on April 24, 2024. The author is Anna Rascouët-Paz, a Brooklyn-based journalist described by Snopes as "fluent in numerous languages" and a specialist in "science and economic topics." The article is categorized under "News" rather than as a formal "Fact Check," which means it does not carry a Snopes truth rating such as "True" or "False." This is notable because the article is clearly performing a fact-checking function—it is investigating the authenticity and context of a viral claim—but it does not assign a formal rating. The reasons for this categorization are unclear.

The article's sources include: the Hermitage Museum exhibit catalog, a blog post from cafedelasciudades.com.ar, Nahoum Cohen's book "Bauhaus Tel Aviv," an eBay listing for a vintage press photograph, newspapers.com records, and various architectural and historical sources about Bauhaus and Tel Aviv. The article also cites UNESCO's World Heritage listing for the White City and a Haaretz article about Jacobus Kann. The sourcing is adequate for the article's stated purpose but notable for its almost exclusive reliance on Israeli, Western, and cultural heritage institutions. No Palestinian sources are cited.

The article's discovery of the 1953 press caption via an eBay listing is a clever piece of investigative work. The caption, from Wide World Photos (an early photo agency), confirms that the image was distributed in April 1953 for a story about Israel's fifth anniversary. This caption provides strong evidence that the photograph was understood as showing Tel Aviv at the time of its publication.

The article's engagement with a reader who pointed out London Garden is worth noting. The article incorporates this feedback and updates its timeframe, demonstrating a willingness to correct and improve based on reader input. This is good practice.

The article does not address the question of whether there exist photographs of Palestinian Arab beach towns from the same period that would show what the viral posts claimed the image depicted. This would have been a useful comparator point: photographs of Jaffa, Haifa, Acre, or Gaza from the 1930s and 1940s would help readers understand what a historically accurate "Palestinian beach before Israel" would actually look like.

The article's treatment of the Ottoman-era land purchase is historically accurate but framed in a particular way. It notes that Kann used his Dutch citizenship to circumvent Ottoman restrictions on land sales to Jews. This is true, but the framing implies a clever workaround rather than a politically charged process of land acquisition that Palestinians experienced as dispossession.

## 6. Verdict

**Rating: Factually correct about the photograph's location, but the framing is incomplete in politically consequential ways.**

The Snopes article correctly identifies the viral photograph as showing Tel Aviv's Bugrashov Beach, not a generic Palestinian landscape. The geographic location, the architectural style, the date range (1942–1953), and the press provenance are all established through diligent research. These factual findings are sound.

However, the article's treatment of the "Palestine before Israel" framing is insufficiently nuanced. The photograph does show a place within what was then Mandatory Palestine. The claim that it showed "Palestine" is not purely false; it is misleading in a particular way: it implies a Palestinian Arab landscape when the photograph shows a Jewish-developed urban center. This distinction matters, and the article elides it by treating the viral claim as a simple error.

The article also presents a developmental narrative of Tel Aviv's construction that, while factually accurate, implicitly endorses a Zionist framing of history. The article tells the story of Jewish refugees building a modernist utopia. It does not tell the parallel story of the Arab population of Palestine, the displacement that accompanied Zionist settlement, or the contested nature of the land. The absence of a Palestinian perspective is a significant omission in an article about a photograph being used in the context of the Israel-Palestine conflict.

A more complete treatment would have included: a discussion of what it means for a photograph of Tel Aviv to be described as "Palestine"; the distinction between Palestinian Arab heritage and the Jewish settlement project; comparative context about neighboring Jaffa; and a more self-aware acknowledgment of the political implications of the article's own framing. The article's factual core is solid, but its interpretive frame is too narrow for the charged context in which it operates. The truth about the Palestine beach photograph is not just that it shows Tel Aviv—it is that what "Palestine" means, and who gets to define it, is precisely what is being contested.
