# The Palestinian flag was banned from Eurovision


## 1. The Claim

Beginning in early May 2024, headlines and social media posts stated flatly that the Palestinian flag had been banned from the Eurovision Song Contest, held in Malmö, Sweden. The claim circulated in variations: that Eurovision had specifically banned Palestinian flags; that pro-Palestinian symbols were prohibited while Israeli symbols were not; that security was confiscating Palestinian flags at the door while allowing the Israeli delegation to participate without comparable scrutiny; and that the European Broadcasting Union (EBU), Eurovision's governing body, was suppressing Palestinian political expression in the context of the ongoing military campaign in Gaza.

The Eurovision Song Contest 2024 was held in Malmö, a Swedish city with a large Palestinian and Muslim diaspora population, and major pro-Palestinian demonstrations were planned and took place concurrently with the event. The Israeli delegation, representing the winning broadcasting organisation Kan, had been at the centre of intense public controversy for months prior to the event. Calls for Israel's exclusion from the competition — analogous to Russia's exclusion in 2022 following its invasion of Ukraine — had attracted widespread public support and a substantial petition. Against this charged backdrop, reports that Eurovision had banned the Palestinian flag became a flashpoint for a much larger political argument.

The claim was picked up by news organisations globally, shared extensively on social media, and characterised in public discourse in ways that varied significantly from "the EBU implemented a general flag policy" to "Eurovision banned Palestinian flags to protect Israel." The gap between the narrowly accurate version and the more expansive version circulated as established fact is the core question this entry addresses.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The EBU flag policy.** The European Broadcasting Union implemented a flag policy for Eurovision 2024 that restricted which flags could be displayed in official spaces — the main stage, the green room, during scoring, and in other designated contest areas. The policy specified that only flags representing countries competing in the contest were permitted in these official spaces. The rainbow Pride flag was explicitly granted an exception to this rule. All other flags — flags of non-participating states, territories, political movements, and organisations — were subject to removal.

The Palestinian flag, because Palestine is not a Eurovision competing nation (it lacks an EBU member broadcasting organisation), fell within the category of prohibited flags under this general rule. But so did the flags of every other non-participating country or territory: the United States, Russia, China, Australia (which participates and was thus exempt), and any other national or political symbol not associated with a competing country. The policy was not formulated as a prohibition on the Palestinian flag; it was formulated as a permission regime for competing-country flags only.

Michelle Roverelli, the EBU's head of communications, stated publicly in May 2024 that the organisation reserved the right to "remove any other flags or symbols, clothing, items and banners being used for the likely purpose of instrumentalising the TV shows" (Roverelli, quoted in Associated Press, May 2024). The word "instrumentalising" is significant and contested: it implies that politically charged symbols are prohibited not on their own terms but because of their use to make political statements within an entertainment context. Critics noted that this framing implicitly treated pro-Palestinian solidarity as political in a way that Israeli flag display was not, since the Israeli flag was the flag of a competing nation and therefore exempt.

**Security confiscation in practice.** On the ground in Malmö, the practical operation of the policy did involve security personnel confiscating Palestinian flags and keffiyehs — the black-and-white checked headscarf associated with Palestinian cultural identity and, in protest contexts, with Palestinian resistance — from ticketholders attempting to enter the arena. Accounts from attendees and reporting by the BBC, AP, the Guardian, and other outlets confirmed that these confiscations occurred. Whether keffiyehs constituted a "symbol" covered by the flag policy was not addressed in EBU communications at the time, making their confiscation a significant expansion of the stated policy in practice.

**The Eric Saade incident.** Swedish singer Eric Saade, who has Palestinian heritage and who had been invited to perform as a guest act in the Eurovision semi-finals, wore a keffiyeh on his wrist during his performance. The EBU subsequently issued a statement saying it "regretted" that Saade "chose to compromise the non-political nature of the event" and that he "was aware of the rules governing performances on the Eurovision stage" (EBU press statement, May 2024). The statement framed the wearing of a keffiyeh as a violation of Eurovision's political neutrality standard.

**The 2025 rule revision.** Following the controversy, the EBU revised its flag policy for Eurovision 2025. Under the revised rules, audience members are permitted to bring any flag into the venue that does not violate the laws of the host country. The performer and delegation restrictions remained stricter, continuing to limit official spaces to competing-country flags. The revision was widely interpreted as a response to the 2024 backlash and implicitly acknowledged that the 2024 policy had been applied in ways that were perceived as disproportionately restrictive.

**The overall picture.** The narrowly stated claim — that the Palestinian flag was banned from Eurovision — is technically accurate in the sense that the Palestinian flag was not permitted in official Eurovision spaces and was removed from attendees at the venue. It is misleading in the sense that it implies a targeted prohibition rather than a general policy that encompassed all non-participating-country flags. Whether the general policy constituted discriminatory enforcement in context is a legitimate political and ethical argument that the technical fact-check framing cannot fully resolve.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The accurate kernel amplified without qualification.** The confiscation of Palestinian flags and keffiyehs at the arena doors was real and documented. Journalistic accounts of these incidents circulated alongside the broader claim. For readers who encountered reporting of actual flag confiscations and then encountered the headline "Eurovision bans Palestinian flag," the headline was legible as a natural description of events they had already read about, rather than as an oversimplification. The accuracy of the underlying event supported belief in the amplified framing.

**Context-driven plausibility.** The 2024 Eurovision controversy occurred against the backdrop of Israel's military operation in Gaza, which had produced globally visible casualties and mass displacement, and which had generated one of the largest global protest movements in recent decades. In this context, a policy applied by an organisation that simultaneously permitted Israeli participation and removed Palestinian symbols from audience members appeared, to many observers, as one element of a pattern of institutional asymmetry in responses to the conflict. Whether or not this characterisation accurately describes EBU intent, it was highly plausible given the broader context. The "Eurovision banned Palestinian flag" framing resonated because it fit a pre-existing and data-supported narrative about institutional bias.

**The epistemic novelty: framing as suppression activates different cognitive registers than framing as policy.** This is the core insight the simplified version exploited. A general policy applied neutrally to all non-participating countries reads, cognitively, as bureaucratic rule-following. A targeted ban on a specific minority community's symbolic expression reads, cognitively, as discriminatory suppression. The same practical outcome — Palestinian flags removed from the venue — is mapped by different framings onto radically different moral categories. Research on motivated reasoning in political contexts (Kunda, 1990, *Psychological Bulletin*) suggests that when a simplified framing is consonant with prior moral commitments — in this case, frameworks in which Palestinian solidarity is systematically suppressed in Western institutions — people apply less critical scrutiny to the framing's accuracy than they would for a politically dissonant claim. The result is that the policy-as-targeting frame circulated with the credibility of a factual report rather than the credibility of a political interpretation, and corrections were received as politically motivated rather than as factually grounded.

**The keffiyeh question created genuine ambiguity.** The confiscation of keffiyehs, which are articles of clothing rather than flags, represented a material expansion of the stated policy whose justification under the flag rules was genuinely unclear. Where the Palestinian flag could at least notionally be described as falling under the "non-participating country flag" rule, a keffiyeh confiscation cannot be grounded in the same policy without an interpretive stretch. People who observed or read about keffiyeh confiscations had reasonable grounds for concluding that the enforcement went beyond neutral rule application. This ambiguity in the actual events made the "targeted ban" framing harder to dismiss than a purely fabricated claim would have been.

**Political stakes and the limits of fact-checking.** The Eurovision 2024 controversy touched on live questions of genocide, colonialism, Western institutional responsibility, and the correct limits of an entertainment context's claim to political neutrality. In this environment, fact-checking interventions that focused narrowly on the technical inaccuracy of "Palestinian flag specifically banned" were often received as attempts to deflect attention from the underlying political reality by pointing to a semantic technicality. This dynamic — where accurate corrections are perceived as politically motivated — is well-documented in research on fact-check backlash (Nyhan & Reifler, 2010, *Political Behavior*; Wood & Porter, 2019, *Quarterly Journal of Political Science*).


## 4. Verdict

**Mixture — the claim is technically accurate but misleadingly framed.** The Palestinian flag was removed from Malmö Arena during Eurovision 2024. EBU security personnel confiscated Palestinian flags and Palestinian cultural symbols including keffiyehs from ticketholders attempting to enter the venue. These events occurred and are well-documented by multiple major news organisations, including AP (2024), the BBC (2024), and The Guardian (2024).

The claim is misleading because it implies a targeted prohibition on the Palestinian flag specifically. The EBU's stated policy was a general flag rule covering all non-participating countries and territories, with the rainbow Pride flag as the sole explicit exception. The Palestinian flag fell under this general rule by virtue of Palestine having no competing EBU member organisation, not because of specific EBU action directed at Palestine. The claim also elides meaningful distinctions between the stated policy and its practical enforcement.

The justified critique of the EBU's approach — that applying "political neutrality" to remove Palestinian symbols while allowing Israeli participation raised legitimate concerns about even-handedness — is supported by evidence. But that critique is a political and ethical argument about the EBU's conduct, not a factual validation of the claim that the Palestinian flag was specifically banned.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Eurovision 2024 controversy illustrates a recurring structural tension in large international entertainment events that include delegations from countries engaged in active conflict. The organisational logic of Eurovision's political neutrality rules — keep political statements off the stage and out of the official programming — was designed for a world in which the participation of any individual country is uncontroversial or at least not the subject of active global political contestation. When a participating country is involved in internationally documented mass civilian casualties, the same rules that normally function as political quietism function differently: they effectively protect the contested participation from political challenge while blocking expressions of solidarity with those affected by the action.

The comparison to Russia's 2022 exclusion was unavoidable and was extensively discussed in media commentary during the controversy. Russia had been excluded from Eurovision 2022 following its invasion of Ukraine, on the grounds that its participation was "incompatible with EBU values." The EBU did not exclude Israel in 2024 under comparable reasoning, arguing that its rules did not mandate exclusion in this case. Whether the two situations are analogous is a matter of ongoing debate. What is not in dispute is that the EBU's choices operated asymmetrically in public perception, and that this asymmetry structured how the flag policy was received and transmitted.

Research on the political economy of international cultural events (Whittle & Garcia, 2019, *International Journal of Cultural Policy*; Fricker & Gluhovic, 2013, *Performing the New Europe*) has documented how events like Eurovision navigate national identity politics through nominally neutral institutional frameworks while inevitably making political choices that the neutrality framing obscures. The Palestinian flag episode sits within this longer pattern of institutionally imposed political neutrality functioning as a form of political decision-making that its framers do not acknowledge as such.

The EBU's 2025 revision of the flag policy — allowing any legally permissible flag in the audience — effectively acknowledged that the 2024 enforcement was untenable and had damaged the organisation's credibility. The Snopes assessment, which rated the claim as a mixture, accurately captures the factual complexity: real events, real restrictions, and real enforcement, but a framing that obscures the general nature of the policy.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rated this claim as a "mixture," reflecting the genuine factual complexity: the Palestinian flag was removed from the venue, but as part of a general non-participating-countries rule rather than a targeted prohibition. The Associated Press, Reuters, and BBC all reported on the flag confiscations and the EBU's policy in ways that provided the necessary context, noting that the rule covered all non-participating country flags.

The claim's trajectory through social media demonstrated a common pattern: an accurate report of a real event ("Eurovision security confiscated Palestinian flags") was condensed and amplified into a more sweeping claim ("Eurovision banned the Palestinian flag") that lost the contextual qualifications. Fact-checkers handling this type of claim face the challenge that the correction requires more words and more context than the claim, which means corrections consistently underperform viral claims in audience reach.

For fact-check organisations, key verification steps for this type of claim include: (1) obtaining and reviewing the exact text of the applicable institutional policy, rather than relying on characterisations of it; (2) distinguishing between stated policy and enforcement practice, since these diverged significantly in the keffiyeh case; (3) identifying all parties to whom the same policy applied, to assess whether enforcement was consistent or selective; (4) comparing the treatment of the claimed restriction with comparable cases (the LGBTQ+ rainbow flag exception; the Russia 2022 exclusion) to contextualise the degree of rule consistency; and (5) being transparent about the distinction between factual assessment and political or ethical evaluation, since readers who have reached an ethical conclusion about the EBU's conduct may experience accurate contextualisation as partisan intervention.

The broader media literacy lesson from this case is that "technically true but misleadingly framed" is among the hardest misinformation categories to address, because corrections require engaging with the framing rather than simply refuting a false fact. The Palestinian flag was removed from Eurovision: this is true. The Palestinian flag was banned from Eurovision: this goes further than the evidence strictly supports. The difference between these two statements is not trivial, but communicating why it matters requires engaging with the politics of how we describe institutional policy choices — an exercise that inevitably involves taking implicit positions on contested political questions.
