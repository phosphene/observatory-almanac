# The Pancreatic Cancer Test That Wasn't: Jack Andraka, Media Hype, and the Distance Between Science Fair and Science

## 1. The Claim

In August 2019, The Epoch Times — a publication not historically distinguished for its scientific rigor — ran a profile of a "15-year-old science buff" who had ostensibly invented a "100 percent effective" diagnostic test for pancreatic cancer. The story was shared widely across social media, buoyed by the irresistible narrative: a teenager, armed with nothing but curiosity and a high school laboratory budget, had accomplished what the entire biomedical establishment had failed to do. The boy wonder was Jack Andraka, and by the time The Epoch Times profile appeared, the claims attached to his name had calcified into a kind of internet folklore: that his test was "168 times faster, 26,000 times less expensive, and 400 times more sensitive" than existing methods, and that it detected pancreatic cancer with 100 percent accuracy.

The Snopes fact check, authored by Alex Kasprak and published on September 4, 2019, rated the claim "Mostly False." The verdict was grounded in a set of facts that, taken together, demolish the sensational version of the story while leaving a more complicated residue. Andraka did, as a 15-year-old high school student, research and prototype a potential test for pancreatic cancer. He did win the grand prize at the 2012 Intel International Science and Engineering Fair (ISEF). And he did construct a device — dipstick-style filter paper coated with carbon nanotubes and mesothelin-specific antibodies — that could detect the presence of mesothelin, a protein associated with certain cancers, in cell culture solutions and in the blood of mice bearing grafted human pancreatic tumors.

But none of that constitutes a "100 percent accurate" cancer-detection test. The Epoch Times story, as Snopes correctly notes, failed to mention that Andraka was 22 years old by 2019, not 15; that the more sensational claims had been significantly tempered over the intervening years; and that those temperings had been acknowledged by Andraka himself. The viral narrative had outrun the underlying science, and the gap between the two is the subject of this entry.

The claim, as it circulated in the popular imagination, rested on three pillars: the speed and cost comparisons, the accuracy figure, and the implication that a working diagnostic device was on the threshold of clinical deployment. Each of these pillars deserves scrutiny, and Snopes provides a framework for that scrutiny. But the fuller story — the one this entry tells — involves not just the factual distortions but the ecosystem that produced them: the TED conference circuit, the science fair apparatus, the media's hunger for hero narratives, and the structural incentives that reward teenage prodigies for overclaiming.

## 2. What's Actually True

It is true that Jack Andraka, at the age of 15, conducted original research aimed at developing a novel diagnostic approach for pancreatic cancer. His work was not trivial. He identified mesothelin — a protein discovered by National Cancer Institute researcher Ira Pastan — as a potential biomarker. He designed a sensor using single-walled carbon nanotubes coated with mesothelin-specific antibodies, adsorbed onto filter paper. When mesothelin bound to the antibodies, the electrical conductivity of the nanotube network changed, producing a measurable signal. This is, in principle, a biosensor of the type that occupies legitimate research programs worldwide.

It is true that Andraka's project won the Gordon E. Moore Award, the grand prize at the 2012 Intel ISEF, which is among the most prestigious science fair competitions for pre-college students. The award carried a $75,000 prize. It is also true that he conducted some of the work at a Johns Hopkins University laboratory, having famously sent out 200 emails to researchers before finding one — Dr. Anirban Maitra, a professor of pathology and oncology — willing to host him.

It is true that the device demonstrated a proof of concept: it could detect mesothelin in buffer solutions and in cell culture media. It could also distinguish between the blood of mice that had been grafted with human pancreatic tumors and the blood of control mice. These were preliminary experimental results, and in the context of a high school science fair project, they were genuinely impressive.

What is also true, and what Snopes documents carefully, is that Andraka's test had not been validated as a diagnostic tool for pancreatic cancer in humans. It had not undergone clinical trials. It had not been peer-reviewed or published in a scientific journal at the time of the viral media coverage. Johns Hopkins itself characterized the results as "highly preliminary" and representing "the very earliest stages of determining whether such a test is feasible or likely." The institution noted that his results had not been confirmed independently, had not been peer-reviewed, and had not been published.

Furthermore, the scientific basis for using mesothelin as a standalone early-detection biomarker was, even at the time, questionable. A 2013 study — referenced by Snopes — concluded that mesothelin was "a weak diagnostic classifier on its own." This is a critical point: even if Andraka's sensor performed exactly as described, the protein it was designed to detect is not reliably elevated in the blood of early-stage pancreatic cancer patients. A perfect sensor for the wrong biomarker is not a useful diagnostic test.

Andraka himself, in a 2014 email to Forbes healthcare reporter Matthew Herper, acknowledged the gap between his initial claims and the reality of the science. He wrote: "While promising, the project really was extremely preliminary and is by no means as sophisticated as some stories say it is... I realize that in retrospect that it was just a high school science fair project and it was a proof of concept experiment and initially I thought that it could get on the market in 1-2 years however I've learned so much over the course of this journey and realized that it will in reality take a lot longer than this."

This is a remarkably candid admission, and it is one that Snopes treats fairly. The Epoch Times story, by contrast, ignored it entirely.

## 3. What Snopes Gets Right and Where It Falls Short

Snopes gets the fundamentals right. The fact check correctly identifies the central distortions: the "100 percent accurate" claim is unsupported by any clinical data; the speed and cost comparisons are based on flawed methodology; the patent was denied; the test had not been peer-reviewed or validated in humans; and Andraka himself had walked back the most sensational claims years before The Epoch Times published its profile. The verdict of "Mostly False" is, on the narrow question of whether the specific claim as stated is true, appropriate.

Where Snopes falls short is in its treatment of the broader phenomenon — the machinery that produced the hype in the first place. The fact check treats The Epoch Times as the primary offender, and it was the proximate trigger for the fact check. But The Epoch Times was a latecomer to the Jack Andraka narrative. The story had been amplified for years by far more credible and influential institutions: the Intel ISEF, which awarded its grand prize; the TED conference, which gave Andraka a platform; the BBC, which profiled him; Forbes, which published his op-eds; and the broader science communication apparatus that treats teenage prodigies as feel-good content regardless of the scientific merit of their work.

The Andraka TED talk, delivered in 2013, was titled "A promising test for pancreatic cancer" and has been viewed millions of times. In it, Andraka presented the project with the confident framing of a product pitch — "you can run 26,000 tests for the cost of one" — and the narrative arc of a hero's journey: a family friend dies of pancreatic cancer, a teenager reads about carbon nanotubes on Wikipedia, sends 200 emails, gets 199 rejections, and finally finds a mentor. The talk is emotionally powerful. It is also, in retrospect, a masterclass in the art of overclaiming framed as inspiration. TED, as an institution, did not subject the claims to meaningful scrutiny before platforming them. Nor did the BBC, which produced a documentary segment on Andraka that further amplified the "100 percent accurate" narrative.

Snopes also does not fully explore the significance of the expert dissent. The fact check quotes George Church, the Harvard DNA sequencing pioneer, who reviewed a draft of Andraka's paper and concluded that many of the claims "may not hold up, or at least require more work to be proven." It quotes Ira Pastan, the discoverer of mesothelin, who stated that the method "makes no scientific sense" and that he didn't "know anybody in the scientific community who believes his findings." These are not minor qualifications. Pastan is not a marginal figure — he is the person who identified the very protein Andraka's sensor was designed to detect. His dismissal of the method's scientific logic is, on its face, devastating. Snopes presents these quotes but does not fully contextualize what they mean: that the scientific community's reception of Andraka's work was, at best, deeply skeptical, and that this skepticism was systematically excluded from the media narrative.

The speed comparison — "168 times faster" — deserves additional scrutiny that Snopes provides but could press further. Andraka's comparison used 14 hours as the benchmark for existing ELISA tests. Church pointed out that a modern CA 19-9 ELISA panel — the actual test used in clinical settings for pancreatic cancer monitoring — takes approximately 1.5 hours, not 14. The 14-hour figure appears to have been derived from an outdated or atypical protocol, and its use inflates the speed advantage by roughly an order of magnitude. The cost comparison is similarly flawed: Andraka compared only the manufacturing costs of his materials against the full price of a clinical ELISA test, which includes profit margins, overhead, labor, equipment depreciation, regulatory compliance costs, and clinical validation expenses. A fair comparison would either include equivalent overhead on both sides or exclude it from both. Neither comparison, as constructed, withstands scrutiny.

The patent denial is another element that deserves more emphasis than Snopes gives it. Andraka applied for an international patent on the device in 2012. The application was ultimately denied on the grounds that it lacked an "inventive step" — the patent examiner's assessment that the approach, while novel in its specific configuration, did not represent a non-obvious advance over existing biosensor technology. This is not, by itself, conclusive evidence that the technology is without merit. Patent examiners are not scientific peer reviewers. But the denial is consistent with the broader pattern: institutions that subjected Andraka's work to formal scrutiny — patent offices, peer reviewers, expert scientists — found it wanting, while institutions that treated it as content — science fairs, TED, media outlets — celebrated it.

There is also a question that Snopes raises only obliquely: the role of the science fair apparatus itself. ISEF is a competition, not a scientific venue. Its judges are evaluating projects within the context of pre-college research, and the standards for winning are not the same as the standards for publication in a peer-reviewed journal or approval by a regulatory body. The problem is not that ISEF awarded Andraka a prize — his project was genuinely impressive for a 15-year-old. The problem is that the prize was then treated, by the media and by the public, as a validation of the science rather than a recognition of the effort. The conflation of "best high school science fair project" with "viable cancer diagnostic" is the root of the distortion, and it is a conflation that the science fair apparatus itself has an interest in maintaining, because the prestige of the competition depends on the perceived significance of its winners' work.

## 4. Why This Matters

The Jack Andraka story matters not because of the specific science — a preliminary biosensor that did not pan out is, in the grand scheme of biomedical research, entirely unremarkable. Thousands of promising approaches fail in the gap between proof of concept and clinical deployment. That is the nature of the enterprise. What makes the Andraka story significant is what it reveals about the science communication ecosystem and the incentives that shape it.

The first issue is the hero narrative. Media outlets, particularly those oriented toward general audiences, prefer stories about individuals to stories about systems. A teenager who cures cancer is a story. A research consortium that incrementally improves a biomarker panel is not, even though the latter is how medical progress actually occurs. The hero narrative is not merely a distortion of the facts; it actively misshapes public understanding of how science works. It suggests that breakthroughs come from lone geniuses rather than from the accumulated labor of thousands of researchers, clinicians, technicians, and administrators working over decades. When the breakthrough turns out to be illusory, the damage to public trust is compounded: the same audience that was told a teenager solved pancreatic cancer is later told, quietly, that he didn't. The correction never travels as far as the original claim.

The second issue is the absence of scientific gatekeeping in popular science communication. TED's vetting process, such as it is, does not include peer review. The BBC's science desk did not, in this case, consult independent experts before producing its segment. Forbes published Andraka's own op-eds without subjecting the underlying claims to expert scrutiny. The Epoch Times, as Snopes notes, ran its profile years after the claims had been publicly walked back, without even the most basic fact-checking — Andraka's age, for instance, is a matter of public record. The result is a media environment in which the most sensational version of a story receives maximum amplification while the necessary corrections receive minimum attention. This asymmetry is structural, not accidental: sensationalism drives engagement, and engagement drives revenue.

The third issue is the specific harm to public understanding of cancer diagnostics. Pancreatic cancer is one of the most lethal malignancies, in part because it is typically detected at a late stage when treatment options are limited. The need for an effective early-detection test is real and urgent. When a story like Andraka's circulates, it creates the impression that such a test is imminent, or even already available. This can lead to false hope among patients and families, and it can distort public perception of the state of the field. It can also, paradoxically, undermine support for the incremental, unglamorous research that actually moves the field forward. If the public believes a teenager has already solved the problem, why fund the consortium?

The fourth issue is the ethical dimension of platforming a teenager. Andraka was 15 when he did the work and 16 when he delivered the TED talk. He was, in every meaningful sense, a minor. The decision to platform him on a global stage, to amplify claims that he was not equipped to defend at a professional level, and to treat him as a public figure in the scientific discourse placed an extraordinary burden on a young person. Andraka's own 2014 email to Herper — in which he acknowledged that the project was "just a high school science fair project" and a "proof of concept experiment" — reads as the reflection of someone who had been thrust into a spotlight he did not fully understand and was attempting to reconcile the narrative that had been constructed around him with the reality of the science. The adults in the room — the TED organizers, the journalists, the ISEF leadership — bear responsibility for the gap between the claims and the evidence. Snopes, by focusing on The Epoch Times as the target of the fact check, does not fully address this dimension.

## 5. Research Notes

The primary source for this entry is the Snopes fact check authored by Alex Kasprak, published September 4, 2019, and rated "Mostly False." The fact check draws on several key primary and secondary sources that are worth noting for readers who wish to investigate further.

Matthew Herper's reporting for Forbes is the most substantial journalistic treatment of the Andraka story. Herper reviewed a draft of Andraka's paper and consulted six expert reviewers, including George Church. His coverage, which spanned multiple articles between 2012 and 2015, is notable for its willingness to subject a feel-good narrative to scientific scrutiny — a service that few outlets provided. Herper's 2015 article, which included the email from Andraka acknowledging the preliminary nature of the work, is particularly valuable.

The scientific literature on mesothelin as a pancreatic cancer biomarker is more nuanced than the popular narrative suggests. Ira Pastan's discovery of mesothelin and his subsequent research on its role in pancreatic and other cancers is foundational. However, as the 2013 study cited by Snopes indicates, mesothelin's diagnostic utility as a standalone biomarker is limited. It is elevated in some but not all pancreatic cancer patients, and its elevation in early-stage disease — the stage at which a useful screening test would need to detect it — is inconsistent. Current research on pancreatic cancer early detection has moved toward multi-analyte panels that combine mesothelin with other biomarkers (CA 19-9, KRAS mutations, circulating tumor DNA, and others) rather than relying on any single protein.

The Johns Hopkins statement, referenced by Snopes, characterized Andraka's results as "highly preliminary" and noted that they represented "the very earliest stages of determining whether such a test is feasible or likely to be accurate enough to be useful." The statement also noted that the results had not been confirmed, had not been peer-reviewed, and had not been published in a scientific journal. This is an institutional position, not an informal assessment, and it carries corresponding weight.

The patent denial is a matter of public record. Andraka filed an international patent application (PCT/US2012/043629) in 2012. The application was ultimately refused on the grounds that the invention lacked an "inventive step" — the standard patent-law requirement that the invention represent a non-obvious advance over the prior art. The refusal is consistent with the assessment of several expert reviewers that the approach, while creative, was not a fundamental advance in biosensor technology.

The broader context of the Andraka story includes the culture of science fairs and their relationship to media narratives. The Intel ISEF, now sponsored by Regeneron, is the largest pre-college scientific research competition in the world. Its winners have, on several occasions, received media attention that far exceeded the scientific significance of their projects. This is not a criticism of the competition itself, which provides valuable opportunities for young scientists, but of the media apparatus that treats its winners as prodigies whose work is ready for prime time. The Regeneron rebranding of the competition in 2020 came with renewed attention to the question of how science fair results should be communicated to the public, though meaningful structural reforms have been limited.

For readers interested in the current state of pancreatic cancer early detection, the relevant literature includes work on circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), liquid biopsy approaches, multi-biomarker panels, and imaging-based screening protocols for high-risk populations. The National Cancer Institute's Pancreatic Cancer Detection Consortium (PCDC) is one of the principal organized research efforts in this space. None of these approaches claim "100 percent accuracy," and all are in various stages of clinical validation — as any responsible scientific effort in this domain would be.

## 6. Verdict

Snopes rated the claim "Mostly False," and on the narrow question of whether the specific claim — that Jack Andraka invented a "100 percent accurate" pancreatic cancer test that was "168 times faster, 26,000 times less expensive, and 400 times more sensitive" than existing methods — is factually correct, the verdict is accurate. The claim is not supported by the evidence. The speed and cost comparisons are methodologically flawed. The accuracy figure is unsupported by any clinical data. The test was not validated as a diagnostic tool for pancreatic cancer in humans. The patent was denied. The discoverer of the target protein called the method scientifically nonsensical. And Andraka himself acknowledged that the project was preliminary and that the most sensational claims were overhyped.

But the "Mostly" in "Mostly False" does a certain amount of work that deserves examination. The partial truth — that Andraka conducted genuinely original research, won a prestigious science fair, and demonstrated a proof of concept — is real. The problem is not that it is false; it is that the gap between what was demonstrated and what was claimed is so vast that the claim, as stated, is substantially misleading even if some of its components have a factual basis. "Mostly False" is, if anything, a generous rating for a claim whose central assertion — "100 percent accurate" — has no clinical support whatsoever.

The deeper verdict, the one that Snopes' format does not fully accommodate, is that the Jack Andraka story is a case study in the systematic failure of science communication at every level. The science fair awarded a prize for an impressive project without adequately distinguishing recognition from validation. TED provided a global platform without subjecting the claims to expert review. Major media outlets amplified the narrative without consulting the scientists who had reviewed the work and found it wanting. The Epoch Times recycled the claims years after they had been publicly walked back. And the correction — when it finally came, from Forbes, from the scientific community, and eventually from Andraka himself — never achieved a fraction of the reach of the original story.

The lesson is not that Jack Andraka is a fraud. He was a teenager who did an impressive science project and got caught in a media machine that rewarded him for overstating its significance and then punished him, gently, when the claims proved unsustainable. The lesson is that the ecosystem — the fairs, the conferences, the media outlets, the social platforms — is designed to produce and amplify exactly this kind of distortion. It feeds on hope, on narrative, on the emotional satisfaction of a story in which a young person saves the world. And it has no corresponding mechanism for the quiet, unglamorous, and essential work of saying: this is preliminary, this is uncertain, this needs more research, and this may not work out.

That mechanism is called science. It is slow, it is methodical, it is resistant to hero narratives, and it is the only thing that has ever actually moved the needle on pancreatic cancer — or any other disease. The next time a story circulates about a teenager who has cured cancer, the appropriate response is not applause but a single question: has it been peer-reviewed? Until the answer is yes, the claim is content, not science. And the distance between the two is where the harm lives.
