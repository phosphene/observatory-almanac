## The Claim

As the clock ticked toward midnight on New Year's Eve 2025, a claim flooded social media with a particular emotional resonance: MTV was shutting down. Videos spread online showing the channel's final broadcast — a music video, appropriately or not, for "Video Killed the Radio Star" by The Buggles, the same song that had opened MTV's very first broadcast on August 1, 1981. The claim, as it circulated, was categorical: parent company Paramount Global was shutting down all MTV channels. The music channel, a fixture of popular culture since Ronald Reagan's presidency, was over.

The claim arrived with apparent evidence. The "Video Killed the Radio Star" footage was genuine. The BBC had reported as early as October 2025 that MTV was planning to axe its music channels in the United Kingdom. On Reddit and X (formerly Twitter), clips of the broadcast ran alongside declarations that an era had ended. For people who had grown up watching MTV in the 1980s and 1990s — when the channel was genuinely central to pop culture, launching artists, shaping aesthetic trends, and constituting a shared visual language for a generation — the news carried a particular weight.

The specific claim investigated by Snopes (Ibrahim, 2026, Snopes.com) was that "Parent company Paramount was shutting down all MTV channels in late 2025." This phrasing — "all MTV channels" — is the crux of what makes the claim a mixture of truth and falsehood. Something genuinely ended on New Year's Eve 2025. But the full picture of what ended, what continued, and what the closures represent within Paramount's broader restructuring is considerably more complicated than a headline permits.

---

## What's Actually True

Several things in the claim are accurate and verifiable. MTV's 24-hour dedicated music video channels in the United Kingdom did shut down on or around New Year's Eve 2025. The channels affected included MTV Music UK, MTV Hits UK, MTV Live, MTV 80s UK, and MTV 90s UK — all of which had operated as continuous music video services, the modern equivalent of what MTV's founding editorial model had promised. These channels ceased their continuous music broadcast operations. The final video played on the UK music channels was indeed "Video Killed the Radio Star," as reported by BBC journalist Jono Reed who posted footage online (Reed, 2025, X/@jonoread).

The closures extended beyond the United Kingdom. According to reporting by entertainment trade outlet Deadline (Tapp, 2026, Deadline), the 24-hour music channels also stopped running in Ireland, France, Germany, Austria, Poland, Hungary, Australia, and Brazil. An unnamed Paramount spokesperson quoted in Variety (Schneider, 2026, Variety) confirmed the UK shutdown in measured corporate language: "MTV's specialist music channels in the UK will no longer operate as linear channels. The flagship MTV UK channel will continue to broadcast… Paramount is reviewing and adjusting its international Pay TV portfolio, given shifts in audience behaviour towards streaming and digital platforms."

The key word in that statement is "specialist music channels." The flagship MTV UK channel — a more general entertainment channel that airs old movies, reality show reruns, and other programming — continued broadcasting. In the US, MTV and MTV2 were still airing reality programming, including *RuPaul's Drag Race*, while MTV Classic was still showing music videos (Ibrahim, 2026, Snopes.com). The channel that closed, in other words, was not MTV in its entirety but specifically those channels whose programming model remained closest to MTV's original mission: non-stop music videos.

This distinction — between MTV-as-brand and MTV-as-music-video-service — is real but also, in cultural terms, somewhat hollow. The UK music video channels that shut down represented the last living remnant of the original MTV editorial model on linear television. The US MTV channel has not been primarily a music video channel since the early 2000s; the transition away from heavy music video rotation toward reality television programming was well underway by the time MTV aired the final episodes of *Total Request Live* in 2008. What died on New Year's Eve 2025 in the UK was less the MTV brand than the last operational version of the thing the MTV brand originally referred to.

Paramount's cost-cutting context is relevant. Through 2025, Paramount sought to reduce costs by approximately $500 million, according to BBC reporting (BBC, 2025), which included shutting down Paramount Television Studios and, separately, the cancellation of Stephen Colbert's late-night talk show on CBS (Rascouët-Paz, 2025, Snopes.com). The international music channel closures fit within this pattern of restructuring. As a Paramount spokesperson noted, the shift reflects audience movement toward streaming and digital platforms — the same structural force reshaping the entire cable television ecosystem.

## Why People Believe This

The social media amplification of this claim is a useful case study in how partial truths achieve categorical framing, and why culturally resonant claims are particularly prone to this distortion.

**The emotional logic of finality.** The "Video Killed the Radio Star" footage provided a perfect narrative anchor. That the last UK music channel broadcast ended with the first video MTV ever aired in 1981 is a poetic symmetry that the internet immediately recognised and amplified. Whether this was deliberately engineered as a symbolic final act or simply the result of someone at MTV UK choosing an appropriately thematic closing song, the footage functioned as a perfect emotional full stop. Narratives that arrive pre-equipped with a satisfying ending — especially a circular, symmetrical one — are extremely difficult to resist and extremely easy to share without qualification. The story was already "complete" before the full picture was established.

**The "death" heuristic in media coverage.** There is a well-documented tendency in media culture to frame institutional changes as categorical endings — "X is dead" — when in reality the change is more gradual, geographically specific, or partial. Research on media narrative framing by Entman (1993, Journal of Communication) established that the frames journalists and social media users choose substantially determine what audiences believe is true. When the loss of five UK music video channels gets framed as "MTV shut down," the categorical framing replaces the more accurate but harder to convey "several of MTV's international 24-hour music channels ceased linear broadcasting." The former fits in a tweet; the latter requires several sentences to convey accurately.

**Epistemic novelty: the nostalgia amplification effect.** A genuinely underappreciated mechanism driving the viral spread of this claim is what might be called the nostalgic amplification effect — the way that claims involving the imputed death of culturally beloved institutions generate sharing behaviour that goes far beyond what ordinary factual claims would produce. People did not share the "MTV shut down" posts merely because they believed them to be true. They shared them because the idea of MTV shutting down was emotionally significant, connecting to memories of adolescence, shared cultural experience, and the particular form of collective loss that attends the disappearance of broadcast media that shaped formative years. This is qualitatively different from the sharing dynamics around, say, a claim about a corporate merger. The research on social sharing motivations by Berger and Milkman (2012, Journal of Marketing Research) identifies high-arousal emotions — including nostalgia-inflected sadness and surprise — as among the strongest drivers of content sharing, regardless of the sharer's confidence in the claim's accuracy.

**The lack of granular familiarity with international broadcasting.** Most of the people who shared the "MTV shut down" claim were not closely familiar with the structure of MTV's UK and international channel portfolio. Without knowing that MTV UK had five specialist music channels plus a flagship entertainment channel, it is difficult to understand why the closure of the specialist channels is partial rather than total. The claim succeeded partly because it required a level of familiarity with the granular architecture of international pay-TV portfolios that most social media users reasonably lacked.

## Verdict

The claim is a mixture: partially true and partially false. MTV's 24-hour dedicated music channels in the United Kingdom did close on or around New Year's Eve 2025, with closures also confirmed in Ireland, France, Germany, Austria, Poland, Hungary, Australia, and Brazil (Tapp, 2026, Deadline). The Buggles' "Video Killed the Radio Star" was played as the final broadcast on the UK music channels — a genuine, documented moment. These closures are real and significant.

However, MTV had not shut down completely. The flagship MTV UK channel continued broadcasting. In the United States, MTV and MTV2 remained on air, primarily airing reality programming, while MTV Classic continued to show music videos (Ibrahim, 2026, Snopes.com). The MTV brand persisted across Paramount's streaming service and social media channels. Snopes rated the claim a "Mixture."

The fuller picture is that what shut down was specifically the last operational version of the original MTV editorial model — continuous music video programming on linear television — in several international markets. The MTV brand as a broader commercial entity remained active. Whether that distinction is meaningful or merely cosmetic depends on what you believe MTV essentially is.

## The Wider Picture

The partial closure of MTV's international music channels in late 2025 is a data point in a longer and more significant structural story about how audiences consume music video and music discovery content, and what that shift means for the institutions that built their identity around linear music video broadcasting.

**The structural shift away from linear broadcasting.** The trajectory MTV has followed — from music video service to reality TV broadcaster to nostalgic brand in the streaming era — mirrors the trajectory of cable television generally. The specific cause of the UK music channel closures, identified by Paramount's own spokesperson, was "shifts in audience behaviour towards streaming and digital platforms." This is an accurate description of a decades-long trend. Audiences who want to watch music videos in 2025 use YouTube, Vevo, TikTok, and streaming platforms — none of which require a cable subscription or a specific broadcast time slot. The linear music video channel as a format was already vestigial; the UK closures represent the formal acknowledgment of a shift that had been underway for twenty years.

**MTV's historical significance.** Understanding why the closures generated the emotional response they did requires some grounding in what MTV actually was during its prime. When MTV launched on August 1, 1981, its first broadcast was "Video Killed the Radio Star" — chosen as a knowing wink about the new medium's arrival. The channel operated 24 hours a day, playing music videos across genres, and in doing so created a new art form (the music video as a mainstream commercial medium) and a new distribution infrastructure for recorded music that eventually rivalled radio in cultural influence. Research on MTV's role in popularising music video aesthetics by Banks (1996, in *Monopoly Television: MTV's Quest to Control the Music*) documents how the channel substantially shaped the visual grammar of popular music in the 1980s. Artists who could exploit MTV's format — Madonna, Michael Jackson, Duran Duran, Peter Gabriel — achieved a kind of fame qualitatively different from what radio alone provided. The channel made the visual component of popular music central rather than supplemental.

**Paramount's financial context.** The closures cannot be understood without the context of Paramount Global's financial difficulties in 2024–25. A $500 million cost reduction effort (BBC, 2025) signals an organisation under significant pressure. The international pay-TV channel portfolio, which requires ongoing licensing, staffing, and transmission costs for channels whose audiences had substantially migrated to digital platforms, represented a logical candidate for cuts. The closure of the dedicated music channels — rather than the flagship entertainment channels — reflects an attempt to preserve the brand-level presence (MTV UK as a general entertainment channel) while eliminating the more specialised and less commercially defensible linear music format.

**The streaming successor question.** A question that the channel closures pose but do not directly answer is whether music video programming has a viable future in any format beyond the aggregated, algorithm-curated model of YouTube and Vevo. MTV launched with a curatorial model — human programmers choosing which videos to play — at a moment when that curation had high value because alternatives were scarce. In 2025, the music video discovery landscape is dominated by algorithmic platforms that serve individual tastes at scale. Whether there is a market for editorially curated music video content — a twenty-first century version of what MTV originally provided — is an open question that the closures do not definitively answer.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check on the MTV closure claim (Ibrahim, 2026, Snopes.com) is a good example of the "Mixture" verdict being used to resist the binary pressure that social media claims typically exert.

**The Mixture rating as an analytical tool.** Many fact-check claims are structured to resist a simple True/False verdict: the facts on which they rest are mixed, geographically specific, temporally qualified, or accurate in one formulation but inaccurate in another. The "MTV shut down" claim is exactly this type: it was simultaneously true (specific music channels did shut down) and false (MTV as an entity did not shut down). The Mixture rating allows Snopes to acknowledge both truths without collapsing them into a single verdict that would misrepresent one of them. This is epistemically correct behaviour, and it demonstrates one of the genuine strengths of the Snopes format.

**Primary source checking.** Ibrahim's fact-check relied on the schedule available on MTV's own website (mtv.com and mtv.co.uk), archived screenshots confirming which channels were still operational, Variety's interview with an unnamed Paramount spokesperson, and Deadline's reporting on the specific international closures. The use of the channel's own scheduling data as primary evidence is appropriate and efficient. The fact that MTV Classic's schedule still showed music videos on January 2, 2026 is direct evidence that something was still broadcasting, regardless of what had happened to the UK music channels.

**The "update pending" limitation.** The Snopes article noted that Paramount had been asked to confirm exactly which international markets had seen closures and that the article would be updated. This is a transparent acknowledgment of an investigative gap that the public statement from Variety's source only partially filled. The responsible practice of flagging pending verification is exemplary, even if the verification gap means the article does not fully resolve the international closure picture. Good fact-checking acknowledges uncertainty rather than filling it with assumption.

**What the verdict does not capture.** The Mixture rating accurately describes the binary claim ("all MTV channels shut down" — false; "some MTV channels shut down" — true). What it does not capture, and cannot in a verdict label, is the cultural significance of what did close. The UK music video channels were not merely "some" channels in an indifferent corporate portfolio — they were the final operational expression of the programming philosophy that made MTV historically significant. The fact that they closed without Paramount issuing a public statement, and that the primary cultural acknowledgment of their ending came from a BBC journalist's social media video of "Video Killed the Radio Star," is itself a story about how media institutions that were once culturally central are now handled at the level of routine corporate portfolio management. That story is real and matters, even if it falls outside the scope of a binary verdict on whether MTV "shut down."
