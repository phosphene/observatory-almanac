# Is Parmesan Cheese Made Using the Stomach Linings of Calves?


## 1. The Claim

In early January 2023, a wave of social media users—primarily on Twitter and Facebook—expressed surprise, and in many cases distress, upon learning that some brands of Parmesan cheese contain rennet, an enzyme complex extracted from the stomach lining of young calves. The discovery prompted a minor viral moment, with posts ranging from genuine shock ("Today years old when I found out Parmesan cheese is made from baby cow's stomach & I could go cry," tweeted @dtheebae on January 9, 2023) to media coverage from outlets including The Sun and the Daily Mail. Many posts cited PETA's documentation of the practice.

The underlying claim is specific: that some Parmesan cheeses—and specifically those bearing the authentic designation of Parmigiano-Reggiano—are produced using animal rennet, an enzyme derived from the inner stomach lining (abomasum) of pre-weaned calves. This claim is not simply about one ingredient in one cheese. It intersects with questions about EU food law, the status of Protected Designation of Origin (PDO) products, the definition of vegetarian food, the ethics of dairy production, and the commercial landscape of alternatives.

What makes this claim more complex than a simple ingredient disclosure is its relationship to regulatory identity. Parmigiano-Reggiano is governed by a European PDO designation that has been in effect since 1996, predating but reinforced by EU Regulation 1151/2012 on quality schemes for agricultural products. Under these designations, the methods of production—including the use of calf rennet—are not merely conventional practices but legally protected definitional requirements for the use of the name. A Parmesan cheese made with microbial or vegetarian rennet would not legally qualify as Parmigiano-Reggiano in the EU. The claim thus requires untangling the difference between "some Parmesan is made this way" and "all authentic Parmigiano-Reggiano is made this way," which are very different statements with different implications for consumers making dietary choices.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes rates the claim as **True**, and Damakant Jayshi's (2023) analysis correctly confirms the essential fact: authentic Parmigiano-Reggiano does require calf rennet under its PDO designation. This is documented by the NZ Vegetarian Society (2013 archival post: "Parmesan cheese is an EU 'protected designation of origin' product and has to be made using calf rennet, so it's definitely not suitable for vegetarians"), The Guardian (Schofield, 2012), The Vegetarian Society UK, and PETA (2017). The information has been publicly available for over a decade before the 2023 viral moment; BuzzFeed's Delaney Strunk documented her own discovery in 2019.

The Snopes article also correctly notes that animal rennet is used in several other traditional European cheeses, including Gorgonzola, Grana Padano, Pecorino Romano, Camembert, Vacherin, Emmenthaler, Gruyère, and Manchego—a list that encompasses many of the most culturally significant European cheese varieties. For vegetarians and vegans, this list represents a significant portion of what would seem to be dairy-only products that nonetheless contain slaughterhouse byproducts.

Snopes also provides appropriate reassurance that vegan Parmesan alternatives exist and that not all cheeses use animal rennet. Many commercial Parmesan-style cheeses sold outside the EU, and many supermarket cheeses labeled "Parmesan" in the United States (which does not enforce the PDO designation), use microbial rennet, vegetable rennet, or fermentation-derived chymosin and may therefore be suitable for vegetarians. The detail matters greatly for dietary practice: a consumer in the United States buying a Kraft "Parmesan" product is purchasing a different cheese from a legal and production standpoint than authentic Parmigiano-Reggiano imported from Italy, even if both containers say "Parmesan."

The core finding is well-established: genuine Parmigiano-Reggiano = calf rennet = not vegetarian. The claim is true with respect to the authentic designation.


## 3. Epistemic Analysis: What the Verdict Leaves Unexplored

Snopes' account is accurate and provides good consumer guidance, but it omits three significant analytical dimensions: the biochemistry of rennet and why it matters to cheese production, the history and current landscape of rennet alternatives and what their existence implies, and the tension between PDO protection and evolving dietary ethics.

**The Biochemistry of Rennet**

Rennet is not a single substance but an enzyme complex. The primary active component is chymosin (also called rennin), a protease that cleaves κ-casein, a protein on the surface of milk's casein micelles. This cleavage alters the electrostatic properties of the micelles, causing them to aggregate—a process that initiates coagulation, separating milk into curds and whey. Foltmann, B. (1992, "Chymosin: A short review on foetal and neonatal gastric proteases," Scandinavian Journal of Clinical and Laboratory Investigation, Supplement 210) established the biochemical role of chymosin in the developing calf's digestive system: it is produced by the abomasum specifically to curdle the cow's milk in the calf's stomach, converting liquid milk into a semi-solid form that the calf can more efficiently absorb. Human cheesemakers, discovering this clotting property empirically thousands of years ago, began sourcing the enzyme by harvesting and drying the stomachs of slaughtered calves.

The reason traditional Parmigiano-Reggiano uses calf rennet—and why this is codified in the PDO rules—relates to both historical continuity and flavor chemistry. Calf rennet contains not only chymosin but also pepsin and other proteolytic enzymes that affect the development of flavor compounds during the long aging process that characterizes Parmigiano-Reggiano (minimum 12 months, with premium varieties aged 24–36 months or more). The specific enzyme profile of calf rennet contributes to the development of glutamate-rich umami compounds, free fatty acids, and volatile flavor compounds that define the cheese's sensory profile. Researcher cheese biochemists including Fox, P.F. et al. (2000, *Fundamentals of Cheese Science*, Springer) have documented how different types of rennet produce different proteolysis profiles during aging, which is why cheesemakers producing PDO cheeses insist on the traditional enzyme source.

**The History and Current Landscape of Rennet Alternatives**

Animal rennet represented the dominant commercial coagulant source until the late 20th century, but the global demand for cheese exceeded the available supply of calf stomachs (particularly as veal production declined following consumer welfare concerns in several markets) and drove sustained investment in alternatives. Three major commercial alternatives now dominate the non-PDO cheese market:

*Microbial rennet* derived from molds (*Rhizomucor miehei* and related species) was commercially developed in the 1960s. It produces a less refined proteolysis profile than calf rennet and at high concentrations can contribute bitter flavor notes during aging, limiting its use in long-aged cheeses—which is one reason why PDO requirements for aged varieties specifically mandate animal rennet.

*Fermentation-derived chymosin (FPC)* represents the most significant technological development. In 1990, the FDA approved the first genetically engineered food enzyme: chymosin produced by inserting the bovine prochymosin gene into fermentation hosts including *Escherichia coli*, *Aspergillus niger*, and the yeast *Kluyveromyces lactis*. The resulting chymosin is biochemically identical to the calf-derived enzyme but produced entirely through microbial fermentation. By the 2010s, FPC accounted for approximately 80–90% of the global supply of rennet used in cheese production (Johnson, M.E. & Lucey, J.A., 2006, Journal of Dairy Science). FPC-produced cheese is suitable for vegetarians under most vegetarian definitions, though it is technically produced through genetic engineering, which creates separate concerns for some consumers.

*Vegetable rennet* derived from fig latex, thistles, and other plant sources has been used in traditional cheesemaking in some Mediterranean and Atlantic regions for centuries and continues in artisanal production contexts, though it lacks the enzymatic uniformity of commercial coagulants.

The existence of this diverse coagulant landscape means that "Parmesan" in the commercial supermarket context almost certainly refers to cheese produced with FPC or microbial rennet, not calf rennet—while "Parmigiano-Reggiano" retains the animal rennet requirement under EU law.

**The PDO Tension with Evolving Dietary Ethics**

The PDO system codifies traditional production methods with the goal of protecting geographic and cultural heritage in food. This is a legitimate and valuable policy objective; it prevents dilution of products with long cultural histories and supports regional agricultural economies. However, when traditional methods include animal-derived components that were not ethically contested at the time of codification, the rigidity of PDO specifications creates a structural tension with evolving dietary ethics. A vegetarian consumer who discovers that they cannot eat authentic Parmigiano-Reggiano is not encountering a surprising new fact—they are encountering the consequence of a regulatory system that locked in 1996 methods that many consumers in 2023 would not consider consistent with vegetarianism.

The epistemic novelty here is this: the viral "shock" response to the Parmesan rennet disclosure in 2023 is itself evidence of a mismatch between consumer dietary identity (many people identify as "vegetarian" but eat cheese, implicitly assuming dairy products do not contain slaughterhouse derivatives) and regulatory reality (EU food law has made it expressly clear for nearly three decades that authentic Parmigiano-Reggiano contains an animal slaughter byproduct). This mismatch is not primarily an informational problem—the information has been publicly available—but a categorization problem. The folk category "vegetarian" in most English-speaking dietary practice means "no meat," while the religious and cultural category more broadly means "no products derived from animal slaughter." Rennet, derived from a slaughtered calf's stomach, falls clearly within the stricter definition but ambiguously within the looser one.


## 4. The Scientific and Historical Record

Cheese production originated in the Fertile Crescent approximately 7,500 years ago, according to archaeological evidence from pottery fragments analyzed by Evershed, R.P. et al. (2008, Nature, "Earliest date for milk use in the Near East and southeastern Europe linked to cattle herding"). Early cheesemakers likely discovered coagulation accidentally when storing milk in pouches made from animal stomachs and observing that the milk separated into curds. This empirical discovery initiated millennia of dairy practice in which animal stomach contents—and eventually the stomachs themselves—were deliberately used as coagulants.

Parmigiano-Reggiano's production history extends to at least the 12th century, with documented references to cheese from the Parma and Reggio Emilia regions appearing in monastic records. The characteristic hard, granular texture and extended aging were already established by the 14th century. The EU's PDO designation, formalized in 1996, codified production practices that had evolved over centuries without significant technological change.

The ethical concern with animal rennet production relates to the source: the abomasum (fourth stomach compartment) is harvested from slaughtered pre-weaned calves, typically male veal calves. The veal industry intersects with dairy production structurally: dairy cows must give birth annually to maintain milk production, and male dairy calves have no role in milk production. In agricultural systems that do not raise male dairy calves for beef or veal, these animals are typically killed shortly after birth—meaning the rennet harvest represents a secondary use of animals that would otherwise be discarded from the production process, which some argue mitigates but does not eliminate the ethical concern.

Snopes correctly notes the 2016 fact-check about wood pulp (cellulose) in grated Parmesan as a related consumer disclosure item, demonstrating a recurring pattern of public shock when the industrial realities of food production become legible.


## 5. EARC Assessment: Where Snopes Adds Value and Where the Gap Lies

**Epistemic Value Added:** Snopes' confirmation is valuable primarily because many consumers who identified as vegetarian, or who were cooking for vegetarian guests, had either never heard this information or had forgotten it. The article correctly frames the information as "not new" (noting documentation going back to at least a 2012 Guardian article and a 2013 Vegetarian Society post) while acknowledging the recurring cycle of public discovery. Providing a list of other affected cheeses beyond Parmesan extends the utility of the article considerably.

**Epistemic Gap — Analytically Incomplete (A):** The article accurately confirms the claim but leaves the most intellectually consequential questions completely unaddressed: the biochemistry of why chymosin matters to cheese production (and why PDO rules mandate it), the rennet alternatives landscape and what it implies for consumer choice, and the tension between traditional food regulations and evolving dietary ethics. These dimensions convert a simple "true" determination into a rich exploration of food science, regulatory policy, and dietary ethics.

**Gap Category: Distorted-but-Grounded** — The claim is not distorted by the viral posts in the sense that the factual core is accurate. The "distortion" is a kind of displacement: the shock cycle treats familiar information as new, and the viral moment creates the impression that a hidden secret has been revealed when the information was never hidden. The gap lies in explaining why this cycle of revelation-and-forgetting recurs for foods with complex production processes.


## 6. Verdict and Summary

The claim is **True**. Authentic Parmigiano-Reggiano, governed by EU Protected Designation of Origin rules established in 1996, must be produced using calf rennet—an enzyme complex extracted from the stomach lining (abomasum) of pre-weaned calves. This makes authentic Parmigiano-Reggiano unsuitable for vegetarians who define vegetarianism as excluding products derived from animal slaughter, including slaughterhouse byproducts.

This is not new information. It has been documented by the Guardian (2012), the New Zealand Vegetarian Society (2013), BuzzFeed (2019), and PETA (ongoing). The 2023 viral shock cycle represents a recurring pattern of public rediscovery rather than genuine disclosure of previously hidden information. The same phenomenon applies to other PDO-designated cheeses including Gorgonzola, Grana Padano, and Gruyère, among others.

The practical consumer implication is straightforward: verify the rennet source on any cheese you purchase if this distinction matters to your diet. In the United States, cheeses labeled "Parmesan" are not regulated under EU PDO rules and are frequently produced with microbial rennet or fermentation-derived chymosin (FPC), making them potentially suitable for vegetarians. Only authentic Parmigiano-Reggiano imported from Italy and bearing the official PDO mark is legally required to use calf rennet.

The broader lesson is that traditional food production carries dietary implications that modern labeling systems do not always make transparent. Rennet is not generally required to appear prominently on cheese labels in either the US or EU, meaning consumers who care about the source of this ingredient must actively investigate. For a culturally significant food category—hard aged cheeses—the dominant global coagulant is now fermentation-derived chymosin, making animal rennet the minority practice even as it remains the legally mandated one for the most prestigious designations.
