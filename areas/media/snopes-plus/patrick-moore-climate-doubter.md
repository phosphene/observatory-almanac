# Greenpeace co-founder Patrick Moore proves climate change is a hoax


## 1. The Claim

Across conservative media, climate-sceptic websites, congressional testimony, viral social media graphics, and fossil fuel industry communications, Patrick Moore is presented as a uniquely credentialed authority whose personal rejection of the scientific consensus on climate change constitutes strong evidence — in some versions, definitive proof — that anthropogenic warming is a fabrication or exaggeration.

The claim rests on a specific biographical framing: that Moore was a founding member of Greenpeace, the iconic international environmental organisation that has made anthropogenic climate change a central focus of its campaigning, and that his personal journey from environmental activist to climate sceptic therefore represents an insider's exposure of the fraudulent foundations of mainstream environmentalism and climate science. The rhetorical logic is: if even the co-founder of Greenpeace has concluded that climate change is a hoax, then the foundations of the scientific and environmental establishment's position must be genuinely questionable, and ordinary people are justified in doubt or dismissal without engaging with the scientific evidence itself.

Moore has been given this platform by entities including the Global Climate Intelligence Group (Clintel), the CO2 Coalition, the Cato Institute, the Heartland Institute, and multiple Republican congressional committees, where he has testified against climate science. In 2019, then-US President Donald Trump tweeted an endorsement of Moore's views, extending the claim to an audience of tens of millions. Moore himself has promoted the claim actively, including in documentary films and interviews in which he has described CO2 as "the currency of life," called climate science "a powerful convergence of interests among governments, scientists, and the media," and asserted that the IPCC is driven by politics rather than science.

Versions of the claim that circulate in social media typically omit the financial and organisational details of Moore's post-Greenpeace career and present his credentials purely through his claimed Greenpeace founding role and a PhD in ecology. Some versions assert that Moore was Greenpeace's founding president or that his departure from the organisation was driven by the organisation being "taken over by extremists" who abandoned science in favour of political activism — a narrative that positions Moore's dissent as scientifically principled rather than ideologically or financially motivated.


## 2. What's Actually True

### Moore's actual role at Greenpeace

The claim that Moore "co-founded" Greenpeace is contested and significantly overstated. Greenpeace was founded in 1971 in Vancouver, Canada, when a group of activists sailed a fishing vessel, the *Phyllis Cormack*, to Amchitka Island in Alaska to protest a United States nuclear test. The primary organising figures generally credited with founding the organisation include Robert Hunter, Jim Bohlen, Paul Cote, Irving Stowe, and Ben Metcalfe — the core of the group that formed the "Don't Make a Wave Committee" which became Greenpeace. Moore, then a 24-year-old graduate student, was among the approximately dozen crew members who participated in the Amchitka voyage and was involved in early Greenpeace Canada operations in subsequent years, including serving on the executive committee. He rose to lead Greenpeace Canada by the late 1970s and was involved in international Greenpeace coordination in the early 1980s.

Greenpeace has formally disputed Moore's claim to the title of co-founder, issuing statements noting that he was a participant in early campaigns rather than an organisational founder and that he played no role in the founding of Greenpeace International. Rex Weyler, an early Greenpeace participant and the author of *Greenpeace: How a Group of Ecologists, Journalists, and Visionaries Changed the World* (Rodale Books, 2004) — the definitive historical account of the organisation's founding — has specifically addressed Moore's claims, stating that Moore arrived after the organisation was already constituted and that referring to him as a founder misrepresents the historical record. The first Greenpeace communication recognising Moore as a "co-founder" appears to have been generated largely by Moore himself and the public relations materials produced by organisations promoting his climate scepticism after his departure from Greenpeace in 1986.

### Why Moore left Greenpeace

Moore has consistently described his departure from Greenpeace as driven by the organisation's move away from science-based environmentalism toward political ideology. Greenpeace has given a different account: that Moore left to pursue commercially conflicting activities, including work for the British Columbia salmon farming industry, the logging industry, and nuclear energy companies. Moore's post-Greenpeace consulting work concentrated in industries whose social acceptability environmental organisations contest, creating a documented pattern of using his former environmental credentials to provide "pro-environment expert" cover for extractive and industrial interests. This is a career pattern recognisable from the history of professional climate and environmental contrarianism documented by Oreskes & Conway (2010, *Merchants of Doubt*, Bloomsbury Publishing).

### Moore's scientific qualifications in climate science

Moore holds a PhD in ecology from the University of British Columbia (1974), which is a genuine academic credential. His doctoral research concerned the ecology of marine benthic communities — specifically the effects of industrial effluent on intertidal invertebrate assemblages in Burrard Inlet. This is legitimate field ecology with no direct bearing on atmospheric physics, climate modelling, or the Earth system science disciplines that form the basis of anthropogenic climate change research. His subsequent publications in ecology are from the 1970s; he has no peer-reviewed publications in climate science, atmospheric science, ocean chemistry, or any of the subdisciplines whose findings constitute the body of evidence supporting the consensus on anthropogenic warming.

The appeal to Moore's authority as a scientist who disputes the consensus is therefore doubly flawed: first, his primary credential claim (co-founding Greenpeace) is exaggerated; and second, even accepting his PhD in ecology at face value, an ecologist's dissent from the findings of atmospheric physics, oceanography, and palaeoclimatology carries no more evidential weight than a cardiologist's dissent from a finding in astrophysics. Scientific expertise is field-specific; a credential in one discipline does not constitute expertise in others.

Anderegg, Prall, Harold & Schneider (2010, *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 107(27), pp. 12107–12109) specifically examined the expertise composition of the group of researchers who had signed public statements opposing the climate consensus and found that this group had substantially lower publication rates and citation levels in climate-relevant disciplines than researchers who supported the consensus. Pat Moore is not credited as a researcher in any of the primary climate science literature, contrary to frequent implicit suggestions in promotional materials.

### The scientific consensus he disputes

The consensus Moore disputes rests on multiple independent evidence streams, none of which depend on the personal views of any environmental activist, founding or otherwise. The basic atmospheric physics — that CO2 and other greenhouse gases absorb outgoing infrared radiation — was established by Eunice Newton Foote (1856, *American Journal of Science and Arts*, 22, pp. 382–383) and John Tyndall (1861, *Philosophical Magazine*, 22, pp. 169–194) in the nineteenth century and has been reproduced in thousands of subsequent spectroscopic and laboratory measurements. The attribution of observed warming to human emissions is supported by the spatial fingerprint analysis (Santer et al., 1996, *Nature*, 382, pp. 39–46), oxygen isotope ratios that distinguish fossil-fuel-derived CO2 from other sources (Keeling, 1979, *Tellus*, 31(2), pp. 174–199), the simultaneous stratospheric cooling and tropospheric warming consistent exclusively with greenhouse forcing, and the consistency between observed warming and the projections made by general circulation models run without knowledge of what would subsequently be observed. None of these findings are affected by Patrick Moore's personal testimony in any direction.

Cook, Nuccitelli, Green, Richardson, Winkler, Painting, Way, Jacobs & Skuce (2013, *Environmental Research Letters*, 8(2), 024024) examined 11,944 peer-reviewed abstracts on climate science published from 1991 to 2011 and found that 97.1 percent of those that took a position endorsed the consensus that humans are causing recent warming. The consensus is not derived from the convictions of environmental activists; it is the cumulative product of thousands of independent research groups publishing reproducible findings across atmospheric physics, oceanography, glaciology, palaeoclimatology, and related fields.

### Financial conflicts of interest

Moore's corporate affiliations after Greenpeace have included paid advisory roles with the Nuclear Energy Institute, consulting work on behalf of salmon farming companies, work reportedly connected to chlorine industry interests (Lubchenco, 2006), and most significantly sustained institutional connection with fossil fuel industry-funded organisations. The CO2 Coalition, with which Moore has affiliations, has received funding from foundations associated with fossil fuel interests. The Heartland Institute, with which he has participated in events, has received funding from ExxonMobil and the Koch network. Brulle (2014, *Climatic Change*, 122(4), pp. 681–694) traced approximately $558 million per year in documented funding to a network of organisations producing contrarian climate communications, with fossil fuel-connected foundations as major donors.

These financial relationships are almost universally absent from the social media graphics, news appearances, and documentary films presenting Moore as an independent authority who has come to his climate scepticism through scientific conviction. Full disclosure of these affiliations would substantially alter the audience's ability to evaluate his testimony as independent expertise.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The appeal-to-authority heuristic and the celebrity defector.** Human reasoning is fundamentally social, and dissent from a prominent insider — particularly one who was historically associated with the organisation being criticised — carries powerful persuasive weight independent of the substantive merits of the dissenting argument. The rhetorical category of the "credible defector" is well-established in propaganda and persuasion: an argument gains credibility from the apparent sacrifice or credibility risk taken by the person making it. A former Greenpeace founder criticising the climate consensus is the defector archetype: the implicit argument is that he has staked his reputation and former activist identity on this view, therefore he must believe it strongly, and that conviction is evidence of its validity.

Cialdini (1984, *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*, William Morrow) documented the authority heuristic — deferring to perceived expertise as a cognitive shortcut — as one of the most reliable and exploitable drivers of persuasion. The heuristic misfires here because neither the activist credential (Greenpeace participation) nor the academic credential (ecology PhD) are relevant to the specific scientific question being disputed (atmospheric physics and global climate attribution). But because audiences are typically not equipped to evaluate the specific disciplinary relevance of credentials — a general ecology PhD versus publishing in *Journal of Geophysical Research: Atmospheres* — the misapplication of the authority heuristic is effectively invisible to those it misleads.

**Greenpeace as a culturally loaded symbol.** For conservative and sceptical-of-environmentalism audiences, Greenpeace represents an extreme of environmental politics. The appeal of Moore's position is partly the apparent reversal: if the extremists themselves have come to doubt, there must be something so obviously wrong that even they can no longer ignore it. This works symmetrically regardless of whether the audience is sympathetic or hostile to environmentalism: for those hostile to it, Moore confirms their prior doubts; for those with no strong prior view, he serves as an authority apparently without the political motivation that might bias pro-environment scientists.

**The narrative of suppressed insider knowledge.** Moore presents his account as an insider exposé of how environmental organisations and climate scientists have captured institutions and produced a consensus that serves political and financial interests rather than scientific truth. This narrative — that a brave dissenter is exposing what institutions want hidden — is among the most reliable templates for viral information spread. Sunstein & Vermeule (2009, *Journal of Political Philosophy*, 17(2), pp. 202–227) and Goertzel (2010, *Psychological Reports*, 106(3), pp. 733–736) examined the psychology of conspiracy acceptance and noted that the irreducibility of conspiracy claims — they cannot be refuted by institutional statements because institutions are presumed complicit — makes them self-reinforcing once adopted.

Lewandowsky, Ecker & Cook (2017, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 26(4), pp. 353–362) documented that corrections of belief claims based on authority misuse require not just providing the correct information but specifically addressing the false authority claim, because simply presenting evidence is insufficient when the audience's confidence lies not in that evidence but in the perceived credibility of the person dismissing it.


## 4. Verdict

**False.** The claim that Patrick Moore's dissent from the scientific consensus on climate change provides evidence that climate change is a hoax fails on every component.

Moore's credential claim — Greenpeace co-founder — is substantially overstated. He was a participant in an early Greenpeace campaign and a later organisational officer, not a formative founding figure, and Greenpeace has disputed this characterisation. His academic credentials are in marine ecology, a field with no direct bearing on the atmospheric physics, ocean chemistry, or climate modelling that form the evidentiary basis of the consensus he disputes. His post-Greenpeace career shows sustained financial relationships with industries that have economic interests in undermining climate science and policy, creating conflicts of interest that are routinely omitted from presentations of his views as independent expertise.

Even setting aside these specific defects, the claim embeds a fundamental logical error: the argument from authority. Scientific consensus is not established or undermined by the personal testimony of any individual, however credentialled. It is established by reproducible empirical findings from independent research groups. No individual's dissent constitutes "proof" that those findings are wrong; evaluating that would require engaging with the findings themselves. The 97 percent consensus on anthropogenic climate change documented in peer-reviewed literature reflects the weight of evidence, not the convictions of environmental advocates. It would remain at that level whether Patrick Moore maintained his Greenpeace membership or not.


## 5. The Wider Picture

Moore's career arc — from environmental movement participant to well-funded industry-aligned contrarian — is a documented archetype in the history of strategic doubt manufacture in science. Oreskes & Conway (2010, *Merchants of Doubt*, Bloomsbury Publishing) profiled several scientists, including Frederick Seitz and Fred Singer, who used academic credentials from one field to dispute scientific consensus in another while receiving substantial funding from industries with economic stakes in those disputes, following a template established by tobacco industry consultants in the 1950s and 1960s. The template involves: (a) a credential that implies scientific legitimacy to general audiences; (b) institutional positioning within organisations that appear independent but are industry-funded; (c) active media engagement that is proportionally more extensive than genuine expertise would warrant; and (d) framing of dissent as courageous independence against institutional orthodoxy.

The effectiveness of this template depends on the gap between how expertise signals work for specialists versus general audiences. Within atmospheric science, whether Patrick Moore endorses or disputes the warming findings is irrelevant to the evidence; no climate scientist regards his position as scientifically meaningful. For general audiences reasoning by authority heuristics, his framing as a credentialled authority dissenting from a consensus carries substantial persuasive weight precisely because the conditions that would make his opinion relevant — expertise, independence, engagement with the primary evidence — are exactly what is absent. Bridging this gap requires equipping audiences not only with the factual errors in the claim but with the basic sociology of expertise: understanding why a credential in one field does not confer authority in another, and why financial conflicts of interest require disclosure before a testimony can be evaluated as independent.

Boykoff (2011, *Who Speaks for the Climate? Making Sense of Media Reporting on Climate Change*, Cambridge University Press) documented patterns in media coverage of climate science that systematically amplified contrarian voices beyond their numerical or evidential weight in the scientific community, a pattern driven partly by journalistic norms of balance and partly by contrarian voices' active media engagement relative to working scientists occupied with research rather than public advocacy. Moore's high media profile relative to his scientific contribution is a direct product of this dynamic.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Patrick Moore's claims about Greenpeace and climate change have been assessed by multiple fact-checking organisations with consistent verdicts.

Greenpeace USA issued fact-checking documents specifically addressing Moore's claim to be a co-founder, stating directly that the organisation's structure and founding history do not support this characterisation and listing the figures who are documented as central to the organisation's founding. These documents are publicly available at greenpeace.org. Greenpeace also documented Moore's post-Greenpeace industry consulting work and the organisations that have funded his contrarian activities.

Climate Feedback scientists have reviewed articles and media appearances featuring Moore's climate claims and responded regarding his credentials (relevant to ecology, not climate physics) and the specific factual claims he makes (for example, that CO2 is not a pollutant and that warming is beneficial, which are contested claims within the scientific literature). Their assessments consistently note that the claims lack supporting peer-reviewed literature.

MediaMatters has documented Moore's use of the Greenpeace co-founder credential in testimony before the US Congress and in media appearances, noting the discrepancy between the claimed credential and the historical record and documenting his financial affiliations with fossil fuel-connected foundations.

For media literacy educators, the Moore case is particularly useful for teaching the credential-relevance requirement as a component of authority evaluation: it is not sufficient to note that a source has credentials of any kind; the credential must be relevant to the specific claim being made. An academic who has done field ecology research on British Columbia intertidal invertebrates does not have a relevant credential for evaluating general circulation models or atmospheric CO2 radiative forcing. Teaching audiences to ask "relevant credentials for this specific claim?" rather than "credentials in general?" substantially reduces the persuasive force of this class of misrepresentation.

The full scientific case on anthropogenic climate change rests on the published peer-reviewed literature in atmospheric physics, oceanography, palaeoclimatology, glaciology, and related disciplines — none of which is affected by the personal testimony of any non-contributing commentator, regardless of their biographical history with environmental organisations.
