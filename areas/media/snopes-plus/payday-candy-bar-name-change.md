## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

Beginning in the summer of 2020 and circulating in renewed waves through early 2021, a meme spread widely on social media claiming that the Payday candy bar—a caramel-and-salted-peanut bar that has been on the American market since 1932—was having its name changed because the name "Payday" was considered offensive to unemployed people. The meme typically presented this claim as a news item or announcement, fitting within a genre of satirical cancel-culture content that parodied real name-change decisions made by brands during the same period, most notably Mars' announcement that it would rebrand Aunt Jemima syrup, Uncle Ben's rice, and other food products criticized for referencing racial stereotypes.

Snopes investigator Dan Evon (2021) assigned this claim the rating **False**. The Payday candy bar was not changing its name. Evon confirmed that The Hershey Company, which manufactures Payday, had made no announcements of any name change. The claim originated not as a genuine corporate announcement but as a satirical internet joke posted to AmericasBestPics.com—a website that publishes comic content, including entirely fabricated news items. The earliest versions of Payday name-change memes appear to trace to at least summer 2020—months before the February 2021 wave that prompted the Snopes investigation.

This entry examines the evidentiary basis for Evon's debunking, the historical context of the Payday bar and its Depression-era origins, the broader cultural phenomenon of cancel-culture parody memes that target product names, the epistemic novelty of satirical fabrication as a distinct category of false claim, and the sociology of meme recycling in social media environments.

## §2 The Documentary Evidence: A Meme, Not an Announcement

The Payday name-change claim lacks any evidentiary foundation in corporate or regulatory record. Evon (2021) confirmed that The Hershey Company had made no announcement of a name change at any point before or at the time of publication. No press release, media statement, earnings call comment, trademark filing, or product packaging change corroborates the claim. The Hershey Company's public communications infrastructure—including its investor relations filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission, its press office, and its product website—contain no reference to any reconsidering of the Payday brand name for any reason.

The claim's origin is traceable to AmericasBestPics.com, a website Evon (2021) characterized as posting "humorous (and sometimes entirely fabricated) content." Additional research into the earliest versions of the meme extends the timeline backward to at least the summer of 2020, when a Facebook page called "With Age Comes Wisdom And Some Really Funny Shit" posted a version of the joke (Evon, 2021). The meme's textual structure follows a template common to satirical product-rename jokes: it inverts the stated rationale for genuine brand renaming decisions (which typically address racial or ethnic representation concerns) and applies it to a politically neutral product name that incidentally touches on a different social category (employment status).

The specific mechanism—claiming that a food brand is renaming a product because the existing name is offensive to some demographic group—belongs to a distinctive genre of social media satire that emerged in 2020 following actual major brand renamings. The renaming decisions that immediately preceded and likely inspired this genre include Mars' announcement in June 2020 that it would rebrand Aunt Jemima syrup (to Pearl Milling Company) and Uncle Ben's rice (to Ben's Original), and the retirement of the Washington Redskins NFL team name in the same month. The Payday meme genre requires no detailed knowledge of these specific cases; it only requires awareness that such renamings were happening, and deploys that awareness as a satirical vehicle.

The Payday bar's actual history provides no plausible justification for an "offensive" name argument outside of the satirical framing. The name "Payday" is a straightforward reference to the financial concept of a paycheck receipt date—an American English compound noun with no ethnic, racial, folk-stereotype, or historically charged connotation. Its claim to potential offensiveness toward unemployed people is deliberately absurdist, requiring the extension of anti-insensitivity logic into obviously non-applicable territory: the joke's comedic mechanism is precisely the apparent absurdity of treating "Payday" as a slur against people without income.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Satirical Fabrication as a Distinct Epistemic Category

The Payday meme exemplifies a category of false claim that is epistemically distinct from both straightforward misinformation (claims sincerely believed and incorrectly stated) and deliberate fraud (claims knowingly false and designed to deceive). Satirical fabrications are claims made in a spirit of parody or comic commentary that nonetheless spread beyond their original satirical context and are received as factual assertions by audiences who lack the contextual cues that would identify them as jokes.

This category was analyzed by Tandoc, Lim, and Ling (2018, "Defining 'Fake News': A Typology of Scholarly Definitions," *Digital Journalism*, Vol. 6, No. 2, pp. 137–153), who proposed a typology of fake news that distinguishes satire/parody from fabrication, propaganda, and misinformation. Their analysis found that satire is distinguished by the author's awareness that the content is false and the assumption that audiences will share this awareness; the failure mode occurs when the satire spreads beyond its originating community into contexts where the audience lacks the genre cues to recognize it as satire.

The Payday meme's spread dynamics illustrate this failure mode precisely. Within the communities where it originated—likely politically conservative social media spaces that shared the satirical intent—the meme functioned as commentary on what its creators viewed as excessive sensitivity in brand naming decisions. As it spread beyond those communities, however, the satirical frame was progressively stripped away, and the content was increasingly shared as a factual news claim rather than a political joke. This decontextualization effect has been documented in computational misinformation research: Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral (2018, "The Spread of True and False News Online," *Science*, Vol. 359, No. 6380, pp. 1146–1151) found that false claims spread significantly faster and farther on Twitter than true claims, and that emotional novelty—the perception that a claim is surprising or emotionally resonant—is a primary driver of this asymmetric diffusion.

The epistemic novelty here is the question of authorial intent and audience reception as independent variables. A claim can be false, known to be false by its creator, and still cause sincere epistemic harm when received as true by audiences in different interpretive contexts. This has implications for how fact-checkers should categorize and approach satirical fabrications: the debunking task involves not only demonstrating that the claim is false but also documenting the genre context that the originating community would have recognized, so that readers can understand why the claim was believable to some audiences and immediately recognizable as satire to others.

## §4 The Payday Bar: Historical Context and Depression-Era Origins

The Payday candy bar's history provides context that sharpens the meme's satirical irony. The bar was introduced to the American market in 1932 by the Hollywood Candy Company of Minneapolis, Minnesota—a date that places its origin squarely in the depths of the Great Depression, when American unemployment reached approximately 24.9 percent of the labor force (Lebergott, 1964, *Manpower in Economic Growth: The American Record Since 1800*, McGraw-Hill).

As Evon (2021) noted, candy bars during the Depression were frequently marketed as nutritional supplements or even meal replacements for consumers struggling to access adequate food. The Payday bar, with its dense coating of salted peanuts over a nougat-like caramel core, was particularly suited to this positioning: peanuts are high in protein and calories, and the bar's substantial weight and caloric density made it a genuinely filling food product at a low price point. *Food and Wine Magazine* cited by Evon (2021) specifically described the Payday as "one of the more filling options" among Depression-era candy bars marketed in this nutritional capacity.

The name "Payday" in this Depression context is an interesting cultural artifact. Rather than referring to a specific payday for employed workers, the name in its 1932 context arguably carried aspirational connotations: a day when one received money and could afford small luxuries like a candy bar. For workers in the Depression era who received wages intermittently, inconsistently, or in reduced amounts, "Payday" may have resonated as a hopeful signifier rather than an exclusive one. The bar's peanut-heavy composition also connected it to the peanut-as-working-class-food cultural tradition associated with baseball stadiums, street vendors, and saloon free lunches.

The brand was acquired by The Hershey Company as part of its broad expansion of the confectionery portfolio through the late twentieth century. By 2021, the bar had been manufactured by Hershey for decades without any indication of corporate sensitivity about the name. The meme's circulation as a parody of Hershey's alleged timidity thus inverts the actual corporate history: the brand's name has persisted through economic upheavals, ownership changes, and significant shifts in confectionery marketing norms without engendering any documented consumer complaints about its relationship to employment status.

## §5 Contextual Framing: The Cancel-Culture Meme Genre and Social Media Decontextualization

The Payday meme belongs to a coherent and identifiable genre of social media content that we might call **cancel-culture parody fabrications**: satirical items constructed to mimic the format of genuine brand rebranding announcements, but applied to product names that have no plausible real-world sensitivity issues, in order to parody what the creator views as excessive political correctness in consumer brand identity decisions.

This genre emerged directly from the 2020 wave of genuine brand renamings. Beyond Aunt Jemima and Uncle Ben's, the same period saw the Cream of Wheat brand retire its depiction of a Black chef (Rastus), Quaker Oats rebrand its Aunt Jemima syrup and pancake mix to Pearl Milling Company, and Land O'Lakes remove the Native American woman from its butter packaging. Each of these decisions was made by corporate brand managers responding to sustained advocacy and declining comfort among consumers with the use of racial and ethnic imagery in food marketing—a practice with roots in nineteenth-century advertising that had been critiqued by scholars and advocates for decades (Kern-Foxworth, 1994, *Aunt Jemima, Uncle Ben, and Rastus: Blacks in Advertising, Yesterday, Today, and Tomorrow*, Greenwood Press).

The cancel-culture parody genre responds to these rebranding decisions by constructing satirical analogues—imagining what an "overapplication" of the same logic would produce. The Payday meme is one of many; similar memes circulated targeting Cracker Barrel (cracker as an ethnic slur), Frosted Mini-Wheats (wheat as a grain associated with European colonialism), and other products whose names have incidental associations with social categories. The satirical target is the principle of brand sensitivity itself, not any specific brand decision.

Thorson and Wells (2016, "Curated Flows: A Framework for Mapping Media Exposure in the Digital Age," *Communication Theory*, Vol. 26, No. 3, pp. 309–328) analyzed how algorithmically curated social media feeds cause content to migrate across audiences with radically different interpretive communities, often stripping content of the contextual markers that define its genre status in its originating community. The Payday meme's migration from conservative satirical communities to general social media feeds illustrates this process: the implicit genre cues present in the originating context (familiarity with the cancel-culture debate, shared ironic framing) are invisible to audiences in different communities who encounter the decontextualized image.

## §6 Verdict Assessment and Research Gaps

Evon's (2021) **False** verdict is well-supported and clearly stated. The claim fails all three basic credibility tests for corporate brand news: no announcement from the company (Hershey), no coverage from trade or mainstream media, and a clearly identified satirical origin point. The meme is a parody that spread beyond its satirical context.

**Epistemic classification**: This is a case of *satirical fabrication*—a claim originally constructed as parody that spread as apparent fact. The key epistemic feature is the gap between authorial intent (satirical commentary) and audience reception (believed as news). This is distinct from deliberate fabrication, which intends deception from the start.

**EARC gap C (Contextual)**: The primary gap in Evon's (2021) treatment is contextual: the article correctly identifies the satirical origin but does not explain the broader cancel-culture parody meme genre to which this item belongs, nor does it analyze the structural features of the meme format (how it mimics genuine brand announcements) that make it plausible to audiences outside its originating context. A fuller contextual treatment would help readers recognize future items in the same genre.

**Research vectors**: The most productive additional investigation would involve tracking the full lifecycle of the meme across platforms from its summer 2020 origin to the February 2021 Snopes debunking—mapping its spread path, the communities that shared it, and the interpretive frames applied at different points. Comparison with other cancel-culture parody memes targeting product names (Cracker Barrel, Nutella for implicit nut-allergy insensitivity, etc.) would reveal the genre's stable features and rhetorical strategies, which would enable more efficient future debunking.
