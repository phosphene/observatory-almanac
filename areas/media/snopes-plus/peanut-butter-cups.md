# Three Reasons You Should Stop Eating Peanut Butter Cups


## 1. The Claim

In March 2017, a Facebook post titled "3 Reasons Why You Should Stop Eating Peanut Butter Cups!" went viral, accumulating hundreds of thousands of shares. Published by David "Avocado" Wolfe — a prolific figure in online pseudoscience media who has built a substantial following through wellness influencing, supplement promotion, and what science journalist Alex Kasprak described as the "willful misrepresentation of science" — the post identified three chemical additives in Reese's Peanut Butter Cups as inherently dangerous substances that consumers should be aware of and avoid.

The post was substantially a word-for-word reproduction of a 2015 post from a website called "Living Traditionally," which had itself circulated widely. The particular additives named in the post were: soy lecithin, described as causing "behavioral and cerebral abnormalities" and linked to breast cancer and fertility problems; PGPR (polyglycerol polyricinoleate), described as causing gastrointestinal problems and allergic reactions in children; and TBHQ (tertiary butylhydroquinone), described as a petroleum-derived toxin causing nausea, vomiting, tinnitus, delirium, collapse, lung and umbilical cell damage, stomach cancer, and intensified ADHD symptoms in children.

All three ingredients are, in fact, present in Reese's Peanut Butter Cups. This is one of the few accurate claims in the post. The question is whether any of the attributed harms is supported by credible evidence at the doses humans actually encounter through food consumption. The answer, in each case, is that the evidence cited is either deeply misrepresented, inapplicable at real-world exposure levels, or simply nonexistent.

The broader pattern the post exemplifies — leveraging the authentic presence of a chemical in a popular food product to attach a cascade of fear-generating but scientifically invalid claims to it — is worth examining carefully, because it is not an isolated phenomenon. The Wolfe post is an example of a repeating template that has generated enormous engagement across social media over the past decade and is responsible for significant consumer confusion about food safety science.


## 2. What's Actually True

All three named additives are real components of Reese's Peanut Butter Cups, confirmed by Hershey Company's own ingredient disclosures. Each plays a defined functional role.

**Soy lecithin** is a phospholipid mixture derived from soybeans, used as an emulsifier in chocolate manufacturing to improve texture and aid in blending. As defined in the U.S. Code of Federal Regulations (21 CFR 184.1400), commercial lecithin is a naturally occurring phosphatide mixture isolated from soy, safflower, or corn oils and is classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the FDA. Lecithin is also found in eggs, sunflower seeds, and liver, and is a normal constituent of the human diet. The FDA's Select Committee on GRAS Substances concluded in 1979 that "there is no evidence in the available information on lecithin... that demonstrates or suggests reasonable grounds to suspect a hazard to the public when they are used at levels that are now current or that might reasonably be expected in the future" (FDA SCOGS Opinion: Lecithin, 1979). Average daily dietary exposure to lecithin in the United States from food additives was estimated at approximately 96 milligrams per person.

**PGPR (polyglycerol polyricinoleate)** is a food-grade emulsifier derived from castor bean oil through polymerization and esterification. It is used in chocolate at low concentrations (typically under 0.5%) to reduce viscosity, prevent fat bloom, and decrease the amount of cocoa butter required in formulation. According to Dunford (2012, *Food and Industrial Bioproducts and Bioprocessing*, Wiley-Blackwell), PGPR improves texture and enhances volume in bakery and confectionery applications. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) adopted a safety opinion on PGPR in March 2017, concluding that the substance "is not of concern with regard to genotoxicity or carcinogenicity" and that human studies "demonstrated that there is no indication of significant adverse effect" (EFSA Panel on Food Additives, 2017).

**TBHQ (tertiary butylhydroquinone)** is a fat-soluble phenolic antioxidant used to extend the shelf life of oils and oil-containing foods by retarding oxidation. It is derived from the petrochemical building block p-cresol through chemical synthesis. In the United States, the FDA permits TBHQ in foods at levels up to 0.02% of fat and oil content. In Europe, EFSA evaluated TBHQ in 2004 and found it "not carcinogenic" and stated that "further genotoxicity studies are unnecessary" based on the available evidence. The substance has been in food use in the U.S. since 1972.

The factual picture, then, is this: Reese's Peanut Butter Cups do contain soy lecithin, PGPR, and TBHQ. Each of these ingredients has been reviewed by multiple independent regulatory agencies and found to be safe at the levels present in foods. The Wolfe post's claims about their dangers are not supported by the scientific literature evaluated at relevant human exposure doses.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty — What the Fact-Checks Miss

The Snopes fact-check by Alex Kasprak (2017) does excellent work cataloguing the specific scientific errors in the Wolfe post, but there is a deeper analytical layer worth examining: the post is not simply wrong, it is *systematically* wrong in a way that reflects a repeating template for food-fear viral content. Understanding the template — rather than just refuting the individual claims — has genuine epistemic value for evaluating future similar content.

The template has four characteristic moves:

**Move 1: Dose suppression.** The Wolfe post's attack on soy lecithin rests substantially on a 1985 study by Bell and Lundberg ("Effects of a Commercial Soy Lecithin Preparation on Development of Sensorimotor Behavior and Brain Biochemistry in the Rat," *Developmental Psychobiology*, January 1985) documenting behavioral and neurochemical abnormalities in rat offspring whose pregnant mothers were fed diets in which 2% to 5% of total food mass was replaced by soy lecithin. At a 5% replacement rate and approximately 10 grams of daily rat food intake, the animals were consuming 500 mg of soy lecithin per day. For a human consuming ~1,800 grams of food daily, a proportionate 5% replacement would represent 90 grams of soy lecithin daily — approximately 938 times higher than the FDA's estimate of 96 mg typical daily human dietary exposure from additives. The study was designed to investigate a specific proposed mechanism in neurological research, not to characterize consumer risk from food additives. Food-fear content routinely cites animal studies at extreme supraphysiological doses as though they were evidence of risk at dietary exposure levels. They are not.

**Move 2: Chemical identity conflation.** The post's breast cancer and fertility claims about soy lecithin appear to be transposed from debate about soy protein, which contains isoflavones — compounds structurally similar to estrogen that have been the subject of considerable scientific and popular debate about possible endocrine effects. That debate remains unresolved (Messina, 2010, "Soybean Isoflavone Exposure Does Not Have Feminizing Effects on Men: A Critical Examination of the Clinical Evidence," *Fertility and Sterility*, 94(3), 1067–1073). However, soy lecithin and soy protein are chemically distinct. Lecithin is a phospholipid mixture that carries minimal soy protein and virtually no isoflavones; even soy-allergic individuals frequently tolerate soy lecithin because of this difference (Kim & Sicherer, 2011, "Living with Food Allergy: Allergen Avoidance," *Pediatric Clinics of North America*, 58(2), 459–470). Collapsing "soy protein" and "soy lecithin" into "soy" is the kind of chemical identity conflation that appears routine in natural health media but is incompatible with basic food science.

**Move 3: The Paracelsus inversion.** Paracelsus's foundational toxicological principle — *sola dosis facit venenum*, "the dose makes the poison" — is one of the best-supported generalizations in all of science. At sufficiently high doses, water is toxic; at sufficiently low doses, arsenic has documented therapeutic uses. The Wolfe post never specifies doses. It simply asserts that certain compounds "have been connected to" or "can cause" certain harms, without indicating the doses at which those harms are documented or how those doses compare to actual food exposures. This dose suppression is not accidental; specifying doses would make the argument collapse.

**Move 4: Regulatory agency hedging.** Regulatory agencies by convention specify safety findings conditionally — "at levels currently used" or "at levels expected in typical diets" — which is appropriate epistemic humility but is easily misread, or deliberately reframed, as suggesting that safety is uncertain. The same principles that lead Snopes investigators to note that "long-term health effects in humans exposed to high or chronic doses of TBHQ...is not a settled issue" are features of scientific caution about all chemicals at all doses — including water. Extracting this caveat and presenting it without dose context creates the impression of unsolved uncertainty about normal dietary exposure when no such uncertainty exists.

These four moves are not unique to this post. They are structural features of a recognizable genre of food-fear viral content. Recognizing the template is more valuable than simply knowing that Reese's Peanut Butter Cups are safe, because the template is applied to new targets continuously.


## 4. The Regulatory Science of Food Additives

Understanding why the Wolfe post's claims fail requires a basic orientation to how food additive safety is established and maintained. The process is neither trivially permissive nor secretively riggable in the direction Wolfe implies.

In the United States, food additives are subject to premarket review under the Food Additives Amendment of 1958, which requires manufacturers to demonstrate safety before use. The FDA operates a parallel track for substances with longstanding safe use histories — the GRAS pathway — under which expert panels review available evidence and conclude whether a substance is generally recognized as safe under the conditions of intended use. Both pathways require evidence from animal toxicology studies across multiple dose levels, assessment of human exposure, and evaluation of any available epidemiological evidence.

The critical feature of this framework, repeatedly missed in food-fear content, is that safety findings are made **relative to actual exposure levels**, not relative to theoretical harm at any dose. When the FDA concludes that soy lecithin is GRAS, it is not asserting that soy lecithin is harmless at any conceivable dose; it is asserting that the evidence does not establish harm at the doses Americans typically encounter through food. This is a bounded claim, not a global one — and it is exactly the appropriate claim to make.

PGPR illustrates the regulatory process operating correctly. The EFSA review published on 3 March 2017 — the same month the Wolfe post went viral — was a routine periodic re-evaluation that confirmed prior safety conclusions based on contemporary evidence including the most recent toxicological studies. The EFSA's conclusion that "human studies with PGPR demonstrated that there is no indication of significant adverse effect" is drawn from controlled clinical trials with human subjects, not merely extrapolated from rodent data. This is precisely the kind of evidence that should be decisive for consumer safety assessment, and it was available at the time of the Wolfe post.

The TBHQ situation is somewhat more complex only because TBHQ is a compound with genuine acute toxicity data at high exposures — occupational overexposure cases and animal high-dose studies do document toxic effects. The question is always whether those effects manifest at concentrations 50–200 times lower than those documented in toxicological research. Both U.S. and European regulatory conclusions are that they do not, for the dosing patterns seen in food. The claim that TBHQ "can cause stomach cancer" derives from a 1986 Japanese study of rats fed TBHQ at extremely high doses over extended periods; subsequent studies of cancer initiation and promotion at food-relevant doses have not reproduced or extended this finding in a way that has changed regulatory conclusions.


## 5. David Wolfe and the Pseudoscience Media Ecosystem

David "Avocado" Wolfe is not simply a misinformed individual who made errors in a Facebook post. He is the center of a multimillion-dollar wellness media enterprise whose revenue model depends on generating and amplifying health fears that he can redirect toward supplement and product sales. Wolfe's business portfolio includes longevity supplements, "zappers" (USB-chargeable devices marketed as health tools), and various branded wellness products. His self-promotional materials have included the claim that he is the "wealthiest hippie on Earth."

The Wolfe post under examination was not original content — it was copied from an earlier post by a site called Living Traditionally, which was itself part of a broader ecosystem of content aggregation around food-fear themes. This layered copying and re-amplification, often without attribution, is characteristic of how pseudoscience content spreads: an initial piece from a lower-traffic site gains traction, is discovered and re-published by a higher-traffic amplifier, and the amplifier's audience drives the reach into the hundreds of thousands of shares. The original author receives no credit and often no financial benefit; the amplifier collects the engagement and the associated revenue.

The specific framing of the post — "reasons you should stop eating [beloved food]" — is a highly successful engagement template that activates both health anxiety and moral concern, creating sharing behavior driven by prosocial motivation ("I'm warning my friends about something dangerous"). People share this content because they genuinely believe they are doing their friends a service, which is precisely why accurate refutation matters: the social harm is distributed through well-meaning intermediaries who are themselves misinformed.

Chen et al. (2014, "Association between Soy Isoflavone Intake and Breast Cancer Risk for Pre- and Post-Menopausal Women: A Meta-Analysis of Epidemiological Studies," *PLOS ONE*, 9(2), e89288) — cited in the context of the breast cancer claim — actually found no consistent association between soy isoflavone intake and breast cancer risk overall, with heterogeneous results by population. This paper was circulating at the time the claims it was presumed to support were made, and directly contradicts the categorical framing of "soy lecithin causes breast cancer."


## 6. Verdict

**Snopes rating: Mostly False.** The three additives named in the viral post — soy lecithin, PGPR, and TBHQ — are genuinely present in Reese's Peanut Butter Cups. That is where the factual content of the post ends. The claimed harms for each additive are either unsupported by evidence at dietary exposure levels, based on studies performed at doses orders of magnitude higher than any realistic human consumption, reliant on conflation of chemically distinct compounds, or simply fabricated from whole cloth with no traceable scientific sourcing.

The post exemplifies a durable template for food-fear viral content that relies on dose suppression, chemical identity conflation, the Paracelsus inversion, and selective framing of regulatory hedging language. Recognizing this template — which re-emerges regularly against new food products and additive targets — is more practically valuable than simply knowing whether Reese's Peanut Butter Cups pose a health risk (they do not, at normal consumption levels). The regulatory science of food additive safety is specifically designed to establish safety at real-world exposure levels, and in each of the three cases named, multiple independent regulatory reviews have concluded that the additives are safe as used.

EARC classification: **A** (Accuracy). The core failure is a set of factual inaccuracies about what the scientific evidence shows — specifically, through misrepresentation of study populations, doses, and conclusions. Gap category: **distorted-but-grounded** — the ingredients named are genuinely present, providing the truth-kernel that makes the false elaborations plausible and sticky.
