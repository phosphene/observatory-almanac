# A school district declared peanut butter and jelly sandwiches racist


## 1. The Claim

The claim entered wide circulation in 2012 following coverage by Portland, Oregon television station KATU and subsequent pickup by conservative media outlets, talk radio, and social media. The story as popularly retold holds that a school district — sometimes identified as Portland Public Schools or a specific Portland-area district — declared, announced, or officially adopted the position that peanut butter and jelly (PBJ) sandwiches are racist, and that this supposedly absurd policy represented an example of political correctness, equity ideology, or woke overreach in public education.

The story became a touchstone in culture war commentary about progressive public education policies and spread across conservative news media, blogs, and social platforms for years after its original publication. It returned periodically — often without reference to its 2012 origin — as evidence of claimed extremism in school diversity and equity programs. The version that circulated most widely presented the story as a policy fact: a school district had officially declared a common lunch item racist, and children were presumably being taught that their sandwiches were racist.

In its most viral forms, the story embodied a rhetorical structure common in certain ideological media niches: absurdist evidence of unhinged institutional progressivism, presented as proof that diversity and equity initiatives in schools had departed from common sense. The image of a child's PBJ sandwich being declared racist was specifically chosen, either consciously or through the dynamics of viral sharing, because it maximised the sense of absurdity and minimised the cognitive effort required to dismiss the underlying pedagogical content without engaging with it.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The origin: Verenice Gutierrez and a teacher discussion.** In 2012, Verenice Gutierrez, principal of Harvey Scott Elementary School in Portland, Oregon, part of Portland Public Schools, was participating in a staff development program focused on culturally responsive teaching — an educational framework concerned with how teachers' implicit assumptions about cultural norms can disadvantage students from different cultural backgrounds. The program, called Coaching for Educational Equity, was led by Glenn Singleton, author of *Courageous Conversations About Race: A Field Guide for Achieving Equity in Schools* (Singleton, G.E., and Linton, C., 2006, Corwin Press).

During discussions about strategies for recognising and reducing ethnocentrism in classroom teaching, Gutierrez reportedly offered the peanut butter and jelly sandwich as an analogy. The point she was making, in its correct context, was about culturally specific assumptions: when teachers use examples from everyday American food culture to make lessons relatable — "you know how a peanut butter and jelly sandwich is..." — they are implicitly assuming that all students share the same culinary cultural reference points. A child from a family whose food culture does not include peanut butter and jelly sandwiches would experience that example as alienating rather than connecting. Gutierrez mentioned tortillas and pita bread as examples of analogous foods in other cultural contexts.

The content of this analogy is pedagogically recognisable as an example of the broader concept of cultural ethnocentrism in education: the tendency to use examples, stories, and reference points that assume a specific dominant cultural background, which can systematically disadvantage students from other cultural backgrounds. Research in this area has a substantial scholarly basis. Ladson-Billings (1994) introduced the concept of "culturally relevant pedagogy" in her study of African American students in elementary schools, arguing that effective educators use students' cultural references in all aspects of instruction (Ladson-Billings, G., 1994, *The Dreamkeepers: Successful Teachers of African American Children*, Jossey-Bass). Gay (2000) extended this framework into "culturally responsive teaching," which argues that cultural cues in instruction substantially affect engagement and learning outcomes for students from cultural minorities (Gay, G., 2000, *Culturally Responsive Teaching: Theory, Research, and Practice*, Teachers College Press).

**What never happened.** No declaration was made that peanut butter and jelly sandwiches are racist. No curriculum was changed to remove PBJ references. No policy was adopted by Portland Public Schools or any other district regarding peanut butter and jelly sandwiches. No parent, student, or teacher was instructed to avoid or treat as racist any specific food item. The peanut butter and jelly sandwich was used as an analogy in a professional development discussion among adults — a discussion that was itself part of a longer program specifically designed for teachers and administrators, not students.

The original news story, as reported by KATU, accurately described what Gutierrez said in the context of a teacher training program. The transformation from "principal uses PBJ as an analogy in teacher training session" to "school district declares PBJ racist" occurred in the subsequent media amplification, not in the original reporting. The two are categorically different claims: one is a description of an analogy used in professional development, the other is a false attribution of an official institutional policy position.

**Culturally responsive teaching as a scholarly framework.** The broader educational practice context is relevant. Culturally responsive teaching (CRT in the pedagogical sense, distinct from Critical Race Theory despite the acronym overlap that sometimes confuses discussions) is a research-supported instructional framework that has been studied in educational settings for more than three decades. A substantial body of peer-reviewed research examines its effectiveness. Villegas and Lucas (2002) argued that culturally responsive teachers understand how students' cultural backgrounds influence their academic experiences (Villegas, A.M., and Lucas, T., 2002, "Preparing Culturally Responsive Teachers: Rethinking the Curriculum," *Journal of Teacher Education*, 53(1), 20–32). Sleeter (2012) reviewed a decade of research and found evidence that culturally responsive approaches improved academic outcomes in communities where cultural discontinuity between home and school culture had been identified as a factor in underperformance (Sleeter, C.E., 2012, "Confronting the Marginalization of Culturally Responsive Pedagogy," *Urban Education*, 47(3), 562–584).

The use of food analogies to illustrate cultural assumption is precisely the kind of concrete classroom-ready illustration that professional development programs use to make abstract concepts accessible to practicing teachers. PBJ is an excellent choice for this purpose in a US context: it is a highly specific food associated with mainstream American childhood culture, it is genuinely unknown in many food cultures worldwide, and it is benign enough that the analogy can be explored without generating defensive reactions. Using it as an example of how a culturally specific reference can inadvertently exclude students is pedagogically sound and not logically equivalent to declaring the sandwich itself racist.

**The racial dimension: why "racist" appeared in coverage.** The professional development program in which this discussion occurred was explicitly focused on race and equity — specifically on how racial and cultural background affects educational experiences and outcomes. The teacher training context involved discussions of racial equity directly. Gutierrez's point was specifically framed in terms of the cultural experiences of Latino and other non-dominant cultural groups in the school's population, and she explicitly mentioned that some of her students' families did not share the cultural background that makes PBJ a relatable example. The word "racist" was not used to describe the sandwich itself in the original account; the coverage framed the discussion as being about race, which it was, and the simplified retelling collapsed all of that context into the claim that the sandwich was declared racist.

**Portland Public Schools' broader context.** Portland Public Schools was, at the time, engaged in district-wide equity initiatives that had generated internal debate and some public controversy. The Coaching for Educational Equity program was one element of a broader district commitment to examining and addressing racial disparities in educational outcomes. The program and its facilitator, Glenn Singleton, had been contracted by other school districts as well and were recognised figures in the national school equity space. The controversy generated around Gutierrez's comments was part of a broader pattern of media backlash against equity programs in Portland and beyond.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**Claim-specific mechanism — the absurdity heuristic and motivated scepticism toward out-group institutions.** The claim that a school district declared a PBJ sandwich racist is accepted readily by people who are already sceptical of educational equity programs, because it has the structure of a reductio ad absurdum that the sceptic already believes the opposing position deserves. If you hold the prior belief that diversity, equity, and inclusion frameworks in schools have become detached from reality, hearing that a school has declared a sandwich racist feels like exactly the kind of evidence you would expect — which makes it feel true without requiring verification. Psychologists call this phenomenon "prior belief confirmation through narrative fit" or, more colloquially, it feels true because it fits the story you already believe about the world (Pennycook, G., and Rand, D.G., 2019, "Lazy, not biased: Susceptibility to partisan fake news is better explained by lack of reasoning than by motivated reasoning," *Cognition*, 188, 39–50).

This mechanism is particularly powerful for absurdist claims. Normally, the more absurd a claim, the more likely a sceptical recipient is to demand evidence. But when the absurdity fits a pre-existing narrative about institutional overreach, the absurdity itself becomes confirming rather than disqualifying — it is read not as a signal that the claim is probably false but as proof of how extreme the out-group institution has become. The PBJ story was a perfect instance: the more absurd it sounded, the more conservatives shared it as evidence of how absurd progressive public education had become.

**Decontextualisation in the "teacher training to policy proclamation" transformation.** The transformation from "analogy used in professional development" to "official declaration" involves a specific decontextualisation move: removing the claim from the professional learning context in which it was made and reinserting it into a policy context in which it never appeared. This move is common in political media coverage of institutional discussions about race and culture: a speaker's exploratory, formative, professional learning-context comment is reported as an institutional policy position.

The transformation is possible because the speaker was a principal — an institutional authority figure — and anything said by an authority figure in a quasi-official setting is liable to be treated as if it represented official institutional position. Teachers and school administrators discussing professional development topics do not typically speak with the same care about the institutional versus personal distinction that elected officials or communications staff are trained to maintain. Gutierrez's off-the-cuff analogy in a staff development session became, in the coverage, a statement of school district policy — not because she represented district policy in that discussion, but because her role as principal blurred the individual-institutional distinction.

**The "common sense" food object as ideological substrate.** Choosing a peanut butter and jelly sandwich as the food item is not accidental in terms of why this story travels so well. The PBJ is simultaneously a highly culturally specific object (not common in most of the world outside the US) and, within US culture, essentially the quintessential innocent childhood food — nursery food, lunchbox food, comfort food. It carries no political charge whatsoever in its normal cultural context. Declaring such a thing racist strikes most American listeners as maximally absurd precisely because the cultural innocence of the PBJ is so complete in American domestic culture. This innocence is itself a product of the cultural specificity that Gutierrez was using it to illustrate — but the irony is lost when the usage is decontextualised.


## 4. Verdict

**False — the claim is a misrepresentation of a teacher training analogy that was transformed into a false policy claim.** No school district declared peanut butter and jelly sandwiches racist. No policy was adopted, no curriculum was changed, and no student was taught that PBJ sandwiches are racist. What occurred was that a Portland school principal used peanut butter and jelly sandwiches as a pedagogical analogy in a professional development session about culturally responsive teaching — specifically to illustrate how culturally specific food references in classroom examples can inadvertently exclude students from non-dominant food cultures.

The underlying discussion — about the importance of not assuming that all students share the same cultural references, and about how teachers' implicit cultural assumptions can affect equitable educational access — is supported by a substantial body of peer-reviewed educational research and is not inherently controversial as a professional learning topic. Whether one agrees with every aspect of the Coaching for Educational Equity program or with Portland Public Schools' equity initiatives more broadly, the claim that a district declared PBJ sandwiches racist describes something that did not happen.

The story is a media distortion that extracts a professional development analogy from its context, strips its pedagogical framing, and replaces it with a false institutional policy claim. It functions as political shorthand rather than as accurate reporting, and its durability reflects the media ecosystem dynamics — viral amplification, decontextualisation, prior belief confirmation — that make it resistant to fact-checking.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The peanut butter and jelly sandwich story is one instance of a recurring media pattern in the coverage of equity, diversity, and inclusion initiatives in public institutions, particularly schools. The pattern has a consistent structure: a discussion, workshop, or professional development session produces a remark or analogy that, when reported in isolation, sounds absurd or offensive; the remark is covered by local or conservative media in a way that attributes it as institutional policy; it is amplified nationally; and the amplified version becomes a durable anecdote used to characterise equity programs as out of touch, extreme, or harmful.

This pattern has been documented by media researchers studying the coverage of critical race theory, DEI programs, and culturally responsive teaching in US media from 2012 onward, with acceleration from 2020. The mechanism involves what Freelon, McIlwain, and Clark (2016) called "connective action frames" in politically networked media — coordinated reporting and sharing that builds cumulative misrepresentation through distributed amplification rather than through any single false claim made in bad faith (Freelon, D., McIlwain, C., and Clark, M., 2016, *Beyond the Hashtags*, Center for Media & Social Impact). Each individual node in the sharing network may believe it is sharing accurate information; the distortion is an emergent property of the decontextualisation process across multiple sharing steps.

The culturally responsive teaching framework that provided the context for Gutierrez's analogy is a legitimate subject of educational policy debate. There are genuine scholarly disagreements about the most effective ways to address cultural discontinuity in schools, about the proper scope of teacher training in equity frameworks, and about the relationship between cultural responsiveness and academic achievement outcomes. These debates involve real empirical questions about effective pedagogy and real normative questions about what public schools owe students from different cultural backgrounds.

The PBJ story as it circulates does not contribute to any of those debates. It functions instead to shortcircuit engagement with them by providing a cartoonish representation designed to trigger ridicule rather than analysis. Scholars of media and education who have studied the coverage of equity initiatives in schools have noted that this pattern of absurdist anecdote — the "can you believe they said this?" story — systematically crowds out substantive policy discussion (Crenshaw, K.W., 2011, "Twenty Years of Critical Race Theory: Looking Back to Move Forward," *Connecticut Law Review*, 43(5), 1253–1352; see also general literature on "moral panic" in education policy coverage).

For those interested in evaluating equity programs in schools on the merits, the appropriate question is not "did someone use an unfortunate analogy in a staff meeting?" but "what does the research evidence show about the effectiveness of culturally responsive teaching approaches in improving outcomes for students from cultural minorities, and what are the implementation risks and resource costs?" That is a question the PBJ story, as circulated, makes harder rather than easier to ask.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

This claim has not received formal treatment from major fact-checking platforms (Snopes, PolitiFact, AFP Fact Check) as of this writing, which represents an interesting gap given how widely it circulated. The absence from fact-checking databases may reflect the claim's ambiguous factual status: because the underlying event (a principal used PBJ as an analogy in professional development) is real, the claim is not cleanly false in the way that a wholly invented story would be. It is a misrepresentation rather than a fabrication, and misrepresentations are structurally harder for fact-checking pipelines optimised around true/false binaries to adjudicate cleanly.

The approach a thorough fact-check would take: (1) identify the original source — the 2012 KATU story and the underlying professional development program; (2) establish what Gutierrez actually said and in what context; (3) distinguish the professional development context from an institutional policy context; (4) confirm that no policy, curriculum change, or official statement by Portland Public Schools declared PBJ sandwiches racist; (5) explain the culturally responsive teaching framework to contextualise the analogy; and (6) trace how the story was amplified and transformed in subsequent media coverage.

Primary source material would include: the original KATU reporting, Portland Public Schools' documentation of its equity programs, the Coaching for Educational Equity program materials, and any public statements from Gutierrez or district administrators. The verification chain is entirely accessible through public records and reporting; the reason this has not been done formally by major fact-checkers is most likely a combination of timing (the story predates the period of most intense fact-checking infrastructure build-out) and the "not cleanly false enough" problem.

For media literacy educators, this story is a high-value teaching case precisely because it involves a real event that was genuinely distorted rather than a wholly invented one. The distortion mechanism — professional development analogy → policy proclamation — is one that students and general readers can understand and apply to future cases. The question "is this describing a speaker's analogy or an institutional policy?" is a high-yield heuristic applicable to a large class of stories involving school equity programs, corporate DEI training, and similar contexts where informal professional learning discussions are routinely excerpted and misrepresented as official positions.

Developing the habit of asking about institutional context — not just "what was said" but "in what setting, by whom, in what capacity, and with what institutional authority" — is among the most practical skills that fact-checking and media literacy practice can cultivate. The PBJ story is an ideal case study for that habit, because the transformation from "analogy said informally in professional development" to "official policy" is legible once you know to look for it, and the consequences of that transformation — a real educator's genuine pedagogical insight converted into a national symbol of absurdist overreach — are sufficiently significant to motivate sustained attention.
