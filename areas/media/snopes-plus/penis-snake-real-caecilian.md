## The Claim

A photograph circulating on social media shows a creature that looks disturbingly phallic, circulated under nicknames like "penis snake," "floppy snake," and "man-aconda." The claim accompanying these images is simple: this is a real animal, not a manipulated photograph, a prop, or a hoax.

That claim is **true** — but it barely scratches the surface of what makes this animal remarkable. The creature is *Atretochoana eiselti*, a species of caecilian, and the story of its biology is far more interesting than its unfortunate resemblance to human anatomy.

---

## What's Actually True

### The Animal Is Real, and It Is a Caecilian

*Atretochoana eiselti* is a real organism, documented in peer-reviewed scientific literature, with preserved specimens housed in the herpetological collection of the Museu Paraense Emílio Goeldi in Belém, Brazil. It is classified as follows:

- **Kingdom:** Animalia
- **Phylum:** Chordata
- **Class:** Amphibia
- **Order:** Gymnophiona (caecilians)
- **Family:** Typhlonectidae
- **Genus/Species:** *Atretochoana eiselti* (Taylor, 1968)

Caecilians are the least-known of the three major groups of living amphibians (alongside frogs and salamanders). They are limbless, largely burrowing or aquatic creatures found across tropical regions of South America, Africa, and South Asia. Most people have never encountered one. This is partly because they tend to live underground or in water, and partly because, unlike frogs, they've never become a cultural fixture.

The popular photographs of *A. eiselti* that circulate online were taken during a 2011 discovery event in the Brazilian Amazon. A portion of the Madeira River near Cachoeira Santo Antônio was temporarily drained to facilitate construction of a hydroelectric project. When the riverbed was exposed, six specimens of the creature were collected from a pool at approximately four metres below normal sea level on 1 August 2011. The discovery was reported in the *Boletim do Museu Paraense Emílio Goeldi: Ciências Naturais* by Marinus Hoogmoed, Julio C. Tupan, and colleagues.

The adult female specimen photographed measured just under one metre in length — approximately 39 inches — making it a genuinely large animal by caecilian standards.

### But It Is Not a Snake

Despite its name in viral circulation, *A. eiselti* is not a snake. Snakes are reptiles. Caecilians are amphibians. The evolutionary distance between them is vast — roughly the same as the distance between a lizard and a frog. There is no meaningful taxonomic or anatomical relationship beyond the fact that both are elongated, limbless vertebrates.

The superficial similarity is a product of convergent evolution: the advantages of a limbless, cylindrical body plan for burrowing and aquatic locomotion have independently emerged in multiple vertebrate lineages. Snakes evolved it. Caecilians evolved it. Legless lizards evolved it. The shared shape reflects shared selective pressures, not shared ancestry.

### The Most Remarkable Fact: It Has No Lungs

The popular discourse around *A. eiselti* focuses almost entirely on its appearance, which is a shame, because the biological reality is far more extraordinary.

*Atretochoana eiselti* is the **world's largest known lungless tetrapod** — the largest four-limbed-lineage vertebrate that breathes without lungs. This is not a marginal claim; it represents a genuine biological superlative.

Lunglessness in tetrapods is unusual. It is found in several species of small salamanders (particularly in the family Plethodontidae, which includes hundreds of species of North American and European salamanders that breathe entirely through their skin and mouth linings) and in one species of frog, *Barbourula kalimantanensis*, a Bornean frog described in 2008. But these are all small animals. The lungless condition imposes constraints on oxygen uptake — the larger the animal, the more body volume relative to skin surface area, and the harder it becomes to extract enough oxygen through the skin alone.

*A. eiselti* breaks that constraint entirely. At nearly a metre in length, it is dramatically larger than any other lungless tetrapod. How?

Research by the 2011 discovery team found anatomical evidence suggesting the answer: the skin of *A. eiselti* is notably wrinkled and folded, substantially increasing its effective surface area beyond what a smooth skin of the same length would provide. The capillaries supplying blood to the skin surface are also unusually close to the outermost layers — separated from the outside world by only about two to three cell layers. This maximises the rate of gas exchange across the skin.

The researchers also noted, somewhat delightfully, that the possibility of cloacal breathing (absorbing oxygen through the posterior opening, as some turtles can do) could not be ruled out. This is the same mechanism sometimes described as "butt breathing" in popular science writing about certain turtle species that overwinter in frozen ponds.

The animal also has sealed internal nostrils — structures called choanae — meaning it cannot breathe through its nose in the conventional sense. It is, in essence, a creature that has abandoned the respiratory architecture shared by most land-adapted vertebrates and found an entirely different solution.

### The Naming and Pre-2011 History

*Atretochoana eiselti* was first formally described in 1968 by Edward Harrison Taylor, based on a specimen that had been in the collection of the Natural History Museum of Vienna. The specimen had been collected in Brazil — though the exact collection date and precise origin were poorly documented, which was unfortunately common for museum specimens acquired in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

For decades after its description, *A. eiselti* was known from essentially nothing: the holotype (the original reference specimen) and one other specimen. Scientists could not study its ecology, behaviour, or physiology, because no one had found it alive in the wild.

The 2011 Madeira River discovery was the first time the species had been found alive in its natural habitat. The discovery of six live specimens allowed researchers to begin actual biological study for the first time, including confirmation of the lungless condition, which had been hypothesised from the anatomy of the preserved specimens but not previously verified in a living animal.

Mark Wilkinson and Ronald Nussbaum published a detailed comparative morphological study in the *Biological Journal of the Linnean Society* in 1997, drawing on the two specimens then known. Their work documented the absence of lungs and the unusual internal anatomy, but genuine fieldwork had to wait until the dam construction project inadvertently exposed the riverbed population.

### Endemic to the Amazon, and Poorly Understood

*A. eiselti* appears to be restricted to the large rivers of the Amazon basin, and may be specifically adapted to deep, fast-flowing river environments. The Madeira River, where the 2011 specimens were collected, is one of the major tributaries of the Amazon — a powerful river with strong currents and variable water levels.

Given how rarely this species has been encountered, its population size and conservation status are essentially unknown. It has not undergone formal IUCN assessment. The habitat pressures it faces from hydroelectric development (ironic, given that a dam project led to the only significant set of live observations ever made) and general Amazon basin environmental change are real but unquantified.

## Why People Believe This

### The Double Disbelief

The curious epistemological structure of the "penis snake" viral moment is a double disbelief. When people first see a photograph, many assume it is fake — either a manipulated image or a prop. The initial reaction is often incredulity precisely because the appearance is so extreme. So when the fact-check arrives confirming it is real, it must work against the presumption of fakery.

This creates an interesting dynamic where the "true" verdict on a fact-check functions as a surprise rather than a confirmation. The claim is true, but the claim's framing — "is this real?" — already encodes the assumption that it might not be.

### Viral Biology

Images of strange-looking organisms have circulated on the internet since its early days. The combination of "looks like something it isn't" and "seems unbelievable" is a reliable engine for sharing. The Deep Web and Reddit communities dedicated to mildly or explicitly phallic-shaped things in nature (the subreddit referenced in the Snopes article, r/mildlypenis, is one example) have a reliable audience.

The pattern repeats across biology: the blobfish (which looks dramatically different from photographs taken in its actual deep-sea pressure environment than from specimens brought to surface), the star-nosed mole, the proboscis monkey, the various species of naked mole-rats. Extreme-looking organisms generate more sharing than ordinary ones, independent of their biological significance.

### The Misclassification as a Snake

Many people who see the viral photographs in headlines calling it a "penis snake" store the information roughly as "there is a snake that looks like a penis." The corrective — that it is actually an amphibian with no relationship to snakes — rarely lodges with the same retention.

This matters epistemologically because it places *A. eiselti* in a mental category (snakes) where it does not belong, and associated facts (venomous? bites? dangerous?) follow wrongly from that categorisation. Caecilians and snakes have entirely different ecologies, behaviours, and threat profiles. *A. eiselti* is not known to be dangerous to humans.

## Verdict

**True, with important nuance.** The animal in the photographs is real — *Atretochoana eiselti* — and is one of the most biologically remarkable vertebrates known to science. It is not a snake. It is a limbless amphibian belonging to the order Gymnophiona (caecilians), and it is the largest known lungless tetrapod on Earth. Its method of breathing (cutaneous respiration through unusually thin, wrinkled skin) is a genuine biological superlative.

The claim "it is a real animal" is true. The implicit framing "it is a snake" is false. The reality — a rare Amazonian caecilian that breathes through its skin, hasn't been studied alive until 2011, and may also absorb oxygen through its posterior opening — is, if anything, more interesting than either the original claim or its debunking.

## The Wider Picture

### Caecilians Are Systematically Underrepresented

The international profile of caecilians relative to frogs and salamanders is dramatically lower than their biological interest warrants. There are approximately 220 described caecilian species — a comparatively modest number, but new species continue to be described. The two other major amphibian orders have multiple research institutions, conservation charities, and dedicated scientific societies devoted to them. Caecilians have nothing comparable.

Part of this reflects scientific access: most caecilians are burrowing, fossorial organisms that are difficult to study. Aquatic typhlonectid caecilians like *A. eiselti* are somewhat more observable in principle, but they live in large, turbid tropical rivers that are not easy to survey.

Part of it also reflects a cultural asymmetry: frogs have been the subject of major conservation campaigns because of the global chytrid fungus crisis (*Batrachochytrium dendrobatidis* and its relative *B. salamandrivorans* have devastated amphibian populations worldwide). Caecilians are presumed to be affected by similar pressures, but they are so understudied that confident statements about their population trends are impossible.

### Lunglessness as an Evolutionary Strategy

The evolution of lunglessness in tetrapods is of significant theoretical interest. The ancestral state for land vertebrates is to have lungs — that is the fundamental terrestrial respiratory adaption. Losing lungs (or more precisely, the secondary evolutionary reduction and loss of pulmonary function) is a reversal of this trajectory.

In lungless salamanders (Plethodontidae), the most widely accepted explanation involves their origin in fast, well-oxygenated mountain streams in the Appalachian region, where the buoyancy reduction from losing gas-filled lungs may have provided a selective advantage for rheotaxis (maintaining position in fast current). Over evolutionary time, the behaviour (and then the anatomy) became fixed.

For *A. eiselti*, no equivalent hypothetical has been established. It lives in large Amazonian rivers, not mountain streams. The selective pressures driving its lung loss remain unknown. This is one of many open questions about a species that, as of 2026, has been observed alive in the wild on essentially one occasion.

### The Amazon as a Generator of Biological Surprises

Brazilian biologist Juliano Tupan, one of the discoverers of the 2011 specimens, made a remark worth preserving: "The Amazon is a box of surprises when it comes to reptiles and amphibians. There are still much more to be discovered."

This is not hyperbole. New species descriptions from Amazonian herpetofauna continue at a significant rate. The region's size, ecological complexity, and relative inaccessibility mean that systematic biological surveys remain incomplete across vast areas. The Madeira River hydroelectric projects — a source of considerable environmental controversy in Brazil — inadvertently created, in the temporary draining of the riverbed, conditions that allowed scientific access to a species that had otherwise been essentially impossible to Study alive.

This irony — that a dam construction project yielded the most significant biological observations ever made of a globally rare species — is one of the genuine peculiarities of how biological discovery works in the contemporary Amazon.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes rates the claim **True**, verifying that the photographs show a genuine organism and providing primary citation to the 2011 *Boletim do Museu Paraense Emílio Goeldi* paper. The coverage correctly identifies the animal as a caecilian, notes it is not a snake, and highlights the lunglessness as the scientifically notable feature.

The standard fact-check approach handles this entry type well: a photograph claim with a binary real/fake verdict is well-suited to the True/False framework. Where the approach leaves something on the table is in the zoological and evolutionary depth. Most readers of a fact-check on this topic come away knowing the animal is real, not knowing whether it's an amphibian or reptile, and certainly not knowing that it is the largest lungless tetrapod ever described or that it may breathe through its cloaca.

This is not a criticism of Snopes specifically — breadth is inherent to fact-checking as a format — but it illustrates why epistemologically rich entries require supplementary depth treatment. The claim being real is almost the least interesting thing about it.

**The epistemological residue:** the "penis snake" memetic frame tends to swamp the scientific content entirely. People who share the photograph are almost never sharing it because they find *Atretochoana eiselti*'s respiratory physiology interesting. They are sharing a phallic-looking thing. The genuine biology travels poorly through that channel, which means that the most remarkable species in this story remains, in most public understanding, nothing more than a funny-looking snake.
