# A Penny Dropped from a Skyscraper Would Kill Someone on the Ground

## 1. The Claim

It is one of those facts that feels irrefutable by sheer arithmetic: a penny, dropped from the top of the Empire State Building — 1,454 feet above Fifth Avenue — would accelerate for the entire descent and arrive at the sidewalk with enough velocity to punch clean through a human skull.

The claim shows up in New York tour guide lore, in urban legend collections, in schoolyard warnings to tourists, and occasionally in physics classrooms as an example of energy accumulation under gravity. The mental model is straightforward: height equals potential energy, potential energy converts to kinetic energy during the fall, kinetic energy equals danger. The Empire State Building is very tall. A falling object from very high up must be moving very fast when it lands. A penny is small, hard, and metal. Therefore: lethal.

It is the kind of claim that seems to require no verification because the logic is internally coherent — and the logic is, in fact, sound, as far as it goes. The problem is not with the logic but with what the logic omits: air.

## 2. What's Actually True

A penny dropped from the Empire State Building would reach a terminal velocity of approximately 30 to 50 miles per hour. It would not kill you. It would sting, and might raise a welt. It would not penetrate skin, shatter bone, or pierce a skull.

The key concept is terminal velocity — the speed at which the drag force of air resistance exactly balances the gravitational force pulling an object downward, producing zero net acceleration. Below terminal velocity, an object accelerates. At terminal velocity, it falls at a constant speed. The terminal velocity of any object depends on its mass, its cross-sectional area, and its drag coefficient — which is itself a function of the object's shape.

A penny is an extremely poor aerodynamic projectile. It is flat, light (approximately 2.5 grams), and small in diameter (about 19 millimeters). Because of its flat geometry, it tumbles and flutters rather than maintaining a stable orientation during free fall. This tumbling behavior dramatically increases effective drag. Louis Bloomfield, a physics professor at the University of Virginia who has studied this question extensively, calculated that the penny's terminal velocity, given its tumbling characteristics, falls in the range of 30 to 50 mph — far below what would be needed to cause serious injury to a person.

This was tested empirically by *MythBusters* in 2003. The team used a ballistic gelatin block as a proxy for human tissue and fired pennies at it using a pneumatic gun at velocities exceeding terminal velocity estimates. The results: the penny did not penetrate the gelatin. It bounced off or left only a shallow surface mark. When the hosts subsequently allowed the penny to fall from height in a controlled outdoor setting, the impact on their skin produced a stinging sensation but nothing more. The skin was not broken.

For comparison: bullets fired from firearms typically travel at 900 to 3,000+ feet per second — roughly 600 to 2,000+ mph — and are specifically engineered for aerodynamic stability, high mass-to-surface-area ratios, and penetrating geometry. A penny achieves perhaps 2 to 7 percent of a bullet's velocity, is not aerodynamically stable, and has a blunt, flat impact geometry. The comparison is not close.

The contrast with genuinely dangerous falling objects is illuminating. Hailstones traveling at similar velocities can cause bruising and, at larger sizes and higher velocities, can kill. The key difference is that golf-ball-sized hailstones have substantially more mass and a more favorable mass-to-area ratio than a penny. More importantly, larger, denser, or more aerodynamically stable objects — a marble, a steel ball bearing, a coin dropped edge-first — could achieve higher terminal velocities and pose greater impact risk. The penny is about the worst possible example of a dangerous falling object: flat, light, tumbling.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The persistence of this belief is a textbook case of intuitive physics failing at the edges of everyday experience.

**Neglect of air resistance as a concept.** In elementary physics education, most problems are idealized to occur "in a vacuum" or with "air resistance neglected." Students learn that objects in free fall accelerate at approximately 9.8 m/s² and that potential energy converts to kinetic energy. What they often don't internalize is how dramatically air resistance modifies these relationships for real objects at real speeds. The result is a population of people who have learned Newtonian mechanics as a vacuum-physics intuition, then apply it to real-world scenarios where air is doing enormous work. The penny dropped from a skyscraper feels like a pure Newtonian problem — height, mass, gravity — and the intuitive model produces a scary number. The air resistance correction is invisible to this mode of thinking.

**Height as an overwhelming intuitive variable.** 1,454 feet is not a number people have experiential calibration for. The Empire State Building is genuinely vast — it is nearly three times the height of Niagara Falls. When confronted with a number that exceeds our experience, the cognitive response is often a simple scaling: more height means more danger. The fact that there is a ceiling on how fast the penny can fall — that the building could be ten times taller and the terminal velocity would be the same — is not available to naive intuition. Height, in everyday experience, consistently increases the danger of falling objects, because we spend our lives at heights where terminal velocity has not yet been reached. Generalizing this to extreme heights is wrong, but it is a sensible extrapolation from ordinary experience.

**The drama of the scenario.** The Empire State Building is a cultural landmark, and the image of a tiny coin falling from its observation deck through a mile of air toward the crowded streets below has narrative power. Stories need stakes. The "penny kills someone" version of the story has dramatically higher stakes than "penny makes a minor sting" — and higher-stakes narratives are more memorable, more shareable, and more likely to survive in the information ecosystem. Accurate corrections tend to be prosaic; the misinformation has better narrative properties.

**Confirmation through apparent authority.** The claim has been repeated by tour guides, textbooks (including some physics textbooks), and casual science communication for decades. When a belief has been encountered repeatedly, from ostensibly authoritative sources, in different contexts, people reasonably treat it as established. The familiarity heuristic mistakes frequent exposure for reliability. Debunking a belief that has been reinforced across multiple authoritative-seeming contexts requires more than a single correction.

**The "small but fast" mental model.** Intuitive reasoning about kinetic energy often overweights velocity and underweights the limitations imposed by mass and drag. A penny dropped from a great height "feels" like it should be traveling very fast by the time it arrives. The intuition correctly captures that velocity matters for impact force but fails to account for the upper bound imposed by terminal velocity, and for the fact that a light, flat, tumbling object is specifically bad at accumulating kinetic energy.

## 4. Verdict

**The claim is false as stated.** A penny dropped from the Empire State Building — or any skyscraper — would not kill a person below. It would reach a terminal velocity of approximately 30 to 50 mph and would cause pain and perhaps bruising but not penetrating injury or death. This has been calculated by physicists and tested empirically.

The epistemologically-loaded dimension of this case is that while the penny claim is false, the broader intuition it encodes — that falling objects from great heights can be dangerous — is entirely correct. The truth is highly object-dependent. A penny: not lethal. A steel ball bearing: potentially serious. A full soda can: almost certainly dangerous. A larger or denser object dropped from a skyscraper ledge could cause severe injury or death. The specific "penny from skyscraper" claim is false precisely because the penny is a particularly bad candidate for the scenario — but the general category of "falling objects from extreme heights are a danger" is not wrong, only the specific case is.

This is what makes the claim epistemologically loaded: debunking the specific claim should not be taken as license for casual carelessness around high-rise buildings. The penny is safe; other objects are not.

## 5. The Wider Picture

This misconception is a useful entry point for several important scientific and pedagogical discussions.

**Terminal velocity and the physics of drag.** The physics of air resistance is more interesting than it might appear from the standard high-school treatment. Drag force scales with the square of velocity — double an object's speed and you quadruple the drag. This means that as an object falls faster and faster, the drag force grows rapidly until it matches gravity's pull, at which point acceleration stops. The terminal velocity of an object is set by the equation where gravitational force equals drag force: `mg = ½ρv²CₐA`, where m is mass, g is gravitational acceleration, ρ is air density, v is velocity, Cₐ is the drag coefficient, and A is cross-sectional area. For a penny, the small mass and large effective area (due to tumbling) produce a low terminal velocity. For a dense, aerodynamically stable object of much larger mass, the terminal velocity can be far higher — artillery shells, for instance, can reach terminal velocities in the hundreds of miles per hour.

**What actually would be dangerous from a skyscraper.** This is worth addressing directly, because the debunking of the penny claim could create a false sense of security about high-rise hazards. Objects significantly more massive or aerodynamically stable than a penny could cause real danger. Tools, building materials, or any object with a favorable mass-to-drag ratio can reach much higher terminal velocities. New York City's construction code requires tool lanyards and safety nets for exactly this reason — not because of pennies, but because of wrenches, bolts, and chunks of masonry. The penny is the worst-case example for the "falling object from great height" claim; it is not representative of the class of objects one might actually encounter falling from urban construction sites.

**The history of the claim's persistence.** This belief appears to have circulated in New York City at least from the mid-twentieth century, carried by word of mouth and eventually amplified by the tourist culture around the Empire State Building. It has been repeated in print media, in guidebooks, and — most persistently — in informal oral tradition. Physics teachers have sometimes used it as a thought experiment, which paradoxically may have amplified it: posing it as a problem invites the conclusion before the correction. The belief has been debunked in popular science contexts repeatedly, but each debunking reaches only a fraction of the people who subsequently encounter the original claim.

**The role of empirical testing in science communication.** The *MythBusters* approach — building a behavioral test rather than relying on abstract calculation — is instructive for science communication. Bloomfield's calculation is accurate but abstract; the ballistic gelatin test is visceral and immediate. Both point to the same conclusion, but the empirical test communicates it at a different cognitive register. This illustrates a broader principle: mathematical demonstrations of safety claims often fail to override intuitive physics, while direct experimental evidence has more corrective power. For claims where the intuitive model is compelling and the mathematical model requires unfamiliar concepts (terminal velocity, drag coefficients), empirical demonstration may be the most effective correction tool.

**Urban legends as safety knowledge heuristics.** There is an interesting argument that the penny-from-skyscraper legend, even if false in its specifics, functions as a reasonable safety heuristic. People who believe "things dropped from skyscrapers can kill you" will be more careful near the edges of tall buildings and more likely to avoid areas below active construction work. The specific false version of the claim (penny = lethal) produces the same behavioral output as the true generalized version (some things dropped from skyscrapers can be lethal): stay away from things falling from buildings. Whether this justifies the misinformation is a separate normative question — most science communicators would argue that accurate information produces better-calibrated safety behavior — but it explains part of the belief's social persistence. It functions as a memorable encoding of a legitimate safety concern.

**Intuitive physics research.** The psychology of naive physics — how people reason about physical objects, motion, and force without formal instruction — is a well-developed research area in cognitive science. Studies consistently show that untrained intuitions about motion are systematically wrong in predictable ways: people overestimate the effect of impetus, underestimate the role of air resistance, mismodel projectile trajectories, and apply Aristotelian rather than Newtonian intuitions in many contexts. The penny-from-skyscraper belief is a specific instance of the broader finding that human intuitive physics is poorly calibrated for extreme scales — either very large or very small — where everyday experience offers no feedback.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The penny-from-skyscraper claim presents a specific challenge: it is a false claim that is simultaneously an intuitive extension of a true principle (things falling from great heights gain velocity and can be dangerous).

**The canonical debunking structure.** Most fact-checking treatments lead with the terminal velocity concept, establish the specific value for a penny (~30-50 mph), and then provide the empirical anchor — typically the MythBusters test or the Bloomfield calculation. The key rhetorical move is to address the "but it's so high up" intuition directly and explain why terminal velocity caps the danger.

**The "more dangerous objects" caveat.** Good fact-checkers take care to note that while the penny is not lethal, other objects dropped from tall buildings can be. This prevents the debunking from generating a false sense of security about construction sites, observation decks, or urban pedestrian zones. The goal is to correct the specific claim without overcorrecting into the opposite false belief ("things falling from skyscrapers are never dangerous").

**The epistemological framing.** The claim is valuable for illustrating the difference between intuitive extrapolation and empirical physics — between what seems like it must be true given a sensible-seeming mental model, and what is actually true when the full physics is applied. Fact-checkers with a pedagogical orientation often use this case to explain terminal velocity and drag, rather than simply recording a verdict.

**Difficulty of correction.** Studies on misinformation correction suggest that beliefs grounded in seemingly valid logical structures are among the most resistant to debunking. "High place + falling object = high velocity" has the structure of a valid inference. Correcting it requires not just providing a counter-fact but dismantling the logical chain and replacing it with a corrected one — explaining terminal velocity, drag, and why the linear height-to-danger mapping fails. This is a higher cognitive load than simply learning a new fact, and corrections that don't explain the mechanism frequently fail to stick.

**Quantitative claims in myth-busting.** This entry also illustrates the importance of specific numbers in fact-checking. "The penny would not kill you" is a verdict. "The penny reaches approximately 30-50 mph due to tumbling-induced drag" is a mechanistic claim that is checkable, falsifiable, and informative. Effective debunking supplies not just the verdict but the quantitative specificity that allows the claim to be tested and remembered.
