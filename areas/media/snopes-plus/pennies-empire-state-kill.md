# "A Penny Dropped from the Empire State Building Could Kill Someone"


## 1. The Claim

The persistent urban legend asserts that a penny dropped from the top of the Empire State Building would gain sufficient velocity during its fall to kill a person standing on the sidewalk below. This claim has become one of the most enduring myths about the physics of falling objects, combining accessible physics concepts with dramatic consequences that capture popular imagination.

The myth typically presents itself as a cautionary tale about the hidden dangers of seemingly harmless actions. It suggests that ordinary objects, when given sufficient height, can transform into lethal projectiles through the simple action of gravity. This narrative structure appeals to both our fascination with physics and our anxiety about unexpected urban dangers.

The claim has achieved remarkable cultural penetration, appearing in educational contexts as a physics problem, in safety warnings about dropping objects from heights, and in casual conversation as an example of counterintuitive physics. Its persistence reflects widespread misunderstanding about terminal velocity, air resistance, and the relationship between height, speed, and kinetic energy.


## 2. What's Actually True

### Terminal Velocity Physics

The fundamental flaw in the penny-dropping myth lies in its assumption that falling objects continue accelerating indefinitely until impact. This assumption ignores the crucial physics concept of terminal velocity:

**Terminal Velocity Definition:** Terminal velocity occurs when the downward force of gravity equals the upward force of air resistance, resulting in zero net acceleration. At this point, the object falls at constant speed regardless of additional height.

**Penny Terminal Velocity Calculations:** Multiple physicists have calculated the terminal velocity of a U.S. penny. Louis Bloomfield of the University of Virginia, a leading authority on the physics of everyday objects, determined that a penny reaches terminal velocity of approximately 30-50 mph (48-80 km/h), depending on its orientation during fall.

**Comparative Energy Analysis:** At terminal velocity, a 2.5-gram penny carries kinetic energy of approximately 0.04 joules. For comparison, a.22 caliber bullet carries approximately 140 joules at muzzle velocity—over 3,000 times more energy. Even a typical paintball carries about 12 joules, 300 times more than a falling penny.

**Distance to Terminal Velocity:** A penny reaches terminal velocity within the first few seconds of fall, typically after falling 20-50 feet. whether dropped from the Empire State Building (1,454 feet including antenna) or from a much lower height, the impact velocity remains essentially the same.

### Aerodynamic Considerations

The penny's aerodynamic properties make it particularly unsuited to function as a dangerous projectile:

**Shape Factor:** A penny's flat, disc-like shape maximizes air resistance relative to its mass. Unlike streamlined projectiles that cut through air efficiently, pennies present large surface areas that interact strongly with air molecules.

**Tumbling Behavior:** Real-world observations show that falling pennies rarely maintain stable orientations. They tumble randomly, presenting alternating surface areas to airflow and creating chaotic aerodynamic forces that further reduce effective velocity.

**Drag Coefficient:** The drag coefficient for a flat disc like a penny ranges from 1.1 to 1.4, compared to 0.04 for a streamlined bullet or 0.47 for a sphere. This high drag coefficient means that air resistance dominates the penny's motion much more quickly than it would for more aerodynamic objects.

### Experimental Evidence

Multiple experimental investigations have directly tested the penny-dropping hypothesis:

**MythBusters Investigation (2003):** The popular science show MythBusters conducted direct experiments dropping pennies from significant heights and measuring their impact effects. They found that pennies bounced harmlessly off surfaces and, when dropped onto exposed skin, caused only minor stinging sensations comparable to being flicked with a finger.

**University Physics Experiments:** Several university physics departments have conducted controlled experiments measuring penny terminal velocities using wind tunnels and high-speed cameras. These studies consistently confirm theoretical calculations of terminal velocities in the 30-50 mph range.

**Wind Effect Studies:** Additional research has examined how urban wind patterns affect falling objects. The Empire State Building, like most skyscrapers, creates complex air currents around its base that would likely deflect a falling penny away from the building rather than allowing it to fall straight down.

### Real-World Impact Analysis

Analysis of the actual effects of penny impacts provides clear evidence against lethality claims:

**Injury Threshold Data:** Medical literature on projectile injuries indicates that fatal penetrating wounds typically require impact energies of at least 60-80 joules, depending on the projectile's shape and the impact location. A penny's 0.04 joules falls far below this threshold by a factor of more than 1,500.

**Blunt Force Considerations:** Even considering blunt force rather than penetrating trauma, a penny lacks the mass needed to cause serious injury. Blunt force trauma typically requires either high velocity with substantial mass or extremely high velocity with modest mass. Pennies achieve neither condition.

**Historical Precedent:** Despite millions of coins being handled in urban environments and occasional dropping from heights, there are no documented cases of penny-related fatalities that match the mythical scenario. This absence of supporting evidence contrasts sharply with well-documented dangers from other falling objects.

### Comparative Object Analysis

Examining other objects commonly dropped from heights provides important context for evaluating penny dangers:

**Genuinely Dangerous Objects:** Items that do pose serious falling hazards include tools, construction materials, and dense objects with aerodynamic shapes. These objects can maintain much higher terminal velocities and carry substantially more kinetic energy.

**Similar Harmless Objects:** Other light, flat objects like playing cards, paper money, or leaves behave similarly to pennies, reaching low terminal velocities and posing minimal impact dangers.

**Mass-Velocity Relationships:** Objects dangerous when dropped typically have either much greater mass (like wrenches or bricks) or much higher terminal velocities due to aerodynamic design (like darts or arrows).


Snopes' "False" rating accurately reflects established physics and experimental evidence. However, the myth's persistence despite clear refutation reveals important epistemological patterns about how humans process information about everyday physics and urban dangers.

**Intuitive vs. Analytical Thinking:** The myth demonstrates how intuitive reasoning about familiar concepts (gravity, height, impact) can systematically mislead us when applied to scenarios involving unfamiliar scales or physical principles like terminal velocity.

**Quantitative Literacy:** The case highlights the importance of basic quantitative reasoning skills for evaluating claims about physical phenomena. Simple calculations of energy relationships would immediately reveal the implausibility of penny-related fatalities.

**Source Credibility:** The myth shows how even false claims can achieve institutional status when repeated across multiple seemingly authoritative sources, emphasizing the importance of tracing claims to primary empirical evidence rather than accepting them based on repetition alone.


## 3. Why People Believe This

### Intuitive Physics Failures

The penny myth reveals systematic failures in human intuitive understanding of physics that make certain misconceptions particularly compelling:

**Height-Velocity Conflation:** People intuitively assume that greater heights always produce proportionally greater impact velocities, ignoring the velocity-limiting effects of air resistance. This assumption works reasonably well for short falls in vacuum but breaks down dramatically for long falls through atmosphere.

**Mass-Danger Correlation:** There is a persistent intuitive belief that heavier objects are always more dangerous when falling, ignoring the crucial role of aerodynamics in determining actual impact characteristics.

**Energy Accumulation Misconception:** The myth reflects misunderstanding about how kinetic energy accumulates during falls. People assume that longer falls always produce more dangerous impacts, not recognizing that terminal velocity places absolute limits on achievable impact speeds.

**Scale Insensitivity:** Human intuition often fails to appreciate the enormous differences in scale between everyday objects and genuinely dangerous projectiles. The energy difference between a penny and a bullet exceeds most people's ability to intuitively grasp.

### Educational System Gaps

The persistence of this myth reveals specific gaps in physics education:

**Terminal Velocity Neglect:** Many physics courses focus on idealized vacuum conditions where objects accelerate continuously under gravity, without adequate emphasis on real-world air resistance effects that dominate most everyday falling object scenarios.

**Quantitative Analysis Skills:** Students often lack the mathematical tools or inclination to perform the basic calculations needed to evaluate energy relationships and impact dangers quantitatively.

**Real-World Application:** Physics education frequently emphasizes theoretical understanding without sufficient connection to practical applications that would help students evaluate everyday claims about physical phenomena.

### Media and Sensationalism

The myth's propagation reveals broader patterns in how dramatic but false claims achieve wider circulation than accurate but mundane truths:

**Danger Appeal:** Claims about hidden dangers naturally attract attention and sharing, regardless of their accuracy. The penny myth satisfies appetite for information about unexpected hazards in familiar environments.

**Authority Reinforcement:** The myth gains credibility when repeated by teachers, television shows, or other seemingly authoritative sources, creating false impressions of expert consensus.

**Correction Asymmetry:** Corrections to dramatic myths often receive less attention and sharing than the original false claims, making accurate information harder to propagate than misinformation.


## 4. Verdict

**Classification:** REJECTED on theoretical and experimental grounds.

The claim fails both theoretical analysis based on established physics principles and direct experimental testing. A penny's terminal velocity and resulting kinetic energy are insufficient to cause serious injury, let alone death, regardless of the height from which it is dropped.

**Epistemological Assessment:** This myth demonstrates how intuitive reasoning about familiar physical concepts can systematically mislead us when applied to unfamiliar scales or scenarios. It highlights the importance of quantitative analysis and experimental verification in evaluating physical claims.

**Educational Value:** The penny case provides excellent opportunities for teaching terminal velocity, air resistance, kinetic energy calculations, and the relationship between theoretical physics and real-world applications. It illustrates how dramatic claims can be evaluated through basic physics principles and experimental testing.

**Recommendation:** Educational materials should use this myth as a teaching opportunity for physics concepts while clearly distinguishing between imaginary dangers (falling pennies) and real hazards (falling construction materials, tools, or other substantial objects).

The claim's persistence despite clear refutation demonstrates the need for physics education that connects theoretical understanding with practical applications and emphasizes quantitative reasoning skills. In the broader context of urban safety myths, this case illustrates how false claims about dangers can distract attention from real hazards and create unnecessary anxiety about imaginary threats.

**Safety Note:** While pennies pose no lethal danger when dropped, other objects (tools, construction materials, glass) genuinely can cause serious injury or death when falling from significant heights. The refutation of the penny myth should not diminish awareness of real falling object hazards in urban environments.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### Comparative Urban Physics Myths

The penny myth belongs to a broader category of urban legends about the physics of falling objects:

- **Ice from aircraft:** Claims about falling ice from airplanes, which involve different physics due to much greater mass and potential aerodynamic properties.
- **Bullet fired upward:** The genuine danger of bullets fired into the air, which involves much higher energies and more aerodynamic projectiles than pennies.
- **Construction debris:** Real hazards from falling construction materials, which provide contrast to the imaginary penny danger.

These comparative cases help illustrate the specific physical factors (mass, aerodynamics, energy) that distinguish genuinely dangerous falling objects from harmless ones like pennies.

### Safety Communication Challenges

The penny case illustrates broader challenges in communicating about real vs. imaginary safety hazards:

**Attention Economics:** Dramatic false claims about dangers often receive more attention than accurate information about real but less sensational hazards.

**Risk Calibration:** Public safety communication must balance warnings about genuine dangers against creating unnecessary anxiety about imaginary threats.

**Educational Effectiveness:** Teaching about real physics principles requires overcoming misconceptions that may seem more intuitive or dramatic than accurate explanations.

### Urban Environment Physics

The myth reflects broader patterns in how people understand physics in urban environments:

**Scale Relationships:** Urban environments involve scales (building heights, population densities, object masses) that exceed everyday experience and challenge intuitive reasoning.

**Complexity Reduction:** People seek simple explanations for complex urban phenomena, sometimes accepting oversimplified or incorrect models rather than engaging with the actual complexity of urban physics.

**Regulation and Reality:** Safety regulations about dropping objects from heights are based on real hazards from substantial objects, not on mythical dangers from coins or similar light items.

### Contemporary Context and Digital Verification

Modern technology provides new tools for verifying or refuting physics claims:

**Video Documentation:** High-speed cameras and internet video sharing allow direct documentation of falling object behavior that can verify or refute theoretical predictions.

**Simulation Software:** Computer modeling of aerodynamics and projectile motion provides accessible tools for testing physical claims without requiring dangerous real-world experiments.

**Crowd-Sourced Verification:** Online communities can collectively verify or debunk physics claims through distributed experimentation and analysis.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addresses this claim with a "False" rating, correctly identifying it as a misconception about terminal velocity and aerodynamic behavior. Their analysis notes that pennies reach terminal velocity well before hitting the ground and that this terminal velocity is insufficient to cause fatal injuries.

The Snopes fact-check references experimental work by physicists and the MythBusters television show, both of which have directly tested aspects of the penny-dropping scenario. They note that pennies are poor projectiles due to their flat shape, light weight, and resulting high air resistance relative to their mass.

However, the Snopes treatment, while accurate, does not fully explore the physics principles that make this myth particularly instructive about common misconceptions regarding falling objects and the complexity of real-world projectile motion.
