# "A penny dropped from the Empire State Building would kill someone"

## 1. The Claim

One of America's most persistent urban legends holds that a penny dropped from the top of the Empire State Building would gain enough velocity during its 381-meter fall to kill a pedestrian on the street below. This claim appears in countless internet discussions, casual conversations, and even some educational contexts as an illustration of gravitational acceleration and the dangers of falling objects.

The legend typically describes the penny accelerating continuously as it falls, reaching lethal velocities by the time it strikes the ground. Some versions claim the penny would pierce a person's skull like a bullet, while others suggest it would cause fatal blunt force trauma. The story often includes dramatic details about the coin's hypothetical impact speed, sometimes citing figures like 200+ miles per hour or comparing its kinetic energy to that of a bullet.

This urban legend has spawned numerous variations involving other tall buildings and different objects. The basic physics principle underlying the claim—that objects gain speed as they fall—seems intuitively correct to most people. The Empire State Building's impressive height (443 meters including its antenna) provides a suitably dramatic setting that makes the extreme velocity claim seem plausible.

## 2. What's Actually True

A penny dropped from the Empire State Building would not kill anyone. The coin would reach a terminal velocity of only 30-50 miles per hour due to air resistance, delivering insufficient kinetic energy to cause serious injury, let alone death.

Terminal velocity occurs when the downward force of gravity equals the upward force of air resistance (drag). At this point, acceleration stops and the object continues falling at constant speed. For a penny, several factors combine to create relatively low terminal velocity:

**Shape and Orientation**: A penny's flat, circular shape creates significant air resistance when falling face-down (the most stable orientation). The coin acts like a tiny parachute, with air pressure building up beneath its surface. Even when tumbling edge-over-edge, the penny's aerodynamic instability creates turbulent airflow that increases drag significantly compared to a streamlined object.

**Mass-to-Surface-Area Ratio**: At 2.5 grams with a surface area of approximately 2.85 square centimeters, a penny has a relatively high drag-to-mass ratio. Heavier, more compact objects can overcome air resistance more effectively and achieve higher terminal velocities.

**Experimental Evidence**: Physics professor Louis Bloomfield at the University of Virginia conducted wind tunnel experiments demonstrating that a penny reaches terminal velocity around 30-50 mph. MythBusters tested the claim directly by dropping pennies from comparable heights and measuring their impact effects on ballistic gelatin (a human tissue simulant). The pennies caused no significant damage.

**Real-World Impact**: When a penny traveling at terminal velocity strikes human skin, it delivers approximately 0.5 joules of kinetic energy. This is roughly equivalent to being flicked hard with a finger—painful but not dangerous. For comparison, a .22 caliber bullet delivers about 140 joules of energy, nearly 300 times more than the penny.

**Comparative Analysis**: The myth likely persists because people underestimate air resistance effects. In a vacuum, a penny dropped from the Empire State Building would indeed reach lethal speeds (over 150 mph). However, Earth's atmosphere provides sufficient drag to limit the coin's acceleration long before it reaches the ground.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The penny-dropping myth persists due to several cognitive and educational factors that make the false claim seem intuitively reasonable.

**Gravitational Intuition**: Most people correctly understand that falling objects accelerate due to gravity, but they overestimate how long this acceleration continues. The concept of terminal velocity—that air resistance eventually balances gravitational force—requires more sophisticated physics understanding than simple gravitational acceleration.

**Height Amplification**: The Empire State Building's dramatic height makes the claim seem more plausible. At 381 meters, the building is tall enough to inspire thoughts of extreme velocities without being so tall that the implausibility becomes obvious. People intuitively grasp that "higher equals faster" without understanding the limitations imposed by air resistance.

**Mass Misconceptions**: Many people incorrectly believe that heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones, a misconception dating back to Aristotelian physics. While this is false in vacuum conditions, it contains a grain of truth in air—denser objects do achieve higher terminal velocities. However, people tend to overestimate how much difference mass makes for small objects like pennies.

**Media Reinforcement**: The myth has appeared in movies, TV shows, and internet videos as a dramatic example of physics principles. These portrayals typically ignore air resistance entirely or dramatically underestimate its effects. The visual drama of objects falling from great heights makes for compelling storytelling, even when the physics is wrong.

**Educational Gaps**: Many physics courses teach gravitational acceleration using vacuum conditions or ideal scenarios that ignore air resistance. Students learn that falling objects accelerate at 9.8 m/s² without adequate emphasis on real-world conditions where drag forces dominate for lightweight objects.

**Anecdotal "Evidence"**: Urban legends often include secondhand stories about pennies allegedly causing injuries when dropped from tall buildings. These anecdotes, though unverifiable, seem to provide empirical support for the claim. In reality, documented cases of penny-related injuries from tall buildings are virtually nonexistent.

**Incomplete Comparisons**: People sometimes compare penny drops to bullet impacts without accounting for the vast differences in mass, velocity, and kinetic energy transfer. A bullet's pointed shape, dense construction, and high initial velocity create impact conditions completely different from those of a tumbling penny at terminal velocity.

## 4. Verdict

**FALSE**. A penny dropped from the Empire State Building cannot kill anyone and would cause minimal injury at most.

The scientific evidence against this claim is definitive:

**Terminal Velocity Measurements**: Multiple independent measurements confirm that pennies reach terminal velocities between 30-50 mph, well below lethal impact speeds. Wind tunnel testing and computer simulations produce consistent results across different research groups.

**Impact Energy Calculations**: The kinetic energy of a penny at terminal velocity (approximately 0.5 joules) falls far short of the energy needed to cause serious injury (typically requiring 10+ joules for penetration of human skin). The penny delivers less energy than a marble thrown by a child.

**Experimental Testing**: Direct tests using ballistic gelatin, crash test materials, and other impact simulators show that pennies at terminal velocity cause no significant damage. MythBusters' testing included dropping pennies from comparable heights and measuring actual impact effects rather than relying on theoretical calculations alone.

**Real-World Absence**: Despite millions of visitors to tall buildings over many decades, no documented cases exist of pennies dropped from extreme heights causing serious injuries or deaths. If the claim were true, emergency rooms in cities with tall buildings would regularly treat penny-impact injuries.

**Aerodynamic Analysis**: Computational fluid dynamics studies of penny aerodynamics confirm that the coin's shape and mass create high drag coefficients that limit terminal velocity regardless of initial drop height.

The myth's persistence demonstrates how incomplete understanding of air resistance can lead to dramatic overestimation of falling object dangers. While dropping any object from a tall building is inadvisable and potentially illegal, the specific claim about penny lethality is physically impossible under real-world conditions.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The penny-dropping myth illuminates broader issues in physics education and public understanding of aerodynamics that extend well beyond urban legends.

**Terminal Velocity Education**: The concept of terminal velocity represents a crucial physics principle that appears in contexts ranging from skydiving to raindrop formation. However, many educational approaches emphasize gravitational acceleration in vacuum conditions without adequate attention to real-world drag effects. This creates persistent misconceptions about falling object behavior that manifest in various urban legends and safety misunderstandings.

**Scaling Intuitions**: Humans evolved to understand physics at human scales involving human-sized objects at human-relevant speeds. Extrapolating these intuitions to extreme conditions—very small objects, very great heights, very high speeds—often leads to incorrect predictions. The penny myth demonstrates how scaling effects in air resistance can dramatically alter expected outcomes.

**Media Physics**: Popular entertainment regularly portrays falling objects in ways that ignore air resistance entirely. Action movies show characters falling from great heights at unrealistic speeds, while science fiction often treats atmospheric flight as if it occurred in vacuum conditions. These portrayals reinforce misconceptions about real-world aerodynamics.

**Risk Assessment**: The penny myth reflects broader tendencies to overestimate dramatic but unlikely risks while underestimating mundane but probable dangers. People who worry about penny impacts from tall buildings typically ignore much more likely sources of injury from falling objects, such as icicles, construction materials, or even larger coins that actually could cause harm.

**Engineering Applications**: Understanding terminal velocity and drag effects is crucial for numerous practical applications: parachute design, automotive aerodynamics, building ventilation systems, and sports equipment optimization. The same physics principles that make penny drops harmless also determine how raindrops fall, how leaves settle, and how aircraft achieve lift.

**Urban Planning**: City regulations about dropping objects from tall buildings exist for good reasons, but they should be based on accurate physics rather than urban legends. Real falling object hazards include tools, building materials, ice formation, and debris—objects with significantly higher terminal velocities and impact energies than coins.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The penny-dropping claim represents one of the most thoroughly debunked urban legends in fact-checking history, with multiple organizations addressing it using different approaches and evidence types.

**Snopes Analysis**: Snopes rates the claim as "False" and provides detailed physics explanations along with references to experimental testing. Their analysis emphasizes both theoretical calculations and empirical evidence, making it accessible to readers without advanced physics backgrounds.

**MythBusters Testing**: The Discovery Channel's MythBusters program conducted extensive testing of the penny claim, including controlled drops, wind tunnel experiments, and impact measurements. Their "Busted" verdict includes dramatic visual demonstrations that make the physics principles clear to television audiences.

**Academic Sources**: Physics professors and researchers have addressed the claim in educational contexts, using it as a teaching example for terminal velocity and air resistance concepts. These academic treatments typically include detailed mathematical analyses alongside experimental verification.

**Emergency Medicine**: Medical professionals and emergency responders have noted the absence of penny-related injuries in urban trauma data, providing epidemiological evidence against the claim's plausibility. Hospital records from cities with numerous tall buildings show no pattern of coin-impact injuries.

**Engineering Analysis**: Aerospace engineers and aerodynamics specialists have analyzed the penny's drag coefficient and terminal velocity using computational fluid dynamics, providing sophisticated confirmation of experimental results.

**Practical Demonstrations**: Science museums and educational programs often include penny-dropping demonstrations that allow visitors to observe terminal velocity effects directly. These hands-on experiences help build intuitive understanding of air resistance principles.

The consensus across all these approaches is unanimous: pennies cannot achieve lethal velocities when dropped in air, regardless of initial height. The claim's thorough debunking has made it a standard example in discussions of urban legends and physics misconceptions.

However, fact-checkers note important caveats: while pennies are harmless, other objects dropped from tall buildings can indeed cause serious injuries or death. The physics principles that make pennies safe do not apply to heavier, more aerodynamic objects like tools, building materials, or dense projectiles. Proper building safety requires preventing all objects from being dropped, not just harmless ones.

The penny myth's educational value lies not just in its debunking but in its illustration of how air resistance affects falling objects differently based on their mass, shape, and aerodynamic properties. For physics educators and fact-checkers, the claim provides an excellent case study in how mathematical analysis, experimental testing, and real-world observation can work together to establish scientific truth and dispel persistent misconceptions.
