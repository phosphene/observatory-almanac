# "A Penny Dropped from the Empire State Building Can Kill Someone"


## 1. The Claim

**Primary Claim:** A penny dropped from the Empire State Building would fall with enough force to kill someone on the ground.

This assertion appears in various forms across popular culture, educational discussions, and casual conversation. Variants include "a penny dropped from a skyscraper becomes a deadly projectile," "coins falling from great heights can penetrate skulls," or more generally "small objects become lethal weapons when dropped from sufficient altitude."

The claim intuitively appeals to everyday physics reasoning: higher drops mean faster speeds, faster speeds mean more force, and sufficient force can cause serious injury. The Empire State Building, at 1,454 feet including its antenna, provides a compelling test case for this intuitive logic.

The myth persists in safety discussions around tall buildings, appears in physics education as a thought experiment, and occasionally surfaces in media coverage of objects falling from buildings. It represents a broader category of physics misconceptions about how small objects behave during long falls.


## 2. What's Actually True

### Terminal Velocity Physics

**Mass and Dimensions:**
- U.S. penny mass: 2.5 grams (0.0025 kg)
- Diameter: 19.05 mm (0.75 inches) 
- Thickness: 1.55 mm (0.061 inches)
- Cross-sectional area: 2.85 × 10⁻⁴ square meters

**Terminal Velocity Calculation:**
Terminal velocity occurs when gravitational force equals air resistance:
V_terminal = √(2mg / ρAC_d)

Where:
- m = mass (0.0025 kg)
- g = acceleration due to gravity (9.8 m/s²)
- ρ = air density at sea level (1.225 kg/m³)
- A = cross-sectional area (2.85 × 10⁻⁴ m²)
- C_d = drag coefficient (≈1.0 for a flat circular disc)

**Result:** A penny reaches terminal velocity at approximately 25-30 mph (11-13 m/s), depending on orientation and atmospheric conditions.

### Kinetic Energy Analysis

At terminal velocity, a penny's kinetic energy equals:
KE = ½mv² = ½(0.0025 kg)(12 m/s)² = 0.18 joules

**Energy Comparison Context:**
- Human skin penetration threshold: ~50-100 joules
-.22 caliber bullet: ~200 joules
- Baseball pitch (90 mph): ~150 joules 
- Paintball impact: ~12-15 joules
- Bee sting energy delivery: ~0.1 joules

A penny at terminal velocity delivers energy comparable to a bee sting—sufficient to notice but far below injury thresholds for intact skin.

### Experimental Evidence

**Louis Bloomfield Experiments (University of Virginia):**
Professor Bloomfield conducted controlled experiments dropping pennies on volunteers from heights sufficient to reach terminal velocity. Results consistently showed minor stinging sensation without injury, bruising, or skin penetration.

**MythBusters Testing (2003):**
The Discovery Channel program tested the penny drop myth using high-speed cameras and impact measurements. Their results confirmed physics calculations: terminal velocity pennies caused no significant injury to test subjects.

**Wind Tunnel Studies:**
Controlled wind tunnel experiments demonstrate that pennies tumble irregularly during falls, creating additional air resistance that can reduce effective terminal velocity below theoretical calculations.

### Real-World Complications

**Tumbling Effects:**
Unlike ideal physics problems, real pennies don't fall in perfect orientation. Tumbling creates variable cross-sectional areas and drag coefficients, typically reducing average fall speed below maximum terminal velocity.

**Urban Wind Patterns:**
Cities create complex wind environments around tall buildings. Updrafts, downdrafts, and wind shear can significantly alter falling object trajectories and speeds. Many objects dropped from the Empire State Building would be caught in updrafts or blown away from the building base.

**Air Density Variations:**
Atmospheric density decreases with altitude, affecting both terminal velocity calculations and actual fall behavior. Objects dropped from 1,400+ feet experience varying air resistance throughout their descent.


### The Persistence Problem

Despite repeated debunking by physics educators, mythbusting shows, and fact-checkers, the penny drop myth persists because it aligns with deep-seated intuitions about falling objects. Correction requires not just factual information but conceptual restructuring of how people think about terminal velocity and energy transfer.

The myth's persistence illustrates what cognitive scientists call "belief perseverance"—the tendency for misconceptions to survive even after corrective information has been provided and understood. People may accept the physics correction intellectually while retaining intuitive belief in the myth.

### Educational Implications

Effective physics education must address common misconceptions directly rather than simply presenting correct principles. Students need opportunities to confront their intuitive predictions with experimental results, particularly around counterintuitive phenomena like terminal velocity.

The penny drop serves as an excellent pedagogical case study because it combines accessible mathematics, testable predictions, and dramatic misconceptions that students care about correcting.

### Risk Assessment Psychology

The myth reflects broader patterns in how people assess risks from unfamiliar physical phenomena. When lacking direct experience, people rely on analogical reasoning that often produces systematic overestimation of low-probability, high-visibility risks.

This pattern appears across domains: people overestimate risks from airplane crashes, shark attacks, and falling objects while underestimating more probable dangers like automobile accidents or household injuries.


## 3. Why People Believe This

### Intuitive Physics Misconceptions

The penny drop myth reflects systematic errors in human physics intuition, documented extensively in cognitive science research. People consistently overestimate the effects of height on falling object lethality due to several cognitive biases:

**Linear Scaling Assumption:** Intuitive reasoning suggests that doubling height doubles danger, ignoring the exponential relationship between air resistance and velocity. This leads to dramatic overestimation of high-altitude drop effects.

**Mass Independence Ignorance:** People often conflate size with mass or fail to appreciate how mass-to-surface-area ratios affect terminal velocity. A penny "feels" substantial in hand but behaves aerodynamically like a leaf.

**Energy Transfer Misunderstanding:** Everyday experience with thrown objects (where human muscle energy determines impact) creates poor intuitions about how small masses behave at terminal velocity.

### Educational Physics Problems

Standard physics education often inadvertently reinforces the misconception by using simplified models that ignore air resistance. Students learn that falling objects accelerate continuously under gravity, creating mental models where height always increases danger.

When air resistance appears in coursework, it's often treated as minor correction rather than fundamental force that dominates the behavior of light, high-drag objects like coins.

### Media Amplification Patterns

The penny drop myth spreads through media coverage that prioritizes dramatic scenarios over accurate physics. News stories about falling objects from buildings often mention the "deadly penny" concept without fact-checking, treating it as established knowledge.

Social media amplifies the myth through viral posts that combine intuitive appeal ("imagine a penny falling 100 stories!") with authoritative framing ("physics proves..."), creating persuasive but inaccurate content.


## 4. Verdict

**REJECTED BUT PRODUCTIVELY WRONG**

The claim that a penny dropped from the Empire State Building could kill someone is factually false based on well-established physics principles and experimental evidence. Snopes' "False" verdict accurately reflects the scientific consensus and available data.

However, the claim's persistence reflects deeper truths about human physics intuition and risk assessment psychology. The misconception arises from reasonable but incomplete mental models about falling objects, and addressing it effectively requires understanding why the false belief feels intuitively correct.

The myth serves educational value by providing a compelling case study in terminal velocity, energy transfer, and the limits of intuitive physics reasoning. It also contributes to legitimate safety consciousness around falling objects, even though its specific fears are misplaced.

**Physics Reality:** A penny reaches terminal velocity around 25-30 mph and delivers roughly 0.18 joules of kinetic energy—comparable to a bee sting and far below injury thresholds for intact skin. Multiple experimental studies confirm that penny impacts cause at most minor stinging sensations.

**Misconception Source:** The false belief stems from intuitive linear scaling assumptions (higher = more dangerous) combined with neglect of air resistance effects that dominate the behavior of light, high-drag objects during long falls.

**Educational Opportunity:** Rather than simply dismissing the claim as "false," physics educators can use the penny drop myth as an entry point for exploring terminal velocity, drag forces, and energy analysis while addressing common misconceptions about falling objects.

**Safety Context:** While pennies pose minimal threat, other objects dropped from heights can cause serious injury. The myth's caution-promoting effects may be prosocial even when its specific claims are incorrect.

The penny drop claim exemplifies how scientific misconceptions can be "productively wrong"—false in their specific assertions but valuable for understanding broader patterns in human reasoning about physical phenomena. Effective correction requires addressing both the factual errors and the cognitive processes that make the false belief feel intuitively reasonable.

## 5. The Wider Picture

### Terminal Velocity in Popular Culture

The penny drop myth connects to broader misunderstandings about terminal velocity across popular culture. Movies and television frequently show characters surviving impossible falls or objects causing unrealistic damage, reinforcing intuitive but incorrect physics models.

Video games often implement simplified physics that ignore air resistance, creating entertainment experiences that contradict real-world behavior. These virtual environments can reinforce misconceptions when players unconsciously generalize game physics to reality.

### Actual Falling Object Hazards

While pennies pose minimal threat, other objects dropped from heights can cause serious injury or death. The physics principles that make pennies harmless don't apply universally:

**High-Density Objects:** Tools, bolts, or construction materials maintain dangerous velocities even with air resistance due to higher mass-to-surface-area ratios.

**Aerodynamic Objects:** Pens, pencils, or arrows can orient themselves to minimize drag, reaching higher terminal velocities with concentrated impact points.

**Glass and Sharp Objects:** Even at modest velocities, broken glass or metal fragments can cause cuts due to concentrated force on small contact areas.

### Building Safety Regulations

The penny drop myth, despite being false, has contributed to legitimate safety discussions about objects falling from tall buildings. Construction codes require netting, barriers, and secured tools precisely because other objects DO pose genuine hazards.

The myth thus serves a prosocial function by encouraging caution around falling objects, even though its specific claims about pennies are incorrect. false beliefs can sometimes promote beneficial behaviors through coincidentally appropriate precautions.

### Physics Education Reform

The penny drop myth has influenced physics curriculum design, with many educators now using terminal velocity demonstrations and falling object experiments to directly address student misconceptions.

Modern physics education research emphasizes "conceptual physics" approaches that prioritize understanding over calculation, recognizing that students can solve problems correctly while maintaining fundamental misconceptions about underlying principles.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes addressed this claim directly in their fact-check database, rating it **False**. Their analysis focuses on terminal velocity calculations and energy transfer principles, concluding that a penny lacks sufficient mass and aerodynamic properties to cause serious injury even when dropped from extreme heights.

The Snopes article cites physics professors and experimental evidence, including Louis Bloomfield of the University of Virginia, who allowed pennies to be dropped on his head from terminal velocity to demonstrate their harmlessness. Snopes emphasizes that while a penny might sting or startle someone, it lacks the kinetic energy necessary for serious injury.

However, Snopes' treatment, while factually accurate, provides limited exploration of why this misconception persists or how it reflects broader patterns in physics intuition. The fact-check serves its debunking function but leaves unexplored the epistemological dimensions of how people reason about falling objects.
