# "Dropping a Penny from the Empire State Building Can Kill Someone"


## 1. The Claim

The claim that dropping a penny from the Empire State Building could kill someone represents one of the most persistent myths in popular physics understanding and urban folklore. According to this widespread belief, the penny would accelerate continuously during its fall from the building's observation deck (approximately 320 meters above street level), reaching sufficient velocity to cause lethal injury upon impact with a pedestrian below.

This claim appears in various forms across educational contexts, popular media, and urban folklore: that the penny would reach bullet-like speeds due to gravitational acceleration, that it could penetrate a human skull with the force of a high-velocity projectile, that it could cause fatal injuries comparable to gunshot wounds, or that it represents a serious public safety hazard requiring legal prohibitions against dropping objects from tall buildings.

The narrative typically assumes that the penny would continue accelerating throughout its fall according to the formula v = gt (where g is gravitational acceleration and t is time), potentially reaching tremendous speeds that would make it a lethal projectile upon impact. Some versions suggest the penny could reach speeds of several hundred miles per hour or approach the velocity of bullets fired from firearms.

More elaborate versions of the myth include specific calculations that ignore air resistance, claims about the penny's ability to embed in concrete or penetrate vehicle windshields, or assertions that multiple pennies dropped simultaneously could create a deadly "rainfall" effect. The myth often serves as a cautionary tale about the dangers of dropping objects from heights and as an intuitive physics example about gravitational acceleration.

The claim has been particularly persistent in urban environments where tall buildings are common, leading to both genuine safety concerns and exaggerated fears about the dangers of falling objects. It appears regularly in educational contexts as an example of physics principles, though often without proper correction of the underlying misconceptions about air resistance and terminal velocity.

The myth has also been amplified by its appearance in popular culture, including references in television shows, movies, and educational programs that either perpetuate the misconception or use it as a dramatic device without clarifying the actual physics involved. This cultural reinforcement has helped maintain the belief across generations despite the availability of experimental evidence to the contrary.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim is false based on well-established physics principles and extensive experimental verification. A penny dropped from the Empire State Building would not kill anyone and would barely cause noticeable injury upon impact.

**Terminal Velocity Physics**: A penny dropped from the Empire State Building would not continue accelerating throughout its fall due to the fundamental physics principle of terminal velocity. Due to its flat, disc-like shape and relatively light weight (2.5 grams for a modern U.S. penny), it would quickly reach terminal velocity—the speed at which gravitational force equals air resistance force, resulting in zero net acceleration and constant velocity for the remainder of the fall.

The physics of terminal velocity involves force balance between gravitational acceleration (mg) pulling the object downward and air resistance force pushing upward. For objects with high drag coefficients relative to their mass, like pennies, this balance is achieved at relatively low velocities compared to more aerodynamic objects.

**Experimental Measurements and Research**: Louis Bloomfield, a physicist at the University of Virginia, conducted controlled wind tunnel tests to determine penny terminal velocities under laboratory conditions. His research found that a penny reaches terminal velocity of approximately 50-80 km/h (31-50 mph), depending on its orientation during the fall and atmospheric conditions. The penny's natural tendency to tumble and flutter during descent significantly increases air resistance compared to a smooth, stable descent pattern.

Additional experimental work by physics educators at various universities has confirmed these terminal velocity measurements using different methodologies, including high-speed video analysis of dropped pennies and computational fluid dynamics modeling of penny aerodynamics. These convergent experimental approaches provide robust evidence for the penny's limited terminal velocity.

**MythBusters Experimental Validation**: The MythBusters television program conducted comprehensive experiments to test the lethal penny myth using multiple experimental approaches. They fired pennies at terminal velocity into ballistic gel models designed to simulate human tissue, used compressed air devices to launch pennies at various speeds, and conducted actual drop tests from tall structures.

The results consistently showed that pennies at terminal velocity created only minor surface impressions in ballistic gel without significant penetration, comparable to being flicked by a finger rather than struck by a dangerous projectile. Even when they artificially accelerated pennies to speeds higher than achievable through free fall, the pennies failed to cause injuries that would be considered serious or life-threatening.

**Comparative Velocity and Energy Analysis**: To provide proper physical context, bullets from typical handguns travel at velocities of 370-1,500 meters per second (1,200-5,000 km/h), while a penny at terminal velocity travels at approximately 14-22 meters per second (50-80 km/h). This represents a speed differential of more than an order of magnitude, placing pennies in the category of minor irritants rather than dangerous projectiles.

The kinetic energy comparison is even more striking: a penny at terminal velocity possesses kinetic energy of approximately 0.1-0.2 joules, while typical handgun bullets possess kinetic energies of 300-1,500 joules. This energy differential of three orders of magnitude explains why pennies cannot cause the type of tissue damage associated with dangerous projectiles.

**Aerodynamic Factors and Drag Analysis**: The penny's flat, circular shape creates significant air resistance through multiple aerodynamic mechanisms that limit its terminal velocity far below what would be achieved by more aerodynamically favorable shapes. These mechanisms include:

*Form drag* from the penny's blunt profile, which creates a large wake region behind the falling object and generates significant pressure differential between front and back surfaces. The penny's flat face presents maximum cross-sectional area to airflow, maximizing form drag effects.

*Induced drag* from turbulence generation around the penny's edges, where airflow separation creates vortices and energy-dissipating wake structures. The sharp edges of a penny create particularly effective turbulence generation compared to streamlined shapes designed to minimize drag.

*Variable drag* from the penny's tumbling motion during fall, which constantly changes its orientation relative to airflow and creates unstable aerodynamic conditions that further increase effective drag coefficient. Wind tunnel studies show that pennies rarely maintain stable orientation during free fall, instead exhibiting chaotic tumbling patterns that maximize air resistance.

**Atmospheric and Environmental Factors**: Real-world conditions around tall buildings create additional factors that further limit penny terminal velocity and impact energy. Wind patterns around skyscrapers create complex airflow conditions including updrafts, downdrafts, and lateral air movements that can significantly affect falling object trajectories and impact velocities.

The Empire State Building, like most tall structures in urban environments, creates its own microclimate of air movement that can reduce effective terminal velocities of small objects compared to free-fall conditions in still air. Building-induced updrafts can actually slow or divert small falling objects, while crosswinds can carry them away from areas directly below the building.

**Material Properties and Impact Mechanics**: The penny's material properties also contribute to its inability to cause serious injury even at terminal velocity. Modern pennies are composed primarily of zinc with a thin copper coating, creating a relatively soft metal combination that deforms upon impact rather than maintaining structural integrity like hardened projectiles.

Upon impact at terminal velocity, a penny lacks sufficient kinetic energy to maintain its shape and penetrate resistant materials like skin, clothing, or other typical surface materials. Instead, the impact energy is dissipated through deformation of both the penny and the impact surface, spreading the force over a larger area and reducing pressure to non-harmful levels.


The persistence of the lethal penny myth demonstrates fundamental gaps in public understanding of physics principles that govern everyday phenomena, revealing how theoretical knowledge and practical applications can become disconnected in popular consciousness.

**Convergent Evidence from Multiple Methodologies**: The conclusion that pennies cannot cause lethal injuries when dropped from tall buildings rests on convergent evidence from multiple independent methodologies: theoretical physics calculations based on well-established principles, controlled wind tunnel experiments measuring actual terminal velocities, ballistic gel impact testing simulating real-world injury mechanisms, and computational fluid dynamics modeling of penny aerodynamics.

This methodological convergence provides high confidence in the conclusion while demonstrating how different scientific approaches can validate and reinforce each other. The consistency of results across theoretical predictions, laboratory experiments, and computer simulations eliminates reasonable doubt about the penny's non-lethal terminal velocity.

**Educational Physics Gaps and Misconception Sources**: The myth's persistence reveals specific gaps in physics education that fail to adequately connect theoretical principles with real-world applications. Many students learn about gravitational acceleration in idealized contexts without sufficient emphasis on when and how air resistance becomes the dominant factor affecting motion.

The mathematical elegance of simple kinematic equations (v = gt, d = ½gt²) can create false confidence in their universal applicability, leading to systematic errors when students apply these equations to situations where air resistance cannot be ignored. Effective physics education requires explicit attention to the conditions under which different equations apply and the factors that determine when simplifying assumptions become invalid.

**Shape-Dependent Physics and Real-World Complexity**: The penny example illustrates how object shape dramatically affects real-world physics behavior in ways that may not be immediately apparent from theoretical treatment. Unlike spherical objects that might more closely approximate idealized falling body problems, flat, disc-shaped objects exhibit complex aerodynamic behavior that significantly limits terminal velocity.

This shape dependence extends beyond simple drag coefficient considerations to include aerodynamic stability, tumbling behavior, and flow separation patterns that create much more complex physics than suggested by simplified models. Understanding these complexities is crucial for developing accurate intuition about real-world physics phenomena.

**Risk Assessment and Proportional Response**: While pennies specifically pose minimal danger when dropped from heights, the broader principle of dropped object safety remains valid for heavier, more aerodynamic items that can achieve dangerous terminal velocities. Proper risk assessment requires distinguishing between objects based on their specific physical properties rather than applying general rules about falling objects universally.

Construction tools, building materials, and other dense, aerodynamic objects can indeed achieve terminal velocities sufficient to cause serious injury or death when dropped from tall buildings. The penny myth correction should enhance rather than undermine legitimate safety concerns about falling object hazards in urban environments and construction contexts.


## 3. Why People Believe This

Understanding this claim requires basic knowledge of terminal velocity physics and the relationship between gravitational force, air resistance, and object shape, but the epistemological error lies in the intuitive assumption that falling objects continue accelerating indefinitely under gravitational influence without consideration of other forces.

**Theoretical Physics versus Real-World Applications**: The persistence of the lethal penny myth demonstrates common misconceptions that arise when simplified theoretical physics models are applied to real-world scenarios without considering all relevant forces. Many introductory physics courses focus on idealized scenarios involving objects falling in vacuum conditions with negligible air resistance, which provides useful theoretical understanding but can create misconceptions when applied to practical situations.

The mathematical elegance of the equation v = gt (velocity equals gravitational acceleration times time) creates compelling intuitive appeal because it suggests unlimited acceleration with time. However, this equation only applies in vacuum conditions or for very short fall times before air resistance becomes significant. For real-world falling objects, especially those with unfavorable aerodynamic properties like pennies, air resistance quickly becomes the dominant force affecting motion.

**Force Balance Analysis and Equilibrium**: At terminal velocity, the downward gravitational force (mg, where m is mass and g is gravitational acceleration) equals the upward air resistance force, creating equilibrium conditions that result in constant velocity. Air resistance force is typically calculated using the equation F_drag = ½ρv²CdA, where ρ is air density, v is velocity, Cd is the drag coefficient, and A is the cross-sectional area.

For a penny, the relatively high drag coefficient due to its flat shape and the substantial cross-sectional area create significant air resistance at relatively low velocities. The force balance equation shows that terminal velocity is achieved when ½ρv²CdA = mg, which can be solved to find v_terminal = √(2mg/ρCdA). This equation demonstrates that terminal velocity depends on the ratio of weight to aerodynamic resistance, explaining why light, flat objects like pennies achieve low terminal velocities.

**Drag Coefficient Considerations**: A penny's drag coefficient varies significantly based on its orientation during the fall, but remains high relative to aerodynamically designed objects regardless of orientation. When falling face-down or face-up, it presents maximum cross-sectional area with a drag coefficient approaching 1.0-1.2 (compared to approximately 0.3 for a sphere or 0.04 for a streamlined airfoil).

When tumbling, which is the most common falling pattern due to aerodynamic instability, the drag coefficient fluctuates but generally remains high due to continuous turbulence generation and changing cross-sectional area presentation. The tumbling motion also creates additional complexity in airflow patterns that further increases effective drag beyond what would be calculated for stable orientations.

**Height Independence and Distance to Terminal Velocity**: Terminal velocity is independent of drop height once sufficient altitude is reached for the object to achieve force balance between gravitational and drag forces. The Empire State Building's height (320m to observation deck, 443m to roof) provides more than adequate distance for a penny to reach terminal velocity multiple times over.

For small, light objects like pennies, terminal velocity is typically achieved within the first 50 meters of free fall, meaning that a penny dropped from the Empire State Building would maintain constant velocity for approximately 85% of its descent. This height independence means that dropping a penny from twice the height would not double its impact velocity, contrary to common intuitive assumptions about falling objects.

**Kinetic Energy and Impact Mechanics**: The kinetic energy of a penny at terminal velocity (KE = ½mv²) can be precisely calculated as approximately 0.1-0.2 joules, depending on specific velocity achieved. This energy level is insufficient for significant tissue damage or penetration through human skin, which typically requires kinetic energy levels of at least 10-20 joules for minor injury and much higher levels for serious trauma.

To provide comparative context, the kinetic energy of a penny at terminal velocity is similar to that of a marble thrown gently by hand, a ping-pong ball hit with moderate force, or a large raindrop during a heavy storm. These comparisons help illustrate why terminal velocity pennies pose minimal safety risk despite falling from great heights.

**Scale Effects and Intuitive Physics Failures**: The relationship between object size, weight, and air resistance creates counterintuitive results where smaller objects may be significantly less dangerous as projectiles than intuition suggests. Human experience with throwing objects at low speeds doesn't provide reliable intuition for terminal velocity effects, aerodynamic behavior, or the force balance that governs high-altitude drops.

The penny scenario illustrates broader difficulties in physics intuition about scaling effects, where properties that seem important at human scales (height of fall, weight of object) may be less significant than properties that are difficult to perceive directly (air resistance, drag coefficients, aerodynamic stability). This scaling intuition failure contributes to the persistence of the myth despite its contradiction of basic physics principles.


## 4. Verdict

**Rating: ENHANCE - Physics Reality Check with Educational Opportunity**

The claim that "dropping a penny from the Empire State Building can kill someone" requires comprehensive enhancement to align popular physics misconceptions with established scientific principles and experimental evidence. The myth contains enough intuitive plausibility to persist while misrepresenting the physics of falling objects, air resistance, and terminal velocity.

**Terminal Velocity Correction**: A penny dropped from the Empire State Building would reach terminal velocity of approximately 50-80 km/h (31-50 mph) within the first 50 meters of fall, then maintain that constant velocity for the remainder of its 320-meter descent to street level. This speed is insufficient to cause serious injury—experimental testing confirms that impact would feel comparable to being flicked by a finger rather than struck by a dangerous projectile.

**Experimental Validation**: The correction rests on convergent evidence from multiple experimental approaches: controlled wind tunnel testing by university physicists measuring actual penny terminal velocities, MythBusters ballistic gel experiments demonstrating minimal impact damage, kinetic energy calculations showing insufficient energy for tissue penetration, and computational fluid dynamics modeling of penny aerodynamics confirming high drag coefficients that limit velocity.

**Aerodynamic Reality**: The penny's flat, disc-like shape creates maximum air resistance relative to its 2.5-gram weight, causing it to tumble and flutter during descent in ways that significantly limit terminal velocity compared to more aerodynamic objects. The drag coefficient for a tumbling penny approaches 1.0-1.2, compared to 0.3 for a sphere or 0.04 for streamlined shapes, explaining why pennies achieve such low terminal velocities despite falling from extreme heights.

**Comparative Energy Context**: To provide proper physical perspective, bullets travel at 370-1,500 m/s while pennies at terminal velocity travel at 14-22 m/s—a speed differential of 25-70 times. The kinetic energy comparison is even more dramatic: pennies at terminal velocity possess 0.1-0.2 joules compared to 300-1,500 joules for typical bullets, explaining why pennies cannot cause the tissue damage associated with dangerous projectiles.

**Safety Nuance and Risk Proportionality**: While pennies specifically pose minimal danger, the enhancement must preserve legitimate safety concerns about other falling objects. Heavier, more aerodynamic items (tools, construction materials, dense objects) can achieve terminal velocities sufficient to cause serious injury or death. Proper risk assessment requires object-specific analysis rather than general rules about falling objects.

**Gap Category: Distorted-but-Grounded**

This misconception reflects genuine physics principles (gravitational acceleration, kinetic energy) that become distorted through incomplete understanding of air resistance effects and terminal velocity limitations. The myth captures real concerns about falling object hazards while misapplying physics principles to specific scenarios where other forces dominate over gravitational acceleration.

**ENHANCE Epistemology**: This case exemplifies enhancement methodology by preserving valid physics concerns while correcting specific misconceptions. The enhancement preserves the legitimate educational value of the penny scenario while correcting the dangerous misconception. **Educational Framework**: The corrected understanding provides excellent opportunities to teach multiple physics concepts: terminal velocity and force balance, the relationship between object shape and aerodynamic properties, the difference between theoretical models and real-world applications, the importance of considering all forces in physics problems, and the role of experimental evidence in validating or refuting physics claims.

**Methodological Implications**: This case demonstrates the value of experimental accessibility in physics education. Unlike theoretical physics concepts that require abstract mathematical treatment, terminal velocity can be directly observed and measured using relatively simple equipment, providing concrete evidence that students can verify independently.

**Contemporary Relevance**: Understanding the actual physics of falling objects becomes important for urban safety education, construction site management, and general scientific literacy in technological environments where such questions arise frequently. Accurate physics understanding enables appropriate risk assessment and safety measures while avoiding both false confidence and unnecessary anxiety about urban hazards.

Rather than dismissing the underlying safety interest, effective communication preserves legitimate concerns while upgrading the physics understanding that informs risk assessment and safety decision-making.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The penny-dropping myth intersects with broader patterns in physics education, public safety understanding, urban mythology about technological dangers, and the relationship between theoretical scientific knowledge and practical understanding of physical phenomena.

**Urban Physics Mythology and Technological Fears**: The penny myth joins a broader category of persistent urban physics legends that reflect anxieties about technological environments and the hidden dangers of modern urban life. Similar myths include claims that glass is a liquid that flows over centuries (causing old windows to be thicker at the bottom), that lightning never strikes the same place twice (contradicted by structures like the Empire State Building itself, which is struck multiple times annually), or that the Coriolis effect determines toilet drain direction in different hemispheres.

These urban physics myths often contain enough scientific vocabulary and plausible-sounding explanations to persist in popular consciousness despite being readily contradicted by experimental evidence or basic physics principles. They reflect broader patterns in how scientific information gets transmitted, distorted, and mythologized as it moves from specialized technical contexts into general cultural knowledge.

The penny myth specifically taps into fears about the hidden dangers of urban environments where human-scale intuition may not adequately assess risks from technological structures like skyscrapers. The myth provides a simple narrative that connects everyday objects (pennies) with dramatic consequences (death), creating memorable but false associations between common activities and extreme risks.

**Public Safety Education and Risk Communication**: While pennies pose minimal danger, the broader principle of dropped object hazards remains a legitimate public safety concern that requires accurate risk communication. Construction sites, tall buildings under maintenance, and aircraft operations must consider the real physics of falling objects, which can indeed be lethal for heavier, more aerodynamically favorable items.

The challenge in public safety education involves distinguishing between objects that pose real risks (tools, construction materials, dense objects) and those that pose minimal risks (coins, paper, lightweight debris) without creating either false confidence or unnecessary anxiety. Effective safety communication requires accurate physics understanding to provide appropriate guidance about actual risk levels.

The penny myth can actually undermine legitimate safety concerns by creating false confidence about dropped object dangers—if people learn that pennies are harmless, they might incorrectly generalize this conclusion to all small objects dropped from heights. Conversely, exaggerated fears about penny dangers can make people skeptical of legitimate warnings about genuinely dangerous falling objects.
