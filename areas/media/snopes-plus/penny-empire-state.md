# "Dropping a Penny from the Empire State Building Could Kill Someone": A Snopes+ Review

## 1. Claim Statement

A penny dropped from the top of the Empire State Building (approximately 1,454 feet high including its antenna) would accelerate to such high velocity that it could kill a person struck on the street below. This claim suggests that the penny's fall from this extreme height would overcome air resistance sufficiently to achieve lethal kinetic energy upon impact.

The assertion often appears in physics discussions, urban safety warnings, and popular culture references to the dangers of dropping objects from tall buildings. It serves as a cautionary tale about the potential consequences of seemingly harmless actions at great heights, and frequently appears in educational contexts as an example of gravitational acceleration and kinetic energy.

## 2. Snopes Assessment

While Snopes has not published a dedicated fact-check specifically addressing the Empire State Building penny scenario, the organization has addressed related claims about falling objects and terminal velocity in various contexts. The absence of direct coverage leaves room for continued propagation of this persistent urban myth.

The lack of authoritative mainstream fact-checking on this specific claim is notable given its frequent appearance in physics education, safety discussions, and popular culture. This gap has allowed the claim to persist without systematic debunking from trusted verification sources.

## 3. Claim Fidelity Audit

### The Physics Foundation

A United States penny weighs approximately 2.5 grams and has a diameter of 19.05 millimeters with a thickness of 1.55 millimeters. These physical characteristics are crucial for calculating its aerodynamic properties and terminal velocity.

When dropped, a penny experiences two primary forces: gravitational acceleration (9.8 m/s²) pulling it downward, and air resistance pushing upward. Air resistance increases with velocity until it equals gravitational force, at which point the penny reaches terminal velocity and stops accelerating.

### Terminal Velocity Calculations

Multiple physics analyses have calculated the penny's terminal velocity. Dr. Louis Bloomfield of the University of Virginia, a physicist specializing in everyday physics phenomena, calculated that a penny reaches terminal velocity of approximately 30-50 mph (48-80 km/h) depending on its orientation during fall.

The penny's flat, disc-like shape creates high air resistance relative to its mass. Unlike streamlined objects that can maintain aerodynamic profiles during fall, a penny tumbles chaotically, presenting varying cross-sectional areas to airflow and maintaining high drag coefficients throughout its descent.

### Experimental Verification

The MythBusters television program conducted practical testing of this claim in their 2003 episode "Penny Drop." Their experiments involved dropping pennies from various heights and measuring impact effects. Results showed that pennies at terminal velocity produce minimal impact force—insufficient to penetrate skin or cause serious injury.

The show's testing included drops from their own constructed tower and impact measurements on ballistic gelatin (a standard material for testing projectile effects). Results consistently showed that terminal velocity pennies lack the kinetic energy necessary for lethal injury.

### Real-World Context

The Empire State Building's unique atmospheric conditions actually work against achieving maximum terminal velocity. The building's height creates significant updrafts and wind currents around its structure, which would further reduce a falling penny's effective downward velocity.

Additionally, the building's setback design and surrounding urban canyon effects create complex airflow patterns that would deflect and slow falling objects. These architectural and environmental factors make the theoretical terminal velocity calculation an overestimate of likely real-world impact velocity.

## 4. Review Epistemology

### The Intuitive Physics Problem

The penny myth demonstrates a common failure in intuitive physics reasoning—the assumption that greater height automatically equals greater danger for any falling object. This intuition ignores the crucial role of air resistance and terminal velocity in limiting the kinetic energy of small, high-drag objects.

Human intuition about falling objects evolved in contexts involving relatively dense, aerodynamic projectiles (stones, spears, etc.) where air resistance plays a minimal role over typical throwing distances. This evolutionary background creates systematic biases in evaluating the physics of small, flat objects falling from extreme heights.

### The Scale Misunderstanding

Popular understanding often conflates the danger posed by different categories of falling objects. A penny and a bowling ball dropped from the same height produce vastly different outcomes, but public perception tends to focus on the height variable while ignoring mass, shape, and aerodynamic properties.

This scale confusion is reinforced by dramatic media portrayals of falling objects and insufficient physics education about terminal velocity concepts. The mathematical relationship between mass, drag, and terminal velocity is counterintuitive and requires specific technical knowledge to understand properly.

### The Precautionary Principle Distortion

The myth may persist partly due to well-intentioned safety messaging that emphasizes extreme caution around tall buildings. While it's undeniably dangerous to drop heavy or aerodynamic objects from height, the penny example represents an overcorrection that reduces credibility of legitimate safety warnings.

This overcorrection demonstrates how precautionary messaging can backfire when it makes claims that are easily disproven, potentially undermining public trust in more valid safety warnings about actually dangerous falling objects.

### The Educational Context Problem

The penny myth frequently appears in physics education as an example problem, but often without proper resolution of the terminal velocity calculation. Students may encounter the scenario as a hypothetical without learning that the answer contradicts the dramatic intuition that motivated the question.

This educational gap reflects broader challenges in physics pedagogy, where dramatic examples are used to engage student interest but may inadvertently reinforce misconceptions if not followed through with complete analysis.

## 5. Conclusion Epistemology

### The Experimental Validation

Multiple independent experimental approaches have confirmed that pennies cannot achieve lethal velocity when dropped from building heights. These include direct measurement studies, ballistic testing, and controlled drop experiments with velocity measurement.

The convergence of theoretical calculations and experimental results provides high confidence in the conclusion that pennies pose minimal danger when dropped from any height achievable in normal urban environments.

### The Comparative Analysis

Objects that ARE dangerous when dropped from buildings share specific characteristics: higher mass-to-drag ratios, aerodynamic shapes that minimize air resistance, and sufficient density to maintain kinetic energy through impact. Examples include tools, bottles, construction materials, and other dense, compact objects.

A ballpoint pen, for instance, weighs roughly the same as a penny but has a much more aerodynamic profile, allowing it to achieve higher terminal velocities and potentially cause injury. This comparison illustrates why blanket warnings about "any object" dropped from height are both inaccurate and less useful than specific guidance about object categories that pose real danger.

### The Risk Communication Challenge

The penny myth represents a broader challenge in risk communication: how to convey legitimate dangers (dropping heavy objects from buildings can kill people) without making specific claims that undermine credibility (all objects become deadly when dropped from sufficient height).

Effective safety communication requires precision about actual risk factors rather than dramatic oversimplifications that can be easily disproven, potentially reducing public receptivity to valid safety warnings.

## 6. The Wider Field

### The Physics Education Opportunity

The penny myth provides an excellent case study for teaching terminal velocity, air resistance, and the limitations of intuitive physics reasoning. When properly resolved through calculation and experimentation, it demonstrates how mathematical analysis can reveal counterintuitive physical phenomena.

Physics educators increasingly recognize the value of addressing popular misconceptions directly, using them as entry points for deeper understanding of physical principles. The penny example offers rich opportunities for exploring drag coefficients, kinetic energy calculations, and the relationship between mass, acceleration, and impact force.

### The Urban Safety Reality

While pennies pose minimal danger, objects dropped from tall buildings remain a significant urban safety concern. Construction sites, window cleaning operations, and building maintenance activities regularly involve materials that could cause serious injury or death if dropped.

Real urban safety protocols focus on securing tools, materials, and equipment; establishing safe zones below work areas; and using protective systems to catch or deflect accidentally dropped objects. These practical measures address genuine risks rather than hypothetical penny scenarios.

### The Media Responsibility Question

The persistence of the penny myth reflects broader challenges in science communication through popular media. Dramatic claims generate more attention than nuanced explanations, creating incentives for oversimplification or sensationalization of scientific topics.

Responsible science communication requires balancing engagement with accuracy, providing sufficient detail for proper understanding while maintaining accessibility for general audiences. The penny example illustrates how incomplete explanations can propagate misconceptions even when the underlying science is well-understood.

### The Terminal Velocity Principle

Understanding terminal velocity has applications far beyond penny-dropping scenarios. The principle explains phenomena ranging from skydiving and parachute design to rain drop formation and particle separation in industrial processes.

Educational approaches that use the penny example as a gateway to broader terminal velocity concepts can provide students with analytical tools applicable to numerous real-world situations, from understanding weather phenomena to evaluating safety scenarios involving falling objects.

### The Myth Persistence Pattern

The penny myth demonstrates a common pattern in scientific misconceptions: dramatic claims that align with intuitive but incorrect reasoning tend to persist despite available contradictory evidence. This pattern appears across multiple domains, from health misinformation to environmental claims.

Understanding why certain myths achieve cultural permanence despite scientific debunking provides insights for improving science communication and public scientific literacy. The penny case offers a relatively low-stakes example for studying these dynamics.

### The Experimental Ethics Consideration

While penny-dropping experiments are generally safe, they raise interesting questions about experimental ethics in urban environments. Even knowing that pennies are unlikely to cause injury, deliberately dropping objects from buildings into public spaces presents ethical considerations about consent and public safety.

These considerations highlight the difference between controlled experimental conditions and real-world testing, emphasizing why many physics demonstrations require laboratory or specially controlled environments rather than public spaces.

## 7. Snopes+ Verdict

**ENHANCE**: The claim that dropping a penny from the Empire State Building could kill someone is **FALSE** and represents a fundamental misunderstanding of terminal velocity physics that deserves comprehensive educational treatment.

A penny's terminal velocity of approximately 30-50 mph is insufficient to cause lethal injury. The penny's light weight (2.5 grams), high air resistance, and tumbling flight pattern prevent it from achieving dangerous kinetic energy regardless of drop height.

### Enhancement Value

This myth requires enhancement beyond simple debunking because it provides an excellent opportunity to explain terminal velocity physics, demonstrate the limitations of intuitive reasoning about physical phenomena, and illustrate the importance of experimental verification in science.

The educational value extends to broader principles of risk assessment, physics education, and science communication. Understanding why this myth persists helps identify common patterns in scientific misconceptions and strategies for more effective public science education.

4. Contrasting with objects that DO pose real danger when dropped
5. Connecting to broader principles of terminal velocity and air resistance

### Public Safety Context

While correcting the penny myth, it's crucial to maintain awareness that many objects dropped from buildings CAN cause serious injury or death. The correction should emphasize the specific physics that make pennies safe while reinforcing legitimate cautions about dropping tools, bottles, construction materials, and other dense or aerodynamic objects from height.
