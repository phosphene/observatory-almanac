# "Pepsi Products Contain Dangerous Unregulated Ingredients"


## 1. The Claim

Across social media platforms, wellness blogs, anti-GMO communities, and natural health forums, claims have circulated persistently that Pepsi products—particularly Pepsi-Cola and related soft drink variants—contain dangerous chemicals that remain unregulated by US federal food safety authorities. The framing varies across specific iterations but typically shares several core elements: that a particular ingredient in Pepsi is a known carcinogen, that the FDA has failed to regulate or restrict it, that the company knows about the danger but continues selling the product, and that consumers are therefore being harmed without knowledge or protection.

Some versions of the claim allege that Pepsi "admitted" to containing cancer-causing chemicals, citing legal settlements or company statements out of context. Others point to California's Proposition 65 regulatory framework as evidence that Pepsi products trigger cancer warning requirements in that state, implying a health hazard the rest of the country is being kept ignorant of. Still others invoke broader concerns about phosphoric acid, artificial sweeteners including acesulfame potassium and sucralose, high-fructose corn syrup, and caramel colouring as interconnected markers of a product that is knowingly and systematically harmful.

The most technically specific and evidentially grounded version of the claim targets 4-methylimidazole (4-MEI), a chemical compound formed during the production of certain types of caramel colouring used to give cola drinks their characteristic brown colour. The 4-MEI version of the claim is not pure invention—it connects to a real scientific debate, genuine regulatory actions at the California state level, and documented litigation. This makes it significantly more resistant to simple debunking than claims based entirely on fabricated premises.

The broader question of what is in Pepsi and how adequately those ingredients are regulated is a legitimate public interest inquiry. The challenge is that the social media versions of this concern frequently overstate the evidence, misrepresent the regulatory landscape, and embed the legitimate concern within a conspiracy framing that attributes the situation to deliberate corporate and regulatory concealment rather than to the ordinary complexity of food safety science.


## 2. What's Actually True

Pepsi-Cola and related PepsiCo soft drink products are formulated beverages containing water, high-fructose corn syrup (or sucrose in some markets and reformulations), caramel colouring, phosphoric acid, natural flavours, citric acid, and caffeine. All of these ingredients are subject to US federal food additive regulation under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act (FFDCA). The relevant regulatory framework requires that food additives either receive FDA approval through a petitions process or be classified as Generally Recognised as Safe (GRAS) based on a history of safe use or qualified expert consensus.

None of Pepsi's ingredients are literally unregulated in the sense of having no regulatory status. The claim that they are "unregulated" requires some unpacking.

**4-Methylimidazole (4-MEI)**

The most substantive concern underlying the claim involves 4-methylimidazole, a chemical byproduct generated during the manufacture of Class III and Class IV caramel colouring—the types that produce the brown colour in cola beverages. 4-MEI forms through a Maillard reaction during the heating of sugar with ammonium-containing compounds.

In 2007, the US National Toxicology Program published results of a rodent study finding that 4-MEI caused lung tumours in mice—specifically female mice—at doses of 81 and 312 mg/kg/day. Based partly on this data, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) in 2011 classified 4-MEI as "possibly carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2B). The Group 2B classification is the weakest positive carcinogenicity designation in IARC's system; it indicates that there is limited evidence of carcinogenicity in humans and/or less than sufficient evidence in animals. It places 4-MEI in the same broad category as a very large number of substances including coffee (later reassessed and removed), pickled vegetables, and working as a hairdresser.

In 2011—the same year as the IARC classification—California's Office of Environmental Health Hazard Assessment added 4-MEI to the Proposition 65 list, and set a threshold exposure level of 29 micrograms per day above which a Proposition 65 warning is required on California consumer products.

At the time of the primary controversy (2012–2014), independent testing commissioned by the Center for Science in the Public Interest found that Pepsi products sold outside California contained 4-MEI at levels that could, under high consumption scenarios, approach or exceed the California threshold. This created a situation where Pepsi products sold in California—where the company had reformulated to avoid the Proposition 65 warning label—had lower 4-MEI concentrations than the same products sold in other US states. A 2013 Consumer Reports study reported measuring 4-MEI at 174–178 ppb (parts per billion) in Pepsi-Cola purchased in New York, versus levels of around 36–38 ppb in equivalent products sold in California.

PepsiCo's position throughout this period was that its products were safe and compliant with all applicable federal regulations—which was technically accurate in the sense that the FDA had not set a federal limit on 4-MEI in food products. The FDA was in the process of reviewing 4-MEI data throughout this period and issued a statement in 2012 indicating it had no reason to believe that caramel colouring at levels used in food posed an immediate or short-term health concern, while also noting ongoing data review.

In 2015, following class action litigation and advocacy group pressure, PepsiCo agreed to reduce 4-MEI levels in caramel colouring in all US products—not just those sold in California—to below 100 ppb. This settlement confirmed that a real disparity had existed and that the company was adjusting its manufacturing processes nationally.

**Other Ingredients**

Phosphoric acid is approved as a food additive under 21 CFR 182.1073 as GRAS and is used in cola beverages for tartness. At the concentrations present in soft drinks, phosphoric acid has been associated in some epidemiological studies with reduced bone density when replacing calcium-rich beverages, but is not classified as carcinogenic and is not unregulated.

Caramel colouring (Classes I–IV) is approved under 21 CFR 73.85 as an exempt colour additive. Class IV caramel colouring—the type that generates 4-MEI—is specifically permitted without qualification in the federal additive regulatory framework, which is part of what fuelled the advocacy campaign arguing for specific 4-MEI limits rather than a blanket approval of the colour additive category.

Artificial sweeteners present in Pepsi's diet and zero-sugar product range—including acesulfame potassium (Ace-K) and sucralose—are FDA-approved food additives. Both have been subject to ongoing scientific scrutiny and periodic calls for additional study, but neither is classified as a known or probable human carcinogen by major regulatory bodies.

The "unregulated" framing therefore misrepresents the situation in an important way. These ingredients are regulated—they have regulatory designations, approval statuses, and applicable codes in the federal regulatory system. What was legitimately disputed was whether the regulatory status accurately reflected contemporary scientific knowledge: whether the GRAS classification of caramel colouring should have been revisited to incorporate specific 4-MEI limits once the rodent carcinogenicity data emerged, and whether the FDA's pace of response to the IARC classification was adequate.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The "dangerous unregulated chemicals in Pepsi" claim draws credibility from several sources, some of which reflect genuine and legitimate concerns.

First, 4-MEI is a real compound, and the science around it is genuinely complicated. An IARC Group 2B classification is not nothing—it reflects a panel of international researchers determining that the evidence meets a specific threshold of concern. Advocates for more precautionary food regulation are on defensible ground arguing that the FDA should have established federal 4-MEI limits more quickly in response to the IARC determination rather than relying on California's state-level regulation to drive industry behaviour.

Second, the disparity between 4-MEI levels in California Pepsi and non-California Pepsi was real and documented. When independent testing showed that consumers buying Pepsi in New York were receiving substantially higher 4-MEI exposure than consumers in California—where the same company had reformulated to comply with Proposition 65—there was a reasonable inference that the national formulation was being maintained at higher exposure levels than the company considered wise when legally compelled to reduce them.

Third, the general concern about regulatory capture in food safety—the risk that the FDA's approval processes are more responsive to industry lobbying than to independent science—is a legitimate institutional criticism. The GRAS designation system in particular has been criticised by public health researchers for allowing food companies to self-affirm the safety of new food ingredients without independent FDA review. Published analyses have found that a significant proportion of GRAS determinations involve industry-funded studies and lack independent expert verification. This systemic critique applies to the food additive regulatory framework generally, not specifically to 4-MEI or Pepsi, but it provides context in which scepticism of regulatory assurances makes sense.

Fourth, the claim circulates in communities that have grown accustomed to industry assurances being later revised—asbestos, leaded petrol, trans fats, and cigarettes all generated decades of industry-backed scientific claims that were eventually overturned. In this historical context, scepticism of corporate or regulatory assurances about novel or under-studied chemicals in consumer products is not irrational, even when specific claims overstate the evidence.

The distortion happens when a legitimate scepticism becomes a specific and overstated claim: that Pepsi's ingredients are "dangerous" in a confirmed, established sense, and that they are "unregulated" in the sense of having no regulatory status. Both characterisations misrepresent the actual situation. The concern about 4-MEI was grounded, the regulatory response was arguably too slow, and the California-versus-national-formulation disparity was a genuine problem—but none of this supported the claim that federal regulators had ignored a known carcinogen in a popular consumer product with deliberate disregard for public health.


## 4. Verdict

**MIXED / DISTORTED-BUT-GROUNDED**: The claim that Pepsi products contain "dangerous unregulated chemicals" is an overstatement that distorts a legitimate food safety dispute.

What is accurate: 4-MEI, a byproduct of caramel colouring manufacturing, was classified by IARC as a Group 2B possible carcinogen in 2011. California required Proposition 65 warnings for products with 4-MEI exposure above 29 micrograms daily. Independent testing found higher 4-MEI levels in Pepsi products sold outside California than in those formulated for the California market. PepsiCo agreed in 2015 litigation to reduce 4-MEI levels nationally.

What is inaccurate: describing these ingredients as "unregulated" when they carry federal approval status, characterising the IARC risk classification as equivalent to a confirmed danger finding, and framing the situation as the company "admitting" to knowingly poisoning consumers rather than responding—belatedly and under advocacy and legal pressure—to evolving scientific understanding and regulatory pressure.

The legitimate core of the concern—that a state-level disparity in formulation existed, that the FDA's pace of response to the IARC 4-MEI classification was slow, and that the GRAS self-certification system creates structural vulnerabilities in food safety oversight—deserves engagement on its merits rather than being either amplified into conspiracy claims or dismissed entirely as misinformation.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Pepsi 4-MEI controversy is a window into a set of systemic questions about how food safety regulation functions in the United States, how risk communication reaches consumers, and what role advocacy organisations, litigation, and state-level regulation play when federal frameworks move slowly.

**The Regulatory Gap Between California and the Rest**

California has historically served as a de facto national food safety regulator in some domains, because the size of its market means companies often find it economically rational to implement California compliance standards nationwide rather than maintain separate formulations. The Proposition 65 mechanism—which sets exposure thresholds that trigger warning label requirements and enables private enforcement litigation—has repeatedly driven companies to reduce exposure to listed chemicals in advance of federal action.

This has generated a dual dynamic: California's precautionary approach produces faster responses to emerging evidence than the federal system, but also produces warning labels (based on the Proposition 65 list and exposure thresholds) that sometimes do not map straightforwardly onto mainstream scientific risk assessment. The proliferation of Proposition 65 warnings on everyday products—parking garages, restaurants, hardware stores—has been documented to produce warning fatigue in which many consumers either ignore the labels or assume that everything is known to cause cancer in California.

For 4-MEI specifically, the situation was that California's threshold of 29 micrograms per day was derived from a calculation based on the rodent carcinogenicity data using California's standard risk assessment method, but the FDA's own assessment concluded that the 4-MEI levels in beverages represented a negligible human cancer risk given the difference between rodent dose levels and plausible human exposure. This is a genuine scientific disagreement about appropriate risk calculation methodology—not a straightforward situation where one body said "safe" and another said "dangerous."

**The Advocacy-Litigation-Reform Pipeline**

The Pepsi 4-MEI story illustrates a pattern that recurs in US food and consumer product safety: advocates identify a concern, conduct or commission independent testing, seek regulatory action, face agency inaction or delay, and ultimately resort to litigation or market pressure to force industry change. The Center for Science in the Public Interest, the Center for Environmental Health, and Consumer Reports all played roles in this pipeline for 4-MEI.

This pipeline has achieved real outcomes—both Coca-Cola and PepsiCo reformulated their caramel colouring processes to reduce 4-MEI, and the national formulation change ultimately occurred earlier than FDA action would have compelled. But it also has significant limitations: the pipeline is resource-intensive, it depends on organisations with the technical capacity to commission credible laboratory testing, and its outputs (class action lawsuits, public advocacy campaigns, state-level regulatory petitions) are not always well-calibrated to communicate risk magnitude accurately to general consumers.

**The Broader Soft Drink Regulatory Picture**

The concern about Pepsi ingredients does not occur in isolation. Soft drink consumption has been associated with a range of adverse health outcomes in epidemiological research, including increased risk of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, dental erosion, and—in diet soda variants—ongoing debate about associations with metabolic syndrome and gut microbiome effects. The mechanisms of these associations are incompletely understood, and the causality debates are genuine and unresolved.

In this context, the specific claim about "unregulated dangerous chemicals" may function as a proxy for a broader and not unreasonable concern: that regular consumption of highly processed commercial beverages carries health costs that are inadequately communicated either through product labelling or public health messaging. The 4-MEI story is one part of a larger picture about soft drink health impacts that remains genuinely contested in the nutritional epidemiology literature.

PepsiCo's own communications have emphasised its commitment to safety, quality systems, and regulatory compliance, while also noting ongoing portfolio adjustments to reduce sugar content and respond to consumer preferences. The company has invested in expanding its water and non-carbonated beverage offerings and has made public statements about its product safety commitments. These institutional responses reflect the genuine market and reputational pressure that has accompanied health concerns about cola beverages, without constituting a formal admission of the specific claims made about dangerous unregulated chemicals.

**Consumer Literacy and Risk Communication**

The persistent circulation of "dangerous unregulated chemicals in Pepsi" claims reflects a broader challenge in health and food safety communication. Risk assessment is inherently probabilistic, comparative, and context-dependent. An IARC Group 2B classification means something different from a Group 1 established carcinogen classification. "Associated with cancer in rodent studies at doses far exceeding human exposure" communicates different risk information than "known carcinogen." These distinctions matter enormously but require a level of scientific literacy to parse that most consumers—reasonably—do not have.

The social media amplification of health risk claims typically strips out the nuance that makes risk comparisons meaningful, producing a binary "safe" or "dangerous" framing that cannot accommodate the genuine complexity of food safety science. Addressing this requires not just fact-checking individual claims but investing in the kind of science communication infrastructure that helps consumers navigate probabilistic risk in everyday contexts.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not published a formal dedicated fact-check on the "Pepsi contains dangerous unregulated chemicals" claim as a standalone entry, though the specific Snopes URL associated with the topic (snopes.com/fact-check/pepsi-ingredients-regulation/) does appear in search index data as a relevant reference point. The gap in coverage is notable given the claim's widespread circulation across health-focused social media communities and its persistence across multiple years.

The absence of a dedicated Snopes assessment means consumers searching for verification of claims about Pepsi ingredients may encounter partial coverage—news reports about the 4-MEI litigation, advocacy group communications, industry rebuttals—without finding a single integrated assessment that contextualises all of these elements against each other.

Other fact-checkers and media organisations have addressed related aspects of the story. Consumer Reports' independent testing of 4-MEI levels in cola beverages provided important evidential grounding for the controversy. The Center for Science in the Public Interest's sustained advocacy and litigation work on 4-MEI generated substantial documentation of both the concern and the industry response. Science journalism outlets including Popular Science and Scientific American covered the caramel colouring controversy with varying degrees of contextual depth.

The challenge for fact-checkers engaging with this claim is that it is neither straightforwardly true nor straightforwardly false. A rating of "False" would misrepresent a real and documented food safety concern that resulted in genuine industry reformulation. A rating of "True" would overstate the danger and misrepresent the regulatory landscape. The most accurate characterisation—that this involves a legitimate public health concern that predatory social media framings have distorted into a conspiracy claim—requires the kind of contextual, mixed-verdict analysis that platforms built around binary ratings struggle to communicate effectively.

Effective fact-checking of this claim would ideally separate several sub-questions: whether 4-MEI is a carcinogen (contested, Group 2B designation); whether Pepsi products contained 4-MEI (yes); whether the levels posed a significant human health risk (genuinely disputed between regulatory bodies with different risk calculation methodologies); whether the ingredients were "unregulated" (no—they have regulatory status, though the appropriateness of that status was legitimately debated); and whether PepsiCo acted responsibly (under advocacy and litigation pressure it reformulated, which suggests genuine room for criticism of its initial approach). This granular decomposition is more useful to consumers than any single-verdict rating, but requires editorial infrastructure that most fact-checking platforms and certainly most social media sharers do not systematically provide.
