## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

In early October 2021, a copypasta meme began circulating across social media platforms — principally Twitter, Facebook, and Instagram — asserting an elaborately fictional origin story for the PepsiCo soft drink brand. The text claimed that Pepsi originated as a product of the "Indiana Poison Works," a manufacturer of low-quality rat poison. According to the meme, when an unusually cold February in 1896 killed off the local rat population and poison sales collapsed, the company's owner — one "Gustav Pepsi" — disposed of excess stock by bottling and selling it to human consumers, guided by the corporate philosophy that "all our customers deserve to die" and "laws aren't real."

Snopes investigator Bethania Palma (2021) assigned this claim the rating **False**. The meme is a deliberately absurdist work of fiction — a copypasta designed for humor — and bears no resemblance to the documented corporate history of PepsiCo. The real Pepsi-Cola was invented by pharmacist Caleb Bradham in New Bern, North Carolina, in the 1890s. There was no "Gustav Pepsi," no Indiana Poison Works, and no rat poison involved in any stage of the company's history.

This entry examines the meme's specific fictional mechanics, the actual founding and early history of Pepsi-Cola, the epistemics of copypasta as a misinformation genre, and the broader phenomenon of absurdist brand mythology in digital culture.

## §2 The Actual History of Pepsi-Cola and Caleb Bradham

The origins of Pepsi-Cola are substantially documented through corporate history records, local journalism from New Bern, North Carolina, and trademark filings. Pharmacist Caleb Davis Bradham (1867–1934) developed the first version of the drink in his drugstore soda fountain around 1893–1894, initially calling it "Brad's Drink." The formulation combined sugar, water, caramel, lemon oil, kola nuts, nutmeg, and other undisclosed natural additives (PepsiCo, *History of Pepsi-Cola*, 2021, company archive at pepsicares.com). The drink was originally marketed as a digestive aid as well as an energy boost — both popular functional claims for soda fountain drinks of the era, as commercial drugstore sodas were commonly sold with implicit or explicit health framing.

Bradham formally renamed the drink "Pepsi-Cola" in 1898, a name that combined "pepsin" (the digestive enzyme) and "cola" (referencing the kola nut ingredient and the broader category established by Coca-Cola's 1886 launch). The trademark for Pepsi-Cola was filed in 1903. By 1905, Bradham had moved the business out of the drugstore context and established formal concentrate production, franchising the syrup to bottling operations across the eastern United States. In 1908, he established a purpose-built office building in New Bern — the first dedicated headquarters for the brand.

The claim that any part of this history involved rat poison is false on every factual dimension. The formulation was developed in a drugstore context oriented toward human consumption and digestive health. It was never produced in Indiana (the meme's fictional setting for the "Indiana Poison Works"). The founder's name was Caleb Bradham, not "Gustav Pepsi." There is no historical record of any rat poison enterprise bearing any relationship to the Pepsi-Cola brand or its predecessor businesses.

Two subsequent bankruptcies (1923 and 1931) during Bradham's tenure are not connected to any poisoning allegations; they were due to market conditions — particularly catastrophic sugar price volatility following World War I that destroyed Bradham's stockpiling strategy. Loft, Inc., a candy and soda company, acquired the rights to Pepsi-Cola in 1931 and under Charles Guth developed the 12-ounce bottle at the 5-cent price point that gave Pepsi its first national competitive foothold against Coca-Cola (Stoddard, 1997, *The Pepsi-Cola Story*, Pepsi-Cola Company archives).

The eventual merger and incorporation that formed PepsiCo, Inc. in 1965 — combining Pepsi-Cola and Frito-Lay — represents the corporate ancestor of the current business, which is a global food-and-beverage conglomerate entirely distinct from any historical rat control enterprise.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Copypasta as a Distinct Misinformation Genre

The "Gustav Pepsi" meme belongs to a specific and analytically underappreciated genre of internet misinformation: the absurdist copypasta. Unlike conventional misinformation, which typically leverages superficially plausible claims and seeks to be believed, absurdist copypasta defines itself partly *by* its implausibility. The humor mechanism depends on the reader recognizing that the content is false — the joke is not a deception but a shared recognition of the fictional absurdity.

This creates a distinctive epistemic challenge that Palma (2021) acknowledged when noting that the meme's "intent appeared to be humor." The challenge is not that absurdist copypasta is inherently harmful; most readers encountering the "Gustav Pepsi" meme immediately recognize it as fiction. The challenge arises at the margins: a small subset of readers who encounter decontextualized fragments of the copypasta — particularly the core claim that Pepsi originated as a poison enterprise — may not have access to the original framing, the ironic context, or sufficient prior knowledge of PepsiCo's history to calibrate their belief accordingly.

The theoretical framework for understanding this marginal-credibility problem is provided by Pennycook et al. (2019), writing in *Psychological Science* (Association for Psychological Science), who documented what they termed the "illusory truth effect" in digital information environments: repeated exposure to a claim increases its rated truthfulness among participants, even when participants initially recognized the claim as false. Copypasta, by design, facilitates high-repetition sharing — the name itself references the "copy and paste" mechanism of exact text duplication. This means that even inherently implausible claims can accumulate perceived credibility through raw exposure frequency.

A further epistemic subtlety: absurdist copypasta often contains embedded factual hooks — plausible-sounding dates, geographic details, corporate names — alongside the absurdist elements. The "Gustav Pepsi" text mentions a specific year (1896) and a specific regional institutional framing ("Indiana Poison Works") that are meaningless as historical claims but provide anchoring detail that partially activates the cognitive processing associated with factual testimony, as documented in Lewandowsky et al. (2012) in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* (SAGE Publications). This phenomenon — call it *anchor contamination* — means that even readers who correctly disbelieve the core absurdist claim may retain fragments of the surrounding detail as having some evidential character.

The "copypasta fact-check problem" is therefore an instance of a more general problem in platform-based information environments: the detection and correction infrastructure built to address sincere (non-ironic) misinformation is structurally limited in its ability to assess or intervene in claims that combine ironic intent with low but non-zero marginal belief effects. Snopes's decision to address the claim reflects a pragmatic resolution: even if most sharers of the copypasta understand it as fiction, the volume of circulation and the small marginal population of literal readers justifies a formal false verdict with documentary basis.

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Palma's (2021) investigation correctly identified the claim as fiction, provided the documented founding history of Pepsi-Cola, and noted the meme's humorous intent. Several contextual threads remained undeveloped:

**The copypasta's origin and authorship**: The Snopes article linked to a 2017 tweet by the account @pixelatedboat as an example of the meme's ironic circulation, but did not trace the earliest known origin of the specific "Gustav Pepsi" text. Copypasta provenance is characteristically difficult to establish because the format is designed for exact replication without attribution. Whether the "Indiana Poison Works" framing originated on Twitter, a forum such as Reddit or 4chan, or elsewhere remains undocumented in the fact-check.

**The secondary claims embedded in the fabrication**: The meme's description of rat poison that "would either kill the rats or make them incredibly strong and angry" contains implicit commentary on Pepsi's reputation for producing an energetically stimulating beverage (through its caffeine and sugar content) and may be a specific riff on the kola nut ingredient and its associated stimulant properties. This satirical layering was not analyzed.

**PepsiCo's actual chemical and regulatory history**: While Pepsi has no rat poison history, both Pepsi and Coca-Cola have genuine regulatory histories that are more complex than the copypasta's fictional version. Early Pepsi-Cola products were marketed with health claims ("Pepsi-Cola invigorates tired nerves and aids digestion") that would not pass modern regulatory scrutiny. The early use of kola nuts as a caffeine source, and the historical presence of trace amounts of cocaine in Coca-Cola before 1903, are documented aspects of early soda fountain history that the public partially conflates, creating a reservoir of "corporate product origins were sketchy" prior belief that makes satirical claims about toxic origins more culturally legible than they might otherwise be.

**Bradham's bankruptcy and its legacy**: The fact that Caleb Bradham lost the rights to his own brand in 1923 bankruptcy proceedings — ultimately dying in relative obscurity in 1934 while the Pepsi brand became a global enterprise — is a genuine human interest dimension of the brand's history entirely absent from both the meme and its fact-check.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Brand Mythology, Satirical Misinformation, and the Copypasta Ecology

The "Gustav Pepsi" copypasta exists within a broader ecology of satirical brand origin stories that circulate on the internet. This genre includes similarly absurdist accounts of the founding of Starbucks, the naming of Google, the formulation of KFC's secret recipe, and other corporate mythology. What distinguishes the Pepsi copypasta from merely funny jokes is its particular engagement with the format conventions of legitimate corporate history writing — specifically, the use of a proper name, a founding date, a geographical detail, and a summary philosophical statement attributed to the founder that parodies the language of mission statements.

The humor mechanism, as documented in the academic literature on satirical misinformation, exploits the gap between form and content: the *form* signals "this is journalism/history," while the *content* signals "this is absurd." Kuipers (2006), writing in the *European Journal of Cultural Studies* (SAGE Publications), analyzed this gap as a feature of what she called "news parody," a genre that achieves its effects by adopting journalistic conventions for comic purposes. The "Gustav Pepsi" meme extends this mechanism to corporate history rather than news.

The practical significance of such memes for brand reputation management is non-trivial. Research in crisis communication by Coombs (2007), published in *Corporate Reputation Review* (Palgrave Macmillan), demonstrated that even demonstrably false negative associations with a brand can produce measurable consumer attitude effects when the false associations are repeatedly encountered — particularly when the false claim aligns with pre-existing "suspicion schemas" consumers hold about corporate behavior. The "all our customers deserve to die" philosophy attributed to "Gustav Pepsi" is an extreme satirical version of broader consumer skepticism about large beverage corporations' prioritization of profit over health.

The relationship between satirical brand misinformation and sincere brand misinformation is further complicated by the phenomenon of "poe's law" — the principle, first stated in the context of internet discussions about creationism by Nathan Poe in 2005 and subsequently applied broadly to extremist and satirical online content, that without explicit markers of intent, it is impossible to distinguish extremely absurd sincere claims from satirical parodies of such claims (Aikin, 2009, *Skeptical Inquirer*, Committee for Skeptical Inquiry). The "Gustav Pepsi" text is almost parodically extreme in its claims ("laws aren't real") — but no less extreme than some sincere viral claims about corporate malfeasance that have gained traction in similar information environments.

For fact-checkers, this context suggests that the appropriate response to copypasta claims is a two-component correction: first, the direct denial of the specific false claim (which Snopes provides); second, an explanation of the genre and the honest kernel (if any) of cultural commentary embedded in the satire, which Snopes does not fully provide and which this entry has attempted to supply.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: FALSE**

The Pepsi-Cola brand was not founded by a person named "Gustav Pepsi" and did not originate as a rat poison company. The claim is a deliberately absurdist copypasta meme designed for humor rather than sincere belief. Pepsi-Cola was invented by pharmacist Caleb Davis Bradham in New Bern, North Carolina in the mid-1890s, trademarked in 1903, and developed into a national brand through early twentieth-century franchise bottling before passing through bankruptcy proceedings and acquisition to become PepsiCo, Inc. in 1965. No part of this history involves pest control, poison manufacture, or any individual named Gustav Pepsi.

**EARC Classification: A (Attribution — fictional copypasta origin)**

The analytical gap here is attributional: the claim requires identifying not only that the core assertion is factually false but that it belongs to a specific genre (absurdist copypasta) with known epistemic properties, distinguishing it from sincere misinformation while accounting for its marginal real-world credibility effects. The appropriate correction addresses both the factual falsity and the genre context.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Earliest confirmed origin and authorship of the "Gustav Pepsi" / "Indiana Poison Works" copypasta text
- Quantitative estimates of the proportion of readers who encounter copypasta claims and rate them as literally true versus satirical
- Complete documentation of Bradham's life and business activities following the 1923 bankruptcy
- Extent to which early Pepsi-Cola health marketing claims were formally scrutinized by pre-FDA regulatory bodies
