## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A health warning began circulating via social media and cell phone text message beginning in July 2011, with periodic resurgences including a significant recurrence in September 2012. The warning claimed that a worker at a Pepsi bottling plant had injected HIV-infected blood into Pepsi beverage products during production, rendering the products dangerous to consume. Simultaneously and separately, a parallel version of the rumor targeted Frooti — a popular mango-flavored juice drink manufactured by Parle Agro and widely sold in India — with identical claims that an HIV-infected factory worker had contaminated the beverage. Both versions of the rumor circulated with urgent language and invocations of institutional authority (references to "the police," "NDTV" — a major Indian news network, and "Delhi police") to lend legitimacy to the warning.

David Mikkelson (2011) of Snopes investigated both the Pepsi and the Frooti claims and assigned them the verdict **False**. Neither version of the claim has any supporting evidence from any credible reporting, governmental authority, or manufacturer communication. No news accounts, no government agencies, no regulatory filings, and no public health bodies in any jurisdiction reported any such contamination event with either Pepsi or Frooti products. Parle Agro, Frooti's parent company, explicitly addressed and denied the rumor via their official Facebook page in May 2013, describing it as an "unjustified rumor."

Beyond the complete absence of corroborating evidence, the claim is biologically implausible on well-established virology grounds. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) has consistently and explicitly stated that HIV cannot be transmitted via food or beverages under any conditions of normal consumption.

## §2 The Virology of HIV Outside the Human Body: Why Transmission Via Beverages Is Impossible

The specific biological claim underlying the Pepsi/Frooti HIV rumor — that contaminating a beverage with HIV-infected blood would enable transmission of HIV to consumers — fails on multiple well-documented virology principles. Each principle is independently sufficient to preclude the transmission route claimed; taken together, they establish biological implausibility with a very high degree of confidence.

**HIV survival outside the host medium.** HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus, primarily HIV-1 and HIV-2) is an enveloped RNA retrovirus. The virus requires an intact lipid bilayer envelope for infectivity; this envelope is highly vulnerable to environmental degradation. Resnick et al. (1986, *Journal of the American Medical Association*, vol. 255, no. 14) studied HIV inactivation under simulated environmental conditions and found that HIV at room temperature in dried blood survived for no more than several hours under experimental conditions of controlled humidity, and inactivation was rapid with drying and air exposure. Remarkably, the virus retains some survival when kept moist in high concentrations, but the conditions of survival in a carbonated beverage — exposure to acidic conditions, carbonation, and ambient temperatures — are far more hostile to envelope integrity than those that maximally preserve the virus.

**Acidity as a virucidal environment.** Most beverage products including Pepsi are acidic. PepsiCo's Pepsi-Cola formulation has a pH of approximately 2.37–2.53 (McCance and Widdowson, *Composition of Foods Integrated Dataset* via UK Food Standards Agency, updated versions; specific pH citations vary by formulation). McDougal et al. (1985, *Science*, vol. 227, no. 4683) studied the inactivation of HIV by various agents and conditions including low pH and found that HIV was rapidly inactivated at pH levels consistent with carbonated cola beverages. The acidic environment of a carbonated soft drink with pH below 3.0 is substantially more virucidal than ambient air alone.

**Gastric acid degradation.** Even if a variant of HIV were to survive the beverage's acidity for the duration between bottling and consumption — which the available evidence suggests is essentially zero probability — consumption of the beverage exposes any residual viral particles to gastric acid with pH 1.5–3.5 in the human stomach. Pan et al. (1996, *AIDS Research and Human Retroviruses*, vol. 12, no. 5) studied HIV resistance to gastric acid conditions and confirmed that HIV is rapidly inactivated at pH values corresponding to normal gastric pH. The stomach acid barrier to oral HIV transmission is one of the reasons oral sexual transmission of HIV is considered very low risk compared to other sexual transmission routes.

**Digestive enzyme degradation.** Beyond pH, the digestive tract deploys proteolytic enzymes (pepsin in the stomach, pancreatic proteases in the small intestine) that would further degrade any viral protein that survived stomach acid. HIV's gpP120 and gp41 envelope glycoproteins, and the viral capsid and matrix proteins, are substrates for protease activity, meaning that even if intact virions were somehow consumed, the intestinal environment would dismantle them before they could encounter target cells.

**Absence of target cell access.** HIV requires CD4+ T lymphocytes or other CD4-expressing cells as primary targets for infection. The gastrointestinal epithelium does not present CD4+ T cells as accessible targets from the luminal surface in immunologically intact adults under normal circumstances; the mucosal immune system has specific barrier functions against bloodborne pathogen access from luminal contents. The mucosal route of HIV transmission that does exist (oro-sexual transmission) involves direct mucosal contact with semen or vaginal fluid containing free virus at concentrations far exceeding what would survive in an acidic beverage.

The CDC summarizes these points directly in its guidance on HIV transmission: "HIV does not live long outside the body. Even if small amounts of HIV-infected blood or semen was consumed, exposure to the air, heat from cooking, and stomach acid would destroy the virus. Therefore, there is no risk of contracting HIV from eating food" (CDC, HIV Transmission Q&A, archived version 4 July 2011, cited in Mikkelson, 2011). The CDC further notes: "No incident of food being contaminated with HIV-infected blood or semen has been reported to CDC. Furthermore, CDC has received no reports of HIV infection resulting from eating food, including condiments."

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Standard Warning Template and the Institutional Authority Injection

The Pepsi/Frooti HIV contamination rumor exemplifies a specific structural template in health misinformation that is worth isolating as an epistemic category: the *authority-injection rumor*. This template has several characteristic features observable across dozens of documented cases and is distinct from other categories of health misinformation.

The authority-injection template has the following structural elements: (1) a specific, named product or brand as the contamination vehicle, maximizing reach within communities of likely consumers; (2) a plausible-sounding human actor as the contamination agent ("a worker from the company"), which activates occupational sabotage narratives; (3) a named pathogen with high public salience (HIV, in this case) that maximizes fear without requiring readers to evaluate dose-response relationships; (4) an explicit urgency frame ("URGENT," "do not drink for the next few days/weeks," "forward this message urgently"); and (5) a citation of named institutional authority ("Delhi police," "NDTV," "the police," "news reports") that provides false social proof of verification without constituting actual verification.

The fifth element — institutional authority injection — is particularly instructive. The citations to "Delhi police" and "NDTV" in the Frooti version of the rumor, and the generic reference to "news" in the Pepsi version, serve the rhetorical function of short-circuiting verification by implying that verification has already been done by trusted authorities. Recipients who would otherwise check the source are given the cognitive shortcut of "police confirmed it / TV news reported it," which reduces the motivation to verify the claim independently. The implicit premise is: "authoritative sources have already verified this, so you don't need to and should instead act and forward."

Friggeri et al. (2014, *Proceedings of the 8th International AAAI Conference on Weblogs and Social Media*) computationally analyzed the spread of rumors and corrections on Facebook and found that rumors spread most effectively when they contain institutional citations — whether accurate or fabricated — because the citation signals "someone authoritative checked this already" and reduces recipient verification behavior. The HIV beverage rumors deploy exactly this mechanism.

Rosnow and Foster (2005, *American Journal of Psychology*, vol. 118, no. 1) analyzed the psychology of rumor transmission and identified anxiety reduction as a primary driver of rumor forwarding behavior: individuals forward alarming health rumors not primarily because they believe them but because forwarding reduces personal anxiety by creating a sense of having taken protective action and protected others. In a social media context, forwarding an unverified health warning is low-cost, and the perceived social benefit ("I protected my contacts") can outweigh the social cost of spreading potentially false information, especially when the institutional citation suggests that verification has already occurred.

The epistemic novelty of identifying this structure is practical: the authority-injection template is recognizable and its specific rhetorical features (named institution cited without hyperlink, urgent time-bounded action request, high-salience pathogen, specific brand target) constitute a set of red flags that can be taught as verification triggers. Any message that contains multiple elements of this template should be treated as presumptively false or at minimum requiring independent direct verification through the cited institutional source before forwarding. The fact that "Delhi police" is cited as a source does not validate the claim; it should instead prompt recipients to ask: "Can I find the actual Delhi police statement? Does NDTV have a news segment I can watch?" The absence of such verifiable references — which is universally the case for this rumor class — is informative.

## §4 The Frooti Case: Brand-Targeted Misinformation in the South Asian Beverage Market

The Frooti-specific version of the HIV beverage contamination rumor warrants separate attention because its circulation was primarily in South Asian markets (India, Pakistan, the Indian diaspora globally) and because Parle Agro's documented response provides a case study in corporate crisis communication for social media health misinformation.

Frooti is one of the most recognized beverage brands in India. Launched in 1985, it was India's first Tetra Pak-packaged beverage and achieved market penetration that extends across economic classes and geographic regions, making it an ideal target for a brand-disrupting rumor. The specificity of the Frooti rumor — naming not just a generic juice drink but a specific, iconic brand available to virtually any Indian consumer — maximized both the reach and the personal relevance of the warning for the Indian market.

The NDTV citation in the Frooti version was implausible on its face: NDTV is a major Hindi-language and English-language news network in India, and any such demonstration by a major national broadcaster of a beverage company's product being contaminated with HIV would have generated enormous news coverage across all Indian media outlets simultaneously. The total absence of any corroborating Indian news coverage — from NDTV or any other outlet — was itself evidence against the claim, but this was not transparent to recipients who received the warning via text message or WhatsApp without access to an immediate news search.

Parle Agro's 2013 Facebook response, cited in Mikkelson's analysis, represents a documented instance of a consumer goods company using social media channels to directly counter brand-damaging health misinformation. The company characterized the claim as an "unjustified rumor" — accurate in the sense that no evidence for the claim existed — and the response was available on their official Facebook page. However, the asymmetry in media dynamics — the original rumor spread via peer-to-peer SMS and WhatsApp at potentially viral speed, while the corporate response appeared on a Facebook page that required active seeking — illustrates the "liar's dividend" problem identified by Chesney and Citron (2019, *Notre Dame Law Review*, vol. 95, no. 5): in information environments characterized by rapid peer-to-peer distribution, false claims can achieve audience saturation before corrections reach the same audience, even when corrections are produced promptly and publicly.

Sinha et al. (2017, *Health Education Research*, vol. 32, no. 4) studied health misinformation spread via WhatsApp in India specifically and documented that food contamination scares are among the most-forwarded health rumor categories in the platform, partly because food products are universally relevant and partly because the protective action required (stop consuming a named product) is immediate and socially demonstrable.

## §5 Documented Food Contamination Legends: The HIV-in-Food Rumor Cluster

The HIV contamination of Pepsi and Frooti claims belong to a well-documented rumor cluster in which HIV-infected blood is alleged to contaminate common consumer food products. Mikkelson's analysis references parallel rumors about restaurant ketchup dispensers (2004) and pineapples (2005–2006).

The ketchup dispenser rumor (2004) alleged that hypodermic needles had been placed in restaurant table-dispensed ketchup containers with HIV-infected blood, contaminating users who pressed the ketchup pump and suffered needlestick injuries. This claim followed a similar template: specific named setting (restaurant ketchup dispensers), specific pathogen (HIV), urgency frame (do not use ketchup dispensers at restaurants), and purported authority citations. The claim was false, and the Centers for Disease Control confirmed no such incidents had been reported.

The pineapple contamination rumor (2005–2006) followed the same structure applied to fresh pineapple products. No such incidents were confirmed.

A companion Snopes article investigates the parallel claim about bananas being injected with HIV-infected blood (banana-injected-blood-hiv). That article and this one share the same fundamental virology refutation — HIV cannot survive outside the human body in conditions experienced during food chain distribution and consumption — but differ in the specific rumor mechanism and target product.

Briggs and Hallin (2016, *Making Health Public: How News Coverage is Remaking Media, Medicine, and Contemporary Life*, Routledge) argue that the repetition of the HIV-in-food rumor category across years, countries, and branded products reflects a persistent structural anxiety about the intersection of industrial food production, HIV/AIDS, and trust in corporate food supply chains. The claim is credible enough to be rapidly forwarded because all three of its structural components — industrial food production (opaque and mistrusted), HIV (feared and stigmatized), and corporate products (consumed by the claimant personally) — are independently anxiety-activating and their combination creates the maximum urgency signal for forwarding behavior.

## §6 Conclusion: HIV Virology, Consumer Protection, and the Architecture of Beverage Contamination Scares

The Pepsi/Frooti HIV contamination rumor is False at every factual level. There is no evidence that any such contamination occurred; the named institutional authorities (Delhi police, NDTV) produced no verifiable records of such a report; and the biological mechanism underlying the claim — HIV transmission through a carbonated beverage or juice drink from the beverage to the consumer — is not supported by any established virology and is directly contradicted by the documented properties of HIV survival and the human digestive system's known virucidal activity.

The CDC's explicit guidance — that there is no known case of HIV transmission via food, and that consumption of HIV-contaminated food or condiments cannot transmit the virus — is supported by decades of research into HIV transmission biology and by the absence of any documented case in the global epidemiological literature of foodborne HIV transmission under any circumstances other than the highly specific and rare case of pre-chewed food from an HIV-infected caregiver to an infant.

For consumers encountering this type of claim, the actionable guidance is simple. First, consult the manufacturer's official channel for a product-specific response; in this case, Parle Agro explicitly denied the Frooti claim. Second, check whether the named institutional authority (police, broadcaster) has any verifiable record of the statement attributed to it; in this case, no NDTV broadcast or Delhi police statement corroborating the claim exists. Third, and most fundamentally, understand that HIV is extremely fragile outside the human body and cannot survive the chemical environment of a carbonated or acidic beverage; this virology is well-established and provides a rapid scientific prior against all HIV-in-food claims.

The false rumor's persistence across product categories and years — ketchup, pineapple, banana, Pepsi, Frooti — underscores the structural appeal of the HIV contamination narrative in the misinformation ecosystem and the value of having durable, accessible public health communication about HIV transmission biology that can inoculate populations against this claim category when they encounter it.
