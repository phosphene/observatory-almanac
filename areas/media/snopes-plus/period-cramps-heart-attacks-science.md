# Period Cramps Are as Painful as Heart Attacks


## 1. The Claim

The claim circulates in several registers, all converging on the same assertion: "doctors confirm" or "science shows" that period cramps can be as painful as a heart attack. The most widely shared version states it as established medical fact, typically accompanied by a screenshot from a University College London webpage or attributed to Professor John Guillebaud, a reproductive health specialist at UCL.

The claim went massively viral in 2024 through a video by Dr. Brian Boxer Wachler posted on both Instagram and TikTok. Responding to a comment reading "Apparently period cramps hurt just as much as heart attacks," Wachler replied "That is not cap" — Gen Z slang for "that is not a lie" — while displaying a screenshot of a UCL article titled "Doctors finally confirm period pain can be as painful as a heart attack." The Instagram post accumulated over 1.2 million likes. Comedian Sasheer Zamata amplified the claim further: "Doctors are now saying that the pain we feel when we experience period cramps is equivalent to the pain we feel when we have a heart attack."

The claim operates on multiple levels simultaneously. On the surface, it presents a scientific comparison between two types of pain. At a deeper level, it functions as a validation claim — an assertion that menstrual pain is "real" and "serious" — using heart attacks as a familiar benchmark of severe suffering. At its deepest level, it is an advocacy claim, arguing implicitly that if period pain is comparable to heart attack pain, then the historical medical under-treatment of dysmenorrhea represents a gendered injustice.

Each of these levels contains something worth taking seriously. But the foundational claim — the scientific comparison itself — does not hold up.


## 2. What's Actually True

**The Origin: One Quote, No Study**

The entire edifice of this claim rests on a single anecdotal statement made by Professor John Guillebaud to the business and technology news outlet Quartz in February 2016. In an article titled "Period pain can be 'almost as bad as a heart attack.' Why aren't we researching how to treat it?" Guillebaud stated that patients had described menstrual pain as "almost as bad as having a heart attack."

Note the critical word: "almost." And note that this was a characterization of what patients had told him, not a finding from any research study. Quartz itself issued a correction at the bottom of the article, noting that "an earlier version of this article quoted John Guillebaud as saying period pain can be 'as bad as a heart attack,' when in fact he said it can be 'almost as bad.'"

This is the entire evidentiary foundation. One professor relaying what some patients told him, with a qualifier ("almost") that was subsequently dropped by virtually every source that cited the claim.

What happened next is a textbook case of citational laundering. The Quartz article was picked up by the UK tabloid The Mirror in 2018, which ran a story headlined "Doctors finally confirm your period cramps might be as painful as a heart attack." UCL then published a brief web article linking to The Mirror piece, stating that "Professor John Guillebaud (UCL Institute for Women's Health) has conducted research showing that period cramps can be as painful as having a heart attack." This UCL article referenced no research paper, no study, no dataset — it simply cited The Mirror, which had cited Quartz, which had quoted Guillebaud making an anecdotal observation. The UCL imprimatur gave the claim institutional authority it never earned.

From there, the UCL text was copy-pasted verbatim into social media posts, blog articles, and health websites. Each reproduction added a layer of apparent verification: "UCL confirms," "research shows," "doctors prove." By the time it reached TikTok in 2024, the claim had been laundered through enough iterations that its origin as a single informal remark was invisible.

No scientific study validating a comparison between menstrual cramp pain and heart attack pain has ever been published. Systematic searches of medical literature databases yield no results for controlled comparisons of these two pain types.

**Why the Comparison Is Scientifically Incoherent**

The claim fails not just for lack of evidence but for deeper reasons rooted in pain science.

*Pain is not a single measurable quantity.* There is no universal pain scale that could produce the statement "X is as painful as Y" as a scientific finding. Pain is a subjective experience modulated by context, expectation, emotional state, cultural framing, prior experience, and individual neurobiology. The International Association for the Study of Pain defines it as "an unpleasant sensory and emotional experience associated with, or resembling that associated with, actual or potential tissue damage." That definition centers subjectivity, not measurement.

Existing pain assessment tools — the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), the Numerical Rating Scale (NRS), the McGill Pain Questionnaire — measure a person's self-reported pain intensity at a given moment. They can tell us that an individual rates their current pain as 8 out of 10. They cannot meaningfully compare a menstrual cramp experienced by one person to a heart attack experienced by a different person and declare them equivalent. The subjective, contextual, and emotional dimensions of pain make cross-modality comparisons scientifically vacuous.

*The pain mechanisms are fundamentally different.* Menstrual cramps (primary dysmenorrhea) arise from prostaglandin-mediated uterine smooth muscle contraction and ischemia. The uterus contracts to shed its lining, and excess prostaglandin production causes excessively strong contractions that reduce blood flow, triggering visceral pain signaled through spinal afferent nerves.

Heart attack pain (if present — many heart attacks are painless or present with atypical symptoms) results from myocardial ischemia: cardiac muscle cells starved of oxygen send alarm signals through cardiac afferent nerves. The pain pathways converge at the upper thoracic spinal segments, which is why cardiac pain can be referred to the chest, arm, jaw, or back.

These are different tissues, different pathological processes, different neural pathways, different temporal patterns, and different clinical contexts. Comparing them is like comparing the "brightness" of a sound and a color — they are not on the same axis.

*Heart attack pain is wildly variable.* As gynecologist Dr. Jen Gunter emphasized in her 2018 critique of this comparison, the assumption that heart attacks involve dramatic, severe chest pain is itself a myth driven by media portrayals. Many heart attacks present with mild or vague symptoms — nausea, fatigue, jaw discomfort, shortness of breath — and some are entirely "silent," detected only after the fact on electrocardiography. Women, in particular, are more likely to experience atypical heart attack presentations. Using "heart attack pain" as a stable benchmark presupposes a uniform experience that does not exist.

**What Is True About Menstrual Pain**

None of this means that menstrual pain is trivial or that the medical establishment has handled it well. Quite the opposite.

*Dysmenorrhea is common and often severe.* Primary dysmenorrhea affects an estimated 50–90% of menstruating individuals, depending on the study and the severity threshold used. Approximately 10–15% of those affected report pain severe enough to interfere with daily activities. For individuals with endometriosis — a condition in which tissue similar to the uterine lining grows outside the uterus — chronic pelvic pain can be debilitating, persistent, and profoundly life-disrupting.

*Menstrual pain has been historically undertreated.* There is substantial evidence that menstrual pain has been systematically dismissed, minimized, and undertreated within clinical medicine. The average diagnostic delay for endometriosis remains approximately 7–10 years from symptom onset. Studies consistently show that women's pain reports are taken less seriously than men's across clinical settings — a phenomenon documented in emergency department studies, surgical consultations, and chronic pain management. This gendered pain gap is real and well-documented.

*Research investment has been disproportionately low.* Conditions primarily affecting women, including dysmenorrhea and endometriosis, have historically received less research funding relative to their prevalence and burden of suffering than many male-predominant conditions. This disparity is quantifiable and represents a genuine inequity in biomedical research priorities.

These truths are important. They deserve to be stated clearly and forcefully. But they do not require — and are not helped by — a scientifically incoherent comparison to heart attacks.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The viral success of this claim is not a failure of individual critical thinking. It is the predictable result of a collision between a real grievance, a broken validation system, and the dynamics of social media engagement.

**The Validation Hunger**

Millions of people who experience severe menstrual pain have been told, explicitly or implicitly, that their pain is not a big deal. They have been told to "just take some ibuprofen," told their pain is "normal," told they are exaggerating. For many, the endometriosis diagnostic delay of 7–10 years represents years of being disbelieved by the medical establishment.

Into this landscape drops a claim — "science proves your pain is as bad as a heart attack" — that offers exactly what this population has been denied: external validation of their lived experience. The claim does not succeed because people are gullible. It succeeds because it fills a vacuum of acknowledgment that the medical system created.

The heart attack comparison is so sticky because heart attacks occupy a unique position in popular consciousness as the paradigmatic medical emergency. Everyone takes heart attacks seriously. No one tells a heart attack patient to "just deal with it." By equating menstrual pain with heart attack pain, the claim borrows the automatic seriousness that heart attacks command and redirects it toward menstrual suffering. It is a rhetorical power move, not a scientific claim, even though it disguises itself as one.

**The Authority Laundering Pipeline**

The citational chain — Guillebaud quote → Quartz article → Mirror headline → UCL webpage → social media — represents a well-oiled machine for transforming anecdote into apparent fact. Each step in the chain adds institutional credibility while stripping away the qualifications and context that kept the original statement within honest bounds.

Guillebaud said "almost as bad." Quartz initially dropped "almost" before correcting. The Mirror turned a description of patient reports into a "confirmation" by doctors. UCL transmuted a newspaper article into "research." Social media dropped every remaining qualifier and turned it into an absolute equivalence.

This process is citational laundering — analogous to money laundering, where each transaction makes the origin harder to trace while the product becomes more usable. By the time Dr. Boxer Wachler shows the UCL screenshot on TikTok, the claim appears to rest on the authority of a world-renowned university. In reality, it rests on a six-word aside from a single professor describing what some patients told him.

**The Platform Incentive Structure**

The claim is perfectly optimized for social media virality. It is surprising ("I didn't know cramps were that bad!"), emotionally charged (validation or shock, depending on the audience), identity-relevant (it speaks directly to a large demographic's lived experience), and actionable (share to spread awareness). All of these properties maximize engagement metrics across every platform.

Platforms algorithmically amplify content that generates high engagement. The period-cramp-heart-attack comparison generates enormous engagement — likes, shares, comments, duets, stitches — because it activates strong emotional responses across multiple demographics. The 1.2 million likes on Wachler's Instagram post are not an accident. They are the predictable output of a content-recommendation system designed to maximize exactly the kind of emotional response this claim generates.

**The False Comparative as Advocacy Tool**

The claim functions as a crude but effective advocacy instrument. In a culture that demonstrably undervalues women's pain, finding a comparison that forces people to take menstrual suffering seriously has real rhetorical power. "Period cramps can be as painful as a heart attack" is a better advocacy slogan than "dysmenorrhea is a prevalent condition associated with significant quality-of-life impacts that deserves greater research investment." The false comparative works as rhetoric precisely because it generates the emotional impact that accurate statements fail to achieve.

This creates an uncomfortable dilemma. Debunking the claim risks being perceived as dismissing menstrual pain — as saying "it's not that bad" — which is exactly the dismissal that created the validation hunger driving the claim's success. Careful fact-checking must simultaneously reject the faulty comparison and validate the underlying reality that menstrual pain is undertreated, underresearched, and too often dismissed.


## 4. Verdict

**The claim is epistemologically loaded.** It presents itself as a scientific finding but is actually a single anecdotal quote that was laundered through a chain of citations until it acquired the appearance of institutional verification. No scientific study has ever compared menstrual cramping pain to heart attack pain and found them equivalent. No such study could be meaningfully designed, because the comparison presupposes a universal pain metric that does not exist.

However, unlike most Truth Vault entries where the claim is simply wrong, this one is embedded in a context of genuine medical failure. The historical under-treatment and under-research of menstrual pain is well-documented. The gendered pain gap is real. The advocacy need is legitimate. The claim is false in its specific content but responsive to a true injustice — and that combination makes it far more resistant to correction than a claim that is simply fabricated.

The epistemological lesson is about the difference between validation and accuracy. The claim that "period cramps are as painful as heart attacks" validates the experience of severe menstrual pain. It does not accurately describe a scientific finding. These are different things, and conflating them — as the viral versions of this claim consistently do — undermines both the credibility of pain advocacy and the integrity of scientific claims.

Menstrual pain deserves to be taken seriously on its own terms, not because it has been compared to something else. The comparison to heart attacks implicitly accepts the premise that menstrual pain only matters if it can be shown to be equivalent to a condition already taken seriously. This is a rhetorical trap: it concedes that menstrual pain's importance is derivative rather than intrinsic.


## 5. The Wider Picture

**The Problem of Pain Comparisons**

This claim belongs to a broader genre of cross-modality pain comparisons: kidney stones vs. childbirth, getting hit in the testicles vs. labor, migraines vs. toothache. All of these comparisons are scientifically meaningless and culturally revealing. They tell us nothing about pain physiology and everything about whose suffering a culture takes seriously.

The impulse to compare pains reflects a deeper cultural anxiety about the subjectivity of suffering. In a world that values objectivity and measurement, subjective experiences like pain are inherently vulnerable to dismissal. Pain comparisons attempt to anchor subjective experience to external reference points — to make private suffering publicly legible. The tragedy is that this anchoring is illusory. No external comparison can validate individual experience, because experience is not the kind of thing that external comparison can access.

Pain science has moved increasingly toward understanding pain as a complex biopsychosocial phenomenon — an interaction of nociceptive signaling, central sensitization, emotional processing, cognitive appraisal, and social context. Within this framework, asking "is X as painful as Y" is a category error, like asking "is this painting as heavy as that symphony." The question assumes commensurability where none exists.

**The Gendered Pain Gap**

The real story behind this claim is not about heart attacks. It is about the well-documented systematic failure of clinical medicine to take women's pain seriously.

Multiple studies have demonstrated that women presenting to emergency departments with acute pain receive less analgesia, wait longer for treatment, and are more likely to be prescribed sedatives rather than pain medication compared to men presenting with similar conditions. A landmark 2001 study in the *Journal of Law, Medicine & Ethics* documented the "Girl Who Cried Pain" phenomenon — the systematic pattern of women's pain being attributed to emotional rather than physical causes.

This pattern extends specifically to menstrual pain. The diagnostic delay for endometriosis — a condition that causes chronic, often severe pelvic pain — remains shockingly long. Studies across multiple countries consistently report average delays of 7–10 years between symptom onset and diagnosis. During this delay, patients are frequently told their pain is normal, psychosomatic, or exaggerated.

The period-cramps-heart-attack comparison is, in this light, a symptom of a broken system. When medicine fails to validate suffering through proper diagnosis and treatment, patients seek validation through other channels. The false comparison is the shadow cast by a genuine absence of care.

**Citational Laundering as Epistemic Hazard**

The mechanism by which Guillebaud's off-hand comment became "UCL research confirms" deserves scrutiny as a general phenomenon. Citational laundering — the process by which a weak original source is cited through enough intermediate sources to acquire an appearance of robustness — operates in academic contexts, journalism, social media, and policy discourse.

The structure is always the same: Source A makes a weak or qualified claim. Source B cites Source A with slightly less qualification. Source C cites Source B as though it represents stronger evidence. By Source D or E, the original qualification has vanished entirely, and the claim appears to rest on a broad base of evidence that does not exist.

In this case: Guillebaud says patients describe pain as "almost as bad as a heart attack" → Quartz reports this with the "almost" → The Mirror reports this without the "almost" → UCL describes this as "research showing" → Social media presents this as "doctors confirm." Five steps from anecdote to apparent institutional verification.

This pattern is not unique to this claim. It operates in nutrition science ("red wine prevents heart disease"), exercise science ("sitting kills you"), psychology ("we only use 10% of our brains"), and many other domains. Recognizing citational laundering as a distinct epistemic hazard — and learning to trace claims back to their original sources — is one of the most important media literacy skills available.

**Advocacy and Accuracy: The Tension**

Perhaps the most uncomfortable dimension of this claim is the tension between advocacy and accuracy. The claim is false. It is also, in a broader sense, pointing at something true: that menstrual pain is inadequately addressed by medicine. This creates a dilemma that fact-checkers and science communicators face repeatedly.

The temptation is to let a false claim slide because it serves a good cause. This temptation should be resisted — not because advocacy doesn't matter, but because advocacy built on false claims is ultimately self-defeating. When the period-cramps-heart-attack comparison is debunked (as it has been, repeatedly, including by Snopes), the debunking can be weaponized by those who want to dismiss menstrual pain entirely: "See, they exaggerate."

The better path is to separate the advocacy from the false comparison. Menstrual pain deserves more research funding, faster diagnosis, better treatment, and greater clinical seriousness — all on its own merits, because the evidence for the burden of dysmenorrhea is robust and does not require propping up with false equivalences.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has addressed this claim twice — first in December 2016 (shortly after the original Quartz article) and again in December 2024 in response to the viral TikTok resurgence. The 2024 article chose not to assign a rating, noting insufficient evidence to "definitively confirm or debunk" the claim. This is editorially defensible given Snopes' rating framework, which requires a specific, verifiable claim to rate — and "period cramps are as painful as heart attacks" resists simple true/false categorization because of its subjective and comparative nature.

**What Snopes does well:** The 2024 article provides thorough provenance tracking, tracing the claim from Guillebaud's 2016 quote through the Quartz article, the correction, the Mirror story, and the UCL articles. It correctly identifies the key absence — no scientific study supporting the comparison — and quotes Dr. Jen Gunter's critique. The social media documentation is extensive, capturing the TikTok, Instagram, Reddit, and Facebook instances.

**Where the Truth Vault adds value:** Several epistemological dimensions benefit from deeper treatment:

*The pain science problem.* The fundamental issue is not just the absence of evidence but the impossibility of the comparison as formulated. Cross-modality pain comparison is not a gap in the literature that might someday be filled. It is a conceptual impossibility given the subjective and context-dependent nature of pain experience. Understanding *why* the comparison cannot be validated, not just that it hasn't been, is important for inoculating against future versions of the same rhetorical move.

*The citational laundering mechanism.* The chain from Guillebaud's qualified anecdote to "UCL research confirms" is a textbook case of a general epistemic pattern that operates across many domains of misinformation. Naming and describing this mechanism explicitly helps readers recognize it in other contexts — nutrition science, psychology, policy discourse — where the same process operates.

*The advocacy-accuracy tension.* The claim's relationship to real gendered inequities in pain treatment is the elephant in the room that fact-checks often acknowledge but rarely address head-on. The risk of debunking — that it will be perceived as dismissing menstrual pain — is real and deserves explicit engagement. A fact-check that simply says "this isn't proven" without acknowledging the systemic context that makes the claim so appealing is doing incomplete epistemic work.

*The specificity of the harm.* Unlike some medical myths, the direct physical harm from believing this claim is minimal — no one is going to hurt themselves by believing their cramps are comparable to heart attacks. The harm is epistemological: it degrades the standard of evidence for medical claims, it makes legitimate menstrual pain advocacy vulnerable to dismissal when the false comparison is debunked, and it normalizes the citational laundering process that fuels misinformation across many domains.

The broader lesson for fact-checking is that some claims resist binary rating systems because they are not binary claims. "Period cramps are as painful as heart attacks" is not a factual assertion that can be verified or falsified. It is a rhetorical strategy wrapped in the language of science. Effective fact-checking of such claims requires not just evaluating the evidence but analyzing the epistemological structure of the claim itself — what kind of claim it is, what kind of evidence could in principle support it, and why it resists the categories fact-checkers typically apply.
