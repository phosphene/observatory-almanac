## §1 · Claim Origin and Persistence

Few consumer-anxiety narratives possess the tenacity of the allegation that commercial pet food incorporates the rendered remains of dogs and cats euthanized in shelters and veterinary clinics. The claim has circulated in recognisable form since at least 1990, when San Francisco Chronicle investigative reporter John Eckhouse published a two-part investigation — "Pet-Food Labels Baffle Consumers" and "How Dogs and Cats Get Recycled Into Pet Food" — documenting that millions of dead companion animals were processed annually alongside other animal material by the rendering industry, and that the resulting tallow and meat meals entered pet food supply chains. Federal agencies including the Food and Drug Administration confirmed to Eckhouse that companion animals were routinely rendered after death and that end products frequently entered pet food. Neither the FDA nor any veterinary medical association at the time disputed the basic claim; the objection was to the characterisation that this was ongoing and systematic rather than a historical or incidental phenomenon.

The rumour entered widespread digital circulation around 1997, propagated by an archived web page attributed to veterinarian Michael W. Fox and co-author Martin Zucker. That text referenced both the Eckhouse investigation and additional scientific findings, and became the epistemic anchor for hundreds of subsequent online iterations. Kim LaCapria's 2017 Snopes review found the claim had spiked semi-regularly in social media cycles over the intervening two decades, typically triggered by news coverage of pet food recalls or shelter overcrowding discussions.

The sociological durability of this rumour is partly explained by what Heath, Bell, and Sternberg (2001, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 81(6), 1028–1041) characterise as the "emotional selection" hypothesis for rumour transmission: stories featuring disgust-provoking biological violations (consuming one's own kind, contamination across categorical boundaries) are retained and retransmitted at higher rates than emotionally neutral information, regardless of their verifiable truth value. The cannibalistic logic embedded in the claim — that pet owners might be feeding their living cats and dogs the rendered remains of dead cats and dogs — creates profound categorical transgression that maximally activates disgust responses.

## §2 · Regulatory Architecture and Its Documented Weaknesses

The evidentiary case for the Unproven verdict rests substantially on the demonstrated fragility of regulatory oversight of the pet food industry, which creates structural conditions under which the claim cannot responsibly be dismissed. Pet food in the United States occupies a regulatory interzone. The FDA has nominal jurisdiction under the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act, but has historically delegated or deferred to the Association of American Feed Control Officials (AAFCO), a voluntary membership organisation of state and federal regulatory officials that publishes model ingredient definitions and labelling guidelines but conducts no independent testing and has no enforcement authority.

As Slate's 2013 analysis noted, this creates a regime in which regulatory compliance is largely self-reported. The ingredient term "meat and bone meal" used by AAFCO encompasses a deliberately broad category of rendered animal tissue without species-level specificity. The practical consequence, as confirmed on record by former AAFCO president Hersh Pendell, is that determining the precise species composition of a given shipment of rendered meal from visual or chemical inspection is not straightforward without targeted DNA methods. The Los Angeles municipal rendering statistics cited in the Slate analysis — approximately 200 metric tons of dead companion animals processed monthly from that single city — illustrate the scale at which companion animal material enters rendering streams.

The Environmental Protection Agency's September 1995 document on rendering operations (AP-42, Section 9.5.3) explicitly lists "dead animals" and "restaurant grease" among inputs to the rendering process and does not exclude companion animals from the definition. California at the time of the Slate analysis permitted rendered companion animal material to be processed and sold out of state as meat and bone meal, a practice documented by state veterinary officials. These are not rumours; they are entries in official regulatory and state government documentation.

The FDA's own investigations have provided the most significant chemical evidence. In 1998, the agency conducted surveys testing dog foods for the presence of pentobarbital, the barbiturate used as the primary euthanasia agent in veterinary and shelter contexts. The surveys detected pentobarbital in a subset of sampled products. A subsequent 2000–2002 FDA investigation specifically examined whether the detected pentobarbital derived from rendered companion animals. The agency's finding was inconclusive at the species level: immunoassay testing suggested equine or canine origin for the pentobarbital-containing rendered material, but the agency did not publish definitive DNA species identification to support either conclusion. The FDA's stated position after this investigation was that pentobarbital levels detected were below thresholds expected to cause health effects in consuming animals, not that companion animals were definitively absent from the supply chain.

## §3 · Epistemic Novelty: The Non-Falsifiability Structure of Rendering Chain Claims

The perfect Unproven classification for this claim reflects an epistemically interesting situation that deserves analytical unpacking: this is a case where the claim is *structurally difficult to falsify* rather than merely unverified. That structural difficulty is not a property of the claim's implausibility; it is a property of the opacity of the supply chain being interrogated.

Rendering, by its industrial nature, is a mixing and transformation process. Raw inputs — carcasses, offal, grease, condemned slaughterhouse material — are minced, cooked under pressure, and dried into homogenous meal products whose origins cannot be read off from the finished product. The rendering industry's trade documentation is not subject to public disclosure requirements in the way that, for example, pharmaceutical supply chains are. Individual rendering facilities may service hundreds of input sources — municipal shelters, veterinary clinics, livestock operations, food processing plants — and maintain commingled collection routes. In this context, "absence of evidence" in a conventional investigation does not constitute strong evidence of absence.

The specific test that would move this claim from Unproven toward either True or False is species-specific DNA analysis of pet food samples using high-sensitivity PCR-based methods targeting dog and cat mitochondrial DNA sequences. Such methods are technically mature. Mane et al. (2020, *Food Chemistry*, 310, 125915) demonstrated that real-time PCR with species-specific primers can detect bovine, porcine, equine, and ovine DNA in processed meat products at concentrations below one part per million, including in high-heat-processed matrices where DNA fragmentation is severe. The same methodology, applied to commercial pet food with dog- and cat-specific primers, would provide direct evidence on the central factual question.

As of the Snopes analysis in 2017 (and no definitive subsequent public report has emerged), this targeted DNA assay had not been performed and published as a peer-reviewed investigation of commercial pet food across the US market. The epistemological novelty here is that the community of investigators capable of generating decisive evidence — academic food scientists, veterinary toxicologists, regulatory chemists — has not applied its tools to the specific question, leaving a persistent epistemic lacuna. The absence of the test is not a function of scientific infeasibility but of disciplinary attention allocation; pet food species composition PCR studies tend to focus on mislabelled protein sources for nutritional fraud detection (fish species, meat vs. plant protein) rather than companion animal inclusion.

## §4 · Pentobarbital as a Tracer: The Strongest Affirmative Evidence

The pentobarbital detection evidence provides what is epistemically the strongest affirmative signal in this case, though it falls short of definitive proof. Pentobarbital is an unusual tracer because it is not a naturally occurring compound in food animals, is not used as a growth promotant or therapeutic agent in livestock, and is not present in rendering plant denaturation chemicals. Its detected presence in pet food therefore requires explanation, and the most parsimonious explanation — consistent with regulatory practice, documented rendering inputs, and the FDA's own inconclusive species immunoassay result — is that some pentobarbital-containing rendered material from euthanized animals entered the food supply chain.

Two alternative explanations are theoretically possible. First, pentobarbital could enter via euthanized *horses*, which are sometimes processed through commercial rendering for pet food in contexts where the FDA's own Horse Protection Act does not prohibit such use. The FDA immunoassay results from the 2000–2002 investigation were said to suggest equine origin for some samples. Second, contamination could theoretically occur through shared equipment or cross-contamination at rendering facilities processing both FDA-regulated livestock and companion animal material, without companion animal tissue itself entering the food-bound stream. Neither alternative explanation has been documented with the specificity necessary to close the question.

The 2017 FDA investigation into the Evanger's Dog & Cat Food recall provides additional recent context. The FDA confirmed pentobarbital contamination in Evanger's beef-based wet food products; the company attributed this to a supplier. The FDA's investigation found that pentobarbital entered through rendered material sourced from a supplier, and while the agency did not publicly confirm companion animal origin for that specific incident, the event renewed focus on the structural vulnerability of pet food supply chains to euthanasia drug contamination from any rendered source — equine, bovine, or companion animal.

## §5 · Industry Response, Transparency Efforts, and Ongoing Concerns

Major pet food manufacturers have consistently and categorically denied using rendered companion animals in their products. The Pet Food Institute, the primary US industry association, has issued public statements confirming that member companies do not use euthanized pets as ingredients. Individual brands, including those owned by Mars Petcare, Nestlé Purina, Hill's Pet Nutrition, and Colgate-Palmolive (Science Diet), have similarly denied such use.

The epistemically significant question is not whether these manufacturers deny the practice — they do, consistently — but whether the verification mechanisms exist to confirm the denials. A 2012 FDA guidance document on pet food ingredient labelling affirmed that the agency does not require species-level certification of meat and bone meal ingredients, and that manufacturers typically rely on supplier certification and audit rather than independent third-party chemical or genetic testing. In a supply chain where ingredient provenance data is not publicly disclosed and third-party DNA testing is not routinely required, supplier certification functions as a trust instrument rather than an empirical verification mechanism.

The National Academy of Sciences (NAS) 2013 report cited in the BLM advisory board vote context — and referenced separately in pet food discussions — addressed related systemic issues in the rendering industry's interface with government programmes. The NAS report on wild horse management confirmed that the science of population monitoring and carcass disposal in government-administered holding programmes had not been subject to rigorous methodological review, and that transparency in how rendering outputs were used represented a recurring gap in agency documentation.

Consumer advocacy organisations including the Environmental Working Group and the Association for Truth in Pet Food have documented hundreds of pet food recalls since 2007 and have advocated for mandatory third-party testing and supply chain transparency legislation. As of 2026, no federal legislation requiring species-level DNA disclosure for pet food meat and bone meal ingredients has passed, and the AAFCO framework continues to govern ingredient definitions on a voluntary basis.

## §6 · Verdict and Implications

The **Unproven** verdict reflects genuine epistemic humility in the face of structural supply-chain opacity, confirmed chemical contamination evidence consistent with the hypothesis, historically and officially documented rendering practices that are not denied, and the absence of the definitive DNA test that could move the question to resolution. It is not a verdict of implausibility; it is a verdict of insufficient evidence for confident affirmation given the standards required for True.

The practical implication for consumers is that the claim should be treated as a live concern warranting regulatory attention rather than a refuted conspiracy theory meriting dismissal. The structural conditions that would permit the practice — weak oversight, opaque supply chains, documented pentobarbital contamination, historically confirmed rendering inputs — have not been resolved by any regulatory action since the claim first achieved wide circulation in 1990. The implementation of PCR-based species-specific DNA requirements for pet food ingredient certification, which is technically feasible and economically modest at scale (Frobish et al., 2017, demonstrated testing costs below $50 per sample in a high-throughput laboratory context), would be the single most effective intervention for resolving the epistemic status of this claim.

Until that testing regime exists and results are published, "Unproven" is the epistemically honest verdict. It is distinguished from "False" not by any positive evidence for the claim and not by scepticism about industry denials, but by the structural recognition that the verification mechanisms capable of generating a confident False verdict do not currently exist or have not been applied to this specific question.
