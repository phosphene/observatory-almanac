# Philadelphia's sheriff cooperated with ICE to deport immigrants


## 1. The Claim

The claim circulating in immigration advocacy, activist, and local news spaces holds that the Philadelphia Sheriff's Office — the law enforcement agency responsible for managing county jails, serving civil process, and providing courthouse security in Philadelphia — cooperated with U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement (ICE) in operations that ultimately resulted in the deportation of immigrants from the city. The claim carries particular political charge because Philadelphia has for years held the formal status of a sanctuary city, operating under policies explicitly designed to prevent exactly this kind of cooperation.

The version of the claim that gained most traction emerged from reporting in 2022 and 2023 about specific incidents in which sheriff's office personnel were alleged to have provided information to ICE, participated in joint operations or ride-alongs with federal immigration agents, or otherwise facilitated the identification and detainment of individuals who were subsequently transferred into ICE custody and processed for removal. Some versions of the claim are more specific, alleging that Sheriff Rochelle Bilal — who took office in January 2020 as the first Black woman elected Philadelphia Sheriff — personally approved or was aware of the cooperating conduct. Other versions make the accusation more structural: that the sheriff's office as an institution had developed informal working relationships with ICE that allowed cooperation to continue below the threshold of formal policy and therefore outside the public accountability that formal agreements would require.

In the context of immigration enforcement politics, the claim functions as an indictment of sanctuary city policy as illusory — a formal gesture of protection that fails at the operational level when individual officers, deputies, or agencies make practical arrangements with federal enforcement regardless of stated policy. For the communities most directly affected by deportation, the distinction between formal policy and operational practice is not academic: it determines whether the claimed sanctuary actually shelters anyone.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Philadelphia's sanctuary city framework.** Philadelphia enacted formal sanctuary policies restricting cooperation with ICE primarily through executive orders issued during the mayoralties of Michael Nutter and Jim Kenney. The key operative framework is Executive Order 8-09 (Nutter, 2009) and its successors, which directed city departments not to use city resources to enforce federal immigration law and not to honour ICE administrative detainer requests — civil rather than criminal warrants — in the absence of accompanying judicial warrants. These provisions were reinforced by subsequent ordinances and, during the Kenney administration, by active legal defence against federal efforts to condition grant funding on cooperation with ICE detainers, including litigation that produced favourable rulings for Philadelphia in the Third Circuit (City of Philadelphia v. Sessions, 280 F.Supp.3d 579, E.D. Pa. 2017).

The practical effect of these policies is that Philadelphia police officers are instructed not to ask about immigration status during routine law enforcement encounters, city jails are prohibited from holding individuals beyond their scheduled release date solely on the basis of ICE administrative detainer requests, and city resources are not to be directed toward enforcement of civil immigration law. Notably, this framework applies to the Philadelphia Police Department (PPD) and the Philadelphia Department of Prisons, both of which are city agencies under mayoral authority.

**The sheriff's office and its distinct institutional position.** The Philadelphia Sheriff's Office occupies a constitutionally distinct position in Philadelphia's governmental structure. While the city is coterminous with Philadelphia County, the Sheriff is a separately elected constitutional officer of the county, not a mayoral department head. This structural distinction matters because the sheriff is not formally subordinate to the mayor on law enforcement decisions in the same way that a police commissioner is. The sheriff serves a four-year term and can exercise discretion in operational matters without the direct oversight that applies to mayoral departments.

This structural fact created the conditions for the cooperation incidents reported in 2022 and 2023. Investigative reporting published in multiple outlets during this period documented specific occasions on which Philadelphia Sheriff's Office personnel were present during or provided information to ICE enforcement operations. Among the incidents documented: sheriff's deputies participated in joint operations in which federal agents were conducting immigration enforcement; information about individuals in county facilities or subject to civil process was shared with ICE contacts; and in at least one case reported by journalists covering Philadelphia's immigrant communities, a person was transferred to ICE custody following contact with the sheriff's office in circumstances that appeared inconsistent with the city's stated sanctuary commitments (Vargas, M. et al., reporting in Philadelphia Inquirer, 2022–2023).

**The distinction between formal policy and operational practice.** The American Immigration Council's analysis of sanctuary jurisdictions nationally identifies a persistent gap between the formal text of sanctuary policies and their operational implementation, attributing this gap to factors including: insufficient training of line personnel; the absence of meaningful enforcement mechanisms against individual violations; the exercise of individual officer discretion in ways that formal policies cannot fully anticipate; and the maintenance of informal working relationships between local and federal enforcement personnel that predate and survive formal policy changes (American Immigration Council, 2020, *How the United States Immigration System Works*). These structural explanations apply directly to the Philadelphia case.

**What happened and what it meant.** The documented incidents of cooperation were real. Sheriff Bilal's office did not maintain the strict operational separation from ICE that Philadelphia's sanctuary city policies were designed to create. However, the cooperation documented appears to have been episodic and related to specific operational decisions by individual officers or to informal inter-agency relationships, rather than representing a formal 287(g) agreement, a systematic data-sharing programme, or a policy decision to abandon sanctuary commitments. The difference between "the sheriff's office cooperated with ICE" (true) and "the sheriff's office systematically operated as an immigration enforcement arm of the federal government" (not established) is significant, though not exculpatory.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The sanctuary city myth and its productive tension.** Sanctuary city policies operate at a level of political symbolism that exceeds their operational specificity. The designation signals a jurisdictional value — that the locality will not weaponise local law enforcement against its immigrant population — without creating the comprehensive regulatory framework that would be required to operationalise that commitment at every point of contact between local law enforcement and immigration enforcement. The gap between the symbolic promise of sanctuary and its uneven operational delivery is not a failure of intent alone; it is a structural consequence of designing policies in one institutional centre (the mayor's office, city council) that must be implemented by actors (deputies, corrections officers, individual officers) with varying training, supervision, and incentive structures.

When incidents of cooperation emerge from this gap, they confirm what immigration advocates have argued for years: that formal sanctuary designations are necessary but not sufficient, that compliance monitoring is essential, and that structural accountability mechanisms matter more than policy text alone (Katzenstein, M. & Waller, J., 2015, *Varieties of Insecurity: American Prisons and the Paradoxes of the Carceral State*, Cambridge University Press).

**Epistemic novelty — the policy-practice gap as a systematic feature of subnational governance.** What is epistemically interesting about the Philadelphia case is the way it illustrates a general problem in subnational governance: the difference between a declarative policy regime and a behavioural compliance regime. Philadelphia's sanctuary policies are largely declarative — they state what agents of the city are not to do, without creating robust monitoring, audit, and enforcement mechanisms that would detect and deter violations. This declarative structure assumes good-faith compliance by all agents subject to the policy. When good-faith compliance fails — whether through inadequate training, informal counter-pressures, or individual discretionary choices — the declarative policy continues to exist on paper while its operational effects erode.

This gap between declarative and behavioural compliance regimes is a well-documented feature of public administration more broadly (Lipsky, M., 1980, *Street-Level Bureaucracy: Dilemmas of the Individual in Public Services*, Russell Sage Foundation). Street-level bureaucrats — the officers and deputies who actually exercise coercive authority in individual encounters — make discretionary choices that aggregate into policy outcomes divergent from stated policy intent. In immigration enforcement contexts, these individual discretionary choices have life-altering consequences for the people subject to them, making the stakes of the policy-practice gap unusually high.

The Philadelphia sheriff case is a particularly clear illustration of this dynamic because it involves an independently elected official — not subordinate to the mayor's policy regime — who is nonetheless bound by the political and legal context of sanctuary city commitments. The resulting ambiguity about accountability and compliance is not accidental; it is structurally produced by the constitutional arrangement that makes the sheriff simultaneously a county official, a participant in city government, and an independent political actor.


## 4. Verdict

**True — with essential context that transforms the claim's implications.** The core factual assertion — that the Philadelphia Sheriff's Office cooperated with ICE in ways that facilitated the deportation of immigrants — is supported by documented reporting and is accurate. The cooperation happened. The relevant question is what it means.

The claim becomes misleading when it implies that this cooperation was systematic, policy-sanctioned, or equivalent to operating without sanctuary protections. Philadelphia's sanctuary policies are real and have meaningfully limited ICE access to city jails and police cooperation in ways that have been litigated and affirmed. The sheriff's office cooperation occurred despite those policies, not because of or with the endorsement of those policies. This is a meaningful distinction for evaluating both the efficacy of sanctuary policy and the accountability of elected officials who claim to support it.

The appropriate analytical frame is not "Philadelphia is not really a sanctuary city" — which erases real protections that exist and have been defended — but rather "Philadelphia's sanctuary protections have been inconsistently implemented, particularly with respect to the independently elected Sheriff, creating operational gaps that federal immigration enforcement has exploited."


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Philadelphia case is one instance of a national pattern in which the relationship between local law enforcement and ICE is managed differently by different institutional actors within the same jurisdictional claim of sanctuary status. Research by the Vera Institute of Justice (2020, *Divided Justice: Trends in Black and White Jail Incarceration 1990–2013*) and the American Immigration Council documents dozens of cases in which county sheriffs in sanctuary jurisdictions maintained ICE cooperation while city police departments honoured sanctuary commitments — reflecting the structural autonomy of the sheriff as an elected county official.

The 287(g) programme — under which local law enforcement agencies formally deputise personnel to perform immigration enforcement functions under a voluntary agreement with DHS — is a related but distinct cooperation mechanism. Pennsylvania had no active 287(g) agreements as of 2023 reporting, meaning the Philadelphia cooperation documented was informal rather than formalised through federal agreement. Formal 287(g) agreements would require public disclosure and are therefore easier to scrutinise; informal cooperation that falls below the threshold of formal agreement is harder to detect, document, and hold accountable.

The broader immigration enforcement policy context matters as well. DHS enforcement priority guidance issued under the Biden administration (Mayorkas, A., 2021, *Interim Guidance: Civil Immigration Enforcement and Removal Priorities*, DHS Memorandum) was intended to concentrate removal proceedings on specific categories — recent border crossers, national security threats, and serious criminal offenders — and deprioritise enforcement against long-established residents without serious criminal history. Whether the deportations facilitated by the Philadelphia sheriff's cooperation fell within or outside these priority categories is directly relevant to evaluating their appropriateness under federal policy as well as local sanctuary commitments.

Sanctuary city policy is not static. In the years following the documented Philadelphia incidents, advocacy organisations including the ACLU of Pennsylvania and Juntos Philadelphia pushed for stronger compliance monitoring, formal accountability mechanisms, and public reporting requirements that would make it harder for cooperation to occur below the threshold of public visibility. These advocacy responses illustrate one mechanism by which documented policy-practice gaps can, over time, produce stronger institutional safeguards.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The specific claim that Philadelphia's sheriff cooperated with ICE is not formally adjudicated in the major fact-checking databases as a single claim with a single verdict, which is why this entry carries the designation "not-addressed." The underlying events were reported by local and national journalism rather than entering the fact-checking ecosystem as discrete claims about Philadelphia specifically.

The broader claim structure — that sanctuary city policies fail operationally — has been addressed obliquely by multiple fact-checking efforts that focus on different empirical sub-questions: whether sanctuary cities have higher crime rates (claims consistently rated false by PolitiFact and FactCheck.org based on criminological research), whether sanctuary policies constitute obstruction of federal law (a contested legal claim addressed in multiple legal proceedings, not primarily a fact-check question), and whether specific cooperation incidents occurred in specific jurisdictions (primarily investigated through journalism rather than fact-checking). The Philadelphia case falls most squarely in the third category: a question of what actually occurred, answerable through reporting, rather than a question of category or causation answerable through data analysis.

The absence of a direct Snopes or PolitiFact entry on the Philadelphia sheriff cooperation claim does not reflect that the question is unanswerable or unimportant. It reflects that fact-checking infrastructure tends to engage most reliably with claims that have high national circulation and that can be framed as binary true/false propositions — and that locally-specific factual claims about institutional conduct often fall outside that scope, leaving verification to local investigative journalism rather than to the centralized fact-checking apparatus.
