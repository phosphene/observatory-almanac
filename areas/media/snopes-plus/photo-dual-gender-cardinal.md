# Does This Photo Show Half-Male, Half-Female Cardinal?


## 1. The Claim

In late February 2021, photographs capturing a northern cardinal with a dramatically split appearance — the right half displaying the characteristic brilliant red plumage of a male, the left showing the brownish hue typical of females — went viral after being posted to Facebook by Jamie Hill, a Pennsylvania birdwatcher with nearly five decades of experience. Hill described the sighting as "once-in-a-lifetime" and provided a technical identification: the bird was a bilateral gynandromorph, meaning it was genetically half male and half female.

Hill also noted the extraordinary theoretical reproductive implications: such a bird, he wrote, would hypothetically possess a functioning ovary on its left side (the anatomically normal position for avian ovaries) and a functioning single testis on its right — meaning it could potentially either lay fertile eggs by mating with a male, or father eggs by mating with a female. This detail amplified the story's viral reach considerably.

The photographs were shared across social media platforms and covered by news organizations from National Geographic to local Pennsylvania outlets. Snopes investigated the claim — that the photographs showed an actual half-male, half-female cardinal — and confirmed the rating as True. Madison Dapcevich engaged with the ornithological literature, consulted expert sources including Keith Russell of Audubon Mid-Atlantic and Daniel Hooper (then a postdoctoral fellow at the Cornell Lab of Ornithology), and provided a substantive summary of gynandromorphism in birds.

The claim and its confirmation are not in dispute. But the theoretical biology of bilateral gynandromorphism — the mechanism of its formation, its implications for understanding sex determination in birds, and its relationship to broader questions in developmental genetics — is significantly richer than even the better-than-average Snopes coverage indicates.


## 2. What's Actually True

Snopes correctly identifies the bird as a bilateral gynandromorphic northern cardinal (*Cardinalis cardinalis*) and explains the visual significance: northern cardinals exhibit striking sexual dimorphism, with males displaying brilliant red plumage and a black facial mask while females are brownish or buffy with red tinges on wings and crest. A bilateral gynandromorph — an organism that is genetically half male and half female, with the division occurring approximately at the body's sagittal midline — would therefore display exactly the half-red, half-brown appearance visible in Hill's photographs.

The article accurately describes the mechanism of gynandromorphism at a introductory level, relying primarily on Daniel Hooper's explanation to National Geographic: in birds, the sex chromosomes are Z and W (rather than the X and Y system of mammals). Males are ZZ and females are ZW. The most widely accepted explanation for bilateral gynandromorphism in birds is that a female egg cell develops with two nuclei — one carrying Z and one carrying W — and is "double fertilized" by two male Z-carrying sperm. The result is a cell containing ZZ material on one side and ZW material on the other, with each subsequently dividing to produce the respective male and female tissue lineages across the developing embryo.

The article also correctly reports that while bilateral gynandromorphism in the wild is rare, it is documented across multiple avian species — at least nine confirmed cases in birds including the rose-breasted grosbeak, house sparrow, black redstart, and domestic chicken, with museum specimens also confirming instances in orchard oriole and black-throated blue warbler. The long-term observation study published in The Wilson Journal of Ornithology — Peer, B.D., & Nolan, P.M. (2014). A bilateral gynandromorph of the northern cardinal observed over an extended period. *The Wilson Journal of Ornithology*, 126(2), 273–278 — which tracked a gynandromorphic cardinal over 40 days across 15 months in northwestern Illinois from 2008 to 2010, is cited and provides valuable behavioral data.

This is a genuinely thorough fact-check that engages substantively with primary literature. The gap is not factual accuracy but depth of theoretical engagement with a phenomenon that has significant implications for the developmental biology and genetics of sex determination.


## 3. Epistemic Novelty: Avian Sex Determination, Developmental Mechanisms, and What Gynandromorphs Reveal

**The avian ZW sex determination system and its differences from XY.** The article correctly identifies that birds use ZW sex chromosomes rather than the mammalian XY system, but this distinction is more theoretically significant than its brief treatment suggests. In the mammalian XY system, sex determination is primarily driven by the presence or absence of the *SRY* gene on the Y chromosome, which triggers a developmental cascade toward male gonadal identity. In the avian ZW system, the situation is more complex and less well-understood.

Evidence accumulated from the late 2000s onward suggests that avian sex determination may have an intrinsic cellular component — that individual cells may "know" their own sex chromosomal identity (ZZ versus ZW) through mechanisms that operate independently of circulating sex hormones. This was dramatically demonstrated by Agate, R.J., Grisham, W., Wade, J., Mann, S., Wingfield, J., Schanen, C., Bhatt, A., & Arnold, A.P. (2003). Neural, not gonadal, origin of brain sex differences in a gynandromorphic finch. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 100(8), 4873–4878, which showed that a gynandromorphic zebra finch (*Taeniopygia guttata*) displayed behavioral and neural asymmetries inconsistent with a purely hormonal explanation. Specifically, the ZZ (male) side of the brain showed male-typical neural architecture even in regions where sex hormone exposure should have been symmetric — because both sides of the brain were bathed in the same circulatory hormonal environment.

This finding, now sometimes called the "cell-autonomous sex identity" hypothesis in avian biology, suggests that at least some aspects of avian sexual development are determined at the cellular level by chromosomal dosage (the number of Z chromosomes) rather than entirely by gonadal hormone secretion. The bilateral gynandromorphic cardinal provides a potential natural test case for this hypothesis in a non-laboratory context: if the bird's behavioral repertoire, vocalizations, and morphological details beyond plumage color show consistent asymmetries tracking the ZZ/ZW split, this would be further field evidence for cell-autonomous sex identity in Cardinalis cardinalis.

**The mechanism of double fertilization and its developmental context.** The Snopes article's description of gynandromorphism formation — an egg with two nuclei doubly fertilized by two sperm — is a reasonable simplification of the primary hypothesis, but the actual developmental biology is somewhat more complex and contested. The "double fertilization" model is one of at least three proposed mechanisms:

1. **Double fertilization (primary hypothesis):** A binucleate egg (produced by incomplete meiotic division) is fertilized by two sperm simultaneously. Each nucleus fuses with one sperm, producing a ZZ and a ZW cell in the same zygote. Subsequent mitosis produces the bilateral chimaerism observed.

2. **Mitotic non-disjunction:** A normally fertilized (ZW) egg undergoes a very early mitotic error in which one daughter cell retains both Z chromosomes while the other egg loses the W. This would produce a ZZ/ZW mosaic through a different pathway.

3. **Early cell fusion:** Two separately fertilized eggs or early-stage embryos (one ZZ, one ZW) fuse into a single embryo — a process well documented in chimeric mammals but less established in birds.

The visual manifestation of strict bilateral (left-right) splitting rather than mosaic patching is specifically predicted by mechanisms in which the initial sex-determining error occurs at the first cell division after fertilization: if the first cell divides into one ZZ and one ZW daughter cell, and if left-right body axis specification is subsequently imposed on this chimeric two-cell state, a strict bilateral pattern results. Mosaic gynandromorphs (where male and female patches are irregularly distributed) suggest a later-occurring error. The bilateral pattern in Hill's cardinal and the Illinois cardinal observed by Peer & Nolan (2014) is consistent with a very early developmental event.

**Behavioral observations and what they reveal.** The Wilson Journal of Ornithology study by Peer & Nolan (2014) is unusually valuable for the behavioral data it provides. Over 40 observation days spanning roughly 15 months, the gynandromorphic cardinal was never heard vocalizing and did not exhibit unusual agonistic (aggressive or threat-display) responses from other cardinals in the area. These observations are theoretically interesting in the context of the cell-autonomous sex identity hypothesis: if individual brain regions had been developing under cell-autonomous ZZ (male) or ZW (female) identities respectively, one might predict asymmetric behavioral expression — for example, vocalization (a primarily left-hemisphere activity in songbirds) showing reduced output if the left hemisphere was the ZW (female) side. The absence of vocalization in the observed bird is consistent with, though not specifically predicted by, this hypothesis.

The reported Peer & Nolan (2014) observation period is too short and the absence of data (the bird was never heard) too ambiguous to draw strong conclusions, but it represents the most systematic behavioral data on a free-living gynandromorphic northern cardinal and is appropriately cited in the Snopes article. What the article does not note is the specific theoretical framework that would make such behavioral data scientifically interpretable.

**The rarity question.** Russell's observation to Snopes that bilateral gynandromorphism may be detectably more common in northern cardinals than in equally common species because the cardinal's strong sexual dimorphism makes gynandromorphs visually obvious is epistemically important. Conservatively, the "observed prevalence" of gynandromorphs in any species is a function of detection probability as well as actual occurrence probability. Species with strong, clearly differentiated sexual dimorphism based on pigmentation — northern cardinals being essentially the paradigm case among North American birds — will generate visible gynandromorphs when underlying ZZ/ZW chimaerism occurs, while species with cryptic or modest sexual dimorphism may have equivalent underlying chimaerism rates that go completely undetected.

This observational selection bias means that the distribution of documented gynandromorphism cases across species tells us more about which species ornithologists can detect than about which species actually experience higher developmental error rates. Correcting for this bias in any attempt to estimate the background rate of early-developmental chromosomal errors would require either systematic genotyping of large bird samples or a model-based adjustment that has not yet been published.


## 4. The EARC Assessment

**Empirical status.** Confirmed. The photographs taken by Jamie Hill on February 20, 2021, are authentic (Hill is a documented expert birder with decades of local presence; the images are consistent with known bilateral gynandromorphic specimens; the identification was confirmed by multiple ornithological experts). The condition itself is a documented biological phenomenon with multiple confirmed cases in birds and other taxa. The species identification as northern cardinal is not in doubt.

**Adequacy.** Better than average for Snopes coverage. The article actually cites the Peer & Nolan (2014) Wilson Journal of Ornithology paper, quotes expert sources (Russell and Hooper) at some length, explains the ZW sex chromosome system, and provides a mechanism for gynandromorphism formation. These are genuine substantive elements beyond what most Snopes fact-checks provide.

**Reanalysis required.** None for the factual verdict. The bird is what it appears to be.

**Contestation.** None. No alternative identification of the bird or mechanism for its appearance has been proposed. The identification as bilateral gynandromorphism is uncontested among expert commenters.

**Gap assigned:** Depth-expandable. The verdict is correct and the Snopes treatment is substantive. The gap is the article's failure to engage with the cell-autonomous sex identity hypothesis, the multiple competing developmental mechanisms for gynandromorph formation, and the theoretical significance of the Hill 2021 sighting for the ongoing behavioral biology literature on sex determination in birds.


## 5. The Broader Stakes

Bilateral gynandromorphism is more than a visual anomaly — it is a natural experiment in the developmental genetics of sexual determination, and each documented free-living gynandromorph represents a potential source of behavioral and morphological data that cannot ethically be created in a laboratory setting. The gradual accumulation of field observations (Hill 2021, Illinois 2008-2010, and the handful of other documented cases) constitutes a sparse but real observational dataset bearing on fundamental questions in sex determination research.

The broader scientific context is that avian sex determination is substantially less understood than mammalian sex determination, and the cell-autonomous sex identity hypothesis remains contested. Agate et al.'s (2003) zebra finch gynandromorph work was influential but based on a single specimen, and the theoretical framework it proposed — that bird cells "remember" their sex chromosomal identity independently of gonadal hormone exposure — has significant implications for understanding how sexual dimorphism is maintained in adult birds during breeding and non-breeding seasons, when hormonal profiles fluctuate widely.

If the Agate hypothesis is correct, then bilateral gynandromorphs like the Hill 2021 cardinal represent natural experiments in which cell-autonomous sex identity effects can potentially be separated from hormonal effects by comparing the two halves of the same animal. This would not be possible with controlled experiments (creating bilateral gynandromorphs artificially is not currently feasible in vertebrates), making accidental field observations uniquely scientifically valuable.

The viral spread of Hill's photographs, and the public fascination with the bird's appearance, is in this sense directly useful: it ensures that future bilateral gynandromorphs observed by amateur birdwatchers will be photographed and reported. The citizen science value of expert amateur ornithologists like Jamie Hill — someone with nearly 50 years of field experience who recognized what he was seeing and documented it carefully — is considerable for a phenomenon where sample sizes are as constrained as they are in bilateral gynandromorph research.


## 6. What This Entry Adds

This Truth Vault entry affirms the Snopes True verdict — the Hill 2021 photographs depict a genuine bilateral gynandromorphic northern cardinal — while adding three elements of substantive theoretical depth:

First, an account of the cell-autonomous sex identity hypothesis in avian biology, grounded in Agate et al.'s (2003) landmark zebra finch gynandromorph study, and an explanation of why this hypothesis makes field observations of bilateral gynandromorphs scientifically valuable beyond their visual novelty.

Second, a clarification of the proposed developmental mechanisms for bilateral gynandromorphism (double fertilization, mitotic non-disjunction, and early cell fusion) and an explanation of why the strict bilateral pattern observed in the Hill and Peer & Nolan specimens is consistent specifically with a very early first-cell-division event.

Third, a discussion of the observational selection bias that makes northern cardinals over-represented in the documented gynandromorph literature — not because they experience higher error rates, but because their extreme sexual dimorphism makes gynandromorphs detectably visible in a way that many other species cannot be.

The verdict is True. The cardinal is genuinely half male and half female. And studying how and why that is possible turns out to reveal some of the most interesting unresolved questions in vertebrate developmental biology.
