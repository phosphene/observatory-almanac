## The Claim

In the first week of November 2024, a photograph of Jupiter circulated widely across Reddit, X (formerly Twitter), 9GAG, Threads, and international social media platforms including Russian-language VK and Polish language sites Demotywatory and Obrazkowo. The caption accompanying many shares read: "NASA Just Dropped Some of the Sharpest Images of Jupiter to Date." The image showed Jupiter's Northern Circumpolar Cyclone region in vivid, swirling colours — deep blues, turquoises, and warm ochres braided together in patterns that immediately drew comparisons from commenters to Vincent van Gogh's famous painting *The Starry Night*.

The claim embedded in the circulating caption was twofold: that the image was from NASA, and that it represented genuinely sharp new imagery of the planet. A secondary implication — not always explicit but operative in the sharing context — was that the vivid, almost painterly colours in the image were a faithful representation of what Jupiter actually looks like. Snopes reporter Aleksandra Wrona investigated the image's provenance.

---

## What's Actually True

The image is genuine. It derives from NASA's Juno spacecraft, specifically from the JunoCam imaging instrument on board, and it accurately depicts a region of Jupiter's atmosphere known as the Northern Circumpolar Cyclone arrangement. The photograph's provenance is verifiable: a version of the image was hosted on the official Mission Juno website, where it was described as "Northern Circumpolar Cyclones" and credited to NASA/JPL/SwRI/MSSS/Gerald Eichstädt — the latter being a prolific and widely recognised civilian image processor who works extensively with JunoCam raw data.

There is an important qualification, which Snopes indicated in its verdict context: the colours in the image have been digitally enhanced. This is not unusual or deceptive — it is standard practice in planetary imaging and is, in the JunoCam case, actively encouraged by NASA. A NASA spokesperson confirmed to Snopes that the version available on the Mission Juno website was "a version with more subtle colors made by prolific image processor Gerald Eichstädt," and that the more saturated version circulating on Reddit was either a further enhancement by a different user or an independent processing of the raw data by someone else. "This happens pretty frequently," the spokesperson said. The image is "real, if color enhanced. Not the closest or clearest view ever."

Mission Juno's website makes the citizen science model explicit. The JunoCam data is released publicly in raw form, and the website actively invites the public to process and manipulate the data: "The types of image processing we'd love to see range from simply cropping an image to highlighting a particular atmospheric feature, as well as adding your own color enhancements, creating collages and adding advanced color reconstruction." The site encourages "citizen scientists to explore new ways to process these images to continue to bring out the beauty and mysteries of Jupiter and its moons."

Gerald Eichstädt's processing notes for the "Northern Circumpolar Cyclones" image are technically detailed: the image was reprojected using a preliminary geometric camera model, cleaned of camera artifacts, adjusted for illumination, linearised to radiometric values, white-balanced using linear factors for each colour channel, and displayed with a gamma correction. All of these steps are legitimate image science processes. They also collectively produce colours that differ from the "true colour" representation a human observer would perceive at Jupiter's cloud tops.

## Juno, JunoCam, and the Science of Seeing Jupiter

The epistemic novelty in this case lies not in simple image authentication but in what it reveals about the relationship between scientific instruments, data processing decisions, and public visual culture — and specifically in the unusual open-science model that Juno's JunoCam programme represents.

NASA's Juno spacecraft launched on August 5, 2011, and entered Jupiter's orbit on July 4, 2016. Its mission is to study Jupiter's interior structure, atmosphere, magnetic field, and auroral magnetosphere. The spacecraft follows a highly elliptical polar orbit, bringing it to within approximately 4,200 kilometres of Jupiter's cloud tops at closest approach (perijove) during each orbital pass before swinging back out to distances beyond the Galilean moons (Bolton et al., 2017, *Science*).

JunoCam was not initially conceived as a primary science instrument. It was added to the payload largely for public engagement and education purposes, with the understanding that its data would be freely released to the public for independent processing. In practice, the instrument has produced scientifically valuable imagery, and its open-data model has generated an active global community of civilian image processors who contribute processed products that rival and often exceed the visual quality of in-house NASA imagery.

The raw JunoCam data is released in JPEG format with overlapping filter strips corresponding to red, green, blue, and methane-band channels. Processing this raw data into a full-colour image requires significant work: the four filter strips are captured in rapid sequence as the spacecraft rotates, so the strips correspond to slightly different moments in time and different portions of the detector, requiring geometric correction, co-registration, and colour assembly. Eichstädt's pipeline, which he has documented publicly, involves sophisticated radiometric calibration. His white-balance factors (0.629:1.0:3.65 for R:G:B, as specified in the image description) dramatically shift the natural colour balance toward blue and away from the brown-red that Jupiter's clouds predominantly display in broadband white light.

The scientific value of this processing is real. Enhanced and manipulated colour processing of planetary imagery is a long-established tradition in planetary science, used to reveal chemical and mineralogical differences in surface or atmospheric features that would be invisible or indistinct in unprocessed natural colour. The Voyager images of Jupiter's Great Red Spot, the Cassini colour composites of Saturn's polar hexagon, and the Hubble Space Telescope's false-colour renderings of giant planet cloud features all involve similar choices about colour representation designed to maximise scientific information content. The Juno/JunoCam images that circulate widely on social media are the civilian version of this tradition — a democratised extension of the science of planetary image enhancement into public culture.

Jupiter's Northern Circumpolar Cyclone region depicted in the circulating image is scientifically significant in its own right. Discoveries from the Juno mission revealed that Jupiter's north pole is home to a cluster of large cyclones — a central polar cyclone surrounded by eight additional circumpolar cyclones arranged in a quasi-regular octagonal pattern. The south pole has an analogous arrangement of five cyclones (Adriani et al., 2018, *Nature*). These cyclone clusters were unexpected before Juno's close polar passes; earlier spacecraft, including Pioneer 10 and 11, Voyager 1 and 2, and Galileo, had not imaged Jupiter's poles at sufficient resolution to detect them. Moore et al. (2018, *Nature*) published complementary thermal imaging showing the cyclones' deep atmospheric structure. The polar cyclone arrangement has remained stable across multiple Juno orbits, which raises as-yet unresolved questions about the dynamical mechanisms maintaining hexagonal and polygonal cyclone symmetry.

The broader question of what "colour" Jupiter "really" is makes the image's reception epistemically instructive. Jupiter's visible appearance depends on observing wavelength, illumination geometry, phase angle, and atmospheric conditions that vary with weather and time. Human eyes at Jupiter would perceive a predominantly tan-beige, cream, and red-brown planet with strong zonal banding. The vivid blues and saturated golds in the circulating JunoCam image are a processing choice, not a perceptual error — they highlight atmospheric structure that is genuinely present, using colour as an encoding choice to convey compositional and dynamic information. When a social media caption describes this as what "Jupiter looks like," it is technically imprecise but not fabricated.

## Why People Believe This

The viral reception of the image — and the "Van Gogh planet" commentary that accompanied it — reveals several intersecting dynamics in how extraordinary planetary imagery enters public consciousness.

**The authority of NASA's name.** The attachment of "NASA" to a striking image functions as a credibility shorthand that dramatically reduces resistance to sharing. The combination of institutional legitimacy with extreme visual spectacle — a giant planet rendered in near-painterly colour — creates a sharing incentive that bypasses evaluative pause. The caption's claim that these were "the sharpest images of Jupiter to date" added a news-value dimension (novelty, superlative) that amplified the incentive further.

**Van Gogh comparisons as cultural amplifier.** The immediate and widespread comparison to *The Starry Night* is not accidental or superficial. The swirling blue-gold patterns in the image genuinely resemble the nocturne's turbulent, atmospheric brushwork. Recent computational research has linked van Gogh's interest in depicting atmospheric turbulence to real fluid dynamics (Aragón et al., 2006, *Physical Review E*, established that the brushstrokes in *The Starry Night* followed Kolmogorov turbulence statistics). The resonance between the image and the painting is therefore scientifically non-trivial, as well as aesthetically potent. Cultural amplification of this kind — a scientific image achieving virality through association with a canonical artwork — is a recurring pattern in space science communication, and JunoCam's open processing model is partly designed to produce exactly this type of aesthetic-scientific crossover product.

**Uncertainty about NASA's image-production conventions.** General audiences have limited exposure to the fact that virtually all professional planetary imagery involves significant post-processing. The assumption that photographs from spacecraft are simply records of "what the camera saw" is widespread and technically inaccurate — every spacecraft image involves calibration, radiometric correction, colour channel combination, and often explicit enhancement choices made for scientific and communicative purposes. In this sense, the JunoCam images circulating on social media are not doing anything different from the practice of all planetary imaging; the open-data model just makes the process unusually visible and participatory.

**The citizen science confusion.** The fact that the specific version of the image circulating on Reddit was processed by a civilian rather than a NASA scientist — or was possibly further enhanced from Eichstädt's already-processed version by an unknown user — introduces a provenance chain more complex than "NASA took a photo." The legitimate answer to "is this a NASA image?" is: "it derives from NASA data, but the version you're looking at was processed by civilian scientists using tools and conventions that NASA provides and endorses." This is accurate and not at all damning, but it is more complicated than the simple yes/no the original claim seemed to invite.

## Citizen Science, Open Data, and the Politics of Space Imagery

The JunoCam open-data model is unusual in NASA's portfolio and worth examining on its own terms. Most NASA flagship mission data is released to the public eventually but processed initially by science teams with access to proprietary calibration data and instruments. JunoCam's data release protocol is designed to be immediate and accessible, with the explicit goal of enabling public participation in image processing.

This model has proven effective at generating public engagement: the Mission Juno website attracts hundreds of independent image processors who upload their work, providing both an archive of processed products and a community of technically skilled civilian scientists who effectively extend NASA's processing capacity for no cost. The processed images generated by this community have been used in scientific presentations, published in accessible science media, and — as the current case illustrates — circulate globally as representations of what NASA's spacecraft "sees."

The political and epistemic question this raises is interesting: when a publicly processed version of a NASA dataset circulates as "a NASA image," and NASA confirms that the underlying data is genuine, is the colour-enhanced product accurately characterised? The answer depends on what claims are made. "This image shows Jupiter's atmosphere as captured by JunoCam" is accurate. "This image shows what Jupiter looks like to human eyes" is not. Most viral sharing of the image made implicit rather than explicit claims about authentic colour representation, and most viewers did not encounter a distinction between authentic data and processed product.

The science communication implication is that open data models, while enormously valuable, create new forms of provenance complexity that require more nuanced media literacy infrastructure than currently exists in most social media contexts.

---

## Verdict

**True, with context.** The photograph of Jupiter circulating in November 2024 derives from genuine NASA data captured by the Juno spacecraft's JunoCam instrument. It shows a real region of Jupiter's atmosphere — the Northern Circumpolar Cyclone region — and was processed by Gerald Eichstädt, a prolific civilian image processor who works with JunoCam data under a model explicitly endorsed and encouraged by NASA. The version circulating on Reddit may have been further colour-enhanced from Eichstädt's already-enhanced original; a NASA spokesperson confirmed this is common and that the image is "real, if color enhanced."

The colours in the image are not naturalistic representations of Jupiter's visible colour. They are processing choices designed to highlight atmospheric structure, consistent with long-standing conventions in planetary imaging. The image's Van Gogh resonance is aesthetically apt and scientifically non-trivial. What the image reveals about JunoCam's open-science model, and about the fluid boundary between scientific and civilian data processing, is more interesting than the simple question of whether the photograph is "from NASA."
