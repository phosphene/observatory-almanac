## The Claim

Beginning in 2021 and continuing through a renewed surge of sharing in early 2024, a photograph circulated widely on social media platforms including Twitter/X, Instagram, and Facebook. The image showed a hunter — specifically a man identified through archival research as Rick Paillet — kneeling behind or beside a dead bear and holding up one of the animal's front paws. The paw, in the image, appears enormous: it appears roughly as large as the man's entire head and significantly larger than both his hands together. Captions accompanying the photograph on various platforms claimed that the image accurately showed "the size of a Grizzly Bear paw."

The implied claim is that grizzly bear paws are this large relative to an adult human — large enough to span a human head, to dwarf the human hand by a factor of three or four to one. This claim generated the widespread engagement that the photograph consistently attracted: the combination of a seeming anatomical impossibility (a paw this big?) with a stated factual grounding (no, grizzly paws really are this big) is a compelling package. The tension between incredulity and the apparent factual authority of a documentary photograph drives repeated sharing.

Snopes fact-checker Madison Dapcevich investigated the claim in March 2024, consulting wildlife experts and tracing the photograph's origins to a 2021 Facebook post from Paillet's hunting network. Snopes rated the claim "Miscaptioned" — meaning the photograph is genuine but the characterisation of what it shows is inaccurate or misleading.

## What's Actually True

The photograph of Rick Paillet and the bear paw is genuine. A reverse-image search conducted by Snopes using Google Lens returned the original posting: a Facebook update from October 19, 2021, from Lucky Duck Decoys, referencing Paillet's Alaskan hunting trip, along with a contemporaneous post on Paillet's own Facebook page dated October 16, 2021, captioned with a claim that he had "killed the giant bear." The photograph's provenance is clear and there is no evidence of digital manipulation of the image itself. The bear existed. The hunt occurred. The photograph was taken.

What the photograph does not accurately represent is the proportional size relationship between the bear paw and the human holding it. The apparent size disproportion is the result of forced perspective — a well-documented photography technique, familiar to anyone who has tried to photograph a fish on a fishing trip, that makes objects closer to the camera appear disproportionately large relative to objects farther away.

Riley Woodford of the Alaska Department of Fish and Game — who also serves as photo editor for the agency's image library, and who therefore has both biological expertise and direct professional experience with wildlife photography — reviewed the photograph for Snopes. Woodford identified several features of the image geometry that indicate forced perspective: "He is kneeling back behind the bear, the hunting guide/photographer is using a low camera angle, and in the 'paw picture' the hunter is holding the paw out at arms-length, thrusting it at the camera." Woodford noted that the hunter's arm is visibly extended in the photograph, with the elbow barely bent — the classic posture of someone presenting an object as close to the camera as possible. Woodford confirmed his view that the animal is genuinely large but that "they are exaggerating the size."

The biology is clarifying here. Kodiak bears (*Ursus arctos middendorffi*), found only on Alaska's Kodiak Archipelago and the largest bears in the world, can weigh up to 1,500 pounds and stand more than ten feet tall on their hind legs (Alaska Department of Fish and Game, accessed 22 Feb. 2024, adfg.alaska.gov). Standard brown bears (*Ursus arctos*) generally weigh between 500 and 900 pounds, though the National Park Service notes weights of up to 1,400 pounds are not unheard of in autumn. On all fours, brown bears stand between three and five feet at the shoulder.

Paw dimensions for brown bears are genuine and can be substantial, but they fall well short of the proportions implied by the photograph. According to iNaturalist's guide for the species — compiled from the naturalist and citizen scientist community's observations — all four feet of average-sized brown bears tend to be between seven and eight inches wide (iNaturalist, accessed 22 Feb. 2024, inaturalist.org/guide_taxa/889098). Large male coastal or Kodiak males may have hind feet measuring up to sixteen inches in length and eleven inches in width. Grizzly bear forepaws — the part of the bear shown in the photograph — carry large curved claws that can add several inches to the apparent reach of the paw, which likely contributes to the visual impression the photograph creates.

An eleven-inch-wide paw is genuinely impressive. But it is not the width of an adult human head. The average adult human head is roughly seven inches wide and nine inches from front to back. An eleven-inch paw can comfortably cup a human head, but it does not dwarf it by the margin the photograph appears to show. The scale inflation produced by the forced perspective is substantial.

The species of the specific bear Paillet killed was not confirmed by Snopes. The photograph originated in Alaska, where both grizzly bears and Kodiak bears (a recognised subspecies) are present in different regions. Woodford noted that grizzly bears and brown bears are "largely considered to be the same species (scientific name *Ursus arctos*)" while grizzly bears are classified as a separate subspecies (*U. a. horribilis*) by the National Park Service due to morphological differences. Without knowing the specific region in which Paillet hunted, and without measurement data for the bear itself, the species question cannot be definitively resolved. The Snopes rating acknowledges this uncertainty.

## Why People Believe This

The bear paw photograph's capacity to generate repeated viral sharing across several years is worth examining as a case study in the specific conditions under which visual evidence misleads.

**The authentic document as cover for the misleading caption.** The photograph is real. The bear is real. The paw is real. None of these facts are in dispute. When people share this image with captions claiming it "accurately depicts" the size of a grizzly paw, the authenticity of the photograph lends epistemic authority to a false or misleading claim that is not itself directly visible in the image. Viewers who check whether the photograph has been manipulated (and find that it hasn't) may conclude that the accompanying factual claim is therefore also accurate. But the photograph's authenticity and the caption's accuracy are independent questions. A genuine, unmanipulated photograph can still be captioned in a way that draws a false inference from what it shows.

**The epistemically novel problem of perspectival ambiguity.** Most viewers who encounter viral images intuitively perform some kind of authenticity check. They look for signs of digital manipulation — implausible edges, inconsistent lighting, obvious compositing errors. Forced perspective images contain none of these artifacts because they are not manipulated at all. The "trick" is entirely in the shooting geometry, and the resulting image is indistinguishable from an image that genuinely shows those proportions. Viewers who know how to detect Photoshop manipulation have no tool for detecting forced perspective from a single image, because forced perspective produces the same image that would exist if the proportions were as depicted.

This is the specific epistemic novelty of forced perspective as a source of visual misinformation: it defeats conventional authenticity-checking. The viewer cannot inspect the image for signs of fabrication because there are no such signs. The only approach that works is expert assessment of the geometry — which requires either a second photograph from a different angle (revealing the setup) or the kind of expert domain knowledge Woodford brought to his evaluation.

**The cultural script of "extraordinary hunter" imagery.** The photograph participates in a well-established genre of hunting documentation photography in which the hunter poses with prey in ways designed to communicate the magnitude of the achievement. Trophy photographs have their own conventions, and one of those conventions is exaggerating the apparent size of the animal — both through genuine selection of large animals and through standard photographic techniques that make animals look larger. Fish-with-fishing-rod photographs, deer-with-hunter photographs, and bear-with-hunter photographs all routinely employ some degree of perspective manipulation as part of the genre's conventions. Consumers of hunting photography have learned to expect this, but consumers who encounter the photograph without that genre knowledge may not apply the appropriate discount.

**The genuine impressiveness of bears.** One complicating factor in assessing the bear paw photograph is that grizzly and brown bears are genuinely remarkable animals with genuinely large paws. An accurate unmanipulated photograph of a large Kodiak bear paw alongside a human hand would itself be striking. The forced perspective in the Paillet photograph takes something genuinely impressive and inflates it — but the inflation is in degree, not in kind. Viewers who accept the photograph as an accurate scale representation are not wildly wrong about the qualitative point that bear paws are large. They are mistaken about the quantitative degree of size difference between a bear paw and a human hand. This "wrong in degree but right in direction" structure means the belief is harder to fully correct than beliefs that are wrong in both direction and degree.

**Social media platform dynamics.** The 2024 resurgence of this image demonstrates a feature of social media virality that applies to an entire category of compelling visual content: photographs that generate strong engagement can be dormant for years and then be rediscovered and reshared by new audiences who have not previously encountered the image or its prior corrections. The fact-checks and corrections that were produced in 2021, when the image first circulated widely, were not visible to 2024 audiences who encountered the unaccompanied image without prior context. Each new wave of virality is, from the perspective of new audiences, an original encounter.

## Verdict

**The claim that the photograph accurately depicts the size of a grizzly bear paw is miscaptioned — the photograph is genuine but the implied proportions are inflated by forced perspective.**

The Snopes rating of "Miscaptioned" is appropriate. The photograph is authentic and has been traced to a specific hunter, a specific Alaskan hunting trip, and a specific social media posting in October 2021. The photograph was not digitally manipulated. But Riley Woodford of the Alaska Department of Fish and Game, reviewing the image geometry, identified clear markers of forced perspective — extended arm, low camera angle, photographer positioned to maximise the apparent paw size. The real dimensions of brown and Kodiak bear paws, while genuinely impressive, do not approach the proportions the photograph appears to show.

Bear paws are large. Kodiak males can have hind foot widths of up to eleven inches. But the average adult human head is approximately seven to nine inches in its horizontal dimensions, and a proportionally accurate photograph of an eleven-inch paw beside a human head would show something impressive but less dramatic than the Paillet image implies.

The important secondary point — confirmed by Woodford — is that grizzly bears and brown bears are the same species (*Ursus arctos*), with grizzlies classified as subspecies *U. a. horribilis* due to some morphological divergence. The Kodiak bear (*U. a. middendorffi*) is a distinct subspecies as well, isolated on the Kodiak Archipelago for at least 40,000 years, and is the largest bear in the world. Without confirmed species and region data for Paillet's bear, the full characterisation of its size cannot be independently verified.

## The Wider Picture

The bear paw photograph belongs to a substantial genre of wildlife and nature photography that generates viral engagement precisely because nature is genuinely astonishing — and because photography conventions within that genre have evolved to maximise the apparent impressiveness of what they document.

**Forced perspective in wildlife and nature photography.** The technique deployed in the Paillet photograph is neither new nor exceptional. Fish-with-angler photographs have used forced perspective for as long as fishing photography has existed. Mushroom photographs, big-game trophy photographs, and nature photographs showing extreme size contrasts routinely employ camera angle and subject positioning to maximise apparent scale. Snopes maintains a dedicated collection of forced perspective examples (Wrona, Aleksandra. "Examples of a Deceptive Photography Trick: Forced Perspective." *Snopes*, 22 May 2023) that documents the breadth of this practice across contexts ranging from political photography (the Snopes fact-check on a Biden-Carter photograph showing apparent extreme size disproportion, also cited in the bear paw article) to nature documentation.

**The morphology of *Ursus arctos*.** The bear species question raised by the photograph is scientifically interesting in its own right. The *Ursus arctos* complex — brown bears, grizzlies, Kodiak bears, and related subspecies — represents one of the more complex taxonomic cases within large carnivore biology. The classification of grizzlies as a distinct subspecies from other North American brown bears reflects genuine morphological and behavioral differences, but those differences exist on a continuum rather than as a clear genetic or phenotypic boundary. Woodford's observation to Snopes — "Evolution happens gradually and when looking at a range of subspecies, it's a spectrum, not a well-drawn line" — accurately encapsulates the current scientific understanding. The Kodiak bear's 40,000-year isolation on the Kodiak Archipelago has produced sufficient genetic differentiation to warrant subspecies status, with distinctive size advantages plausibly linked to the abundant marine food sources available in the archipelago's salmon-rich ecosystem.

**Human-bear scale perception as a conservation factor.** There is a sociological dimension to how humans perceive bear size that goes beyond the question of whether a specific photograph is accurately captioned. Perceptions of bear size influence attitudes toward bear conservation, hunting regulation debates, and human-wildlife conflict management. Bears that appear extremely large in viral photographs may be perceived as more dangerous than they actually are, or — in hunting contexts — may be valorised in ways that encourage the targeting of the largest individuals, which can have population-level effects. The Snopes article does not pursue this dimension, but it is relevant background to why wildlife biologists and wildlife management agencies take an interest in the accuracy of scale representations in public wildlife photography.

**Photography as evidence and the visual skills required to interrogate it.** The bear paw photograph case illustrates a general point about visual epistemics in the social media era: photographs that are not digitally manipulated can nonetheless convey false impressions. The standard debunking toolkit — reverse image search, metadata inspection, manipulation artifact detection — is necessary but not sufficient for evaluating viral visual claims. Photography geometry, the conventions of specific genres, the domain knowledge required to assess what "normal" looks like in a given context — these are additional competencies that readers need, and that formal media literacy training rarely provides.

Woodford's ability to identify the forced perspective from the photograph alone depended on his expertise in both wildlife biology and wildlife photography. He knew what an authentic bear-paw-beside-a-human-hand photograph would look like if the proportions were as dramatised, and he could therefore identify the geometric features that indicated the proportions were being exaggerated. This expertise is not widely distributed. For most viewers, the image is simply a photograph of a big paw. Building public capacity to read images at this level of geometric sophistication is a significant challenge for media literacy education.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes treatment of the bear paw photograph is a model for the "Miscaptioned" rating category — a category that handles a specific and important type of misinformation: genuine content given a false or misleading frame.

**The "Miscaptioned" rating.** This rating category is one of Snopes's more analytically useful distinctions. It separates the question of whether a piece of content (an image, a video, a document) is genuine from the question of whether the claims made about it are accurate. Both questions matter, and they can have different answers. In the bear paw case, the image is genuine (not manipulated, traceable to a real event), but the accompanying claim (paw size accurately depicted) is false or misleading. The "Miscaptioned" rating captures this distinction in a way that "False" alone would not.

**Expert consultation as methodology.** The Snopes approach in this article — consulting both a wildlife biologist (Woodford) with photographic expertise and tracking down the photograph's origin through reverse image search — represents good practice for this category of claim. Establishing provenance (was the photograph taken when and where claimed?) and soliciting expert analysis of what the photograph shows are distinct investigative steps, both necessary.

**The use of biological data.** The article's inclusion of iNaturalist data on brown bear paw dimensions and NPS data on bear height and weight provides the calibrating context necessary to assess the scale disproportion. Without knowing what bear paws actually measure, the claim that the photograph exaggerates their size is hard to assess quantity. The biological data gives readers a way to independently evaluate the verdict by comparing stated paw dimensions against apparent proportions in the photograph.

**What the article cannot establish.** Dapcevich's note that Snopes contacted Paillet for further details and did not receive a response is a standard transparency practice: the absence of response from the subject is documented rather than concealed. The article appropriately hedges on species and region — it cannot confirm whether the bear was a grizzly, a coastal brown bear, or a Kodiak bear without information Paillet did not provide. This epistemic honesty about what cannot be confirmed is appropriate and models the kind of careful claim-scoping that good fact-checking requires.
