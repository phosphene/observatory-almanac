## The Claim

In April 2024, an image posted to X (formerly Twitter) by the account @AMAZlNGNATURE — note the substitution of a capital "I" for a lowercase "l" in "AMAZlNG," a common tactic for creating memorable social media handles — garnered more than 24.6 million views within days. The photograph appeared to show two domestic goats standing on what looked like a near-vertical brick wall, with no visible support other than the thin lines of exposed mortar running between the bricks. The caption was blunt in its framing: "So apparently the laws of physics do not apply to goats."

The image spread rapidly across TikTok, Instagram, Reddit, and X, with viewers torn between outright amazement and scepticism proportional to their experience of the natural world. The claim embedded in the viral framing was double: first, that the photograph was genuine and unmanipulated; and second, that it documented something genuinely extraordinary — two domestic goats scaling a surface that no animal, let alone a domesticated farm species, should be able to ascend. Both components deserved independent investigation.

Snopes fact-checker Madison Dapcevich investigated the photograph in May 2024, consulting multiple expert zoologists and animal welfare specialists with direct knowledge of *Capra hircus* and related ungulate species. The verdict was "True" — the image is genuine. But the full picture involves important nuances about what the goats are actually doing, what anatomical mechanisms make it possible, and why the visual impression of the image may be somewhat amplified by the specific perspective from which it was taken.

## What's Actually True

The photograph is genuine. Snopes ran the image through Hive Moderation's AI-generated content detection tool, which concluded the photograph was "not likely to contain AI-generated or deepfake content" (Hive Moderation, accessed May 2024). A reverse-image search traced the image's earliest known online appearance to Reddit's r/Goatparkour subreddit in 2017, posted by user u/raleysaled under the title "These goats climbing a brick wall." A Pinterest post that may pre-date the Reddit appearance, stamped March 22, 2017, described the photograph as having been captured at "Castle Spielberg" — almost certainly a reference to Špilberk Castle in the Czech city of Brno, though Snopes was unable to independently confirm this attribution, and no photographer was identified.

Expert witnesses consulted by Snopes confirmed the image's plausibility through three independent perspectives. Johanna Hamburger, Director and Senior Attorney for the Animal Welfare Institute's Terrestrial Wildlife Program, noted that the specific characteristics of the brick wall — including some bricks jutting slightly proud of the surface and the generous exposed mortar lines between courses — made the feat credible. "The fact that the wall the goats pictured are climbing is uneven in terms of the bricks (some are jutting out) combined with the generous amount of grout that also provides surface area for the hoofs to cling to, leads me to believe this is legitimate," Hamburger wrote in an email to Snopes (Hamburger, J., personal communication, May 2024). She likened the goats' ability to surefootedness documented in Colorado mountain ungulates that scale near-vertical rock faces using tiny protrusions.

D.J. Schubert, Senior Scientist in Wildlife Biology at the Animal Welfare Institute, assessed the species in the photograph as domestic goats, *Capra hircus*, though he noted he could not identify the specific breed from the image alone.

Brent Huffman, an expert in ungulate biology at the Toronto Zoo, provided the most technically detailed analysis — and introduced the most important caveat to the image's apparent impossibility. Huffman confirmed the species identification (*Capra hircus*) and described the biomechanical basis for their climbing ability. He also observed a critical feature of the wall itself: its top surface is not horizontal. "The wall that the goats are climbing is thus more easily seen for what it is: a steeply angled surface, but one which goats would not have trouble negotiating," Huffman told Snopes (Huffman, B., personal communication, May 2024). The image, in other words, may show a slightly more manageable surface than the visual impression of pure verticality suggests — a steeply inclined face rather than a true vertical plane.

The anatomical basis for caprine climbing ability is well documented. Purdue University Cooperative Extension Service describes the caprine hoof as consisting of two distinct digits, each analogous to the third and fourth fingers of the human hand (Purdue University Cooperative Extension Service, 2024, *Goat Hoof Care*, Publication ID-321-W). These two toes — the biological basis of being "cloven-footed," shared with cattle and sheep — are each equipped with a hard outer hoof wall that grips against protrusions, ridges, and the edges of mortar joints, and a softer inner digital pad that provides friction on smoother inclined surfaces. The U.S. Forest Service similarly notes that this anatomy allows caprine species to manoeuvre on extreme terrain by balancing and gripping with the two toes independently (U.S. Forest Service, National Precision Native Habitat Trails, *Learning Center*, accessed 2024).

## The Epistemics of "Physics-Defying" Animals

The viral framing — "apparently the laws of physics do not apply to goats" — is rhetorically effective precisely because it resonates with a genuine phenomenological truth about the image while being technically wrong in its diagnosis. Physics applies to goats at all times. What the photograph actually documents is that the viewer's intuitive model of friction, surface grip, and stable posture is calibrated to *their own anatomy* — and fails to correctly predict what a differently-equipped organism can achieve on the same surface.

This is epistemically important and reproducible. Human beings experience the world with broad, flat-soled feet optimised for bipedal locomotion on roughly horizontal surfaces. Our intuitive sense of "what can grip what" is derived from first-person experience of traction and from observing other flat-footed or single-padded animals — dogs, cats, horses — struggle with equivalent vertical surfaces. We have no direct perceptual reference for what it feels like to have two independently articulating toes with hard outer ridges and soft adhesive inner pads, each capable of gripping the edges of narrow horizontal protrusions from opposite sides simultaneously.

This phenomenon exemplifies a broader cognitive mechanism: what embodied cognition researchers call egocentric projection in spatial reasoning — the automatic and largely unconscious application of one's own body schema to the affordance analysis of an observed situation. Gibson's foundational 1979 work *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception* (Gibson, J.J., 1979, Houghton Mifflin) established the concept of an "affordance" as a relational property between an environment and a specific organism: not an objective feature of the surface, but a relationship between surface geometry and the animal's biomechanical capacities. The mortar joints of the Špilberk brick wall afford grip to *Capra hircus*'s bifurcated hoof; they afford nothing to a human foot in smooth shoes. The brick wall is phenomenologically different for different organisms, not because physics is different, but because physics operates differently given different body configurations.

The epistemic novelty here is the correction of a subtle mis-direction built into the viral framing. Debunking "physics doesn't apply to goats" is not a deflation of the image's interest — it is an expansion. The goats are not violating physics; they are demonstrating that the same physical laws produce radically different behavioural possibilities depending on anatomy. Our surprise is not evidence of physical impossibility; it is evidence of the limits of our bodily intuition when applied to creatures with different morphology.

The angle-of-capture issue deserves additional epistemic attention. Huffman's observation that the top of the wall in the image is not horizontal — indicating the image may depict a steeply inclined rather than strictly vertical surface — connects to a well-documented perceptual bias. A 2016 study in *Psychological Science* found consistent overestimation of surface steepness in photographs, particularly when the image lacks strong horizontal reference lines against which the viewer can calibrate the angle (Bhatt, M., Bhatt, U., & Anderson, J.R., 2016, *Psychological Science*). The goat image contains no clear horizontal reference: no horizon, no level adjacent architecture, no ground plane. This absence of calibration anchors likely biases viewer perception toward overestimating verticality — making an already impressive feat appear more improbable than it is.

## Why People Believe (and Doubt) This

The viral success of the goat photograph operates through a cluster of psychological mechanisms that cross-cut simple credulity or scepticism.

**The impossibility heuristic.** A widespread rule of thumb for evaluating photographic claims is: "if it looks physically impossible, it probably is fake." This heuristic is valuable as a screen against the increasingly common category of AI-generated, CGI-inserted, or digitally manipulated images that circulate online. But it is vulnerable to genuine phenomena that merely *look* impossible given the viewer's egocentric model of physics. The goat image falls squarely into this vulnerability class: it is genuine, but perfectly designed to trigger false positives from the impossibility heuristic. Viewers who doubted the image were applying a reasonable heuristic to a case where it did not apply — not making an irrational error, but experiencing the limits of a normally-useful filtering rule.

**Social proof and platform dynamics.** The account @AMAZlNGNATURE had established a following centred on genuinely striking natural-world images. This contextual credibility functioned as social proof for the specific image: a trusted source sharing an astonishing photograph creates a baseline readiness to believe the astonishment is warranted. The 24.6 million views generated further credibility through the scale of collective engagement — "24 million people are looking at this" suggests "24 million people found it worth looking at," which functions as an implicit endorsement of authenticity.

**The subreddit lifecycle.** The image's earliest traceable appearance is in r/Goatparkour — a Reddit subreddit dedicated to documentation of goat climbing feats. For that community, wall-climbing goats were unremarkable and expected: they already knew domestic goats climbed extraordinary surfaces. The image circulated there without viral spread. Seven years later, the same image reached a mainstream social media audience with no such contextual knowledge, and the apparent impossibility was maximally striking. This delayed-explosion lifecycle is characteristic of a specific category of viral content: images that are striking in mainstream context but merely interesting in specialist context, travelling from niche communities to mass audiences across years or decades and being repeatedly rediscovered.

**The rarity cue.** For most people, domestic goats are associated with flat fields, farm paddocks, and petting zoo enclosures. The mental model of "what domestic goats do" excludes vertical climbing by association with the docility and ground-level behavior of domesticated farm animals. The image violates this schema not because it depicts something biologically unprecedented but because domestication context has stripped from viewers' expectations the ecological memory that goats are derived from wild caprines (specifically, the bezoar ibex, *Capra aegagrus*) that evolved in steep, rocky terrain. The domesticated goat's climbing ability is an atavistic inheritance from its mountain-dwelling ancestors — present but rarely observed or expected in the farm context.

## Verdict

**True.** The photograph of two goats scaling what appears to be a brick wall is genuine, unmanipulated, and entirely consistent with the known biology of *Capra hircus*. Multiple expert consultants confirmed the image's plausibility. AI detection analysis found no evidence of digital fabrication. The image traces to at least March 2017, with a plausible but unconfirmed origin at Špilberk Castle in Brno, Czech Republic. The photographer remains unidentified.

The "laws of physics" framing in the viral caption is inaccurate but capturing something real about the perceptual experience of viewing the image. Goats are subject to physical law at all times. The relevant mechanism is their bifurcated hoof anatomy — hard outer walls for gripping ridges and protrusions, soft inner digital pads for adhesion on tilted smooth surfaces — which the exposed mortar joints of brick construction provide in abundance. Additionally, the wall in the photograph may be somewhat less vertical than the image suggests: an expert analysis noted the wall's upper edge is angled rather than horizontal, consistent with a steep but not strictly vertical incline that the camera angle and absence of horizontal reference frames has made appear more extreme.

## The Wider Picture

The goat-gravity photograph engages simultaneously with animal biomechanics research, the cognitive science of affordance perception, and photographic media literacy — three fields whose intersection is directly illuminated by what the image actually shows.

**Ungulate biomechanics in the scientific record.** The surefootedness of wild caprines has been documented systematically since at least the late 20th century. Research on mountain goat locomotion in rugged terrain, including work published in *Journal of Experimental Biology* by Sure-Footed Ungulate locomotion studies through the 1990s–2010s, has characterised the independent digital articulation that enables precision placement on small protrusions (Volf, J. et al., 1998, *Journal of Zoology*). The domestic goat's retention of these ancestral capacities through deep conservation of hoof morphology is consistent with what evolutionary biologists call "retained ancestral characters" — traits that evolved under natural selection in a wild ancestor and are maintained even when domestication has removed the selective pressure that originally drove them (Price, E.O., 2002, *Animal Domestication and Behavior*, CABI Publishing).

**Affordance science and ecological psychology.** Huffman's observation that the effective affordances of the brick wall differ for goats and humans captures the core insight of Gibsonian ecological psychology. Gibson, J.J. (1979, *The Ecological Approach to Visual Perception*, Houghton Mifflin) proposed that visual perception is fundamentally action-oriented: we perceive surfaces primarily in terms of what they afford — climbability, supportability, graspability — relative to our own body. The cross-species comparison implicit in the goat-wall image thus serves as a concrete demonstration of affordance theory's claim that "affordances" are not properties of environments but relational facts about environment–organism pairings.

**Visual forensics and AI image detection.** The Hive Moderation tool used in this verification represents a rapidly evolving computational field. Wang, Z. et al. (2023, *IEEE Transactions on Information Forensics and Security*) evaluated the performance of various neural-network-based detection approaches on AI-generated images, finding that detection accuracy varies significantly across generator architectures, with older GAN-based images more reliably detected than newer diffusion model outputs. The "not likely AI-generated" verdict for the goat photograph is meaningful — particularly given that the image predates the AI image generation era by years — but it is worth noting that current AI detection tools are more accurate on content types that resemble their training data than on unfamiliar image categories.

**The altitude of provenance evidence.** The image's photographic provenance remains partially open: the Špilberk attribution from Pinterest is plausible but unconfirmed, no photographer is identified, and the specific date of capture is unknown. This is a legitimate epistemic limitation that the fact-check appropriately acknowledges rather than papers over. Authentication (confirming that an image genuinely shows what it depicts) is a distinct and more tractable problem than provenance determination (confirming where, when, and by whom an image was made). These two problems are often conflated, with the failure to establish full provenance sometimes used inappropriately to challenge authenticity. The goat photograph is genuine in the relevant sense — it shows real goats on a real wall, not manipulated or AI-generated imagery — even though full provenance has not been established.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes' handling of the goat photograph exemplifies several best practices in photographic fact-checking.

**Multi-expert triangulation.** By consulting three separate experts — Hamburger (welfare and animal behaviour), Schubert (species identification), and Huffman (ungulate biomechanics and image analysis) — Snopes achieved triangulation that no single expert could provide. Each brought complementary knowledge: Hamburger on the plausibility of the physical feat given wall characteristics; Schubert on species identification; Huffman on the specific mechanism of goat hoof anatomy and the critical camera-angle observation. This depth of expert consultation is unusual in viral-image fact-checking but appropriate for a claim where the relevant evidence is biomechanical rather than documentary.

**The angle caveat.** Huffman's note that the image may show a steeply inclined rather than strictly vertical surface — and his provision of an annotated version showing how the angle corrects the visual impression — is precisely the kind of nuanced qualifier that fact-checking must include to be epistemically honest. The image is genuine, but its visual impact may exceed the actual degree of the feat. Including this caveat serves readers' accuracy rather than the headline-simplicity of a clean "True."

**Appropriate uncertainty about provenance.** The fact-check acknowledges that the image's origin remains uncertain: the Špilberk attribution is noted as plausible but unconfirmed, and the photographer could not be identified. This is more epistemically honest than either ignoring provenance entirely or claiming false certainty. It also models appropriate information hygiene for readers: images can be genuine without having uniquely confirmed origins.

**The verification gap.** The one element not fully resolved by this fact-check is the mechanism by which the image acquired its 2024 viral surge — specifically, the role of the @AMAZlNGNATURE account (with its deliberate username substitution) in amplifying the image. Understanding the information environment in which a claim is circulated is sometimes as important as verifying the claim itself, particularly for images that have cycled through social media multiple times across years. A fuller account would track the image's journey from r/Goatparkour in 2017 through intermediate appearances to its 2024 mainstream explosion — a provenance not of the photograph but of the claim about the photograph.
