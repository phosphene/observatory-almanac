## The Claim

Beginning no later than February 2, 2024, when it appeared on a Russian-language Facebook page named POFU - Good Humor (a post since deleted), an image began circulating online that depicted a young boy standing in a pastoral setting — a meadow or light woodland — holding a framed drawing that appeared to show a self-portrait. The accompanying caption, which persisted across multiple re-shares in various languages, read: "The boy drew this beautiful picture, at first he was very upset, he thought it was ugly."

By the end of 2024, the image had been shared across a very large number of Facebook pages and accounts — Snopes journalist Jordan Liles performed a reverse-image search revealing "numerous posts, primarily on Facebook" — and had also appeared on Threads and X. At least two distinct versions of the AI-generated image were in circulation: one showing the particular boy described above, and a second version showing a different-looking boy also holding a self-portrait, sharing the same caption. On November 18, 2024, a Facebook page named "I Love Pets" posted the image with the full caption. Comment sections on multiple posts showed hundreds of responses from users who believed the image was authentic and responded to the implied emotional narrative: "So excellent and beautiful. You are a very gifted young man." "I think this picture is amazing. This young boy is very talented."

A Snopes reader emailed on December 12, 2024, specifically requesting a review of the image's authenticity and noting their suspicion that the image was AI-generated. The picture is entirely fabricated. Both the boy in the photograph and the artwork he appears to hold are artificial intelligence creations.

## What's Actually True

There is no authentic element to this image. It is entirely fabricated using AI image generation tools. The source of the fabrication is probably a Russian-language Facebook page, with the earliest identified version appearing in February 2024, after which the image spread through social media as an emotionally compelling viral artefact.

**The visual evidence of fabrication.** Several identifiable visual artefacts mark the image as AI-generated:

*Irregular frame geometry.* The bottom edge of the drawing the boy is holding terminates prematurely and does not form a straight line comparable to the clearly defined left, top, and right sides of the illustrated frame. This is a characteristic failure mode of AI image generators when asked to produce objects with rigid geometric edges within organic compositional contexts: the network produces plausible texture and detail for much of the object but fails to enforce geometric consistency at every boundary, resulting in irregular, non-linear edges where sharp corners should be.

*Plant compositing error.* Plants in the image appear simultaneously in front of and behind the drawing being held by the boy. This spatial impossibility — an object appearing both in the foreground layer and the background layer relative to a third object — reflects the known failure of diffusion-based image generators to maintain coherent depth-layer relationships when compositing multiple distinct subject elements (the boy, the drawing, the natural background). The error is subtle enough to be missed in casual viewing but apparent on close inspection, and it is a structural artefact rather than a perceptual ambiguity.

*Ear anomaly.* A small white artefact is visible on the boy's right ear. This type of point anomaly — a region of texture or colour that does not correspond to any anatomically or compositionally motivated element — is familiar to researchers studying AI image failure modes and represents a localised synthesis error where the model's generated values do not correspond to any coherent spatial feature of a real photograph.

*Overly smooth skin texture.* Users with experience viewing large quantities of AI-generated imagery often notice what Snopes described as an "overly-smooth appearance" in the subject's skin and overall image texture — a characteristic of current diffusion model synthesis in which high-frequency texture detail is statistically averaged in ways that differ from genuine photographic noise and natural skin variation.

**Multiple versions.** The existence of a second AI-generated image with a different-looking boy carrying the same caption indicates the underlying content pattern is intentional — a prompt or workflow was used to produce multiple image variations for the same emotional narrative, suggesting deliberate content production rather than a single spontaneous creation.

## Why People Believe This

The image was effective as engagement bait — generating comments, likes, and shares at scale — because it was constructed to exploit well-documented psychological mechanisms in social media engagement behaviour.

**Emotional manipulation via perceived childhood vulnerability.** The narrative encoded in the caption deploys a specific emotional trigger: a child who doubts his own creative work, experiences performance anxiety or self-criticism, and then — the story implies — overcomes this to produce something "beautiful" that others can recognise and affirm. This narrative triggers multiple overlapping emotional responses: sympathy for the child's initial self-doubt (prosocial empathy), admiration for apparent talent (aesthetic appreciation), and a desire to participate in affirming the child's value (social validation behaviour). The comment "You are a very gifted young man" is precisely what the caption is designed to elicit — it serves the engagement metric of comment-writing by directing the commenter's motivation appropriately.

**The role of the image's aesthetic construction.** The AI-generated image is aesthetically pleasing by conventional standards of child photography: the boy is rendered with appealing features, the setting is bright and naturalistic, the drawing in the frame appears detailed and charming, and the overall composition is warm and harmonising. This aesthetic quality is not accidental — the prompt or images used to generate it were almost certainly optimised for appealing child portraiture in the style of popular social media content about children. The result is an image that reads as authentic precisely because it corresponds closely to the conventions of the genre it imitates: the kind of photograph a parent might share on social media to celebrate their child's artistic achievement.

**Epistemic novelty: The parasitic narrative structure.** The epistemically novel feature of this content — and of the broader category of AI-generated "inspirational" content that targets social media engagement — is what might be called *parasitic narrative structure*. Real social media users do share genuine photographs of their children's artwork, genuine emotional moments of parental pride, genuine instances of children overcoming self-doubt. These real posts generate engagement because the underlying human experience — a child's vulnerability and creativity — is genuinely moving. The AI-generated engagement bait parasitises this legitimate category of content by producing images and captions that have all the surface characteristics of a genuine heartwarming post without any genuine underlying event. The viewer who responds with sympathy and affirmation is responding to the narrative structure, not to a real child, because the narrative structure is the only thing that is real.

This structural pattern differs from simple image fakery. A fabricated image of a news event (say, a fake photograph of a building fire) asks viewers to believe an event occurred. An engagement bait image of this kind does not necessarily ask viewers to believe anything specific — it asks viewers to feel something. The emotional response it solicits does not depend on the literal truth of the caption. Many viewers who commented on this image probably did not think carefully about whether it was a real photograph; they responded to the emotional prompt ("this child deserves praise") and provided the requested response. The claim to authenticity is almost incidental; the mechanism is affect, not belief.

**Facebook's demographics and the sharing pattern.** Research on demographic differences in susceptibility to AI-generated content has consistently found that older users are more likely to share fabricated content containing emotionally salient imagery associated with children, family, and patriotism (Guess et al., 2019, *Science Advances*). The specific platforms and page types through which this image propagated — Facebook pages with names like "I Love Pets," emotional support and family communities — are associated with demographic profiles more likely to share this content without careful scrutiny. This is not a claim about intelligence or critical capacity; it reflects differences in platform use, digital literacy formation, and the statistical properties of the social networks through which content travels.

---

## Verdict

**The image is entirely fake.** The boy, the drawing, and the setting are all AI-generated. No authentic human subject or artwork is involved. The visual artefacts identified — irregular frame geometry, depth-layer failures, ear anomaly, and atypical skin texture — are consistent with AI image generation and constitute direct observable evidence of fabrication. The existence of multiple versions with different-looking boys but identical captions indicates deliberate production of engagement bait content rather than a single accidental error. Snopes rated this image **Fake**.

The broader content category it belongs to — AI-generated emotional manipulation content designed to drive engagement metrics on social media — is a growing and relatively under-scrutinised form of information environment pollution. Unlike political misinformation, it does not make false factual claims about events or people; its harm is subtler: it degrades the epistemic quality of social media spaces by introducing synthetic emotional narratives that compete with genuine human experience for attention and engagement.

## The Wider Picture

The boy-with-drawing image is a specimen of a content category that researchers and platform safety teams have identified as distinct from conventional misinformation but producing measurable harms at scale.

**AI engagement bait as an information environment category.** Research by Megan Loe (KAGS TV, 2024) and by journalists covering the AI-content beat has documented a systematic practice of producing AI-generated images with emotionally manipulative captions for the specific purpose of generating engagement metrics on Facebook and similar platforms. This practice exploits the algorithmic design of platforms that reward high-engagement content with increased distribution — creating a positive feedback loop in which fabricated emotional content achieves greater reach than authentic content, reinforcing production incentives. The images produced in this category typically depict: children performing apparently impressive tasks; elderly people in emotional moments; animals in endearing or peril-escape scenarios; and scenes of domestic virtue (a grandmother baking, a child helping a parent). These categories map directly onto the emotional response landscapes most reliably associated with comment-writing and sharing behaviour across the broadest demographic cross-sections of social media users.

**The detection challenge for positive emotional content.** Platform AI-detection and content moderation systems are primarily trained and calibrated on harmful content categories: hate speech, violence, sexual content, politically extremist material. Positive emotional image content — even when fabricated — typically does not trigger automated removal systems because it does not fall into these categories and because the fabrication itself is not inherently harmful in the way that harmful content is. A child with a drawing violates no content policy. The harm is not in the individual image but in the aggregate effect on the information environment when millions of such images displace authentic human content in users' feeds and communities. This aggregate harm is harder to operationalise in content policy terms than individual-image harms, and platforms have not yet developed comprehensive responses.

**Artistic fraud and AI generation.** There is a specific sub-problem latent in this case that deserves examination: the fake drawing. The boy in the image appears to be holding a detailed, charming self-portrait appropriate to a child's artistic development. This drawing is also AI-generated. This means that the image fabricates not only a photograph of a child but also the child's artwork — it creates a false impression of a creative act that never occurred. Viewers who respond to the drawing's quality ("You are a very gifted young man") are responding to the output of a machine, misattributed to a child. This specific dynamic — attributing creative merit to a purported human artist who does not exist — represents a form of fraud in the conceptual space of artistic attribution, even if no material harm flows from the specific error in most cases. If the practice of attributing AI-generated art to fictional human children becomes widespread, it could degrade public intuitions about what children's art looks like — potentially making genuine children's art seem less impressive by comparison to an AI-generated idealised version.

**The empathy infrastructure and its manipulation.** Ultimately, this case reveals that social platforms have built a sophisticated infrastructure for mobilising human empathy at scale — the algorithm, the emotional design of content post types, the comment section, the reaction buttons. This infrastructure is genuinely capable of connecting people around real shared human experiences. It is simultaneously exploitable by anyone capable of producing emotionally resonant content inputs, regardless of whether those inputs reflect actual events. The AI revolution in image generation has dramatically lowered the cost of producing high-quality emotionally resonant visual content, effectively democratising the ability to exploit the empathy infrastructure. What previously required a professional photographer, a willing child model, and a real illustrated drawing now requires a text prompt and seconds of compute time.

Research on the downstream effects of sustained exposure to emotionally manipulative AI content on social media has not yet been fully conducted at the scale that the phenomenon warrants. Preliminary work on "misinformation fatigue" (Roozenbeek et al., 2020, *Royal Society Open Science*) documents that repeated exposure to false content — even when identified as false after the fact — can degrade trust in authentic content over time, as readers lose the ability to reliably distinguish genuine from fabricated emotional narratives. The specific mechanism by which AI-generated emotional content (as opposed to factual misinformation) affects social trust and authentic community formation remains an important open research question.

**The comparison to other debunked AI images.** Snopes' prior reporting on AI-generated images — including pictures of Mickey Mouse dropping a toddler at Disneyland and quadruplet sisters celebrating their 90th birthday — provides context for the boy-with-drawing image as part of a systematic pattern of AI-generated human-interest content. These cases share a common architecture: a believable scenario presented with realistic imagery, emotionally curated to produce specific engagement responses. What links them is not the subject matter but the production logic: optimising AI image generation output for social media engagement rather than for truth, artistic value, or authentic human connection. The pattern is consistent and growing.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**The reverse-image search as primary tool.** Journalist Jordan Liles used a reverse-image search as the first analytical step, which served two functions: identifying the breadth of the image's circulation (establishing that it is a significant viral phenomenon rather than an isolated post) and locating the second version of the AI-generated image with a different-looking boy but the same caption. This second discovery is analytically crucial — it establishes that the content was deliberately produced in multiple variations, which is strong evidence of intentional content production rather than a single organic creation. A genuine photograph of a real event has one source; multiple AI-generated variations with identical captions have a production workflow.

**Artefact identification as AI detection.** In the absence of an automated AI detection tool that provided a definitive result, Liles relied on visual artefact identification: the irregular frame edge, the front/back plant inconsistency, the ear anomaly, the smooth texture. This methodology is both appropriate and durable: AI artefacts of the type described are visible to trained human observation and constitute direct evidence of AI synthesis, not probabilistic inference. The specific artefacts identified are documented as consistent failure modes of diffusion model image generation in research literature (Corvi et al., 2023, *IEEE/CVF Conference on Computer Vision and Pattern Recognition*), giving the methodology a firm technical basis.

**The engagement bait framing.** Liles explicitly identified the emotional caption as "engagement bait" designed to "evoke emotions and prompt users to like, comment, and share the content." This is an important addition to the standard Snopes verdict structure: it names the content's purpose, which is manipulative intent rather than mere error or misunderstanding. This framing contextualises the fabrication within a deliberate content production strategy, which is more accurate than treating the image as a neutral fake with no productive purpose.

**The limits of source attribution.** The fact-check identifies the Russian-language Facebook page POFU - Good Humor as a probable early source, notes the page had been deleted, reports that Snopes contacted the page's owners and received no response, and commits to updating the article if a response is received. This is appropriately cautious: source attribution for viral social media content is genuinely difficult, earlier versions may exist than the one identified, and the page's deletion means the original publishing context is no longer available for inspection. The responsible position is to note the probable source without asserting definitive origin.
