## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A photograph showing cardboard boxes bearing labels reading "Boneless Pork Rectums" — and in some versions "Inverted Boneless Pork Rectums" — with a "Product of USA" designation prompted widespread online sharing beginning around 2007. The image originated on a Taiwan-based internet forum (Forumosa) posted by a user describing it as taken outside a local dumpling shop, which the user noted as evidence of what appeared to be a genuine product from an American food company. The visceral reaction to the boxes' label content drove recurring waves of viral spread: the image resurfaced periodically through the 2010s, each time attracting fresh expressions of horror and disgust, and in one iteration the Tyson Foods logos were clumsily edited to replace them with McDonald's logos, accompanied by a story that the product was used in McDonald's McRib sandwiches.

Snopes investigator David Mikkelson (2014) assigned the core claim the rating **True**. Despite Tyson Foods' refusal to verify the photograph specifically, Mikkelson found no reason to doubt the image's authenticity. More importantly, he established affirmative context: pork rectums, including the inverted variety, are a real commercial food product used in dishes including chitlins (chitterlings) and traded by multiple companies internationally. The photograph documents a genuine category of commercially processed pork offal that is unremarkable to anyone with knowledge of the offal trade, though highly surprising to American consumers whose food literacy has been shaped by a retail environment that largely conceals the anatomical origins of processed products.

The McDonald's McRib elaboration — that the boxed pork rectums appear in McRib sandwiches — was assessed as False by Mikkelson (2014). The image is identical to the original 2007 posting and the story of its origin differs from the Taiwan context when recirculated with the McDonald's attribution, suggesting a fabricated escalation layered atop a genuine image. This entry examines the genuine product, the offal trade, the disgust psychology driving the claim's viral behavior, and what the McRib elaboration reveals about the mechanics of shock misinformation extension.

## §2 The Product and Its Provenance: Offal Commerce as Routine Industry

The central factual finding — that "Boneless Pork Rectum" and "Inverted Boneless Pork Rectum" are real commercial product categories — is verifiable through multiple independent sources that Mikkelson (2014) traced and which have subsequently expanded in availability through trade databases. The items appear in import/export commodity databases, appear in USDA export data under specific product codes, and are traded by multiple American and international food companies to markets in Asia, Latin America, and Europe where offal consumption is commonplace.

The relevant USDA product classification for pork rectum falls under the broader category of pork variety meats and edible byproducts. The specific terminology "inverted" in "Inverted Boneless Pork Rectum" refers to a processing technique in which the organ is turned inside-out during cleaning and preparation, exposing the inner mucosal surface for thorough washing — a standard procedure in offal preparation that describes a physical process, not a product type distinct from the non-inverted version. The inversion technique allows for more thorough removal of intestinal contents and cleaning of the interior surface. This preparation step is analogous to the standard preparation of intestine casings used in sausage manufacture, which has been practiced for centuries without attracting similar consumer alarm precisely because the final product (a sausage) does not carry descriptive labeling identifying its casing origin.

Commercial pork rectum products are used predominantly in two contexts. The first and most historically significant in the American context is the preparation of chitterlings (also spelled chitlins or chit'lins), a traditional dish with deep roots in African American Southern cuisine, with origins in the necessity-driven use of all pork parts during the era of slavery and sharecropping. Chitterlings consist of the small intestines of pigs — a closely related but anatomically distinct organ from the rectum — cleaned, prepared, and either boiled or fried. The dish has been documented in American food writing since at least the 18th century and has been analyzed ethnographically as a culturally significant food that marks regional Southern identity, intergenerational family ties, and the African American culinary heritage that emerged from extreme deprivation (Warnes, 2004, *Hunger Overcome: Food and Resistance in Twentieth-Century African American Literature*, University of Georgia Press). The second context is export to markets, particularly in East Asia, where intestinal and rectal portions of pigs are integrated into mainstream cookery: pig intestine is a component of widely popular Taiwanese dishes such as *zhu chang mi chang* (intestine with sticky rice), Hong Kong *cheung fun* variations, and numerous Chinese regional specialties.

The export dimension is what explains the Forumosa Taiwan context of the original photograph. The "Product of USA" designation on the Tyson boxes indicates product manufactured under USDA inspection and exported to Asian markets — a conventional and substantial segment of American pork's commercial output. The United States is one of the world's largest exporters of pork variety meats specifically because American consumer preferences strongly favor muscle meat cuts, creating a surplus of variety meat components that command higher prices in export markets with different culinary traditions.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Anatomy of Disgust and the Western Offal Blind Spot

The rainbow-grape case turned on botanical unfamiliarity; the pumpkin spice case turned on toxicological literacy. The pork rectum case turns on a different kind of epistemic gap: the systematic concealment of slaughter anatomy in processed food presentation, and the resulting brittle cultural disgust that surfaces when that concealment fails.

The sociology of food disgust has been extensively theorized. The foundational work is that of Rozin and Fallon (1987, *Psychological Review*, Vol. 94, No. 1), whose disgust theory distinguishes between core disgust (responses to animals and body products), animal-nature disgust (responses to reminders of human animal nature, including meat and its preparation), and contamination disgust (responses to contact with disgusting objects). Pork rectum packaging triggers all three categories simultaneously: it is a body product (core disgust), it is an explicit anatomical reminder (animal-nature disgust), and the label naming makes the contamination association inescapable.

The key insight from cross-cultural food studies is that disgust responses to offal are culturally variable in ways that are highly specific to post-industrial Western food culture and to white Anglo-American consumer culture in particular. The disgust is not universal. As Julia Csergo (1999, in *Food: A Culinary History*, ed. Flandrin & Montanari, Columbia University Press) documented, offal has historically been central to European cuisine and remains so in cultures where nose-to-tail butchery practice has not been displaced by industrial muscular-tissue-only consumption patterns. The French tradition of *abats* (offal), the British tradition of tripe and other variety meats, the Spanish *callos* (tripe stew), the Scottish haggis, and the Chinese and Taiwanese traditions cited above all represent contexts in which pork rectum and related products would be recognized as ordinary food rather than the objects of shock.

The disgust response in American consumer culture specifically traces to processes of industrial food processing and marketing that occurred primarily in the 20th century. Before mass refrigeration and centralized slaughter, American households were substantially more likely to encounter animal anatomy in food preparation — hog-killing day on American farms was a seasonal event involving full-body processing. The progressive movement of food preparation from household to factory, and the parallel development of branded packaged products that concealed anatomical origins behind appealing names and packaging, created what food studies scholars have called the **processed food amnesia** — a culturally constructed ignorance of where food comes from that is maintained by deliberate industry packaging choices (Pollan, 2006, *The Omnivore's Dilemma*, Penguin Press; Schlosser, 2001, *Fast Food Nation*, Houghton Mifflin).

The epistemic novelty in this case is the *inverse* of most food panic claims: rather than alleging that something harmful is present that is being concealed, the viral reaction to the pork rectum boxes is driven by ordinary industrial practice being *revealed* without the usual packaging mediation. The boxes are labeled accurately. The product is what it says it is. The shock is not a discovery of deception but a confrontation with the systematically obscured material reality of how pork is processed and commercialized — a reality that operates continuously but becomes visible only in specific moments of label transparency.

This has broader implications for food literacy and food system transparency. Consumer alarm at accurate product labels reflects a consumption culture that depends on selective ignorance for comfort — a form of socially maintained not-knowing that serves obvious psychological purposes but creates vulnerability to viral shock when the concealment is momentarily breached.

## §4 The McRib Elaboration: Anatomy of a False Extension

The McRib sandwich at McDonald's has been a perennial subject of food myth and controversy since its introduction in 1981. Because the McRib is a boneless pork patty shaped into a faux-ribbed form and served on a hoagie-style bun with barbecue sauce, it does not obviously correspond to any familiar cut of pork, inviting speculation about its composition. Various claims about McRib ingredients circulate: that it contains "restructured meat" (true in a technical sense — it is a processed pork patty), that it contains stomach lining (false), that it contains pork heart and lip (these have appeared in nutritional context discussions), and that it contains pork rectum (false, per available evidence).

The edited version of the pork rectum box photograph — with Tyson logos replaced by McDonald's logos — appears to have been created as a shock extension of the original viral image. Mikkelson (2014) noted the editing is "quite poor" (visible artifacts, inconsistent logo geometry) and that the story of origin differs from the original Forumosa context. The structural pattern of this extension follows what internet folklore researchers have documented as **false escalation grafting** — the attachment of a known shocking image to a more high-profile target brand or institution to amplify outrage and extend reach. McDonald's as a target offers superior viral potential to Tyson because it is more widely known, connects to existing fast food anxiety discourse, and has higher emotional salience for American consumers than a wholesale meat processor whose branding most consumers would not recognize.

The actual composition of the McRib patty is documented in McDonald's ingredient disclosures and has been independently analyzed. The patty is a restructured boneless pork product containing pork shoulder (Boston butt) as the primary ingredient, along with water, dextrose, and preservatives. USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) regulations require that restructured pork products disclose all ingredients on label; McDonald's disclosures are consistent with these regulatory requirements and do not list pork rectum. The claim that McRib contains pork rectum is false and separately debunked (see Snopes fact-check on McRib ingredients, which reaches the same false conclusion).

The viral life of the pork rectum image / McRib story combination illustrates how genuine shocking images develop secondary false claims as they circulate — the original image acquires narrative accretions that reframe it for different audiences and purposes. Source images that trigger disgust are particularly susceptible to this kind of secondary fabrication because the disgust response suppresses critical evaluation: an audience already viscerally repulsed by the image is less likely to scrutinize the claim's logic or provenance.

## §5 The Offal Trade: American Export Geography and Product Flows

A dimension of this case that receives insufficient attention in consumer-facing fact-checking is the substantial commercial scale of American pork variety meat exports, of which pork rectum is one component. The U.S. Meat Export Federation (USMEF) publishes annual data on pork product exports disaggregated by product category and destination market. In 2022, U.S. pork variety meat exports (including stomachs, hearts, kidneys, livers, and intestinal products) totaled approximately 578,000 metric tons with a value of approximately $1.3 billion (USMEF, 2023 Pork Export Statistics). The largest destination markets were Mexico, Japan, South Korea, and the Philippines, with China and Hong Kong also significant.

This export flow exists precisely because the U.S. domestic market for variety meats is relatively limited compared to the volume produced from American hog slaughter. Every hog slaughtered produces a full complement of organ meats, intestinal components, and other variety meats regardless of demand; the choice is between export, pet food production, or rendering. The differential in cultural acceptance of variety meats between the U.S. domestic market and Asian export markets creates a consistent price arbitrage that makes export commercially rational and maintains a substantial bilateral trade flow.

The boxes photographed in the original 2007 Forumosa image — Tyson products bearing USDA inspection marks and "Product of USA" labels stacked outside a Taiwanese dumpling shop — are thus a window into a routine commercial relationship that is invisible to American consumers but economically significant and operationally continuous. The "horror" of the image from the American consumer perspective is not a discovery of something unusual but a disclosure of something normal.

## §6 Verdict, Knowledge Gaps, and Downstream Epistemic Effects

Snopes' verdict of **True** for the core claim (the boxes are real Tyson products containing pork rectum) is fully supported. Pork rectum is a real commercial product, its presence in export-destined food processing is routine, and the boxes photographed are consistent with standard wholesale food container labeling for international pork variety meat trade.

The **EARC gap** is assigned **R (Relational)**: the knowledge needed to adequately contextualize this claim is primarily cultural and economic — understanding the offal trade, the cross-cultural variation in disgust responses, the systematic concealment of anatomical origins in American consumer packaging, and the export economics of American pork. This relational gap is broader than a simple empirical fact-check; it requires situating the claim within food systems knowledge that the mainstream American consumer information environment does not provide.

Key take-away propositions:
1. Pork rectum, including the inverted boneless variety, is a real, commercially traded, USDA-inspected food product exported from the United States to international markets.
2. The photograph is genuine; the product it shows is not fraudulent, disgusting as it may appear to American consumers acculturated to processed-meat packaging conventions.
3. The disgust reaction to the image reflects a culturally specific Western food literacy gap — the systematic concealment of anatomical reality in consumer packaging — rather than any objective contaminant or food safety violation.
4. The McRib elaboration is false: McDonald's McRib patty contains pork shoulder, not pork rectum, and ingredient disclosures under USDA FSIS rules are publicly available and consistent with this.
5. The American disgust response to offal products is culturally particular, not universal: pork intestinal products are mainstream ingredients in Taiwanese, Chinese, throughout East Asian, Southern American African American, and European culinary traditions.

The deeper epistemological challenge surfaced by this case is the question of what food literacy actually requires for democratic food system participation. The pork rectum box is disturbing to American consumers primarily because they have been systematically not taught where food comes from, in a commercial food system that profits from that ignorance. Fact-checking the authenticity of the image is the easy part; the harder work is articulating what knowledge makes the image unremarkable — and why that knowledge is systematically absent.
