# Does a Urine-Revealing Pool Chemical Exist?

## §1. The Claim

Somewhere in the hazy oral tradition of childhood summers, a story circulates with the persistence of chlorine fumes: swimming pools contain a special chemical — invisible under normal conditions — that will change color the instant someone urinates in the water. The offending swimmer will be surrounded by a telltale cloud of vivid dye, usually described as red, purple, or deep blue, marking them unmistakably as the guilty party. Humiliation follows. Expulsion from the pool follows. In some versions, your parents are called.

The myth is remarkably consistent across cultures and decades. British children hear about the dye in municipal leisure centres; Australian kids are warned of it at public pools from Sydney to Perth; American children encounter it at YMCA pools, summer camps, and neighborhood swim clubs. The specific color varies — red is the most common in North American tellings, purple dominates in British versions, and blue appears in Australian accounts — but the core architecture is always identical: a chemical reagent, invisible until triggered, that reacts specifically and dramatically with human urine to produce a visible color change in the surrounding water.

The story functions as a near-perfect deterrent. It is told by authority figures — parents, lifeguards, swim instructors — to very young children, typically between the ages of four and eight, at precisely the moment when those children are entering an environment where the temptation to urinate without leaving the water is highest. The claim is delivered with complete conviction, often with embellishments: the chemical was "just added this morning," the lifeguards "can see it from the tower," the color "doesn't wash off your skin for days." Some versions add that the chemical was developed by NASA, or by the military, or by "scientists in Switzerland." The institutional attribution varies, but the implication is always the same: this is real technology, deployed by competent adults, and you will be caught.

What makes the myth particularly resilient is its unfalsifiability from the child's perspective. No child wants to be the one who tests it. The deterrent effect is therefore self-reinforcing: because nobody deliberately urinates in the pool to check, nobody can report that nothing happened. And on those occasions when a child does urinate undetected, the absence of a color change is either not noticed or attributed to the chemical having been "used up" or "not added today." The myth survives precisely because it discourages the one experiment that would refute it.

The belief is not confined to children. Surveys of adults reveal surprisingly high rates of acceptance. Many adults who were told the story as children continue to believe it well into adulthood, never having encountered a reason to question it. Others suspect it may be false but remain uncertain — after all, pool chemistry is genuinely complex, and most adults know very little about what is actually in the water they swim in. The gap between the public's understanding of pool chemistry and the reality is vast, and the urine-indicator myth nests comfortably in that ignorance.

The claim spans many countries and at least five decades of documented circulation, making it one of the most geographically widespread and temporally durable urban legends in the swimming-pool domain. Barbara Mikkelson at Snopes first catalogued it in December 2000, noting its deep roots and multicultural presence (Mikkelson, B., 2000, "Piscine of the Crime," Snopes.com, updated 6 September 2023). But the story is far older than the internet. It has been traced in oral tradition to at least the 1950s, and possibly earlier, existing as a piece of parental folklore handed down alongside warnings about swimming after eating and the dangers of running on wet tile.

## §2. What's Actually True

No urine-detecting chemical dye exists in commercial swimming pools. None. This is not a matter of it being rare, expensive, or limited to high-end facilities. The product does not exist. No pool supply company manufactures it, no chemical distributor sells it, and no pool maintenance protocol calls for it. As Mikkelson reports, the pool supply industry *routinely* receives requests for "urine-indicator dye" from pool operators who have heard the myth and want to purchase the product — and must routinely explain that no such product exists (Mikkelson, 2000).

The reasons are not administrative but chemical. As one pool chemist quoted in the Snopes article states plainly: "There is no chemical that can function as an indicator for urine in a pool." The statement is not hyperbolic. It reflects a genuine and formidable analytical chemistry problem.

To understand why, consider what urine actually contains. Human urine is approximately 95% water. The remaining 5% consists primarily of urea (the dominant organic solute, typically 9–20 g/L), creatinine (a breakdown product of muscle metabolism, approximately 1–2 g/L), ammonia, uric acid, various electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride), and trace organic compounds including amino acids, hormones, and metabolites (Schlenk, D. & Benson, W.H., 2001, *Target Organ Toxicity in Marine and Freshwater Teleosts*, Taylor & Francis). Now consider the environment into which this urine is introduced: a swimming pool containing tens or hundreds of thousands of liters of water that already contains dissolved organic material from every swimmer's body — sweat, skin oils, sunscreen residue, cosmetics, saliva, nasal secretions, and shed epithelial cells. The pool water also contains the disinfectant itself (hypochlorous acid from chlorination) and its reaction byproducts.

A hypothetical urine-indicator dye would need to react *specifically* with one or more components of urine while ignoring chemically similar compounds already present in the water from other sources. Urea, the most abundant urine marker, is also present in sweat at concentrations of roughly 1–2 g/L (Sato, K. et al., 1989, "Biology of Sweat Glands and Their Disorders," *Journal of the American Academy of Dermatology* 20(4): 537–563). A dye that responds to urea would therefore fire continuously in any pool with more than a few swimmers, since human sweat introduces urea at concentrations that, when diluted, overlap with the range produced by urination events. The indicator would produce constant false positives. The pool would be permanently purple.

This was captured memorably by what Mikkelson describes as an "old Boston-area poolman" who observed that if such a dye existed, "every municipal pool in Boston would be bright purple" (Mikkelson, 2000). The remark is not merely colorful — it is chemically accurate.

What actually happens when urine enters a chlorinated swimming pool is well characterized and considerably less dramatic than a color change. The chlorine in pool water (present as hypochlorous acid, HOCl) reacts with nitrogenous compounds in urine — primarily urea, creatinine, and ammonia — to form chloramines, a class of disinfection byproducts (DBPs). The most significant of these are trichloramine (NCl₃), cyanogen chloride (CNCl), and various organic chloramines. Weaver et al. (2009) demonstrated through controlled chlorination experiments that urea reacts with hypochlorous acid to produce trichloramine as a dominant volatile byproduct, along with cyanogen chloride and dichloroacetonitrile ("Volatile disinfection byproducts resulting from chlorination of urea: implications for swimming pools," *Analytical Chemistry* 81(16): 6285–6291).

These chloramines are not visible — they do not produce a color change. But they are not harmless either. Trichloramine (NCl₃) is a volatile, pungent compound that is the primary cause of what most people recognize as "the pool smell." This is a critical and underappreciated irony: the smell that the public associates with a *clean* pool is in fact the smell of chlorine reacting with organic contaminants, including urine. A genuinely clean, well-maintained pool with no organic load would have virtually no smell at all. The "pool smell" signals contamination, not sanitation (Li, J. & Blatchley, E.R., 2007, "Volatile disinfection byproduct formation resulting from chlorination of organic–nitrogen precursors in swimming pools," *Environmental Science & Technology* 41(19): 6732–6739).

In other words, while there is no visible urine indicator, there is an olfactory one — but it indicates the cumulative organic load of all swimmers, not the act of any individual swimmer, and it operates through a mechanism (chloramine volatilization) that the public systematically misinterprets.

## §3. Why People Believe This

The urine-indicator dye myth is not a garden-variety misconception. It does not persist because people reason poorly about chemistry — though that contributes. It persists because it was *manufactured* to persist. This is a case of what we might call a "social technology myth": a false belief that was deliberately created and propagated by identifiable social actors (parents, lifeguards, pool administrators) to achieve a specific behavioral outcome (deterring children from urinating in pools). The myth then escaped its origins and was widely misidentified as fact.

This distinction matters epistemically. Most urban legends arise through organic processes of narrative mutation and memetic selection (Brunvand, J.H., 1981, *The Vanishing Hitchhiker: American Urban Legends and Their Meanings*, W.W. Norton). A story about a hitchhiker who vanishes from the back seat evolves through retelling, gradually acquiring plausibility-enhancing details. But the urine-indicator myth shows strong evidence of *intentional* deployment. Parents do not stumble into this belief through a chain of misunderstandings — they are *told* it by their own parents or by pool staff, often with the explicit instruction that it should be passed on to children. It is, in effect, a coordinated social lie, perpetuated across generations not because anyone believes it but because it *works*.

The myth's effectiveness as a deterrence tool does not require it to be true. This is its most interesting feature. A child who believes the dye exists modifies their behavior regardless of whether the dye is real. The myth functions as a behavioral technology — a piece of social engineering that operates through the manipulation of beliefs rather than the deployment of actual chemical technology. It is, in the terminology of game theory, a credible threat that maintains its credibility precisely because it is never tested.

The propagation mechanism follows the classic "friend of a friend" (FOAF) narrative structure that Brunvand identified as the hallmark of urban legends (Brunvand, 1981). When adults discuss the urine-indicator dye, the confirmatory incident — the time someone was actually caught — always happened to someone else's friend, at a different pool, in a different city. The specificity varies (it was a boy at the Hoboken YMCA; it was a girl at a council pool in Sheffield), but the structural feature is constant: the speaker has never personally witnessed the dye in action, but they know someone who has. This recursive hearsay structure makes the claim effectively unfalsifiable through social channels, since any individual's failure to witness it can be attributed to the contingency of their own experience.

The role of institutional authority in lending plausibility cannot be overstated. When a parent tells a child that the pool contains urine-detecting dye, the claim arrives backed by the full apparatus of adult credibility. When a lifeguard repeats it — and lifeguards frequently do — it acquires the additional weight of professional expertise. The child has no framework for distinguishing genuine chemical knowledge from social fiction delivered by credentialed sources. The institutions themselves (YMCAs, community pools, holiday resorts) became unwitting co-conspirators, their mere existence as serious, managed environments lending credibility to the claim that serious, managed technology was in use.

There is also a fascinating historical vector. Barbara Leaming's 1985 biography of Orson Welles records that Welles and the playwright Charlie MacArthur actually pulled a version of this prank in approximately 1937, using a real dye (not a urine indicator, but simply a dye triggered near someone pretending to urinate) at a party (Leaming, B., 1985, *Orson Welles: A Biography*, Viking Press). While the Welles-MacArthur incident used planted dye rather than a genuine urine reagent, it represents an *actual instance* of the scenario the myth describes — someone publicly humiliated by colored water around them in a pool. It is plausible that this real incident, or others like it involving simple pranks with water-soluble dyes, contributed to the myth's origin or early spread. Someone *did* do it — just not with real urine-detection chemistry.

Popular culture has further cemented the myth. The Nickelodeon television series *The Adventures of Pete & Pete* featured an episode titled "Splashdown" in which the characters encounter a product called "Wee-Wee See," an explicit dramatization of the urine-indicator concept. The 2010 Adam Sandler film *Grown Ups* includes a scene in which a pool's water turns blue around a character who urinates in it. These cultural representations do not merely reflect existing belief — they actively reinforce it, providing vivid visual "evidence" that lodges in memory and becomes difficult to distinguish from documentary sources. For many adults, their most concrete mental image of the urine-indicator dye comes not from any real pool experience but from a film or television show.

## §4. Verdict

**False.** Confidence: **Very High.**

No commercially available urine-indicator dye exists for swimming pools. No chemical reagent has been manufactured that can selectively detect urine in a chlorinated aquatic environment containing multiple organic contaminants from human bodies. The claim fails on straightforward analytical chemistry grounds: the target analytes (urea, creatinine, ammonia) are not unique to urine and are present in pool water from other sources, primarily sweat.

The myth is not and never was a misunderstanding of real science. It is a piece of deliberate social fiction — a behavioral deterrent manufactured by parents and pool operators and propagated across generations, cultures, and continents with remarkable fidelity. Its persistence speaks not to public gullibility but to the power of authority-backed claims in domains where the audience lacks independent verification capacity.

It is worth noting that the myth's falsity does not eliminate its social function. Even now, knowing the dye does not exist, many parents continue to tell their children it does. The myth operates as a *useful* fiction — a noble lie in the Platonic sense — and its continued deployment raises genuine ethical questions about the legitimacy of deception in the service of hygiene.

## §5. The Wider Picture

### Manufactured Deterrence as a Category

The urine-indicator dye belongs to a broader family of myths that might be called "manufactured deterrence narratives" — false claims deliberately created and propagated by authority figures to control the behavior of subordinates, typically children. The category is large and culturally significant:

**Razor blades in Halloween candy.** Perhaps the most studied manufactured deterrence myth in the sociological literature. Joel Best's systematic examination of every reported case of Halloween candy tampering in the United States from 1958 to the present found that the threat was overwhelmingly fictional, with almost no confirmed cases of strangers harming children through adulterated treats (Best, J. & Horiuchi, G.T., 1985, "The Razor Blade in the Apple: The Social Construction of Urban Legends," *Social Problems* 32(5): 488–499). Yet the myth persists, drives policy (many communities X-ray candy), and powerfully modifies behavior. Like the pool dye myth, it serves as a deterrent (don't accept candy from strangers) that functions regardless of its truth value.

**"The dog can smell your fear."** This one is more complex because it contains a kernel of truth. Dogs can detect certain volatile organic compounds associated with human stress responses, including cortisol and adrenaline metabolites present in sweat (D'Aniello, B. et al., 2018, "Interspecies transmission of emotional information via chemosignals: from humans to dogs (*Canis lupus familiaris*)," *Animal Cognition* 21(1): 67–78). However, the popular version — that a dog will *attack* if it smells fear — dramatically overstates both the specificity of canine olfactory threat detection and the causal link between fear-detection and aggression. The myth is propagated to teach children not to run from dogs, which is genuinely good advice, but the mechanism cited is largely fabricated.

**"The policeman will take you away."** A direct behavioral threat using institutional authority as the deterrent, with no factual basis (police do not arrest children for misbehavior in shops) but enormous effectiveness with preschool-age children.

These myths share a common structure: an authority figure deploys a false factual claim to modify the behavior of someone with less knowledge and less power. The deception is *interested* — it benefits the deceiver (or at minimum, benefits the social order the deceiver is maintaining) — and it is *durable* because the target population lacks the resources to verify it.

### The Ethics of Useful Fiction

The manufactured deterrence category raises a genuine ethical question: is it permissible to lie to children when the lie serves a prosocial purpose? The pool dye myth deters urination in shared swimming water, which is a legitimate hygiene goal. Urine in pools is not merely aesthetically unpleasant — as discussed in §2, it drives the formation of chloramines and other disinfection byproducts with real health consequences.

However, the cumulative effect of manufactured deterrence myths may erode children's trust in adult testimony more broadly. A child who discovers the pool dye myth is false — and most children eventually do — learns not only that *this particular claim* was untrue but that *adults will lie to them for instrumental reasons*. Whether this erosion of epistemic trust is offset by the short-term behavioral benefits is an open question in developmental ethics.

### The Real Chemistry of Urine in Pools

What urine actually does to pool water is less dramatic than a color change but chemically significant. When urea (present in urine at approximately 2% concentration, and also in sweat at approximately 0.1%) encounters hypochlorous acid (HOCl, the active sanitizer in chlorinated pools), a series of reactions occur. The simplified pathway proceeds through monochlorourea and dichlorourea intermediates to produce trichloramine (NCl₃) as the dominant volatile product (Weaver et al., 2009). Trichloramine has a characteristic pungent odor and is a significant respiratory irritant — it is the primary compound responsible for "pool smell" and has been associated with increased rates of asthma among competitive swimmers and pool workers (Bernard, A. et al., 2003, "Lung hyperpermeability and asthma prevalence in schoolchildren: unexpected associations with the attendance at indoor chlorinated swimming pools," *Occupational and Environmental Medicine* 60(6): 385–394).

The "pool smell" is thus itself a kind of involuntary indicator — not of any individual swimmer's urination, but of the aggregate organic nitrogen load in the water. Combined chlorine levels (the sum of mono-, di-, and trichloramine concentrations) are a standard water-quality metric in pool management, and elevated combined chlorine is a reliable signal that organic contaminants, including urine, are present at elevated levels.

### Could a Selective Biosensor Theoretically Work?

Modern biosensor research has made progress on the selective detection of urine-specific biomarkers in aqueous environments, though not in the form of a visible-spectrum dye. Enzyme-linked biosensors targeting urease activity (the enzymatic hydrolysis of urea to ammonia and carbon dioxide) have been demonstrated in laboratory settings (Dhawan, G. et al., 2009, "Recent developments in urea biosensors," *Biochemical Engineering Journal* 44(1): 42–52). More recently, immunoassay-based approaches targeting kidney-specific proteins such as uromodulin (Tamm-Horsfall protein), which is the most abundant protein in human urine and is absent from sweat, have been explored as highly selective urine markers (Serafini-Cessi, F. et al., 2003, "Tamm-Horsfall glycoprotein: biology and clinical relevance," *American Journal of Kidney Diseases* 42(4): 658–676).

A uromodulin-specific colorimetric biosensor could, in principle, distinguish urine from sweat in pool water. But the engineering challenges are formidable: the sensor would need to function in a harsh chemical environment (high chlorine, variable pH, high ionic strength), detect nanomolar protein concentrations against a complex organic background, and produce a visible signal in hundreds of thousands of liters of water. The cost, maintenance burden, and false-positive management of such a system make it impractical for commercial pool deployment with any foreseeable technology. The urine-indicator dye remains, for all practical purposes, impossible — not logically impossible, but technologically unrealizable at a cost anyone would pay for the purpose of embarrassing children.

## §6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Barbara Mikkelson's Snopes article on the pool urine-indicator myth, originally published in December 2000 and last updated in September 2023, is a model of a particular style of fact-checking: the folklore-informed cultural essay. Mikkelson does not merely declare the claim false and cite an expert — though she does both. She traces the myth's cultural genealogy, identifies its narrative structure, catalogs its variants and sightings across media, and situates it within the broader ecosystem of beliefs about swimming pools. The article quotes the pool chemist's definitive statement ("There is no chemical that can function as an indicator for urine in a pool") but surrounds it with the kind of contextual richness that makes the debunking *interesting* rather than merely authoritative.

This approach reflects Mikkelson's background in folklore studies, a disciplinary orientation that distinguishes Snopes's foundational work from the more politically oriented fact-checking that dominates the contemporary landscape. Mikkelson treats the myth as an artifact worthy of study, not merely an error to be corrected. Her article identifies the "sightings" — the appearances of the myth in cultural texts — with the precision of a folklorist cataloging variants of a tale type. The Orson Welles biography. The Pete & Pete episode. The Adam Sandler film. Each sighting is documented not as evidence for the claim but as evidence of its propagation, a crucial distinction that many fact-checkers fail to make.

What Mikkelson does not engage, understandably, is the deeper chemistry. The Snopes article correctly states that no such dye exists but does not explain in detail *why* — the analytical chemistry problem of selectivity in a complex organic matrix, the false-positive issue with sweat-derived urea, or the chloramine chemistry that represents the actual interaction between urine and pool water. This is not a criticism; Snopes is a general-interest fact-checking site, not an analytical chemistry journal. But it means that the reader who wants to understand the chemistry, as opposed to merely knowing the verdict, must look elsewhere.

More broadly, the pool dye myth presents a challenge for fact-checking methodology that Mikkelson navigates skillfully but that deserves explicit articulation: how do you fact-check a claim whose origins are *deliberately* fictitious? Standard fact-checking assumes that claims arise from some combination of genuine belief, misinterpretation of evidence, and motivated reasoning. The pool dye myth does not fit this model cleanly. It was not a misunderstanding that spread accidentally — it was a useful fiction propagated intentionally by people who knew it to be false. The parents who told their children about the dye were not misinformed; they were tactical. The lifeguards who reinforced the story were not ignorant of pool chemistry; they were leveraging a convenient social technology.

This means that "false" is an accurate verdict but an incomplete one. The myth is false *as a factual claim about chemistry*, but it is *functional* as a social technology. A complete account of the claim must engage both dimensions — the factual and the functional — and Mikkelson, to her credit, does this implicitly through her cultural contextualization, even if she does not theorize it explicitly.

The pool dye myth should remind fact-checkers that not all false claims are created equal. Some are errors. Some are lies told for personal gain. And some are *cooperative fictions* — socially maintained falsehoods that serve collective functions and persist not because people are fooled but because the fiction is, in some sense, preferred to the truth.

Roger Michel, whose 2000 Boston Herald article is cited by Mikkelson, captured this dynamic in his headline: "Myth of a Pool Urine Indicator Scares Pants off Reader" (Michel, R., 22 September 2000, *The Boston Herald*). The myth scares — and that is precisely the point. The fear is the product, not the dye.
