# The Placebo Effect Is Just Fake Results

## The Claim

In both scientific discourse and everyday language, the phrase "it's just the placebo effect" functions as a dismissal — an explanation that explains away rather than explains. When someone reports feeling better after a treatment that supposedly shouldn't work, the common rebuttal is: "That's just placebo." The implication is that the improvement is not real, not biological, not meaningful — that it exists only in the patient's head, and therefore doesn't count.

This dismissal is wrong in ways that are scientifically interesting and clinically important. The placebo effect is real. It produces measurable changes in brain activity, hormone levels, immune function, and symptom experience. It works even when patients know they are receiving a placebo. And understanding it correctly changes how we think about the relationship between the mind and body, about what clinical trials measure, and about what counts as genuine healing.

This entry is tagged "phenomenologically-real" — the phenomenon is genuine, demonstrable, and mechanistically studied. The error is the dismissive framing.

## What the Evidence Shows

### Placebos Produce Measurable Neurobiological Changes

The definitive neurobiological evidence for the reality of the placebo effect came from a series of elegant studies beginning in the 1970s. Jon Levine, Newton Gordon, and Howard Fields at UCSF demonstrated in 1978 that placebo analgesia — pain relief produced by taking an inert pill believed to be active — could be blocked by naloxone, a drug that antagonizes opioid receptors. If placebo analgesia were purely imaginary, a drug blocking opioid signaling should have no effect on it. But it did. This proved that placebo pain relief operates through the endogenous opioid system — the same system activated by morphine.

This finding was foundational: it demonstrated that the mind, through the mechanism of expectation, could activate real biological systems with real pharmacological consequences. Placebos don't just change how patients report their experience; they change the biology underlying that experience.

### The Wager et al. 2004 fMRI Studies

Tor Wager and colleagues at Columbia University published landmark neuroimaging studies in *Science* in 2004 showing that placebo analgesia is accompanied by distinct patterns of brain activity. When participants were told that a cream being applied to their arm would reduce pain (even though the cream was inert), they reported less pain from a subsequent heat stimulus — and their brain activity showed reduced activation in pain-processing regions including the thalamus, insula, and anterior cingulate cortex. The subjective pain reduction was mirrored by objective changes in neural processing.

Subsequent neuroimaging work has confirmed and extended this picture. Placebo responses involve:

- **Endogenous opioid release** — measurable via positron emission tomography (PET) using radioactive tracers that bind to opioid receptors; placebo administration increases opioid binding in pain-regulatory regions.
- **Dopamine activation** — particularly in Parkinson's disease, where sham treatments have been shown to trigger dopamine release in the striatum, producing measurable motor improvements.
- **Changes in neurotransmitter activity** — including serotonin and endocannabinoid systems, depending on the condition and context.

These are not imaginary changes. They are physically measurable alterations in brain chemistry.

### Immune System Modulation

The placebo effect extends beyond pain into immune function. Studies of conditioned immune responses — drawing on work by Robert Ader and Nicholas Cohen who first demonstrated immune conditioning in the 1970s — show that the immune system can be trained to respond to a neutral stimulus (a conditioned placebo) with real immunological changes.

In autoimmune disease research, patients who have learned to associate an immunosuppressive drug with a distinctive taste can later show immune suppression in response to the taste cue alone — even when receiving a placebo. This has been demonstrated in lupus models and in human patients, with measurable reductions in white blood cell counts and immune activity markers. The conditioning operates through neural pathways connecting the brain to the immune system, including the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis and sympathetic nervous system innervation of lymphoid organs.

### Open-Label Placebos

Perhaps the most surprising finding in recent placebo research is that **open-label placebos** — placebos given to patients who are explicitly told the pills contain no active ingredient — still produce significant therapeutic effects. This challenges the intuitive model of placebo as operating through deception.

Ted Kaptchuk's group at Harvard has been the primary research team investigating open-label placebos. A 2010 study published in *PLOS ONE* found that open-label placebo pills, given to patients with irritable bowel syndrome who were told the pills were inert but that "placebo effects can be powerful," produced significant improvements in symptom severity compared to no treatment. A 2016 study published in *PAIN* showed similar results in patients with chronic lower back pain.

The magnitude of effect in these studies is not trivial — patients show 30% improvement in symptom ratings or more, improvements comparable to some active drug treatments in the same conditions. And these patients *knew* they were taking placebos.

How does this work if the mechanism is expectation? Several explanations have been proposed: conditioning responses (the act of taking pills is itself conditioned to therapeutic responses through prior medical experiences), therapeutic alliance with the treating clinician (which persists regardless of what the pill contains), and the possibility that explicit knowledge that a pill is inert does not fully override implicit neurobiological expectation systems. The mechanisms remain partially understood, but the phenomenon is robustly replicated.

### Placebo Effects Across Conditions

The placebo effect has been documented across a remarkable range of conditions:

**Pain:** Most extensively studied. Placebo analgesia involves opioid, dopamine, and endocannabinoid pathways. Context, ritual, practitioner warmth, and expectation magnitude all modulate the effect size.

**Parkinson's disease:** Sham deep brain stimulation and inert injections produce measurable dopamine release and motor improvement. Studies by de la Fuente-Fernández and colleagues using PET imaging confirmed real striatal dopamine release.

**Depression:** Meta-analyses of antidepressant trials show that approximately half to two-thirds of the antidepressant effect measured in clinical trials is shared with placebo arms. This does not mean antidepressants are placebos — the drugs have real additional effects — but it demonstrates that the expectation of improvement generates substantial neurobiological change.

**Asthma:** Sham bronchodilators produce measurable improvements in lung function and patient-reported symptoms, though smaller than active drugs.

**Irritable bowel syndrome:** One of the conditions most studied for open-label placebo effects. Kaptchuk's group has demonstrated robust placebo responses involving changes in autonomic nervous system activity.

## The Correct Way to Think About Placebo

### "It's Not the Drug, It's the Placebo" ≠ "The Improvement Isn't Real"

This conflation is the core error. When we say that a treatment's benefit is "just placebo," we sometimes mean that the benefit is not due to the treatment's pharmacological action. That is a valid scientific point relevant to understanding mechanism. But it has been extended to mean that the benefit itself is illusory or invalid — and that is wrong.

If a patient reports significantly less pain, moves more freely, and has measurably lower blood pressure after a placebo treatment, those outcomes are real. The mechanism is psychological and neurobiological (expectation activating opioid systems), but the outcome — reduced pain, better function — is genuine. Telling the patient that their improvement "doesn't count" because it was mediated by expectation rather than a pharmacological molecule is both scientifically inaccurate and clinically harmful.

The deeper point is that the mechanism/outcome distinction is not unique to placebo. We do not dismiss the analgesic effects of morphine because the mechanism (opioid receptor agonism) involves subjective pain perception — which is, after all, also a mental event. Pain is always a brain state. Whether that brain state is altered by an opioid molecule or by an expectation that activates the brain's own opioid system, the outcome — less pain — is equally real. The placebo effect involves a different route to the same destination, not a counterfeit destination.

### What Placebo Effects Reveal About the Mind-Body Interface

The placebo effect is one of the most powerful demonstrations available that the mind and body are not separable systems. Expectation, learned associations, social context, and symbolic meaning can activate real biological pathways — opioid, dopamine, immune, autonomic — and produce measurable changes in physiological function. This is not mysticism; it is neuroscience.

The study of placebo effects has become a serious scientific enterprise, contributing to our understanding of:
- The neurobiology of expectation and prediction
- Pain modulation systems
- The role of therapeutic ritual and context in healing
- Conditioning and the immune system
- The neurobiological basis of hope

Far from being a nuisance variable to be minimized in clinical trials, the placebo effect is an active research area with genuine insights to offer.

Fabrizio Benedetti, one of the most prolific placebo researchers, has framed the field's project as mapping the psychobiology of the patient-clinician relationship: every interaction in which a person believes a treatment might help activates neurobiological systems shaped by learning, expectation, and context. These systems are the evolved infrastructure of healing behavior, present because organisms that responded to social signals of safety and care with physiological down-regulation of stress and pain responses had survival advantages. The placebo effect is not a quirk or an artifact; it is a feature of vertebrate nervous system architecture.

Kirsch's work on expectancy theory — the hypothesis that expectancy, not classical conditioning, is the primary driver of placebo effects — suggests that what matters most is the conscious or non-conscious anticipation of a particular outcome. This framing integrates placebo with the broader literature on expectation in the brain: predictive processing accounts of neural function treat the brain as a prediction machine that constantly generates expectations and updates them based on sensory feedback. Placebo effects may be understood as the somatic dimension of top-down predictive control: the brain predicts relief, activates the biological systems associated with relief, and the body responds.

### What Placebo Doesn't Do

Important caveats: placebo effects are not unlimited. They are most robust for subjective symptoms — pain, nausea, fatigue, mood, anxiety — where there is a direct pathway from expectation and brain state to symptom experience. They are weaker for physiological parameters that are less directly modulated by the central nervous system. Placebo does not cure bacterial infections, reverse diabetes, or shrink tumors (though there are rare, contested case reports of tumor regression under extraordinary psychological circumstances). 

Placebo analgesia does not produce surgical anesthesia. The distinction matters: placebos can reduce a patient's *experience* of pain through endogenous analgesic systems, but those systems are not potent enough to make surgery safe. The power of placebo is real and substantial; it is also bounded.

The boundary between "subjective symptoms" and "objective disease" is not sharp, however. Many chronic conditions involve both subjective symptom burden and objectively measurable physiological pathology. In conditions like irritable bowel syndrome, fibromyalgia, and functional neurological disorder, the relationship between the measurable physiology and the patient's reported experience is complex and not fully understood. Placebo effects in these conditions may be particularly large precisely because the symptom experience itself has a large neurobiological component that responds to expectation and context. This does not mean these are "all in the head" in the pejorative sense; it means the nervous system is a significant part of the causal pathway, and placebo mechanisms operate through the nervous system.

### The Ethics of Placebo

Understanding that placebos produce real effects raises genuine ethical questions for clinical practice. The traditional use of "disguised placebos" — giving patients inert treatments while leading them to believe the treatments are active — involves deception and violates informed consent principles. The open-label placebo research is important precisely because it suggests that the deception may not be necessary for the biological effect to occur, opening a path toward ethical use of placebo effects in clinical care.

Some clinicians argue for "honest placebos" — treatments described accurately to patients but designed to maximize contextual healing effects through ritual, attention, warmth, and expectation. Whether and how to deploy placebo mechanisms ethically in clinical practice is an active area of medical ethics discussion.

Kaptchuk's open-label placebo research suggests a specific ethical path: telling patients truthfully that they are receiving a placebo, explaining what the research shows about placebo mechanisms, and inviting them to engage with the process. The surprising finding that this approach still produces significant therapeutic effects opens a space for deliberate, honest deployment of contextual healing mechanisms without deception. The clinical implication is that the therapeutic relationship, the ritual of the clinical encounter, the act of receiving care and forming an expectation of improvement, are themselves treatments — not adjuncts to treatment. They can be designed, optimized, and provided with the same intentionality as pharmacological prescribing. This is a significant reconceptualization of what medicine does and how, and it remains underexplored in clinical training and practice.

## Why the Myth Persists

### Language Habits in Science

Scientific language has encoded the dismissive interpretation. In clinical trial design, the goal is to exceed "placebo-controlled" baselines — treatments are tested against placebo, and a drug that doesn't beat placebo is considered ineffective. This framing, necessary for rigorous evaluation of pharmacological effects, has trained researchers and clinicians to treat placebo as the zero point, the "nothing," the baseline to exceed. The everyday language follows: "just placebo" means "just zero."

This linguistic habit is understandable as a product of clinical trial methodology, but it has propagated a category error: the zero baseline of drug-vs.-placebo comparison has been mistaken for a claim about the absolute magnitude of the placebo response. When a trial shows that a drug produces 40% improvement and placebo produces 30% improvement, the "drug beat placebo by 10 percentage points" framing is accurate for the drug's marginal contribution. But the 30% improvement in the placebo arm is not zero — it is 30%, which may represent substantial clinical benefit. Calling that 30% response "just placebo" conflates the drug's incremental margin with the placebo arm's absolute value.

This conflation has clinical consequences. Physicians who have internalized the "just placebo" dismissal may be less attentive to the contextual factors — warmth, clear explanation, demonstrated care, expectation-setting — that shape the magnitude of placebo response in their own practice. Every clinical encounter has a placebo component, and the magnitude of that component varies based on factors the clinician controls. The research on placebo mechanisms is directly relevant to clinical practice, not just to drug development.

### Conceptual Cartesianism

Western medicine has historically operated with a Cartesian separation of mind and body, where "real" illness is physical and "psychological" illness is somehow less real or less worthy of treatment. Within this framework, a response driven by "psychological" mechanisms (expectation) is categorized as not-real. The placebo effect challenges this framework directly, and the challenge has been slow to propagate through medical culture.

The Cartesian framework has deep institutional roots. The structure of medical specialization separates neurology from psychiatry, psychiatry from internal medicine, and behavioral medicine from all of the above — institutional divisions that reflect and reinforce the view that mind-mediated effects are a distinct category, operating through lesser or less legitimate mechanisms than molecular pharmacology. The discovery that placebo analgesia operates through the same opioid receptors as morphine is a direct empirical challenge to this hierarchy of mechanisms. Opioid receptor activation by endogenous peptides triggered by expectation is not less real than opioid receptor activation by exogenous morphine molecules. The pathway to the receptor is different; the receptor and its effects are the same.

The pace of change in medical culture on this question has been faster in pain medicine and psychiatry than in other fields, partly because these are the domains where the evidence for contextual effects is strongest and the limitations of purely pharmacological approaches most evident. The biopsychosocial model — developed by George Engel in the 1970s and influential in medical education since the 1980s — was a deliberate attempt to move medicine beyond Cartesian dualism, with mixed success at the level of clinical practice. Placebo research provides some of the strongest empirical ammunition for the biopsychosocial model's claims about the irreducibility of psychological factors in physical disease.

### Misuse by Alternative Medicine

The placebo effect has been heavily recruited by proponents of alternative medicine practices as a defense: "Even if the mechanism is placebo, the patient feels better, and that's what matters." This is partly true but overextended — it is used to justify any practice regardless of evidence, and to resist investigation of specific mechanisms. The association of honest placebo science with the defense of pseudoscience has made some researchers reluctant to emphasize placebo potency, for fear of being misunderstood as endorsing ineffective treatments.

The misuse creates a real communication problem. Placebo researchers who document large, real placebo effects find their work excerpted selectively by homeopathy advocates, crystal healing practitioners, and other alternative medicine proponents who use it to argue that their treatments' benefits — attributable to placebo — are real and therefore the treatments are vindicated. The argument conflates two things: (1) the placebo effect is real and produces genuine benefit, and (2) treatments that work only through placebo are therefore legitimate and worth promoting. The first is supported; the second does not follow. Placebo-only treatments carry costs: financial cost to the patient, risk of delay in seeking effective treatment for serious conditions, and the epistemic cost of a medical culture that cannot distinguish mechanism from outcome.

The most defensible position — that placebo effects are real, clinically significant, and deserve serious study, while treatments that work exclusively through placebo should be evaluated against ethical alternatives that deploy the same mechanisms without deception — is nuanced enough to be misrepresented by advocates on multiple sides. Researchers who emphasize placebo potency get misread as validating pseudoscience; researchers who emphasize that placebo-only treatments shouldn't be prescribed get misread as denying the reality of the placebo effect. The misuse problem partly explains why the honest science has been slow to enter clinical culture.

### The Medical Paternalism of Dismissal

There is also a paternalistic dimension to the "just placebo" dismissal that deserves acknowledgment. When a physician tells a patient that their improvement after a treatment was "just the placebo effect," the implication is that the improvement wasn't really what the patient thought it was — that the patient's experience of their own body is being overridden by the physician's theoretical framework. If a patient reports significantly reduced pain, improved mobility, and better sleep following a treatment, and if those outcomes are real and measurable, the physician's insistence that the mechanism was placebo-mediated does not negate the patient's experience. It explains the mechanism, which is a different thing.

The dismissal can be particularly harmful in conditions where subjective symptom improvement is the primary clinical outcome — chronic pain, irritable bowel syndrome, depression, fatigue disorders. In these conditions, the patient's reported experience is the condition. If placebo mechanisms can produce real improvement in that experience, understanding and deploying those mechanisms is not a lesser form of medicine; it is medicine doing what medicine is for. The dismissal of these improvements as "not real" reflects a category mistake about what counts as real in the context of symptom-based illness.

## Summary

**The claim:** The placebo effect is "just" fake or imaginary results — a psychological artifact with no real biological content.

**The verdict:** Wrong. Placebo effects produce real, measurable neurobiological changes: endogenous opioid and dopamine release (confirmed by naloxone blockade and PET imaging), changes in brain activity during pain processing (fMRI), immune system modulation through conditioning, and measurable improvements in physiological parameters. Open-label placebos produce significant therapeutic effects even when patients know the pills are inert. Wager et al. 2004 and subsequent neuroimaging studies have documented the neural signatures of placebo analgesia. "It's the placebo effect" correctly identifies the mechanism; it does not correctly imply that the improvement isn't real.

The appropriate conclusion is that the expectation of healing — shaped by ritual, relationship, and context — is a genuinely active biological process. Understanding this should make medicine more humble about what it means to heal, more attentive to the therapeutic context in which treatments are delivered, and more willing to study what it cannot fully explain.

### Why This Matters Beyond Academic Debate

The practical stakes of getting this right are not trivial. Several downstream consequences flow from the prevailing dismissal of placebo:

**Clinical trial design:** The standard two-arm trial (drug vs. placebo) measures a drug's marginal contribution over and above contextual healing effects. This is appropriate for regulatory purposes, but it means that therapies that work primarily or entirely through contextual mechanisms — some psychotherapies, many surgical procedures, much of general practice medicine — are poorly evaluated by this framework. The placebo-controlled RCT, necessary for drug evaluation, is not necessarily the right tool for evaluating context-dependent therapeutic interventions. This is increasingly recognized in the trial methodology literature, with adaptive designs and novel comparator arms being developed.

**Surgical placebos and overtreatment:** Several major surgical procedures have been tested against sham surgery (where patients are sedated, an incision is made, and sutured closed, but the actual surgical intervention is not performed) and found to be no more effective than the sham. Arthroscopic surgery for knee osteoarthritis is the most prominent example, following a landmark 2002 trial by Moseley et al. Vertebroplasty for spinal compression fractures was similarly challenged by sham-controlled trials. These findings suggest that some surgical procedures are delivering primarily placebo effects — powerful effects, but not attributable to the specific anatomical intervention. The appropriate response is not to ban these procedures immediately, but to understand what they are delivering and whether equivalent benefit could be obtained through less invasive contextual interventions.

**The therapeutic alliance:** Research on psychotherapy outcomes consistently shows that the quality of the therapeutic relationship — the alliance between patient and therapist — is one of the strongest predictors of outcome, often stronger than the specific technique used. This is a large-scale placebo-adjacent effect: the expectation, care, attention, and meaning that the therapeutic relationship provides are the mechanism. Different orientations of therapy (CBT, psychodynamic, humanistic) have broadly similar overall outcomes, a finding called the "Dodo bird verdict" (all have won and all shall have prizes), consistent with relationship factors being primary. Training clinicians in the skills that produce strong therapeutic alliances is therefore not "teaching placebo" as if that were embarrassing — it is training clinicians in the mechanisms that make therapy work.

**Global health and traditional medicine:** In low-resource settings, where access to pharmacological treatments is limited, contextual healing mechanisms are not adjuncts to medicine; they may be the primary resource available. Traditional healing practices that deploy ritual, community, narrative, expectation, and the healing relationship are deploying placebo mechanisms. Understanding these mechanisms scientifically — rather than dismissing them as superstition or endorsing their specific disease theories — provides a more productive framework for global health practice. The goal is to identify what components of traditional healing practices produce genuine benefit through documented mechanisms, and to preserve and build on those components while declining to endorse accompanying disease theories that are not supported.
