# "The Placebo Effect Is 'Just' Imaginary — It Doesn't Involve Real Physiological Changes"


## 1. The Claim

The assertion under review holds that placebo effects are "merely psychological," "all in the head," or "just imaginary"—implying that while patients may *report* feeling better after receiving inactive treatments, no genuine physiological changes occur in their bodies. This perspective treats placebo responses as artifacts of subjective reporting rather than legitimate biological phenomena involving measurable alterations in brain chemistry, hormone levels, immune function, or pain processing networks.

The claim typically emerges in medical contexts where placebo effects are dismissed as confounding variables to be controlled rather than therapeutic mechanisms to be understood. It reflects a mind-body dualism that separates psychological states from physiological processes, suggesting that "real" medical effects must involve direct biochemical intervention rather than expectation-mediated biological cascades.

**Contemporary manifestations** of this dismissive attitude appear across medical education, clinical practice, and public health discourse. Medical students often learn about placebos primarily as research controls rather than therapeutic phenomena, with limited exposure to the extensive literature on placebo physiology. Clinical practitioners may dismiss patient improvements that cannot be attributed to specific pharmacological mechanisms, viewing them as "not real medicine" or "just psychological."

Insurance systems and healthcare policies frequently exclude treatments whose primary mechanisms appear to be placebo-mediated, even when these treatments produce measurable clinical outcomes. This institutional skepticism reflects deeper assumptions about what constitutes legitimate medical intervention versus "non-specific" effects that supposedly lack biological validity.

**Research culture** within evidence-based medicine sometimes treats placebo responses as statistical noise to be eliminated rather than biological signals to be understood. Clinical trials routinely subtract placebo group improvements from active treatment effects, potentially overlooking valuable insights about mind-body healing mechanisms. This methodological approach, while scientifically appropriate for determining drug-specific effects, may inadvertently reinforce dismissive attitudes toward placebo phenomena.

Public health messaging often warns against "unscientific" treatments that work primarily through placebo mechanisms, without acknowledging that these mechanisms themselves represent scientifically validated biological processes. This communication strategy may protect consumers from ineffective treatments but also propagates misconceptions about the relationship between psychological and physiological aspects of healing.

**Pharmaceutical industry perspectives** on placebo effects often focus on them as obstacles to drug approval rather than insights into human healing capacity. Clinical trials that show large placebo responses may be viewed as failed experiments rather than successful demonstrations of mind-body interaction. This commercial framing influences research priorities and funding allocation in ways that may limit investigation of placebo mechanisms.


## 2. What's Actually True

The claim exhibits several problematic assumptions that require unpacking:

**Assumption 1: "Imaginary" equals non-physiological.** This creates a false dichotomy between psychological and biological processes, ignoring that all mental states have neural correlates and that expectation itself is a brain-based process with measurable consequences.

**Assumption 2: Subjective improvement without direct chemical intervention cannot be "real."** This dismisses the well-documented ability of psychological states to trigger physiological cascades, including hormone release, neurotransmitter activity, and immune system modulation.

**Assumption 3: Placebo effects are uniform phenomena.** In reality, placebo responses vary dramatically across conditions, individuals, and contexts, with some involving clear physiological mechanisms while others may indeed be primarily perceptual.

The claim's framing as either "real" or "imaginary" oversimplifies a complex phenomenon that operates across multiple biological systems and involves measurable changes in brain activity, biochemistry, and physiological function.

**Definitional problems** compound the fidelity issues. The term "imaginary" implies non-existence rather than mind-mediated causation. Brain imaging demonstrates that imagination itself involves measurable neural activity with downstream physiological consequences. Expecting pain relief triggers activity in prefrontal and anterior cingulate cortex regions that send inhibitory signals to spinal cord pain processing centers—a cascade of real neural events, not imaginary processes.

Similarly, the distinction between "psychological" and "physiological" becomes meaningless when psychological states consistently produce measurable physiological changes. The claim's implicit dualism assumes that mind and body operate independently, contradicting decades of neuroscientific research demonstrating their functional integration.

**Mechanistic oversimplification** appears in assumptions that legitimate medical effects must involve direct drug-receptor interactions rather than more complex biological cascades. This perspective ignores that most therapeutic effects involve multiple steps between initial intervention and clinical outcome. Placebo responses demonstrate that these cascades can be initiated through expectation and conditioning rather than only through exogenous chemical administration.

The claim also conflates placebo effects with placebo treatments, failing to distinguish between the biological mechanisms (placebo effects) and the clinical interventions (placebo treatments) that trigger them. Dismissing placebo effects as imaginary would logically require dismissing all psychological contributions to healing, including psychotherapy, behavioral interventions, and mind-body approaches with documented efficacy.


Multiple lines of research demonstrate that placebo effects involve measurable physiological changes across diverse biological systems:

**Pain and Analgesia Research:** Jon-Kar Zubieta and colleagues at the University of Michigan published landmark research in the *Journal of Neuroscience* (2005) showing that placebo analgesia triggers the release of endogenous opioids in the brain. Using PET scanning with radioligand tracers, they demonstrated increased binding of the body's natural painkillers to opioid receptors in pain-processing regions during placebo treatment. Participants who showed greater placebo responses had higher levels of opioid release, providing direct biochemical evidence for the physiological basis of placebo pain relief.

**Extended pain research** has revealed multiple mechanisms underlying placebo analgesia. Some studies show opioid-mediated responses that can be blocked by naloxone, while others demonstrate non-opioid mechanisms involving dopamine, serotonin, and GABA systems. This diversity suggests that different types of placebo interventions may activate distinct neurochemical pathways.

Spinal cord imaging during placebo treatments reveals reduced activity in pain transmission neurons, demonstrating that placebo effects operate at multiple levels of the nervous system. These findings contradict claims that placebo responses involve only higher-order cognitive processes without affecting peripheral pain mechanisms.

**Parkinson's Disease Studies:** Fabrizio Benedetti's research group has repeatedly shown that placebo treatments for Parkinson's symptoms trigger measurable dopamine release in the striatum, the brain region most affected by the disease. Using PET imaging, they documented that patients expecting improvement from sham treatments showed increased dopamine activity comparable to that produced by actual anti-Parkinson's medications. This finding is because Parkinson's involves well-understood neurochemical deficits, making placebo-induced changes directly observable.

**Motor function studies** in Parkinson's patients reveal that placebo responses can improve objective measures including movement speed, rigidity, and bradykinesia—outcomes that cannot be explained by subjective reporting bias. Video analysis and electronic motion sensors confirm that expectation-induced dopamine release produces real improvements in motor control.

The Parkinson's research has been extended to other movement disorders and neurological conditions, consistently showing that placebo responses involve the same neurotransmitter systems affected by the diseases themselves. This pattern suggests that placebo mechanisms access therapeutic pathways through top-down neural control rather than requiring external pharmacological intervention.

**Brain Imaging of Placebo Analgesia:** Tor Wager and colleagues at Columbia University published influential research in *Science* (2004) using fMRI to map brain activity during placebo pain relief. They found that placebo treatments reduced activity in the anterior cingulate cortex, thalamus, and insula—key regions in the brain's pain processing network. Importantly, the degree of brain activity reduction correlated with participants' reported pain relief, showing that subjective improvements matched objective neural changes.

**Network connectivity analysis** of placebo responses reveals coordinated changes across multiple brain systems. Expectation activates prefrontal control regions that modulate activity in limbic emotional centers and sensory processing areas. This distributed neural response demonstrates that placebo effects involve complex brain-wide coordination rather than localized changes in single regions.

Real-time neurofeedback studies show that individuals can learn to enhance their placebo responses by observing their own brain activity, providing additional evidence that these responses involve trainable biological processes. This research suggests potential therapeutic applications for enhancing mind-body healing through neurofeedback-assisted placebo training.

**Immune System Modulation:** Research by Ader and Cohen beginning in the 1970s established that placebo effects can influence immune function through conditioned responses. More recent work has shown that placebo treatments can affect inflammatory markers, antibody production, and cytokine levels—demonstrating that expectation-based responses extend beyond the nervous system to impact immune function.

**Psychoneuroimmunology research** has documented specific pathways through which psychological states influence immune function. Stress hormones like cortisol, neurotransmitters like norepinephrine, and neuropeptides like substance P all serve as communication molecules between nervous and immune systems. Placebo responses can modulate these signaling pathways, producing measurable changes in immune cell activity.

Clinical studies of placebo effects in autoimmune conditions like rheumatoid arthritis and inflammatory bowel disease show improvements in objective measures including joint swelling, laboratory inflammatory markers, and tissue inflammation visible on imaging studies. These outcomes cannot be attributed to subjective perception changes alone.

**Cardiovascular Effects:** Studies have documented placebo-induced changes in heart rate, blood pressure, and cardiac output, with some research showing that these effects can be blocked by specific pharmacological agents, indicating involvement of identifiable physiological pathways.

**Autonomic nervous system research** reveals that placebo responses can influence both sympathetic and parasympathetic activity. Heart rate variability analysis shows that relaxation-based placebo interventions increase parasympathetic tone, while stimulating placebos can activate sympathetic responses. These changes occur automatically without conscious control, demonstrating involuntary physiological mechanisms.

Blood pressure studies using ambulatory monitoring show that placebo treatments for hypertension can produce sustained reductions in blood pressure outside clinical settings. The magnitude and duration of these effects suggest involvement of physiological regulatory mechanisms rather than temporary psychological influences.

**Open-Label Placebo Research:** Perhaps most remarkably, Ted Kaptchuk's research group at Harvard has demonstrated that even when patients are explicitly told they're receiving placebos—so-called "open-label placebos"—they still show physiological improvements in conditions like irritable bowel syndrome. This finding suggests that placebo mechanisms operate at levels below conscious awareness and involve genuine biological processes rather than mere expectation or deception.

**Open-label studies** challenge traditional assumptions about the necessity of deception for placebo effects. Patients who knowingly receive placebo treatments still show improvements in objective measures including bowel movement frequency, abdominal pain ratings, and quality of life scores. These findings suggest that ritual, attention, and therapeutic context can trigger healing responses independent of specific expectation content.

Neuroimaging during open-label placebo treatments reveals brain activity patterns similar to those seen with concealed placebos, indicating that conscious knowledge of treatment inactivity doesn't prevent physiological responses. This research supports models emphasizing automatic conditioning and contextual healing responses over conscious expectation as primary mechanisms.

**Nocebo Effects:** The flip side of placebo responses—nocebo effects where negative expectations cause measurable harm—provides additional evidence for physiological mechanisms. Research shows that nocebo responses can trigger stress hormone release, immune suppression, and cardiovascular changes, demonstrating that psychological states can produce objective biological consequences in both positive and negative directions.

**Nocebo research** demonstrates that negative expectations can worsen pain, increase side effects, and even cause tissue damage in some cases. These harmful effects involve the same biological systems as beneficial placebo responses but operate in reverse. The symmetry between placebo and nocebo effects supports biological rather than purely psychological explanations.

Studies of medication side effects show that patients who expect adverse reactions are more likely to experience them, with objective measures confirming increased symptom severity. This research has practical implications for informed consent procedures and patient counseling in clinical settings.

**Neurochemical Pathway Studies:** Research using pharmacological blocking agents has revealed that different types of placebo responses operate through distinct biochemical pathways. Placebo analgesia can be blocked by naloxone (an opioid antagonist) in some cases but not others, suggesting that some placebo pain relief works through endogenous opioid systems while other mechanisms involve non-opioid pathways. Similarly, placebo effects on anxiety and depression involve serotonin and dopamine systems that can be selectively blocked by specific medications.

**Receptor-specific research** has mapped how different placebo interventions activate distinct neurotransmitter systems. Analgesic placebos may involve mu-opioid receptors, while antidepressant placebos affect serotonin and norepinephrine systems. This specificity suggests that placebo mechanisms can target particular therapeutic pathways depending on the condition and treatment context.

The convergent evidence from these diverse research approaches demonstrates that placebo effects involve real, measurable physiological changes mediated by identifiable biological mechanisms. While the triggers may be psychological—expectation, conditioning, therapeutic context—the downstream effects operate through the same neurochemical and physiological systems targeted by conventional medications.

**Temporal dynamics** of placebo responses reveal both immediate neurochemical changes (within minutes) and longer-term adaptations (over days to weeks) that mirror the timecourse of conventional drug treatments. This temporal complexity demonstrates that placebo effects involve both acute biochemical responses and sustained physiological modifications.

**Dose-response relationships** in placebo research show that stronger expectation manipulations generally produce larger physiological effects, following patterns similar to conventional drug pharmacology. This relationship provides additional evidence for systematic biological mechanisms rather than random psychological fluctuations.


## 3. Why People Believe This

The scientific literature on placebo mechanisms spans neuroscience, endocrinology, immunology, and pain research, utilizing objective measurement techniques including neuroimaging, biochemical assays, and physiological monitoring. Key methodological approaches include:

**Neuroimaging studies** using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), positron emission tomography (PET), and electroencephalography (EEG) to map brain activity changes during placebo responses.

**Biochemical analysis** measuring levels of endogenous opioids, dopamine, serotonin, cortisol, and other neurotransmitters and hormones before, during, and after placebo administration.

**Pharmacological blocking studies** using specific receptor antagonists (like naloxone for opioid receptors) to determine whether placebo effects operate through identifiable neurochemical pathways.

**Physiological monitoring** tracking heart rate, blood pressure, skin conductance, immune markers, and other objective measures of biological function during placebo treatments.

**Double-blind, placebo-controlled designs** that separate placebo responses from natural recovery, regression to the mean, and researcher bias.

This methodological diversity provides converging evidence from multiple angles, strengthening confidence in findings about placebo physiology while revealing the complexity of mind-body interactions in therapeutic contexts.

**Advanced neuroimaging techniques** have revolutionized understanding of placebo mechanisms by enabling real-time observation of brain activity during expectation and response phases. Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) scanning can track specific neurotransmitter systems, while magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) measures brain chemistry changes at the molecular level.

Multimodal imaging approaches combining structural, functional, and neurochemical measurements provide comprehensive pictures of placebo-induced brain changes. These studies reveal that placebo responses involve coordinated activity across multiple brain networks, including cognitive control systems, emotional processing centers, and sensory perception areas.

**Longitudinal study designs** track placebo responses over time, revealing that some effects involve immediate neurochemical changes while others develop through longer-term conditioning processes. This temporal complexity demonstrates that placebo mechanisms operate across multiple timescales and biological systems.

**Genetic studies** have identified polymorphisms associated with placebo responsivity, particularly in genes affecting dopamine, serotonin, and opioid systems. This research suggests that individual differences in placebo sensitivity reflect measurable biological variation rather than psychological suggestibility alone.

**Cross-species research** using animal models has demonstrated placebo-like responses in non-human subjects, providing additional evidence for biological rather than purely cognitive mechanisms. Conditioned immune responses and learned pain modulation in laboratory animals suggest that placebo phenomena involve evolutionary conserved biological processes.


## 4. Verdict

**AUGMENTED UNDERSTANDING REQUIRED**

The claim that placebo effects are "just imaginary" and lack physiological basis is contradicted by extensive scientific evidence demonstrating measurable biological changes during placebo responses. However, the claim contains a grain of truth that requires careful augmentation rather than simple rejection.

**What's True:** Placebo effects are indeed triggered by psychological processes—expectation, conditioning, therapeutic context—rather than direct biochemical intervention from active compounds.

**What's Incomplete:** The claim fails to recognize that psychological processes *are* biological processes, mediated by brain activity and capable of triggering measurable changes in neurotransmitter release, hormone levels, immune function, and physiological responses.

**Evidence overwhelming contradicts the "just imaginary" characterization:**
- Neuroimaging studies showing brain activity changes during placebo responses
- Biochemical measurements revealing neurotransmitter and hormone release
- Pharmacological blocking studies demonstrating specific receptor involvement
- Physiological monitoring documenting changes in heart rate, blood pressure, and immune function
- Cross-species research showing placebo-like responses in animal models
- Genetic studies linking placebo responsivity to measurable biological variation
- Open-label placebo research showing effects persist even without deception

**Evidence supporting the complexity of placebo mechanisms:**
- Different types of placebo responses involve distinct neurochemical pathways
- Individual differences in placebo sensitivity reflect genetic and neurobiological variation
- Placebo effects show dose-response relationships similar to conventional drugs
- Temporal dynamics mirror those of pharmacological interventions
- Effects extend beyond subjective symptoms to objective clinical measures

**The Augmented Reality:** Placebo effects represent a fascinating intersection of psychology and physiology, demonstrating the brain's remarkable ability to translate expectations into biological changes through well-characterized neurochemical pathways. Rather than being "merely" psychological, placebo responses illustrate the profound integration of mind and body in human health and healing.

The phenomenon deserves recognition as a legitimate area of medical research and potential therapeutic application, while maintaining appropriate scientific rigor in distinguishing placebo mechanisms from other forms of treatment efficacy.

**Implications for medical practice and research:**
- Therapeutic context, expectation, and relationship quality are active treatment components
- Clinical trial design must account for measurable placebo effects as active comparators
- Personalized medicine approaches could optimize individual placebo sensitivity
- Ethical frameworks must balance transparency with preservation of therapeutic benefit
- Integration of mind-body approaches requires understanding of underlying biological mechanisms

The evidence overwhelmingly supports viewing placebo effects as real physiological phenomena triggered by psychological processes, rather than imaginary responses lacking biological basis. This understanding opens new avenues for therapeutic enhancement while maintaining scientific integrity in medical research and practice.

**Educational and clinical applications:**
- Medical training should include comprehensive coverage of placebo mechanisms and clinical applications
- Patient communication strategies can ethically harness placebo mechanisms to optimize therapeutic outcomes
- Healthcare policies should recognize placebo-mediated benefits as legitimate therapeutic value
- Research priorities should include investigation of placebo enhancement techniques and personalization strategies

The placebo effect represents one of the most compelling examples of mind-body integration in human biology, demonstrating that the distinction between "psychological" and "physiological" effects is often artificial and counterproductive. Understanding these mechanisms provides insights into human healing capacity while supporting evidence-based approaches to optimizing therapeutic interventions across diverse medical contexts.

## 5. The Wider Picture

Understanding placebo mechanisms has profound implications for medical practice, research methodology, and our conception of mind-body relationships:

**Clinical Practice:** Recognition of placebo physiology has led to increased attention to therapeutic context, doctor-patient relationships, and treatment environment as active components of healing rather than mere background factors. Some clinicians now explicitly harness placebo mechanisms through careful attention to expectation, ritual, and therapeutic alliance.

**Patient communication strategies** have evolved to incorporate understanding of placebo mechanisms without deception. Providers increasingly recognize that positive expectation, treatment confidence, and therapeutic relationship quality can enhance outcomes for any medical intervention. This knowledge informs approaches to informed consent, treatment explanation, and follow-up care.

Medical education is beginning to include placebo research in curricula addressing mind-body medicine, patient communication, and holistic approaches to healing. This educational shift helps future providers understand the biological basis of psychological influences on health while maintaining scientific rigor.

**Research Design:** The discovery of measurable placebo physiology has complicated clinical trial interpretation, as "placebo-controlled" studies may actually involve comparing active treatments to other active treatments (placebo mechanisms) rather than to truly inert controls. This has led to more sophisticated research designs and greater attention to placebo response variability.

**Three-arm trial designs** including both placebo and no-treatment control groups can separate placebo-specific effects from natural history and regression to the mean. These studies provide more complete pictures of treatment efficacy by measuring both drug-specific and placebo-mediated contributions to clinical outcomes.

Adaptive trial designs that account for individual placebo responsivity may enable more personalized treatment approaches. Patients who show strong placebo responses might benefit from different therapeutic strategies than those with minimal placebo sensitivity.

**Personalized Medicine:** Individual differences in placebo responsivity—linked to genetics, personality traits, brain structure, and prior experiences—suggest that placebo mechanisms could be tailored to individual patients, potentially enhancing therapeutic outcomes through personalized expectation management.

**Genetic testing** for placebo-relevant polymorphisms may eventually enable prediction of individual placebo sensitivity. Patients with genotypes associated with strong placebo responses might benefit from interventions that enhance expectation and therapeutic context, while those with low placebo sensitivity might require different approaches.

Neuroimaging-based biomarkers of placebo responsivity could guide treatment selection by identifying individuals most likely to benefit from mind-body interventions. This personalized approach could optimize therapeutic outcomes while minimizing side effects and costs.

**Ethical Considerations:** The reality of placebo physiology raises complex questions about whether deliberately triggering placebo responses through deception or incomplete disclosure is ethically justified, leading to exploration of transparent approaches like open-label placebos.

**Informed consent procedures** must balance honesty about placebo mechanisms with preservation of therapeutic benefit. Research on open-label placebos suggests that transparency about placebo effects doesn't necessarily eliminate them, potentially resolving some ethical dilemmas about deception in therapeutic contexts.

Healthcare policy debates increasingly address whether treatments that work primarily through placebo mechanisms should be covered by insurance systems, given evidence that these mechanisms involve real physiological processes with measurable clinical benefits.

**Neuroscientific Understanding:** Placebo research has advanced understanding of top-down brain processes, showing how cognitive and emotional states can influence perception, pain processing, motor function, and autonomic nervous system activity through descending neural pathways.

**Brain network research** reveals how placebo responses involve coordination between prefrontal control systems, limbic emotional centers, and sensory processing regions. This network perspective has influenced broader understanding of how psychological interventions can produce physiological benefits across diverse medical conditions.

Studies of placebo mechanisms have contributed to models of predictive processing in the brain, where expectations actively shape perception and physiological responses rather than passively interpreting sensory input. This theoretical framework has implications extending far beyond placebo research.

**Complementary Medicine Integration:** Recognition that placebo mechanisms involve genuine physiological processes has informed more nuanced evaluation of alternative and complementary therapies, distinguishing between treatments that work primarily through placebo mechanisms versus those with additional specific effects.

**Evidence-based evaluation** of complementary approaches now considers placebo-mediated benefits as legitimate therapeutic outcomes while still distinguishing them from treatment-specific effects. This approach enables more comprehensive assessment of therapeutic value across diverse healing traditions.

Integration of mind-body approaches into conventional medical settings has been facilitated by scientific understanding of placebo mechanisms. Hospitals increasingly offer meditation, acupuncture, and other complementary therapies based partly on evidence that these interventions can trigger beneficial placebo responses.

**Philosophy of Medicine:** The placebo phenomenon challenges reductive approaches to healing that separate mind from body, supporting more integrated models of health and illness that acknowledge psychological factors as legitimate biological influences rather than confounding variables.

**Biopsychosocial models** of disease incorporate placebo research findings to emphasize interactions between biological, psychological, and social factors in health outcomes. This framework provides theoretical foundation for comprehensive approaches to prevention and treatment that address multiple levels of causation.

The placebo literature contributes to philosophical debates about the nature of consciousness, the relationship between subjective experience and objective reality, and the mechanisms through which meaning and expectation influence biological processes.

**Public Health Implications:** Understanding placebo mechanisms has relevance for population-level interventions, health communication strategies, and policy approaches to improving health outcomes through non-pharmacological means.

**Health communication research** applies placebo principles to optimize public health messaging about prevention behaviors, treatment adherence, and healthcare utilization. Messages that enhance positive expectation and self-efficacy may improve health outcomes through biological pathways identified in placebo research.

Community-based interventions incorporating placebo principles could potentially enhance effectiveness of preventive programs by optimizing therapeutic context, social support, and positive expectation within community settings.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not directly addressed the specific claim that placebo effects lack physiological reality, though the fact-checking platform has covered related medical topics including alternative medicine efficacy and the distinction between correlation and causation in health claims. The absence of Snopes coverage on this particular assertion may reflect the claim's emergence primarily in scientific and medical discourse rather than viral social media contexts.

However, the fundamental question of placebo mechanisms falls within Snopes' scope of scientifically verifiable claims, particularly given widespread public misunderstanding about how placebos work and their legitimate role in medical research and treatment.

**Fact-checking challenges** for placebo claims involve complex scientific literature that requires careful interpretation. Unlike simple factual assertions that can be verified against documented sources, placebo research involves interpreting experimental data, understanding research methodologies, and distinguishing between different types of placebo effects. This complexity may explain why mainstream fact-checkers have not extensively covered placebo misconceptions.

The medical nature of placebo claims also creates potential liability concerns for fact-checking organizations. Addressing questions about medical treatments requires careful attention to regulatory considerations and professional scope limitations. Fact-checkers may prefer avoiding detailed medical claims to prevent accusations of practicing medicine without proper credentials.

**Institutional biases** within medical education and practice may contribute to the persistence of placebo misconceptions even among healthcare professionals. If medical training emphasizes placebo effects primarily as research controls rather than therapeutic phenomena, practitioners may graduate with incomplete understanding of placebo physiology. This educational gap could limit the effectiveness of fact-checking efforts that rely on medical professional consultation.
