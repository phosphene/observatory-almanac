# Plans Are Underway for a "March for Science"

## The Claim

In the final week of January 2017, a claim began circulating on social media that plans were underway for a large-scale "March for Science" in the United States. The claim emerged on Reddit — specifically in the r/politics subreddit — where users began floating the idea of a mass demonstration modeled on the Women's March that had drawn millions of participants on January 21, 2017, just one day after the inauguration of Donald Trump as the 45th President of the United States. Within hours of the initial Reddit thread, a Facebook event page materialized, followed rapidly by a dedicated Twitter handle and a rudimentary website. The claim, as fact-checked by Snopes, was straightforward: were plans actually underway for such an event, or was this aspirational social media noise without organizational substance?

The political context made the claim both plausible and urgent. In the first week of the Trump administration, a series of executive actions and agency directives sent tremors through the American scientific community. The Environmental Protection Agency was instructed to remove climate change data from its public-facing website. The Department of Agriculture issued a directive restricting scientists at the Agricultural Research Service from publishing press releases, new blog posts, or social media content. The Department of Health and Human Services reportedly froze grants and communications from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Employees at multiple agencies — the National Park Service, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, the Department of Energy — reported receiving instructions to limit or halt public communications, particularly on topics related to climate change (Friedman, 2017, New York Times).

These restrictions were not abstract. They represented what communication scholars call a "chilling effect" on scientific speech — the phenomenon whereby the threat of consequences suppresses expression even absent explicit censorship (Schauer, 1978, "Fear, Risk and the First Amendment," Boston University Law Review). For federal scientists who had spent careers producing publicly funded research intended for public benefit, the sudden gag orders were disorienting. The claim that scientists were organizing a march was, in this context, a claim about whether the scientific community had reached a political breaking point.

What made the claim worth checking was partly its novelty and partly its implausibility. Scientists had, by longstanding professional convention, avoided mass political action. The norms of scientific objectivity — what sociologist Robert Merton formalized as "organized skepticism" and "disinterestedness" in his classic articulation of the scientific ethos (Merton, 1942, "The Normative Structure of Science," reprinted in *The Sociology of Science*, 1973) — created powerful cultural pressures against overt political engagement. A march was not a letter to a journal editor or a congressional testimony. It was bodies in the street. Whether that was actually being organized, or merely wished for, was a factual question.

## What's Actually True

Snopes rated the claim **True**. Plans were indeed underway. The verification was unambiguous: by the time Alex Kasprak published his fact-check on January 25, 2017, the March for Science had a functioning organizational structure, a public-facing Facebook page that had already attracted hundreds of thousands of followers, a Twitter account with an active community management team, and a named set of organizers who were communicating with the press. The organizers confirmed to Snopes that they intended to release a formal date and platform statement by January 30, 2017.

The timeline of the march's genesis reveals the speed with which a latent desire for collective action crystallized into organizational reality. On January 21, hours after the Women's March concluded, Reddit users began proposing a science-themed equivalent. By January 22, a handful of volunteers had claimed the Facebook page "Scientists' March on Washington" and begun coordinating through shared Google documents and Slack channels. By January 24, the nascent organization had a steering committee, a preliminary communications plan, and the beginnings of a satellite march network in other cities. The velocity was remarkable — sociologists of collective action would recognize it as a case study in how pre-existing grievances, combined with a triggering event and the connective tissue of social media, can produce rapid mobilization (Tufekci, 2017, *Twitter and Tear Gas: The Power and Fragility of Networked Protest*, Yale University Press).

The march eventually took place on April 22, 2017 — Earth Day, chosen deliberately as a symbolic anchor. The main event in Washington, D.C. featured a rally on the National Mall followed by a march to the Capitol. Bill Nye, the popular science communicator, served as an honorary co-chair and delivered a keynote address. Mona Hanna-Attisha, the pediatrician whose research exposed the Flint water crisis, spoke alongside earth scientists, biomedical researchers, and science educators. Organizers took pains to frame the march as "non-partisan" — a defense of scientific methodology and evidence-based policy rather than an alignment with any political party.

The scale exceeded most initial predictions. Estimates placed participation at approximately one million people worldwide, spread across more than 600 satellite marches in cities on every continent, including Antarctica, where researchers at the Palmer Station held a small demonstration (March for Science, 2017, official post-event report). Cities including London, Berlin, São Paulo, Sydney, Tokyo, and Nairobi hosted marches with thousands of participants. The event was organized by a newly incorporated nonprofit, the March for Science Inc., which subsequently pivoted to ongoing advocacy work focused on science funding, evidence-based policy, and scientific integrity in government.

The non-partisan framing, however, was contested from the start. Critics within the scientific community argued that any march timed to a specific administration's inauguration, however it was rhetorically packaged, was inherently partisan in practice. Supporters countered that defending the integrity of scientific communication was a procedural, not ideological, position — that one could support evidence-based governance without endorsing any party platform. This tension was never fully resolved and would become a persistent theme in post-march evaluations.

## Why This Happened

The March for Science was not spontaneous. It was the surface expression of structural tensions that had been building for decades — tensions between the authority claims of science and the legitimacy claims of democratic politics, between the professional norms of scientific detachment and the civic duties of scientists as citizens, and between competing models of how science should relate to public life.

To understand why January 2017 was a tipping point, it helps to understand what sociologists of science call "boundary-work." Thomas Gieryn's foundational analysis showed that the boundaries between science and non-science are not natural features of the intellectual landscape but are actively constructed and maintained through rhetorical and institutional labor (Gieryn, 1983, "Boundary-Work and the Demarcation of Science from Non-Science: Strains and Interests in Professional Ideologies of Scientists," *American Sociological Review*, 48(6), 781–795). Scientists invest enormous effort in demarcating legitimate science from pseudoscience, from politics, from ideology — and this boundary maintenance serves both epistemic and social functions. It protects the cognitive authority of scientific claims, but it also protects scientists' professional autonomy from political interference.

The Trump administration's early actions were experienced by many scientists as a direct assault on this boundary. When agency communications were restricted, the implicit message was that political authority trumped scientific authority within the government's own knowledge-producing institutions. This was not, from a Science and Technology Studies (STS) perspective, an entirely new phenomenon. Historians of science have documented numerous instances of political interference with scientific communication, from the suppression of government reports on acid rain in the 1980s to the editing of climate change reports under the George W. Bush administration (Mooney, 2005, *The Republican War on Science*, Basic Books). But the speed, breadth, and public visibility of the January 2017 actions raised the perceived threat to a new threshold.

The shift toward political activism also reflected generational changes within the scientific community. Younger researchers, many of whom had entered science during the Obama administration's investments in climate research and biomedical innovation, had different expectations about the relationship between science and governance. They were also more likely to be active on social media, more familiar with the organizing tactics of movements like Occupy Wall Street and Black Lives Matter, and more willing to challenge the traditional norm of scientific aloofness. Survey data collected by the Pew Research Center in the months after the march found that 86% of scientists in the American Association for the Advancement of Science (AAAS) believed it was appropriate for scientists to engage in policy debates about scientific issues, up from 77% in similar surveys a decade earlier (Pew Research Center, 2017, "Public and Scientists' Views on Science and Society").

The sociological literature on collective action suggests that the march was facilitated by what sociologists call "suddenly imposed grievances" combined with "organizational readiness." The concept, developed by Walsh (1981, "Resource Mobilization and Citizen Protest in Communities Around Three Mile Island," *Social Problems*, 29(1), 1–21), describes situations where an unexpected external shock activates latent networks and resources. The scientific community had extensive pre-existing organizational infrastructure — professional societies, university departments, federal laboratory networks, online communities — that could be rapidly repurposed for political mobilization once the perceived threat crossed a threshold. The Women's March, held just days earlier, provided both a template and a permission structure: if millions could march for women's rights, scientists could march for science.

What made this sociologically remarkable was precisely how unusual it was. Scientists had, with rare exceptions, avoided mass demonstrations throughout the twentieth century. Even during the nuclear weapons debates of the Cold War, when physicists like Leo Szilard and Linus Pauling engaged in public advocacy, the dominant mode was expert testimony and elite lobbying rather than popular mobilization. The Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, founded in 1945 by Manhattan Project veterans, wielded its Doomsday Clock as a rhetorical device aimed at policymakers and the educated public — not as a rallying symbol for street protests. Carl Sagan's advocacy on nuclear winter and environmental issues in the 1980s was conducted through television appearances and bestselling books, not marches. The March for Science represented a categorical departure from this tradition of elite engagement, and its participants — including many early-career researchers and graduate students with no prior experience of political activism — were conscious of crossing a professional boundary that their mentors had scrupulously maintained.

## Verdict

**Propositional status: True.** Plans for a March for Science were concretely underway in January 2017. The claim as fact-checked by Snopes was accurate at the time of publication, and the event subsequently materialized on an even larger scale than its organizers initially projected.

The march was planned, announced, organized, and executed. It took place on April 22, 2017, drew an estimated one million participants across 600+ cities worldwide, and received extensive international media coverage. The organizers were identifiable, their goals were publicly articulated, and the timeline from initial concept to realized event followed a clear organizational trajectory. There is no ambiguity in the factual determination.

What the march achieved is a more contested question. In the short term, it demonstrated that the scientific community could mobilize at scale and generated significant media attention for concerns about scientific integrity in government. In the medium term, its legacy is mixed. The March for Science organization continued to operate after 2017, advocating for evidence-based policy and supporting science-friendly candidates in subsequent elections. Several participants subsequently ran for political office, and some won. But the specific policy outcomes attributable to the march — as opposed to broader political dynamics — are difficult to isolate, which is a perennial problem in assessing the impact of protest movements (Amenta et al., 2010, "The Political Consequences of Social Movements," *Annual Review of Sociology*, 36, 287–307).

The march's most significant limitation was arguably the one its critics identified from the outset: it could not resolve the tension between claiming non-partisan status and engaging in what was, by any reasonable analysis, political action directed at a specific administration's policies. Whether this constitutes a limitation or a feature depends on one's theory of the proper relationship between science and democracy — a question to which there is no empirically settled answer.

## The Wider Picture

The March for Science sits at the intersection of two long-running crises in the relationship between science and public life: the crisis of science communication and the crisis of democratic legitimacy in technocratic governance.

The dominant paradigm in science communication for most of the twentieth century was what scholars call the "deficit model" — the assumption that public skepticism toward science is caused by a deficit of knowledge, and that the remedy is simply more and better communication of scientific facts. This model, as Bauer, Allum, and Miller demonstrated in their comprehensive review of 25 years of survey research, has proven empirically inadequate (Bauer, M.W., Allum, N., and Miller, S., 2007, "What Can We Learn From 25 Years of PUS Survey Research? Liberating and Expanding the Agenda," *Public Understanding of Science*, 16(1), 79–95). Public attitudes toward science are not primarily driven by knowledge levels but by trust, values, cultural identity, and institutional credibility. Simply telling people more facts does not reliably change their attitudes or behavior.

The implications of this finding for the March for Science are profound. If the deficit model is wrong — if public resistance to scientific authority is not caused by ignorance but by deeper structural and cultural factors — then a march organized around the message "science is real" may be preaching to the converted without addressing the roots of public skepticism. The march's most enthusiastic participants were overwhelmingly people who already valued science; its messages were unlikely to reach or persuade those whose distrust of scientific institutions was rooted in economic marginalization, cultural alienation, or historical experiences of harm by scientific and medical establishments (Scheufele, D.A. and Krause, N.M., 2019, "Science Audiences, Misinformation, and Fake News," *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 116(16), 7662–7669).

More recent scholarship in science communication has moved toward what is variously called the "dialogue model," the "engagement model," or "public engagement with science" (PES). This approach rejects the one-way transmission of facts from experts to publics and instead emphasizes two-way dialogue, mutual respect, and the recognition that non-expert perspectives contain legitimate knowledge and values that scientific institutions need to hear (Irwin, 2006, "The Politics of Talk: Coming to Terms with the 'New' Scientific Governance," *Social Studies of Science*, 36(2), 299–320). From this perspective, the March for Science was at best a mixed signal — it elevated the visibility of scientific concerns, but its format (a one-directional demonstration) was structurally aligned with the deficit model rather than the dialogue model.

The history of scientists as political actors provides a longer arc of context. The atomic scientists' movement of the 1940s and 1950s — led by figures like Leo Szilard, who drafted the letter Einstein signed warning Roosevelt about the possibility of atomic weapons, and who later founded the Council for a Livable World to lobby for arms control — established the template for scientific political engagement as elite advocacy. The Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists' Doomsday Clock, set at two minutes to midnight in its first iteration and still maintained today, became an iconic symbol of scientists' claim that their technical expertise gave them special authority to speak on matters of existential risk. Carl Sagan extended this tradition into mass media in the 1980s, using his celebrity to advocate on nuclear disarmament and environmental protection while carefully maintaining his scientific credentials (Daukantas, 2017, "Carl Sagan's Life and Legacy as Scientist, Teacher, and Skeptic," *Physics Today*, 70(1), 38–45).

What distinguished the March for Science from these precedents was its populist character. Where Szilard lobbied senators and Sagan wrote for *Parade* magazine, the March for Science put graduate students and postdocs on the National Mall with hand-painted signs reading "Ice Has No Agenda — It Just Melts." This shift from elite advocacy to mass mobilization reflected both the democratization of political organizing through social media and a growing sense among scientists that elite channels were insufficient. But it also raised questions that remain unresolved: Does mass mobilization enhance or erode scientific authority? If scientists are seen as just another interest group marching in the street — alongside labor unions, environmentalists, and partisan activists — does this strengthen or weaken the public's willingness to defer to scientific expertise on technical matters?

The post-2017 trajectory suggests that the March for Science opened a door that has not closed. Subsequent years saw increased scientific engagement in electoral politics, with organizations like 314 Action (now Science for America) recruiting and supporting scientists running for political office. The COVID-19 pandemic of 2020–2023 dramatically escalated the stakes of the science-politics relationship, as public health recommendations became flashpoints of partisan conflict. The marchers' warning that the politicization of science posed societal risks proved prescient, though the dynamics proved far more complex and intractable than a march could address. Whether science can be genuinely non-partisan in a society where virtually every domain of knowledge has become a contested political resource is not a question that any single demonstration — however large and well-intentioned — can resolve.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes' approach to this claim was straightforward confirmation journalism: the organizers existed, they had stated their plans publicly, and the infrastructure for the event was verifiable. Alex Kasprak's fact-check contacted the organizers, confirmed the Facebook page and social media presence, and reported their stated timeline for releasing a date and platform. The verdict — **True** — was unambiguous.

But the claim itself posed a distinctive methodological challenge for the fact-checking format. Most fact-checks evaluate claims about events that have occurred or statements that have been made. "Plans are underway" is a claim about an ongoing process oriented toward a future event. Verifying it required assessing not just whether people said they were planning something, but whether the organizational indicators — institutional formation, leadership, communications infrastructure, timeline specificity — were sufficient to distinguish genuine planning from aspirational talk. Snopes handled this by focusing on concrete organizational artifacts: the named committee, the Facebook page growth trajectory, the stated deadline for a platform release, and on-the-record statements from organizers. This is a credible verification methodology for process claims, though it necessarily could not confirm whether the march would ultimately occur as planned — only that the planning was real.

Under the Observatory's EARC framework, this claim receives a rating of **A (Accept)**. The factual determination is sound. Plans were underway; this is confirmed by multiple independent sources and by the subsequent occurrence of the event itself. There is no serious basis for disputing the core claim.

What the original Snopes fact-check necessarily could not provide — given its format and the temporal constraints of real-time fact-checking — was the deeper contextual analysis that distinguishes confirming a news event from understanding its significance. The fact-check confirmed that organizers existed and had plans. It did not — and was not designed to — explore why the scientific community reached this particular breaking point at this particular moment, what the sociological precedents for such mobilization were, whether the march's non-partisan framing was sustainable, or what the likely effects on public trust in science might be.

This is not a criticism of Snopes; it is an observation about the structural limitations of the fact-checking format. Fact-checking is fundamentally propositional: it evaluates whether a specific claim is true or false. But the significance of a claim — what it means, why it matters, what it connects to — is not itself a propositional matter. The March for Science was real. The question of whether scientists should march, and what happens to scientific authority when they do, requires a different kind of analysis than fact-checking can provide. That analysis belongs to the domain of science and technology studies, the sociology of knowledge, and the history of science — disciplines that engage with the irreducibly normative dimensions of how science and democracy relate to each other. The fact-check gives us the foundation. The understanding requires building on it.
