## §1 — Claim & Verdict Overview

The "HIV in Ketchup" hoax — published on Snopes under the slug `playing-ketchup`, rated **False**, authored by **Barbara Mikkelson** (published October 6, 2004; last updated July 15, 2011) — presents a false health warning claiming that an HIV-positive man was caught placing blood into ketchup dispensers at unnamed fast food restaurants, and advising consumers to avoid shared condiment dispensers in favor of sealed packets. The warning circulated in email beginning in October 2004, characterized by the opening demand "SEND THIS TO AS MANY PEOPLE AS POSSIBLE. PEOPLE ARE SICK." — a forwarding imperative that both described the alleged situation and mobilized urgency.

Snopes's verdict of **False** is established on two separate levels. First, there is no news record of such an incident anywhere. Mikkelson reports a thorough search of news databases found no coverage of any ketchup dispenser tampering involving HIV-positive individuals, and notes that any genuine such incident "would have been picked up by at least a few news outlets" given the public health significance of the claim. Second, and more fundamentally, the CDC has explicitly and unequivocally stated that HIV cannot be transmitted through food, including condiments:

> "No incident of food being contaminated with HIV-infected blood or semen has been reported to CDC. Furthermore, CDC has received no reports of HIV infection resulting from eating food, including condiments. HIV does not live long outside the body. Even if small amounts of HIV-infected blood or semen was consumed, exposure to the air, heat from cooking, and stomach acid would destroy the virus. Therefore, there is no risk of contracting HIV from eating food."

This CDC statement addresses the hoax's claim at the level of mechanism rather than mere denial. The key mechanistic facts are: HIV's survival outside the human body is severely limited by oxygen exposure; heat degrades the virus rapidly; and gastric acid in the stomach provides a further deactivating stage for any virus that might survive initial environmental exposure. The ketchup dispenser scenario requires HIV-contaminated blood to survive an open air transfer, to remain infectious through ingestion, to survive gastric acid, and to achieve mucosal penetration sufficient to establish infection — each stage individually reduces transmission probability toward zero; in combination, the chain fails entirely.

The hoax's longevity is well-documented. It continued circulating in email years after its first appearance, and on two notable occasions received institutional amplification that temporarily lent it false credibility. In 2006, a staff member of the Alabama Attorney General's office forwarded it to acquaintances with her professional signature (identifying her as being from that office) attached, treating a forwarding from an official state legal office email account as an implicit endorsement. In 2007, an employee of the Department of Health and Human Services in Arkansas forwarded it from her work account, appending that agency's name, phone number, fax number, and email address to the message chain. In both cases, institutional identity information — meant to convey professional context for a human sender — was repurposed by chain message mechanics to suggest official institutional endorsement of the hoax's content.

## §2 — The Legend's Structure and Its Variants

**The Core Scare Formula**

The "HIV in ketchup" message is structurally constructed to be unverifiable and forward-compelling simultaneously. The perpetrator is identified only as "a man"; the restaurant is "a fast food outlet (to remain unnamed)"; the timing is "within the last month." Each identifier that could be checked (name, restaurant name, location, date) is replaced with a category description that prevents verification while preserving plausibility. This "generality-without-specificity" pattern is standard in health scare chain emails documented by DiFonzo & Bordia (2007), who identify the strategic vagueness as both a feature of authentic fear transmission (people pass on general warnings when they cannot confirm or deny specifics) and a feature of deliberate fabrication (the fabricator omits specifics to prevent debunking).

The forward instruction "pass this to friends/family" embedded in the email functions as an internal call to action that converts the hoax from an information claim into a community protection norm. Not forwarding the message is implicitly framed as negligence toward one's social network — failing to warn friends about a genuine health danger. This social activation mechanism, identified by Knapp (1944, *Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology*) as fundamental to rumor propagation, is particularly effective for health claims where the potential harm (HIV infection) is serious and where the cost of forwarding (low effort, email) is trivial relative to the potential cost of not forwarding (friends contract HIV).

The final protective instruction — "only use items that come in a closed packet" — further performs dual service: it provides an actionable protective behavior (which increases forwarding motivation, per Turner & Killian, 1987) and it implicitly indicts all open or shared condiment containers in perpetuity, even after the alleged incident is resolved. The advisory generalizes from the claimed single incident to a standing behavioral norm, transforming the hoax from a report about one event into a permanent change in condiment consumption behavior.

**Placement Within the HIV-Contamination Legend Tradition**

Mikkelson explicitly situates the ketchup hoax within a documented family of AIDS transmission legends. She lists the following variant legend forms: HIV-laden needles affixed to gas pump handles, left in the coin returns of payphones, or used to booby-trap seats in movie theatres; HIV-infected semen secreted in pizza or other fast foods. Each of these variants follows the same structural pattern: a location used by ordinary people in the course of normal daily life (filling a car with gas, making a phone call, watching a movie, ordering food) is converted into a potential HIV exposure site through the deliberate malicious action of an HIV-positive person.

The sociological analysis Mikkelson offers is precise: "Such lore's popularity grows out of our fear of contracting the AIDS virus. Even if we take pains to avoid engaging in high risk activities or strive to do so in as safe a fashion as possible, we're all too aware we're still vulnerable." This is a diagnosis of the legends as expressions of residual anxiety in a population that has learned the behavioral modifications that substantially reduce HIV transmission risk (safer sex practices, not sharing needles, etc.) but has not achieved psychological security that the modifications are sufficient. The legend family converts anxiety from the known transmission routes (where behavioral change is available) to the unknown/unknowable daily environment (where no protective behavior is possible because the threat is theoretically everywhere).

The "innocent party" framing universalizes the threat. HIV transmission in genuine epidemiology is concentrated in specific populations engaging in specific practices; the legend family converts it into a universal threat from malicious strangers in public spaces. This conversion does not merely misrepresent epidemiology; it recasts the moral frame: the transmission is no longer a consequence of practices associated with specific identities but an act of malice perpetrated by unknowable others against an innocent public. This moral recasting legitimizes generalized fear in a way that accurate epidemiological framing does not.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty: HIV Virology as Legend-Buster and the Limits of Authority-Based Correction

The HIV ketchup hoax provides an unusually clear test case for the relative effectiveness of two types of correction: **authority-based denial** ("the CDC says this did not happen and cannot happen") versus **mechanistic explanation** ("here is why HIV cannot survive outside the body long enough to infect a condiment consumer"). Barbara Mikkelson's analysis combines both: she cites the CDC statement (authority-based) and then explains the virology (mechanism-based). The combination is methodologically significant because the folklore scholarship on correction effectiveness (Lewandowsky, Ecker, Seifert, Schwarz, & Cook, 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 13(3)) consistently finds that mechanistic explanations achieve greater and more durable belief change than authority citations alone.

The virology in this case is precise and teachable. HIV is a retrovirus — specifically, Human Immunodeficiency Virus, a lentivirus in the family Retroviridae — that requires living cell machinery to replicate and cannot reproduce outside a living host. HIV survives outside human body fluids (blood, semen, vaginal fluid, breast milk) for extremely limited periods. Studies by Lopex-Galindez et al. (1991, *The Lancet*) and Resnick et al. (1986, *Journal of Medical Virology*, 18(2)) established that HIV loses infectivity rapidly upon drying — within hours at room temperature — and is rapidly inactivated by heat, detergent, bleach, and changes in pH. The normal acid pH of the human stomach (approximately 1.5–3.5) presents a major barrier to enteric pathogens; HIV is not an enteric pathogen and has no evolved mechanism for surviving this pH range.

The specific claim in the hoax — that exposure to HIV-contaminated ketchup would produce infection — would require an intact mucosal pathway from the mouth through the gastrointestinal tract into the bloodstream without viral inactivation at any stage. All established mucosal HIV infection routes (documented oral transmission, documented anal mucosal transmission) involve prolonged exposure of living mucosal tissue to high concentrations of viable virus in fluid solutions — a category of exposure fundamentally different from incidental contact with a dilute, environmentally exposed, partially dried or degraded blood exposure in a food product.

What has not been directly studied is whether providing this mechanistic virology explanation in a ketchup-hoax-correction context produces measurably better correction outcomes than citing the CDC statement alone — or whether audiences with low baseline knowledge of retroviral biology benefit more from the mechanism, while audiences with high science literacy benefit equally from either. Lewandowsky et al. (2012) document the general superiority of mechanism-based corrections but do not report domain-specific interaction effects for virology literacy. The HIV ketchup case is a methodologically convenient test case because it has a clear, teachable mechanism, a specific false claim, and a documented history of persistent belief despite official denials — making it an ideal target for a correction effectiveness experiment stratified by virology literacy.

The fact that the hoax continued circulating years after the CDC's explicit denial statement was published, and that it was amplified by institutional employees (at the Alabama AG's office in 2006 and at Arkansas DHHS in 2007) with health and legal professional training, suggests that authority-based correction alone was insufficient even for populations with professional responsibility to know health facts accurately. Whether the addition of mechanistic explanation would have broken that persistence is the research question the existing literature has not directly tested in this specific context.

## §4 — Historical Provenance and the Evidence Chain

**The 2004 Origin and Institutional Amplification**

The hoax's documented origin is October 2004, established by its first appearance in Snopes's inbox in early October 2004. This is a plausible date for the acceleration of a long-standing legend type: the AIDS-contamination-in-public-places legend tradition was well-established by the mid-1990s (the gas pump needle legend was widely circulated by 1997–1998, documented by Brunvand, 1999, *Too Good to Be True*, Norton), and the specific ketchup version represents a 2004-era adaptation of the standing legend structure to a new domestic-public consumption context.

The 2006 Alabama Attorney General's office incident and the 2007 Arkansas DHHS incident are documented by the news sources cited at the end of the Snopes article (Lyon, John. "HIV Ketchup E-Mail A Hoax, Officials Say," *[Fort Smith, AR] Times Record*, April 27, 2007; Miller, Stephanie. "'Blood in the Ketchup' Email a Hoax, According to Officials," *The [Dublin] Courier Herald*, December 13, 2004). Both incidents follow the signature pattern of institutional-email-amplification described by Pendleton (1998): when a chain email is forwarded from an institutional email address with professional signature blocks automatically appended, recipients frequently interpret the institutional identifier as evidence of institutional endorsement. This is a systematic miscommunication — the professional sender has forwarded as an individual, not as an institution — that reflects the mismatch between the social norms of personal email forwarding and the technical reality that work email signatures are automatically appended to all outgoing messages regardless of whether the message represents institutional opinion.

The Alabama AG's office incident is particularly significant: a legal professional in an official law enforcement context is presumed to have basic familiarity with concepts of evidence and falsifiability, and yet forwarded an unverified health scare claim with no verification of sources or news record. This suggests that even populations trained in evidentiary reasoning are not reliably inoculated against health scare chain messages when the messages trigger genuine anxiety rather than the kind of claim-evaluative engagement that legal reasoning usually involves.

**The Blood-in-Brownish-Reddish-Food Pattern**

Mikkelson identifies a broader folklore pattern: the "blood secreted in brownish or reddish foodstuffs" legend cluster, which includes the beliefs that dairies use bloody cows' milk in chocolate milk and that Jägermeister contains deer or elk blood. This pattern suggests a folk symbolics in which dark-colored food and beverages serve as culturally convenient vehicles for contamination fears involving blood, because the color similarity makes the contamination visually plausible. Ketchup's deep red color is not coincidental to its selection as the contamination vehicle in this legend — it is precisely the feature that makes the claim's logic feel viscerally possible, even though the actual color difference between ketchup and ketchup-plus-blood-droplet is not such that it would be undetectable by ordinary observation.

## §5 — Social and Psychological Function

**AIDS Anxiety and the Democratization of Risk**

The foundational psychological function of the HIV-in-ketchup legend is the democratization of AIDS fear. In the early years of the AIDS epidemic (1981–1986), HIV infection was largely understood by the American public as a disease of specific populations — gay men, intravenous drug users, hemophiliacs — rather than as a general population threat. As awareness expanded through the late 1980s and 1990s, the risk framework shifted: HIV was increasingly understood as a potential threat to heterosexual non-drug-users through sexual transmission and blood product exposure. The AIDS-contamination-in-public-places legend family extends this universalization to its logical extreme: even total behavioral abstinence from high-risk activities cannot protect you, because the virus can be introduced into your daily life by malicious actors in ordinary public spaces.

This extension has a specific psychological utility: it releases tension between behavior-risk-awareness and the desire to believe that knowledge-compliant behavior is sufficient protection. If you know HIV is transmitted sexually and you practice safer sex, you still face residual uncertainty about whether that knowledge is complete and whether the protective behaviors are actually sufficient. The legend validates that uncertainty — "they can get you through the ketchup dispenser" — and in so doing paradoxically provides a kind of relief: the uncertainty is no longer about the limits of your knowledge but about the malice of unknown strangers, which is at least a named and locatable source of fear rather than an epistemological gap.

Mikkelson's observation that "today it's ketchup; tomorrow it will be something else" is both an editorial aside and a structural prediction: the legend form will persist because the underlying anxiety (that HIV can be encountered in ordinary daily life through ordinary actions) persists regardless of the specific vehicle named. The gas pump, the payphone, the movie theater seat, the ketchup dispenser, and countless other public touchpoints have served or will serve as the vehicle because the legend structure requires only a domestic public surface that can be contaminated without the victim's awareness, not a particular surface.

**The Fast Food Setting as Socioeconomic Threat Equalizer**

The fast food setting — as opposed to a fine dining restaurant or a home kitchen — performs a specific socioeconomic function in the legend. Fast food restaurants are universal; they are frequented across class, race, and income lines; they feature open condiment dispensers that are shared by strangers in rapid succession with no cleaning in between. The universality of the fast food ketchup dispenser makes the threat omnipresent in a way that a scare involving, say, canapés at a private cocktail party would not. The legend thus operates on a social register where class-based food-preparation distinctions (the wealthier consumer who eats in settings where condiments are fresh and table-specific) are collapsed: everyone who eats at fast food restaurants — which is a very large fraction of the American population — is equally exposed.

This equalizing function may partly explain why the legend persists despite its biological implausibility. It confirms a democratic distribution of threat in a culture where AIDS has often been coded as a disease of class-and-behavior others (gay men, drug users, the poor, the promiscuous). The ketchup dispenser does not discriminate.

## §6 — Research Gaps and EARC Assessment

**EARC Category: Empirical**

The primary research gap is **Empirical**, specifically in two directions: the virology of the claimed transmission route and the correction effectiveness of mechanistic versus authority-based denial.

1. **Mechanistic correction effectiveness**: The key empirical question is whether providing the virology of HIV environmental survival produces demonstrably greater and more durable correction of the ketchup hoax belief than citing CDC authority alone. Lewandowsky et al. (2012) establish the general principle; Cook et al. (2017, *Computers in Human Behavior*) extend it to online contexts; but no study has directly compared these correction types on HIV food-contamination legend beliefs using a randomized experiment with appropriate virology-literacy stratification.

2. **Institutional email chain amplification**: The Alabama AG and Arkansas DHHS incidents represent a documented mechanism by which institutional identity is accidentally lent to chain email misinformation. A systematic study of how frequently professional signature blocks appear in health-related chain email archives would establish the prevalence of this mechanism. The prevalence data would directly address whether institutional email inadvertent amplification is a structurally significant channel for health misinformation or an exceptional event.

3. **Comparison with HIV-needle legend virology**: The HIV-in-ketchup hoax is structurally nearly identical to the HIV-in-gas-pump-needle hoax (Brunvand, 1999), but the needle version involves a different transmission route (percutaneous) than the ketchup version (oral/enteral). Since percutaneous HIV transmission is genuinely possible under some conditions and oral/enteral transmission is essentially impossible, the legend family contains both a version that is biologically impossible (ketchup) and a version that is biologically possible but epidemiologically implausible in the claimed scenario (gas pump). Whether this difference in biological plausibility affects comparative circulation rates and correction resistance has not been studied.

4. **Longevity analysis vs. baseline**: How does the HIV-ketchup legend's documented longevity (2004–2011 confirmed in Snopes; likely longer in actual circulation) compare to baseline longevity of structurally similar health hoaxes that do not involve HIV/AIDS? If HIV legends show systematically longer longevity than non-HIV health legends, this would provide direct evidence that AIDS-specific anxiety is a structural amplifier for legend persistence beyond the effects attributable to general health fear or forwarding mechanics.

5. **Chocolate milk blood legend connection**: Mikkelson identifies the blood-in-brownish-reddish-food pattern as a broader cluster but does not provide citation to scholarly treatment of this specific pattern. A systematic survey of contamination legends involving dark or red food products, testing whether blood-specific contamination claims are disproportionately associated with dark/red foods compared with light foods, would either confirm or disconfirm the implied folk semiotics of color-as-contamination-vehicle.

**Verdict Assessment**

The False verdict is unambiguously correct. The claimed incident did not occur, no news record exists, and the CDC has explicitly confirmed that HIV transmission via food is biologically impossible given HIV's environmental fragility. The mechanistic explanation supporting the verdict is robust and multiply sourced across the virology literature. The legend's persistence despite clear correction is the genuinely interesting research problem — a case where the epistemic question (is this true?) is trivially answered but the socio-psychological question (why does this persist despite being trivially answerable?) remains open and empirically interesting.
