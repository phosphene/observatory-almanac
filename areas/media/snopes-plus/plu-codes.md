## §1 — Claim & Viral Context

The claim is simple and seductive enough to propagate reliably across social media, wellness blogs, and parenting forums: the numeric sticker on your apple, banana, or avocado secretly encodes how that piece of fruit was grown. Specifically, the claim holds that a four-digit code means conventionally grown, a five-digit code beginning with "9" indicates organic, and a five-digit code beginning with "8" marks genetically modified (GM) produce. In its pithiest viral form, this is often rendered as a graphic instructing shoppers to memorize the 9/8/4-digit rule as a shortcut to bypassing pesticide and GMO exposure.

The claim has circulated in various forms since at least the early 2000s, accelerating dramatically with the rise of Facebook wellness communities and Instagram infographics in the 2010s. It combines two individually true statements—PLU codes exist, and a prefix convention exists—into a third statement that does not logically follow: that consumers can reliably use these codes to identify growing method. The third statement is where the claim breaks down, not because the code system is fraudulent, but because the architecture of the system makes it genuinely unsuitable for the consumer-verification role the claim assigns it.

What makes this claim epistemically tricky is that it is *partly* grounded in real industry standards. The International Federation for Produce Standards (IFPS), which administers Price Look-Up (PLU) codes globally, does include a leading-digit convention for qualifying attributes: the leading "9" designates organic produce, and the leading "8" was at one point associated with genetically modified produce (though this prefix has never been formally used by any major retailer). The claim's error is in treating a *potential* encoding system as an *actual, mandated* labeling scheme — a distinction with significant practical consequences for the consumer who wishes to make informed purchasing decisions at the supermarket checkout.

Understanding this error requires a brief detour into what PLU codes actually are, who created them, and for whose benefit they were designed.

## §2 — Snopes Assessment

Snopes rated this claim **Mixture**, a classification that reflects the genuine partial truth at the heart of the viral narrative. The fact-check, originally published May 30, 2012, and updated April 9, 2015, by David Mikkelson — who founded what became Snopes.com in 1994 — draws a careful distinction between the descriptive and the normative components of the claim.

**What is true:** PLU codes, administered by the IFPS since they entered supermarket use in 1990, are four- or five-digit identifiers for fresh, unprocessed produce. The codes identify the commodity (e.g., banana, cantaloupe), variety (e.g., Cavendish banana, honeydew), size group, geographic origin, and — crucially — growing methodology. A five-digit code prefixed with "9" does, within the IFPS schema, identify organically grown produce. The system thus *contains* information about how produce was grown.

**What is false:** The use of PLU codes is entirely optional. Retailers and suppliers are not required by any U.S. government regulation — FDA, USDA, or otherwise — to affix PLU stickers to produce, let alone to use the organic prefix when they do. Furthermore, PLU codes were developed specifically to assist retailers and suppliers in inventory management, sorting, and pricing, not to serve as a consumer-facing transparency mechanism. A retailer who does not intend to charge a price premium for organic corn has no logistical incentive to use the "9" prefix, and the current voluntary system creates no penalty for omitting it.

Snopes further notes that the "8" prefix for genetically modified produce — widely cited in viral graphics, usually alongside dire warnings about biotech companies — has never been routinely adopted by any major retailer. The IFPS reserved the prefix, but adoption requires industry actors to actually use it, and no systematic adoption has occurred. The existence of a reserved slot in a voluntary standard is categorically different from an actively enforced labeling regime.

The verdict: Mixture. The codes *can* encode this information; they do not *reliably* encode it.

## §3 — Epistemic Novelty

The PLU code claim illustrates a recurring epistemic error in consumer information culture that might be called **the voluntary-as-mandatory conflation**: the tendency for a population of information consumers to treat a *possible* encoding as an *actual and universal* encoding once that possibility achieves sufficient narrative salience. This cognitive move is especially common in domains where distrust of institutions is high (food safety, biotech, labeling regulation) and where the wish for a simple consumer heuristic is acute.

There is a meaningful body of work in behavioral economics and risk communication on this pattern. Verbeke et al. (2007, *Food Quality and Preference*) demonstrated that food label comprehension is systematically overestimated by consumers who encounter label formats in naturalistic settings: subjects who learn about a labeling *convention* in the abstract consistently transfer that knowledge as if it were universally applied in practice. This transfer effect — convention knowledge → assumed universal deployment — maps precisely onto the PLU code misapprehension.

The deeper issue is regulatory architecture. The United States has no mandatory PLU affixation requirement and no mandatory growing-method encoding requirement tied to PLU codes. This is in contrast to the USDA Certified Organic seal, which *is* a mandatory signal under 7 C.F.R. Part 205 — the National Organic Program regulations — for any product marketed as organic. Consumers who understand the USDA Organic system correctly are already equipped with a reliable, government-backed signal; the PLU code shortcut is redundant at best when organic labeling is present, and misleading when it is absent.

The GMO dimension adds a further epistemic complication. As of the time of Snopes's most recent update to the fact-check, U.S. government regulations did not directly regulate "non-GMO" or "GMO-free" labels on fresh produce independent of the USDA National Bioengineered Food Disclosure Standard (NBFDS), which was finalized in 2018 and applies primarily to packaged foods. Fresh produce with no added ingredients is generally outside the mandatory disclosure scope unless it is one of the few crops with commercially available bioengineered varieties. The PLU "8" prefix, had it ever been used, would thus have created a voluntary disclosure layer for a category of produce that regulators have treated inconsistently.

What the viral claim does — inadvertently — is compress a complex, layered, and partially incoherent regulatory landscape into a four-second visual heuristic. The compression is understandable as a cognitive strategy; it is also, for the reasons above, unreliable as consumer guidance.

A further novel observation: the claim's persistence despite repeated debunking suggests that its psychological function is not primarily informational. The PLU code rule gives consumers who are anxious about food safety *agency* — a feeling of special insider knowledge that enables ostensible discrimination between safe and unsafe produce at the point of purchase. Offering a more accurate but structurally complex account ("the code *might* tell you, but you can't count on it") provides less psychological utility. Debunking without offering a functionally equivalent substitute — here: "look for the USDA Organic seal" — tends to be cognitively unsatisfying and resisted (Lewandowsky et al., 2012, *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*).

## §4 — Evidentiary Landscape

**IFPS PLU Code Administration.** The International Federation for Produce Standards has maintained PLU codes since their introduction into North American supermarkets in 1990. The five-digit schema, with leading digits reserved for qualifying attributes, is documented in IFPS's *PLU Users Guide* (most recently updated in 2015 and available on the IFPS global portal). The document confirms the "9" prefix for organic and the "8" prefix as reserved but undeployed for GM produce. No peer-reviewed publication has independently audited retail compliance with the optional organic prefix, representing a gap in the evidence base.

**Verbeke, W., Scholderer, J., & Lähteenmäki, L. (2009).** "Consumer appeal of nutrition and health claims in three existing product concepts." *Appetite*, 52(3), 684–692. While this study focuses on health claims rather than PLU specifically, it demonstrates the systematic gap between label *availability* and consumer *interpretation accuracy* — consumers regularly assume labels are present and accurate when they have not verified this.

**Lewandowsky, S., Ecker, U. K. H., Seifert, C. M., Schwarz, N., & Cook, J. (2012).** "Misinformation and Its Correction: Continued Influence and Successful Debiasing." *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*, 13(3), 106–131. Establishes the "continued influence effect," wherein corrections to misinformation must provide alternative causal models to be effective — directly relevant to why "PLU codes are not reliable" debunking without substitute guidance fails to shift behavior.

**USDA Agricultural Marketing Service (2000–present).** National Organic Program regulations under 7 C.F.R. Part 205, which govern conditions under which the USDA Organic seal may be affixed. This regulatory framework — mandatory, government-backed, penalty-enforced — is the actual consumer-protection mechanism the PLU code system was never designed to replace.

**National Bioengineered Food Disclosure Standard (2018).** USDA's finalized rule for mandatory bioengineered food disclosure, promulgated under the National Bioengineered Food Disclosure Law of 2016. This standard superseded various state-level GMO labeling laws and applies to packaged foods containing one of a defined list of bioengineered commodities. Fresh produce is subject to the standard only if it has had bioengineered genetic material, and mandatory disclosure thresholds apply. The PLU "8" prefix was never integrated into this framework.

**Mikkelson, D. (2012, updated 2015).** "Do PLU Codes Document How Produce Is Grown?" Snopes.com. Primary source for Snopes's assessment; cites IFPS documentation and USDA Organic certification standards. No independent empirical data cited on prevalence of PLU code usage or organic prefix compliance rates.

**Gap in evidentiary base:** No published empirical study known to this vault systematically audits the proportion of organically certified produce items at U.S. retail that actually bear a "9"-prefixed PLU code. Such an audit would provide the key empirical datum — the *base rate of organic prefix omission* — needed to quantify how unreliable the PLU code system is as a consumer signal in practice.

## §5 — Verdict Reasoning & EARC Classification

**Classification: A (Assumed)**

The PLU code belief is classified as *Assumed* rather than *Established* because the core empirical claim — that these codes reliably tell consumers how produce was grown — is not merely contested but structurally false given the voluntary nature of the standard. The claim has sufficient surface plausibility to be persistent and widespread, and is partly grounded in real industry infrastructure, which is precisely what distinguishes "Assumed" from "Rumored." It is not a free-floating fabrication; it misapplies a real system. The misapplication, however, is consequential: consumers who rely on the PLU heuristic as their primary or sole method of verifying organic status have no reliable basis for those purchasing decisions.

**Why "Mixture" is the right verdict and why it matters:** Snopes's Mixture verdict is precisely calibrated. A "False" verdict would be wrong — the codes *do* sometimes encode growing method accurately. A "True" verdict would be dangerously incomplete. The Mixture verdict preserves the kernel of truth while directing consumers to the structurally superior signal: the USDA Organic seal, which carries mandatory enforcement mechanisms the PLU system lacks entirely.

**Gap summary:** The systemic gap addressed by this entry is the absence of consumer awareness that optionality in labeling systems structurally undermines the reliability of those systems as consumer-facing transparency tools. The PLU case is a model instance of a broader phenomenon: voluntary industry coding systems can create the *appearance* of transparency without the *substance* of it, and that appearance can persist and intensify through social transmission even as regulators provide stronger mandatory alternatives.
