# Pluto is no longer a planet (and the controversy around that decision)


## 1. The Claim

On 24 August 2006, the International Astronomical Union voted to remove Pluto from the category of planets, reclassifying it as a "dwarf planet" — a new category created by the same resolution. The decision immediately became international news and generated a cultural response out of proportion to most decisions in astronomy. Within days, Pluto's demotion was front-page news worldwide, spawned jokes, protests, children's letters to observatories, and a sustained popular and scientific controversy that continues nearly two decades later.

The claim that "Pluto is not a planet" has since been taught as established scientific fact in school curricula in most countries, presented as an example of science self-correcting and updating its classifications as knowledge advances, and used in popular culture as shorthand for surprising institutional decision-making. The statement is now so widespread that questioning it often reads as eccentricity or nostalgia rather than scientific precision.

The reality is more interesting. "Pluto is not a planet" is correct by the formal convention adopted by the IAU in 2006, but the resolution that established this status was passed by a small, unrepresentative fraction of IAU members, used a definition that is scientifically problematic by the standards of several active research communities, and was subsequently challenged by formal counter-proposals. The decision to reclassify Pluto was, and remains, genuinely contested — not in the sense of being driven by public sentiment, but in the sense of being scientifically disputed by professional planetary scientists with credible methodological objections to the IAU definition itself.

This entry treats the "Pluto is not a planet" claim as institutionally established but scientifically contested: a classification decision rather than an empirical discovery, and one where the classification remains disputed within the relevant expert community.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Pluto's physical properties have never been in doubt.** Pluto orbits the Sun at an average distance of approximately 39.5 astronomical units (AU), in a region of the outer solar system called the Kuiper Belt. It has a diameter of approximately 2,377 km — roughly two-thirds the diameter of Earth's Moon — and a mass approximately 0.0022 times that of Earth. It has five known moons, of which Charon is so large relative to Pluto (approximately half Pluto's diameter) that the two are sometimes described as a binary system orbiting a shared center of mass located in space between them rather than inside either body. Its orbit is significantly more eccentric and inclined to the ecliptic plane than the eight classical planets. Its surface is predominantly nitrogen ice, with carbon monoxide and methane ices, and it has a thin, seasonally variable atmosphere. These physical facts are not in dispute.

**Why Pluto was reclassified: the IAU 2006 definition.** Before 2006, the solar system had no formal IAU-approved definition of "planet." Pluto had been classified as a planet by convention since its discovery by Clyde Tombaugh in 1930, based on its designation as the ninth object of significant size orbiting the Sun discovered after Neptune. The definitional question became urgent when similar-sized objects began to be discovered in the Kuiper Belt — most pressingly when Eris, discovered in 2005 by Brown, Trujillo, and Rabinowitz (Brown et al., 2005, *Astrophysical Journal Letters*), was found to be slightly larger than Pluto. Keeping Pluto as a planet while excluding a larger object was difficult to justify; keeping both opened the door to adding dozens of similar objects.

The IAU General Assembly in Prague in August 2006 considered several competing definitions. The one adopted — Resolution B5 — defined a planet as an object that: (a) orbits the Sun; (b) has sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid body forces so that it assumes a hydrostatic equilibrium (nearly round) shape; and (c) has "cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit." Pluto satisfies criteria (a) and (b) but fails criterion (c): it orbits within the Kuiper Belt, sharing its orbital neighbourhood with thousands of other objects, and does not gravitationally dominate its region of the solar system. Dwarf planets are defined as objects satisfying (a) and (b) but failing (c). Pluto was accordingly reclassified.

**The vote was not representative of the planetary science community.** The 2006 IAU General Assembly in Prague was attended by approximately 2,700 astronomers out of the IAU's total membership of roughly 9,000 at the time. The final vote on Resolution B5 was cast by approximately 424 members — fewer than 5% of the total IAU membership — after most conference attendees had already departed for the final day of the meeting. Several planetary scientists who were present during the main sessions were on return flights or buses to the airport when the resolution passed. The vote was 333 in favour, 91 opposed, and 77 abstaining (Soter & Tyson, 2006, *Natural History*; Stern & Levison, 2002 provides pre-vote context).

The small and geographically concentrated voting electorate has been a persistent point of criticism. Many of the world's leading planetary scientists — including the researchers who discovered the Kuiper Belt objects that precipitated the debate — were not present or did not participate in the vote. This does not invalidate the IAU's authority to make definitional conventions, but it substantially complicates the claim that the reclassification represents an expert consensus.

**Scientific objections to the "clearing the neighbourhood" criterion.** The third criterion — orbital neighbourhood clearing — is the crux of the definitional controversy among scientists. The objection, made most prominently by Alan Stern (Principal Investigator of the NASA New Horizons mission to Pluto) and others, is that no planet in our solar system would pass this criterion if applied proportionally — at the distances of the outer solar system and the timescales of solar system dynamical evolution, "clearing" requires substantially more gravitational dominance than inner planets need. Applied universally, Earth orbits in a region populated by asteroid co-orbitals and near-Earth objects and would arguably fail a strict neighbourhood-clearing test (Stern & Levison, 2002, *Highlights of Astronomy*; Stern, 2007, *Icarus*).

Furthermore, the definition applies only to objects orbiting the Sun, meaning that exoplanets — objects orbiting other stars — cannot by IAU definition be planets, even when they are clearly analogous to Jupiter or Earth. This has struck many researchers as a definitional failure of principle: a classification system for planets that structurally excludes the thousands of confirmed exoplanets is not a general theory of planetary character but a restricted convention for our own solar system. The New Horizons mission to Pluto (2015), led by Stern, returned data showing Pluto has geological activity, a layered atmosphere, complex surface chemistry, and internal dynamics that suggest ongoing processes — observations that in Stern's view make it scientifically incoherent to deny Pluto planetary classification.

Geophysical definitions of planet, favoured by Stern and planetary geologists, would define a planet as any object in hydrostatic equilibrium — that is, any object massive enough to have been rounded by gravity — regardless of orbital history or neighbourhood. Under this definition, Pluto and at least a dozen other solar system bodies (Eris, Makemake, Haumea, Ceres, and several moons including Europa, Ganymede, and Titan) would qualify as planets. The class would be large, but proponents argue that this reflects the actual nature of planetary-scale objects rather than an artificial restriction to eight.

**The IAU's authority and the convention's status.** The IAU is the body internationally recognised as the authoritative naming and classification organisation for astronomical objects. Its conventions are what is officially taught, incorporated into textbooks, and used in formal publications. "Pluto is not a planet" is correct by IAU convention — in the same way that the name "Uranus" is correct by convention, not by nature. It is a classification decision, not an empirical discovery. Whether to accept the IAU's 2006 definition as the right way to classify solar system bodies is a legitimate scientific and philosophical debate about taxonomy, not a debate about observed facts.


## 3. Why People Believe This — and Why the Controversy Persists

**The story has an emotional register unlike most taxonomy debates.** Pluto was a planet for 76 years before its reclassification. For most of that time it was the outermost known member of the solar system — a distant, cold, lonely world at the frontier of solar space. Generations of children grew up with nine planets as a cultural fact. The emotional resonance of Pluto's status is partly nostalgic and partly connected to how the solar system functions as a cosmological self-map — a shared spatial framework through which human culture has understood its own position in the universe. Changing that framework disturbs something surprisingly deep, which explains the volume and persistence of public reaction.

**The media framing was "demotion."** Virtually all mainstream media coverage in 2006 and since used the word "demotion" — a hierarchical word that implies punishment or failure, as if Pluto did something wrong and was being penalised. This framing was reinforced by the creation of the word "plutoed" (voted American Dialect Society's Word of the Year for 2006) meaning to demote or devalue. The language of demotion implied a judgment about Pluto's worth rather than a definitional refinement, and the emotional charge of this framing made the controversy stickier and more polarised than it would have been if the coverage had described it as reclassification.

**Epistemic novelty — scientific classification as a social process.** The Pluto controversy is a remarkably clear public example of something that philosophers of science have long argued but that popular science communication rarely conveys: that scientific classification is a social and conventional process, not a discovery process. There is no fact of the matter about whether Pluto "really is" a planet in the way there is a fact of the matter about its diameter. Planetary classification is a decision to group objects under a label based on criteria that are chosen by humans for particular purposes. Different purposes justify different criteria. The IAU chose orbital-dynamical criteria (neighbourhood clearing) partly to keep the planet count manageable. Geophysicists prefer hydrostatic-equilibrium criteria because those capture the physically meaningful threshold of body formation. Neither set of criteria is more "correct" in an objective sense — they reflect different scientific purposes and values.

This point is profoundly counterintuitive for people whose model of science is primarily of discovery rather than convention. When people believe Pluto "was discovered to not be a planet," they are misunderstanding the IAU decision as an empirical finding rather than a definitional convention. Correcting this requires conveying a somewhat sophisticated philosophy-of-science point: that naming, classification, and taxonomy are human acts that impose order on continuous natural phenomena, and that reasonable people with full knowledge of the facts can disagree about where the lines should be drawn. The Pluto case is a uniquely compelling public example of this point, and its pedagogical value in science education contexts is considerable.

**The 2015 New Horizons data reactivated the debate.** The NASA New Horizons spacecraft's flyby of Pluto on 14 July 2015 returned the most detailed images and data ever obtained from Pluto, transforming it from a blurry dot in telescopic images to a world with mountain ranges of water ice, glaciers of flowing nitrogen, a heart-shaped basin named Tombaugh Regio, a complex layered atmosphere, possible subsurface ocean, and evidence of active geological processes. The richness of this data contradicted the cultural assumption that Pluto was a geologically dead, featureless outer-solar-system rock. For Alan Stern and proponents of the geophysical definition, New Horizons vindicates the view that Pluto is a geologically active world not substantially less interesting or complex than any of the eight classical planets. The data renewed advocacy for revisiting the 2006 classification.


## 4. Verdict

**Institutionally established but legitimately contested.** "Pluto is not a planet" is correct by the IAU convention adopted in 2006 and is what is taught in most educational contexts. It is not false in the sense of misrepresenting an objective fact — it accurately describes Pluto's status under the operative classification system. However, the claim that this represents settled scientific consensus overstates the position. The IAU vote was passed by fewer than 5% of IAU members, the "clearing the neighbourhood" criterion is substantively criticised by active planetary scientists, alternative geophysical definitions with scientific merit would restore planetary status to Pluto, and the New Horizons data has reinforced the empirical case that Pluto is a complex, active world. This is a case of contested classification in an area where reasonable scientific experts genuinely disagree, and communicating it as a straightforward correction of popular error misrepresents the state of the debate among researchers.

Snopes has not addressed this claim directly, which likely reflects both its complexity and the fact that it is not a factual error in the ordinary sense — it is a classification controversy where both "Pluto is a planet" and "Pluto is not a planet" can be defended depending on which definition of planet one adopts.


## 5. The Wider Picture

The Pluto reclassification sits within a long history of solar system taxonomy revisions, most of which generated less public controversy only because the objects involved had less cultural significance. Ceres was classified as a planet when discovered in 1801, reclassified as an asteroid in the 1850s as the asteroid belt was populated with similar objects, and is now classified as a dwarf planet alongside Pluto. The history of the boundary between planets and asteroids offers an earlier precedent for the same kind of definitional adjustment that occurred in 2006.

The larger scientific issue concerns how astronomers should handle what is called the "continuum problem" in planetary classification: solar system objects exist on a continuous spectrum of size, mass, orbital characteristics, and compositional types, and any hard line drawn between "planet" and "not planet" will inevitably appear arbitrary at the boundary. The eight classical planets are genuinely different in scale from Kuiper Belt objects — Jupiter's mass is about 2,500 times Pluto's — but Pluto is not itself at the extreme end of Kuiper Belt objects. Eris, discovered in 2005, is similar in size to Pluto; Makemake and Haumea are smaller but genuinely large; dozens of additional Kuiper Belt objects may eventually prove to be in hydrostatic equilibrium. The practical question for any definition is not whether Pluto is in some metaphysical sense a planet but what definition best serves scientific communication, educational clarity, and public understanding of solar system structure.

The controversy also highlights the peculiar institutional structure of planetary science naming: the IAU holds nominal authority, but its membership does not represent all active planetary scientists, its decisions are made at triennial general assemblies by whoever happens to be present, and its resolutions cannot be enforced — they are conventions, not regulations. Stern and other dissenting scientists have simply continued to describe Pluto as a planet in their own work and public communications without IAU sanction. This suggests that the "Pluto is not a planet" claim has genuine institutional authority but lacks the enforcement that would accompany a comparable decision in a more regulated field.

The discovery of a hypothetical additional large object in the outer solar system — sometimes called "Planet Nine" — would likely require revisiting the definitional questions entirely. Brown and Battygin (2016, *Astronomical Journal*) proposed the existence of a distant planet based on gravitational anomalies in the orbits of distant Kuiper Belt objects, and if confirmed this would add a new member to the solar system with mass approximately 10 times Earth's, which would unambiguously satisfy all three IAU criteria. How the IAU and popular culture would respond to this — whether the "nine planets" mnemonic would be re-adopted in some form, what would happen to Pluto's status — is an open question that the 2006 definition has not resolved.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Snopes has not published a dedicated entry on Pluto's planetary status, which is understandable: the claim does not have the simple true/false structure that most Snopes entries address. It is not a false rumour or deliberate hoax but a legitimate scientific and definitional controversy. Major astronomy communication organisations including NASA, the European Space Agency, and the IAU's own public communications arm have generally adopted the 2006 definition and present Pluto as a dwarf planet, which is the conventional position.

Science journalism and science education resources that handle this topic well typically convey: that Pluto was reclassified in 2006 under a specific new definition; that the definition was contested at the time and remains controversial among planetary scientists; that the classification is a convention rather than a discovery; and that "dwarf planet" is a formal IAU category with specific criteria, not simply a smaller version of a planet.

The media literacy lesson that the Pluto controversy offers is about the nature of scientific classification and the relationship between institutional authority and scientific consensus. Institutional authority (an IAU vote) does not automatically produce scientific consensus (agreement among active researchers). Classification decisions involve human choices about how to carve up continuous natural phenomena, and reasonable experts can have legitimate disagreements about where the lines should fall. Science communicators who present "Pluto is not a planet" as a simple scientific correction — analogous to correcting the claim that the Moon is made of cheese — are misrepresenting the state of the debate.

A more accurate framing for general audiences is: "By the most widely used official definition, Pluto is classified as a dwarf planet rather than a planet, but this definition is contested within astronomy, and different classification systems used by different researchers would give different answers. The classification reflects a human definitional choice, not a discovery about Pluto's physical nature, which is well understood." This framing is less clean than "Pluto is not a planet," but it is more honest about both the science and the sociology of science.

For science educators in particular, the Pluto story offers a rare opportunity to teach the nature of scientific classification: that taxonomy is a tool humans construct to organize knowledge, that reasonable scientists disagree about the best tools for particular purposes, and that "what is the right definition?" is often a more interesting and productive question than "what is the right answer?" given a particular definition. These are lessons that have value across every domain of scientific inquiry where classification boundaries are contested — from species concepts in evolutionary biology to diagnostic categories in psychiatry to particle classifications in high-energy physics.
