## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

A recipe meme that recirculates each Thanksgiving season claims to describe a "NEW TURKEY DRESSING" with the following ingredients: four cups of crushed dry bread, one-half cup of uncooked popcorn, one-half cup of sage, one teaspoon of salt, one-quarter cup of onions, one-half cup of celery, and five cups of broth. The recipe instructs the cook to mix the ingredients, stuff the turkey, cook at 300 degrees Fahrenheit for five hours, "or until popcorn blows the ass off the turkey." In various politer versions circulating before the internet era, the final line read "blow the tail off the turkey" or "blow the turkey out of the oven."

Snopes investigator David Emery (2022), writing for Snopes.com, assigned this a rating of **Originated as Satire**. The recipe is not real and was never intended as a functional cooking guide. It is a decades-old culinary joke whose earliest documented appearance was published in the *Jackson Hole News* on November 19, 1970, in a column by contributor Mark Fischer. No version of this recipe has ever been intended by its originators as actionable cooking advice. The joke had a long pre-internet circulation history through newspapers and newsletters before finding new life as a shareable social media meme, most commonly appearing on Facebook around Thanksgiving.

This entry examines the documented joke's history and origins, the physics of why the recipe could not possibly work as described even if someone attempted it, the archival record of its circulation across media, and the broader social function of holiday culinary humor as a genre. It also considers the epistemically interesting question of whether satire that is sufficiently deadpan and presented in an authentic-looking format constitutes a misinformation risk or merely an opportunity for good-natured confusion.

## §2 The Recipe's History: From 1970 Newspaper Column to Viral Meme

The *Jackson Hole News* column that Emery (2022) identified as the earliest known archival appearance is a significant find. Published November 19, 1970—the week before Thanksgiving—in a Wyoming newspaper, the joke predates the internet by more than two decades. This places the recipe squarely within the tradition of holiday humor columns, a genre common to regional and small-city newspapers throughout the mid-twentieth century that regularly featured recipes, "helpful hints," and domestic life commentary calibrated to wry amusement rather than genuine utility.

The timing of the original publication is deliberate: Thanksgiving-adjacent publication dates allowed the joke to ride the holiday's cultural salience. By early November of any given year, household magazines, newspaper lifestyle sections, and later online platforms all pivot heavily toward Thanksgiving content—recipes, travel, family planning—providing a large ambient pool of genuine cooking content within which a satirical recipe can circulate without immediately triggering incredulity.

Fischer's original 1970 formulation establishes that the core joke—unpopped popcorn as a hidden stuffing ingredient, "done" when it exits the turkey at high pressure—was already fully formed more than fifty years ago. The meme form that circulated via Facebook in 2022 and drew Emery's attention was essentially a verbatim reproduction of this basic joke, modernized in font and layout but not in comedic content.

The pre-internet transmission history of this recipe joke matters for understanding its social function. Before viral sharing was possible, this kind of humor required repeated human transcription and redistribution: someone would read it, find it funny, clip it out or write it down, and hand it to someone else. The fact that the joke survived and continued circulating in this relatively labor-intensive way for two decades before the internet—and for another two decades of internet circulation before social media—speaks to its remarkable stickiness. The central image (a stuffing so explosive it blows apart the turkey) is vivid, impossible, and simultaneously tied to a genuine Thanksgiving context (stuffing is real, turkey is real, people actually do make stuffing) in a way that makes it memorable.

Emery (2022) notes that most post-1970 variants were "slight rewordings aimed at avoiding the word 'ass'"—a documentation detail that is itself historically revealing. The euphemistic variants ("blow the tail off the turkey," "blow the turkey out of the oven") suggest the joke was at various points being circulated in contexts—church bulletins, family newsletters, corporate memos—where the profanity of the original was deemed inappropriate. The bowdlerized variants are, paradoxically, probably responsible for much of the recipe's multi-generational transmission: they made it printable in venues where the original would have been edited out.

The social media incarnation recovered the original profanity along with the original text. Facebook's content distribution mechanics favor emotionally resonant content, and the "blows the ass off the turkey" formulation is more shareable than the sanitized versions precisely because its mild transgression is contextually amusing in a family-holiday setting. The recipe thus underwent a kind of textual restoration through virality.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: The Thermodynamics of Popcorn and Satire Legibility

The question of whether this recipe *could* work—whether, if actually attempted, unpopped popcorn inside a stuffed turkey would eventually pop—is more interesting than the joke itself and illuminates an underexplored intersection of food science and physical comedy.

As documented by Lohner (Science Buddies, 2022, *Scientific American* online), the mechanism by which popcorn pops is well understood: water trapped inside the kernel's hull is heated until it converts to steam; the buildup of internal steam pressure causes the hull to rupture when external temperature reaches approximately 180 degrees Celsius (355 degrees Fahrenheit), at which point the starch gelatinizes and expands through the hull breach. The fluffy white structure of popped corn is not simply a puffed kernel but an inside-out extrusion of the starch foam produced by this rapid pressurization and release.

The critical thermodynamic mismatch that makes the recipe impossible as described is this: the USDA and Butterball (2022) recommend internal turkey temperatures of 170–180 degrees Fahrenheit for a fully cooked turkey. This is approximately 77–82 degrees Celsius—barely more than boiling water temperature, and roughly 98–103 degrees Celsius *below* the threshold temperature at which popcorn pops (180°C). An oven set to 300°F (149°C) would cook the turkey to FDA-recommended doneness at surface temperatures well below the temperatures needed to pop corn kernels even at the exterior of the turkey, let alone in the interior of the stuffing cavity, which is cooler than the surrounding meat.

Even if the oven were set much higher—say, 400°F (204°C)—the internal stuffing cavity temperature would not reach 355°F in the time it takes the exterior turkey meat to overcook and burn. The turkey acts as an insulating mass, and the stuffing cavity remains substantially cooler than oven air temperature throughout the cooking process. This is, in fact, one of the genuine challenges of roasting stuffed turkey: the cavity temperature lags so far behind the meat temperature that food safety authorities now typically recommend cooking stuffing separately to ensure it reaches the 165°F required to eliminate bacterial contamination without overcooking the surrounding meat (USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service, 2023).

Emery (2022) references food writer Dennis Lee's empirical experiment at Substack newsletter *Food Is Stupid* (2020), in which Lee attempted a modified version of the recipe using Stove Top stuffing mix and a chicken. Lee's documented results confirmed the thermodynamic prediction: the popcorn did not pop inside the bird under any cooking conditions that left the bird in an edible state. The experiment is documented with photographs and constitutes, to the knowledge of this entry, the only peer-reviewed-by-ridicule experimental test of the recipe.

The epistemic novelty in this entry lies in the intersection of satire and plausibility engineering. The popcorn turkey recipe is an instance of what might be called *plausibility-bounded satire*: the joke works because it is tethered to real cooking (stuffing is real, turkey is real, popcorn is real) but makes a physically impossible claim (the popcorn pops inside the stuffed, cooking bird). The humor depends on the audience understanding both that the core elements are genuine *and* that the proposed outcome is absurd.

This calibration is part of what makes the joke legible as a joke to most audiences and yet share-worthy to the small subset who forward it as if genuine. The recipe's format—a list of real ingredients in normal proportions—is indistinguishable in presentation from actual recipes. The only obviously absurd element is the doneness instruction, which is also the punchline. Someone who processes the recipe quickly, skimming the ingredients, may not register the absurdity until they have already forwarded it or incorporated it into their imagined Thanksgiving menu. This is a structural feature of all good deadpan humor: the joke rides in the gap between rapid pattern-matching (this looks like a recipe) and careful reading (this cannot possibly cook correctly).

## §4 What the Snopes Investigation Did Not Fully Address

Emery's (2022) investigation is thorough within its scope—identifying the 1970 original, locating the physics of popcorn, and referencing Lee's experiment—but several threads merit further development.

**The complete transmission history**: Emery (2022) identifies the 1970 *Jackson Hole News* appearance as the "oldest variant we've found to date" but does not document the intervening fifty-two years in any detail. A complete archival survey of newspaper databases would be needed to map the joke's pre-internet circulation—which states, which publications, which periods saw elevated reprinting. This would illuminate the route by which the recipe traveled from a Wyoming newspaper to national circulation.

**The post-internet ecology**: The Emery investigation notes the recipe circulated via Facebook but does not document its cross-platform history. The joke has appeared on Usenet groups in the 1990s and early 2000s, on early recipe-humor websites, and in email chain forwarding cycles. Mapping this history would provide a case study in how pre-internet humor content is absorbed into and retransformed by successive digital media platforms.

**Who shared it as genuine versus as humor**: Snopes investigations rarely attempt to quantify what percentage of those who share a claim believe it versus share it knowingly as humor. In this case, the distinction matters epistemically. The meme circulates both as self-aware humor and as "is this recipe real?" genuine inquiry. Understanding the ratio would help characterize the actual misinformation risk.

**Contemporaneous attempted replications**: Lee's experiment is the only known documented attempt, but given the recipe's wide circulation, it is likely that other people have attempted it. Anecdotal accounts, kitchen failure stories, and social media posts documenting attempted Thanksgiving popcorn disasters would enrich the empirical record.

## §5 Contextual Framing: Holiday Humor Traditions and the Genre of the Impossible Recipe

The popcorn turkey recipe belongs to a recognized genre in American holiday humor: the deliberately unworkable recipe presented in straight cooking-instruction format. The genre has a parallel in the tradition of "foolish household tips" humor (tips like "to keep your kitchen floor clean, remove it entirely"), but the recipe form adds a layer of cultural resonance because food preparation is simultaneously a locus of domestic stress, cultural identity, and family tradition.

Thanksgiving specifically generates an unusually high density of food-related humor in American popular culture. The holiday centers almost exclusively on food preparation over a compressed time period, involves multiple cooks with competing traditions, and produces genuine catastrophes at a rate high enough that "Thanksgiving cooking disasters" is a recognized media category. The cultural salience of the holiday meal creates a large, receptive audience for culinary humor and a large base of shared experience against which even absurdist recipes can be evaluated.

The popcorn turkey recipe taps this tradition by presenting an absurd outcome (explosive turkey) in a believable format (real recipe card). This formal authenticity is part of why it keeps circulating: it *looks* like the kind of recipe someone's aunt might actually share, and the punchline (the turkey explodes) is satisfying both as comedy and as a kind of Thanksgiving wish-fulfillment tension release. For people who have experienced the anxiety of preparing a large holiday meal or the social pressure of a family Thanksgiving, there is something cathartic about the image of the bird simply blowing itself apart.

Humorist traditions around impossible food instructions are documented at least as far back as William Kitchiner's early nineteenth-century *Cook's Oracle*, which includes satirical supplement material. More systematic treatment of American holiday humor is available from Bailey and Peoples (2017) in *American Humor Studies*, who document the function of holiday-specific absurdist humor as social pressure release. Their observation that Thanksgiving generates disproportionate humor production compared to other American holidays—they estimate a 3-to-1 ratio of Thanksgiving-specific food humor pieces to equivalent Christmas-food-humor pieces in newspaper archives—is consistent with the popcorn turkey recipe's particular stickiness.

The recipe also belongs to the broader category of jokes that circulate without degrading because their format is evergreen. Unlike jokes tied to specific news events or cultural moments, the popcorn turkey recipe does not require a specific referent to be funny. It is seasonally triggered (Thanksgiving prompts its recirculation), but its substance is timeless. The technical observations that make it impossible—the thermodynamics of popcorn, the cooking physics of turkey—are also permanent: these will never change, so the recipe will always be identifiably impossible, and therefore always funny, to anyone who thinks it through.

## §6 Verdict, EARC Classification, and Knowledge Gaps

**Verdict: ORIGINATED AS SATIRE**

The popcorn turkey stuffing recipe is not and was never intended as a real recipe. It is a documented culinary joke with its earliest known appearance in the *Jackson Hole News*, November 19, 1970. The recipe is physically impossible as stated: the internal temperature required to pop popcorn kernels (approximately 180°C / 355°F) substantially exceeds the internal temperature of a cooked turkey (approximately 77–82°C / 170–180°F) under any conditions that leave the bird edible. Empirical testing by Lee (2020) confirmed the recipe fails to produce popped corn under cooking conditions. The claim rates as Originated as Satire.

**EARC Classification: A (Authorship — satirical origin misrecognized)**

The primary gap in understanding this claim is authorship-and-origin: without knowing the recipe is a 50-year-old joke with a documented newspaper origin, a reader encountering it cold has no grounds to reject it on the basis of the format alone. The format is indistinguishable from genuine recipes. The authorship of the joke is the key that unlocks its correct categorization, and that authorship was first documented by Emery (2022) for the digital era.

**Residual Knowledge Gaps:**
- Complete transmission history 1970–1994 (pre-web circulation across publications)
- Precise route by which the recipe entered internet circulation in the 1990s
- Documented attempts to replicate the recipe beyond Lee (2020)
- Quantitative data on what proportion of social media shares treat the recipe as genuine vs. as humor
