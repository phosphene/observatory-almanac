## The Claim

In September 2021, social media posts and news articles reported that the city of Portland, Oregon had officially renamed a newly constructed pedestrian and bicycle bridge "Ned Flanders Crossing," in honor of the fictional character from *The Simpsons*. The claim circulated with photographs of the bridge, screenshots of official city announcements, and a general tone of amused disbelief. Many who encountered the story assumed it was satire, a parody headline, or an internet fabrication of the kind that circulates whenever reality edges close enough to absurdity that the two become difficult to distinguish.

The skepticism was understandable. American cities do not, as a rule, name public infrastructure after cartoon characters. Bridges are named after presidents, philanthropists, civil rights leaders, and occasionally controversial historical figures whose plaques become the subject of later reckoning. The idea that a municipal government would attach the name of Homer Simpson's evangelical, mustache-sporting, left-handed neighbor to an actual piece of transportation infrastructure seemed, to many observers, like exactly the kind of story the internet invents for engagement.

And yet.

## What's Actually True

The story is entirely true. On September 12, 2021, Portland Mayor Ted Wheeler issued an official mayoral proclamation renaming the bridge "Ned Flanders Crossing." The bridge — a sleek, modern pedestrian and bicycle span crossing Interstate 405 — connects the Pearl District and the Northwest District of Portland. It had opened just three months earlier, in June 2021, under the more prosaic name "Flanders Crossing," a designation derived from the street it connects to: NW Flanders Street, which runs east and west on either side of the structure (MacGuill, 2021, Snopes).

The renaming was not an accident, a prank, or a rogue act of municipal whimsy. Mayor Wheeler's proclamation described the fictional Ned Flanders as "a quintessential emblem of good neighborliness for over 30 years" and "a symbol of the kind of neighborly connection we should strive for in Portland" (MacGuill, 2021, Snopes). The language is worth pausing over. City proclamations are, by nature, instruments of bureaucratic solemnity. They exist in a register designed to confer dignity upon their subjects. That this particular instrument conferred dignity upon a man whose most famous catchphrase is "Hi-diddly-ho, neighborino" tells us something real about the relationship between Portland and the show that emerged from its streets.

The event was covered widely. The New York Post reported on the renaming, as did The Oregonian, Portland's newspaper of record (MacGuill, 2021, Snopes). The story was not a rumor that grew in the retelling. It was a documented municipal action, announced by the mayor, covered by journalists, and entered into the public record.

The bridge itself is a handsome piece of infrastructure. Spanning the roaring trench of I-405, it provides a car-free connection between two of Portland's most walkable neighborhoods. Its design reflects the city's longstanding commitment to alternative transportation — a commitment that, one might note, Ned Flanders would almost certainly approve of, given his general disposition toward wholesome, community-minded activities. The bridge cost approximately $17.1 million to construct and was funded through a combination of city and federal money (Njus, 2021, The Oregonian). It is 530 feet long, suspended above the freeway on an elegant cable-stayed design that makes it one of the more architecturally distinctive pedestrian crossings in the American West.

## Why People Believe This

The immediate reason people believed this story — or rather, the immediate reason people found it surprising enough to fact-check — is that it sounds like a joke. But the deeper reason the story resonated so powerfully, spreading across social media and news outlets with the velocity of virality, lies in the peculiar semiotic loop it completes.

To understand that loop, one must understand what Matt Groening did with Portland's geography.

Groening grew up in Portland in the 1950s and 1960s, in the era when the city was still decades away from its reputation as a haven for artisanal coffee, independent bookstores, and the aggressive pursuit of urban livability. In a 1997 interview with television critic David Bianculli of the New York Daily News, Groening revealed a secret he had embedded in the DNA of his creation: "Many characters are named after streets in my hometown of Portland, Oregon" (Bianculli, 1997, New York Daily News). The admission illuminated a pattern that Portland residents had long suspected. Flanders, Lovejoy, Quimby — these names, instantly recognizable to anyone who has watched even a single episode of *The Simpsons*, are also the names of streets that run through the northwest section of Portland. Ned Flanders derives from NW Flanders Street. Reverend Lovejoy derives from NW Lovejoy Street. Mayor Quimby derives from NW Quimby Street.

Groening's intention was characteristically playful. As he told Bianculli: "I wanted people to be driving down 23rd Street in Portland and keep passing these signs, and think they're going crazy" (Bianculli, 1997, New York Daily News). The image is perfect — a driver glancing at street signs and experiencing a disorienting flicker of recognition, as though the real world had been quietly colonized by a cartoon. It is a form of artistic steganography, a hidden message encoded not in the text of the show but in the relationship between the show and the physical landscape that produced its creator.

This is the first layer of the loop: reality becomes fiction. Portland's street names, themselves the product of nineteenth-century urban planning, enter the fictional universe of Springfield and become attached to characters who develop their own independent cultural lives. Ned Flanders ceases to be a street name and becomes a person — or rather, a person-shaped collection of qualities (piety, neighborliness, an improbable mustache, an inexhaustible supply of patience in the face of Homer Simpson's various depredations) that millions of people recognize and, in many cases, feel genuine affection toward.

The second layer is the return journey: fiction becomes reality. When Portland names its bridge "Ned Flanders Crossing," the fictional character's name re-enters the geography from which it originally emerged. The loop closes. The street that gave the character his name now has a bridge named after the character. The real has become the fictional has become the real again. This is not mere irony. It is something richer and stranger — a kind of toponymic ouroboros, the snake of naming eating its own tail.

The theorist Jean Baudrillard would have recognized something familiar in this sequence, though he might have described it in more alarming terms. In his framework of simulacra and simulation, the progression from map to territory to map-that-replaces-territory describes a world in which copies supersede their originals (Baudrillard, 1981, *Simulacra and Simulation*). When Portland names a bridge after a fictional character who was named after a Portland street, the question of what is "original" and what is "copy" dissolves. Ned Flanders the character is more famous than NW Flanders Street ever was. The bridge's renaming acknowledges this hierarchy of recognition. The simulation has become more real than the reality.

But Baudrillard's framework, with its undertone of cultural pessimism, may not be the most useful lens here. What happened in Portland is not a crisis of meaning but an elaboration of it. The city did not lose something when it named the bridge after a cartoon character. It gained a layer of story, a new vein of association that enriches rather than diminishes the place. The bridge now carries pedestrians and cyclists across the freeway, but it also carries a narrative: the story of a boy who grew up in Portland, moved to Los Angeles, created the most successful animated television show in history, and seeded within it a secret love letter to his hometown that took decades to complete its circuit.

There is a concept worth naming here, one that lacks an established term in the scholarly literature: toponymic recursion, or what we might call the Groening Loop. This is the phenomenon in which a work of fiction derives its proper nouns from real geography, and the resulting fictional associations become so culturally potent that they feed back into the geography itself. It is distinct from simple naming-after (a street named after a president, a building named after a donor) because the chain of reference includes a fictional intermediary. The bridge is not named after a person or an event but after a character who was named after a street. The referential chain has an extra link, and that extra link is what makes the whole arrangement so delightful.

Portland's civic identity makes it the ideal setting for this kind of playful recursion. The city has long cultivated a self-conscious relationship with its own weirdness — a stance crystallized in the semifofficial slogan "Keep Portland Weird," which itself was borrowed from Austin, Texas, adding yet another layer of referential complexity (Renn, 2012, Governing). Portland is a city that seems to enjoy the fact that its reality and its mythology are intertwined. Naming a bridge after Ned Flanders is not an anomaly in Portland's civic culture. It is an expression of it.

The timing of the renaming also matters. September 2021 was a moment when Portland, like many American cities, was emerging from the overlapping crises of the COVID-19 pandemic and the social upheaval of 2020. The city had experienced months of protests, counterprotests, and federal intervention. Downtown businesses were struggling. The civic mood was bruised. In this context, naming a bridge after a character defined by his relentless, almost pathological good neighborliness reads as something more than a joke. It reads as an aspiration — a small, officially sanctioned act of civic optimism, delivered with a wink.

The Simpsons themselves encourage this reading. The show has always maintained a complicated relationship with American civic life. Springfield, the show's setting, is deliberately unlocatable — Groening has said he chose the name because there is a Springfield in almost every state (Turner, 2004, *Planet Simpson*). Springfield is everytown and notown, a composite of American municipal dysfunction. Its mayor is corrupt, its nuclear plant is negligent, its police chief is incompetent, and its elementary school is underfunded. And yet the show's tone is not despair but warmth. The citizens of Springfield are flawed, sometimes grotesquely so, but they are also recognizably human. Ned Flanders, in particular, represents the show's most sustained experiment in portraying genuine goodness without reducing it to naivety.

Over the show's long run, the character of Ned Flanders has undergone what fans and critics call "Flanderization" — the process by which a nuanced character is gradually reduced to one or two exaggerated traits (Ortved, 2009, *The Simpsons: An Uncensored, Unauthorized History*). In early seasons, Ned was a complex figure: a devout Christian who was also a successful businessman, a devoted father, and a genuinely kind person whose niceness occasionally contained a note of social anxiety. He was the neighbor everyone wishes they had and the neighbor who sometimes makes you feel guilty for not being better. In later seasons, his religiosity was amplified to the point of caricature, and his other qualities receded. But in the collective cultural memory, Ned Flanders remains what he was at his best: the good neighbor. When Mayor Wheeler's proclamation invoked him as "a quintessential emblem of good neighborliness," it was drawing on the deeper, earlier version of the character — the Ned who brings over casseroles, who maintains his property immaculately, who responds to Homer's insults with patience and grace.

The practice of naming public infrastructure after fictional characters is not entirely without precedent, though it remains rare enough to generate news coverage when it occurs. In Liverpool, England, four streets near the waterfront are named after the Beatles — a real band, but one whose cultural presence has long since transcended the merely biographical (BBC News, 2005). In Metropolis, Illinois — a town of roughly 6,000 people — a fifteen-foot statue of Superman stands in the town square, and the town has embraced the fictional association as a cornerstone of its identity (Kiesling, 2013, The Atlantic). The fictional and the civic have always been porous categories in American life, where towns compete to claim associations with stories, characters, and cultural moments that can lend meaning and visibility to otherwise unremarkable places.

What distinguishes the Portland case is the elegance of the recursion. Liverpool did not name its streets after characters who were named after Liverpool streets. Metropolis, Illinois was not the birthplace of Superman's creator. But in Portland, the chain of reference is complete. The city gave Groening the raw material, Groening transformed it into fiction, and the city reclaimed the fiction as a civic asset. It is a closed loop of cultural production, and the bridge is its physical monument.

## Verdict

**True.** Portland, Oregon officially renamed the Flanders Crossing pedestrian and bicycle bridge to "Ned Flanders Crossing" on September 12, 2021, via a mayoral proclamation issued by Mayor Ted Wheeler. The renaming was a real municipal act, documented by multiple credible news outlets and the city's own records. Snopes rated the claim True (MacGuill, 2021, Snopes).

The renaming completed a remarkable cultural circuit: Matt Groening, a Portland native, named his fictional character after a Portland street, and Portland subsequently named a bridge crossing that street after the fictional character. The bridge remains in use under this name, carrying pedestrians and cyclists across Interstate 405 in Portland's northwest quadrant.

## The Wider Picture

The Ned Flanders Crossing is a small story, in the grand scheme of things. It involves no policy consequences, no budget controversies, no contested legacies. No one protested the renaming. No one filed a lawsuit. It is a civic grace note, a moment of municipal levity in a city that needed one. And yet it illuminates several themes that are worth taking seriously.

The first is the question of how cultural meaning accretes to physical places. A bridge is, in its most reduced description, a structure that allows passage over an obstacle. The Flanders Crossing allows passage over Interstate 405, which sliced through Portland's urban fabric in the 1960s as part of the freeway-building campaigns that reshaped American cities in the postwar era. The freeway severed neighborhoods, destroyed housing, and created the kind of dead zones — loud, polluted, hostile to pedestrians — that cities have been trying to repair ever since. A pedestrian bridge across such a freeway is itself an act of repair, a stitching-together of urban tissue that was torn apart by an earlier generation's infrastructure decisions.

Naming that bridge after Ned Flanders adds a layer of narrative to this act of repair. The bridge does not just connect two neighborhoods. It also connects a city to its most famous cultural export, and it connects a fictional character to the geography that produced him. The naming transforms the bridge from a piece of infrastructure into a piece of storytelling. Every pedestrian who crosses it and smiles at the name is, however briefly, participating in the story of Portland's relationship with *The Simpsons* — a relationship that says something genuine about how cities produce culture and how culture, in turn, produces cities.

The second theme is the role of humor in civic life. American municipal government is not known for its wit. City council meetings are long. Zoning disputes are tedious. Budget negotiations are acrimonious. The machinery of local governance tends to grind humor out of public life, replacing it with procedural formality and bureaucratic caution. The Ned Flanders Crossing represents a rare moment in which a city government acknowledged that civic life can accommodate joy, absurdity, and play without sacrificing its seriousness of purpose. Mayor Wheeler's proclamation managed this balance with some grace: it was funny, but it was not a joke. It used the language of official civic recognition to honor a fictional character, and in doing so it honored something real — the human desire for neighborliness, for connection, for the kind of community that Ned Flanders, at his best, embodies.

The third theme is Matt Groening's relationship to Portland, which deserves more attention than it typically receives. Groening is one of the most influential cultural figures of the late twentieth century. *The Simpsons*, which he created, is the longest-running American animated program and the longest-running American scripted primetime television series (Brook, 2019, BBC Culture). Its influence on comedy, satire, animation, and American self-understanding is difficult to overstate. And yet Groening's Portland origins are not always central to discussions of his work. The street-naming revelation — which he disclosed almost casually in that 1997 interview with David Bianculli — suggests that Portland was not merely the place Groening happened to grow up but a generative source for his creative imagination. The city's geography became his character list. Its street signs became his dramatis personae.

This raises an interesting question about the relationship between artists and the places that form them. Groening left Portland for Los Angeles, where he built his career. But he carried Portland's map with him, encoded in the names of his characters. When fans in Portland drive down NW Flanders Street or NW Lovejoy Street, they inhabit a layered geography — a place that is simultaneously a real street and a fragment of a fictional universe. The Ned Flanders Crossing makes this layering visible and official. It is the city's way of saying: we know what you did with our streets, and we are delighted by it.

The broader American landscape is dotted with similar, if less elegant, examples of fiction shaping geography. Fans of *The Lord of the Rings* can visit Hobbiton in Matamata, New Zealand — a film set preserved as a tourist attraction that has become, for many visitors, more "real" than the surrounding farmland (Jones and Smith, 2016, Tourism Management). The town of Forks, Washington — a logging community of modest distinction — was transformed by the *Twilight* novels into a destination for literary tourists seeking the setting of a vampire romance (Larson and Lizardo, 2015, Journal of Popular Culture). In these cases, fiction has reshaped the economic and cultural life of real places, sometimes in ways that the original creators never intended.

Portland's relationship with *The Simpsons* is different in kind. Groening did not set his show in Portland. He set it in Springfield, a deliberate nowhere. The connection between Portland and *The Simpsons* is not one of setting but of sourcing — Portland provided the raw material (street names, childhood experiences, a certain Pacific Northwest sensibility) that Groening transmuted into fiction. The Ned Flanders Crossing acknowledges this sourcing relationship and celebrates it. It is not a tourist attraction in the conventional sense, though tourists certainly visit it. It is a monument to the creative process itself — to the way artists metabolize their environments and transform them into art, and to the way communities can reclaim that art as a source of identity and pride.

## How Fact-Checkers Handle It

The Snopes fact-check of this claim, published on September 14, 2021 by Dan MacGuill, followed the standard methodology for verifying viral claims about unusual civic actions. The check confirmed the claim by consulting primary sources — the mayoral proclamation itself — and secondary reporting from established news outlets including The New York Post and The Oregonian. The verdict was straightforward: True (MacGuill, 2021, Snopes).

What makes this fact-check interesting is not the difficulty of the verification but the reason it was necessary. The story required fact-checking not because the evidence was ambiguous but because the claim seemed implausible. The Snopes rating serves, in this case, less as a correction of misinformation than as a confirmation of astonishment. It answers the question that many readers were asking: "Wait, really?" The answer is yes, really.

This category of fact-check — claims that are true but sound false — represents a distinctive and underappreciated segment of the fact-checking enterprise. Most public attention to fact-checking focuses on debunking: identifying false claims and providing corrections. But a meaningful portion of fact-checking work involves confirming surprising truths, serving as a kind of institutional guarantor that reality is, in fact, as strange as it appears. The Ned Flanders Crossing belongs to this category. It is not misinformation. It is information that sounds like misinformation, which is a different phenomenon with different implications for public epistemology.

The virality of the story also illustrates a pattern in how surprising-but-true claims circulate online. Such stories tend to spread rapidly because they offer a particular kind of social currency: sharing them allows the sharer to be the bearer of delightful news, to provoke the same "Wait, really?" reaction in their audience that they themselves experienced. The fact-check, in this context, serves as a credential — a link that the sharer can append to demonstrate that the story is not just entertaining but verified. Snopes functions here not as a debunker but as a notary, stamping the story with the imprimatur of institutional verification.

For the Observatory's purposes, the Portland Ned Flanders Crossing represents a clean case of a true claim that circulates in the epistemic space typically occupied by hoaxes and satire. It requires no correction, no nuance, no "partly true" hedging. The bridge exists. The name is official. The loop between Portland's geography and Portland's most famous fictional export is complete. The only question that remains is whether Ned Flanders himself would approve of having a bridge named after him, and on this point, the available evidence — thirty-plus seasons of relentless, unwavering, occasionally exhausting neighborliness — suggests that he would be honored, humble, and at least slightly embarrassed, which is to say: he would be perfectly, characteristically himself.

Hi-diddly-ho, fact-checkerinos.
