# The Term 'Going Postal' Comes from Multiple Post Office Shootings in the 1980s

## 1. The Claim

The phrase "going postal" originated from a series of workplace shootings committed by United States Postal Service (USPS) employees during the 1980s and early 1990s, supposedly demonstrating that postal workers are particularly prone to workplace violence. This claim suggests that the term reflects a genuine pattern of postal employee violence that was so notable it entered common usage as a euphemism for workplace rage and violent behavior. The narrative often implies that working conditions at post offices—including stress, bureaucracy, job insecurity, or workplace culture—create unusually high risks of employee violence compared to other occupations.

The claim typically references several high-profile incidents of postal employee violence, particularly the August 20, 1986 shooting in Edmond, Oklahoma, where postal worker Patrick Sherrill killed 14 coworkers and wounded six others before taking his own life. This and other incidents are presented as evidence of a systematic problem within the postal service that made postal employees synonymous with workplace violence in American culture.

Some versions of the claim extend to suggest that postal workers commit workplace violence at rates significantly higher than other occupations, or that the postal service workplace environment is inherently more likely to trigger violent behavior. The term's widespread adoption is portrayed as reflecting genuine statistical reality rather than media-driven perception or coincidental timing of several highly publicized incidents.

## 2. What's Actually True

The phrase "going postal" did indeed originate from media coverage of workplace violence incidents involving postal employees, particularly the 1986 Edmond shooting and several subsequent incidents through the early 1990s. However, statistical analysis reveals that postal workers are not more likely to commit workplace violence than employees in other sectors, making the term more reflective of media narrative and cultural perception than actual occupational risk patterns.

The etymological connection is well-documented. The term first appeared in print in the St. Petersburg Times on December 17, 1993, in an article by Mike Royko, though it was already in use colloquially. The phrase gained widespread adoption following several highly publicized incidents: Patrick Sherrill's 1986 rampage in Edmond, Oklahoma; Thomas McIlvane's 1991 shooting at a Royal Oak, Michigan post office that killed four; and Joseph Harris's 1991 attack in Ridgewood, New Jersey that killed four people including his supervisor.

These incidents received extensive media coverage partly because of their shocking nature and partly because they involved government employees attacking a federal workplace. The postal service's high public profile—virtually every American interacts with postal workers and facilities regularly—made these incidents feel particularly close to home and generated significant media attention.

However, comprehensive analysis of workplace violence statistics contradicts the implication that postal workers are unusually prone to violence. Research by the U.S. Department of Labor's Bureau of Labor Statistics shows that postal workers' rate of workplace homicides is actually lower than the national average for all occupations. Between 1992 and 1998, the postal service experienced 0.26 workplace homicides per 100,000 employees annually, compared to 0.70 per 100,000 for all workers nationally.

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) conducted extensive analysis of workplace violence across sectors and found that certain industries—including retail, transportation, and security services—have significantly higher rates of workplace violence than postal services. Jobs involving cash handling, working alone, or dealing with the public typically show higher violence rates than postal work.

Several factors contributed to the disproportionate attention paid to postal workplace violence. First, the incidents that occurred were particularly deadly and dramatic, involving multiple fatalities that generated extensive media coverage. Second, they occurred within a relatively short time period, creating the appearance of a trend even though the absolute numbers remained small. Third, the federal government workplace context made these incidents subject to more extensive investigation and documentation than similar incidents in private sector workplaces.

The postal service workforce is also notably large—over 600,000 employees—meaning that even statistically normal rates of workplace violence would produce a substantial absolute number of incidents. When these incidents receive disproportionate media attention due to their federal workplace context, they can appear to represent a unique occupational hazard even when the underlying rates are typical or below average.

Research has identified the actual risk factors for workplace violence, which are primarily situational rather than occupational. These include: dealing with the public, especially in service or enforcement roles; handling money or valuable goods; working alone or in isolated areas; working during evening or night shifts; and working in high-crime areas. Postal work involves some of these factors (public contact, valuable goods) but generally includes protective factors like working in pairs, supervised environments, and established security protocols.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The belief that postal workers are uniquely prone to workplace violence persists due to a combination of media amplification, availability bias, cultural stereotypes about government employment, and the dramatic nature of the incidents that created the phrase. These factors work together to maintain a narrative that contradicts statistical evidence.

Media coverage patterns significantly shaped public perception of postal workplace violence. The incidents that generated the "going postal" phrase were genuinely shocking—mass casualty events in familiar public spaces committed by seemingly ordinary government employees. News organizations provided extensive coverage, including detailed background reporting on the perpetrators and speculation about causes. This intensive coverage created disproportionate public awareness of postal violence compared to similar incidents in other workplaces.

Availability bias makes dramatic, well-publicized incidents seem more common than they actually are. When people can easily recall specific examples of postal workplace violence—particularly vivid, shocking cases like the Edmond shooting—they overestimate the frequency of such incidents. Meanwhile, workplace violence in other sectors may receive less memorable coverage, leading to underestimation of its prevalence elsewhere.

The timing of incidents contributed to the perception of a postal violence "epidemic." Several high-profile cases occurred within a relatively short period during the late 1980s and early 1990s, creating the appearance of an emerging trend. This clustering was likely coincidental, but human pattern-recognition tendencies led people to see systematic causation rather than statistical variation.

Cultural stereotypes about government employment and bureaucracy made postal workplace violence seem plausible as a uniquely government problem. Popular perception of government work often includes themes of frustrating bureaucracy, job security that might protect poor performers, and rigid hierarchies that could create workplace tension. These stereotypes provided a ready-made explanatory framework for postal violence that seemed to confirm existing beliefs about government workplace dysfunction.

The federal employment context made postal incidents more likely to be investigated, documented, and publicized than similar private-sector events. Federal workplace violence triggers extensive investigation by multiple agencies, generates detailed public reports, and receives congressional attention that private workplace incidents typically don't. This systematic attention creates more visible and memorable records of postal violence compared to equivalent incidents elsewhere.

The postal service's unique position in American society—as both a government service and a daily presence in most communities—made incidents there feel particularly significant and threatening. Unlike violence at other government facilities that most people rarely visit, post offices are familiar community institutions where ordinary citizens conduct routine business, making violence there feel more personally relevant and concerning.

Union and management tensions within the postal service provided additional explanatory narratives for workplace violence. Labor disputes, restructuring efforts, and conflicts over working conditions created ready-made explanations for employee frustration that could seemingly lead to violence. While similar tensions exist in many workplaces, the public nature of postal service labor relations made these conflicts more visible and available for causal attribution.

The phrase's catchy, alliterative quality made it memorable and likely to spread through popular culture. "Going postal" is linguistically appealing—easy to remember, say, and repeat—which contributed to its rapid adoption and persistence. More accurate but less memorable phrases might not have gained similar cultural traction.

## 4. Verdict

**PARTIALLY ACCURATE BUT MISLEADING**: The phrase "going postal" did originate from workplace violence incidents involving postal employees in the 1980s and early 1990s, but the implication that postal workers are unusually prone to workplace violence is not supported by statistical evidence.

The etymological claim is accurate—the term emerged from media coverage of several highly publicized postal workplace shootings, particularly the 1986 Edmond, Oklahoma incident. These events were genuine, shocking, and did generate the cultural association between postal employment and workplace violence that the phrase represents.

However, the broader implication that postal workers commit workplace violence at higher rates than other occupations is false. Comprehensive analysis by federal agencies shows that postal workers' workplace violence rates are actually below the national average for all occupations. Industries involving cash handling, public contact, or isolated work typically show higher violence rates than postal services.

The persistence of the postal violence narrative reflects media coverage patterns, availability bias, and cultural stereotypes rather than statistical reality. The incidents that created the phrase were genuinely dramatic and memorable, but they were not representative of broader patterns in postal workplace safety or violence rates.

The term's cultural impact demonstrates how powerful media narratives can shape perception in ways that persist despite contrary evidence. While the phrase accurately reflects its historical origins, its continued use perpetuates misconceptions about occupational violence patterns that aren't supported by data.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The "going postal" phenomenon illustrates broader dynamics in how society processes and understands workplace violence, media narrative construction, and the relationship between statistical reality and cultural perception. This case reveals how dramatic but statistically rare events can create lasting cultural associations that resist correction even in the face of contradictory evidence.

Workplace violence research has evolved significantly since the incidents that created the "going postal" phrase, developing more sophisticated understanding of risk factors, prevention strategies, and statistical patterns. This research consistently shows that workplace violence is primarily driven by situational factors—job characteristics, work environment, and external stressors—rather than specific occupational categories. The focus on postal workers as uniquely violence-prone reflects an earlier, less nuanced understanding of workplace violence causation.

Media amplification effects play crucial roles in shaping public perception of occupational risks. The postal incidents received extensive coverage partly due to their dramatic nature and partly due to systematic factors that make federal workplace violence more newsworthy than equivalent private sector incidents. This coverage differential creates distorted public understanding of relative risks across different work environments.

The case also highlights how cultural narratives can become self-perpetuating once established. The "going postal" phrase itself may contribute to continued attention on postal workplace incidents while similar events elsewhere receive less notice or different framing. This creates a feedback loop where cultural expectations influence media coverage, which reinforces cultural beliefs.

Labor relations and workplace safety advocacy have been affected by the postal violence narrative in complex ways. While the attention may have contributed to improved workplace violence prevention programs, it may also have stigmatized postal employment and created unnecessary anxiety among postal workers and their families. The narrative can serve as a convenient explanation for workplace problems that deflects attention from more systematic issues like inadequate staffing, outdated equipment, or poor management practices.

Government employment faces unique scrutiny that can distort perception of workplace problems. Federal employees' salaries, benefits, and working conditions are matters of public record and political debate in ways that private sector employment generally isn't. This visibility can make government workplace problems seem more significant or systematic than equivalent issues in private workplaces, contributing to narratives like the postal violence association.

The evolution of workplace violence prevention has been influenced by high-profile incidents like those that created the "going postal" phrase. However, effective prevention strategies focus on evidence-based risk assessment, early intervention programs, and systematic approaches to workplace safety rather than occupation-specific assumptions. The postal service has implemented comprehensive workplace violence prevention programs that could serve as models for other organizations, but these developments often receive less attention than the historical incidents that created the cultural association.

Economic and social changes in the postal service—including automation, workforce reductions, and changing mail volume due to electronic communication—have created new workplace stressors that may be more relevant to employee wellbeing than the historical violence incidents. However, the persistence of the "going postal" narrative may overshadow attention to these contemporary workplace challenges.

The case also demonstrates the limitations of cultural phrases and metaphors in accurately representing complex social phenomena. Language shapes thought, and widespread use of "going postal" may reinforce inaccurate beliefs about workplace violence patterns while making it more difficult to have evidence-based discussions about occupational safety and violence prevention.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Fact-checking organizations approach the "going postal" claim with varying levels of nuance, generally acknowledging the phrase's origins while attempting to correct misconceptions about postal worker violence rates. However, the complexity of the claim—which involves both etymological accuracy and statistical implications—creates challenges for traditional fact-checking formats.

Snopes provides a "Mixture" rating that accurately reflects the claim's complexity. They confirm that the phrase did originate from postal workplace violence incidents while clarifying that postal workers are not statistically more prone to workplace violence than other occupations. Their analysis effectively separates the historical accuracy of the phrase's origins from the broader implications about occupational violence patterns.

The approach acknowledges that both aspects of the claim contain elements of truth and falsehood, making simple "true" or "false" ratings inadequate. This nuanced rating system better serves readers who need to understand both the phrase's legitimate historical origins and the inaccurate statistical assumptions it may promote.

However, fact-checkers face challenges in addressing the cultural and psychological factors that maintain belief in postal worker violence proneness. Simply providing statistical evidence may not be sufficient to overcome availability bias, media-driven perceptions, or cultural stereotypes that make the narrative appealing regardless of its accuracy.

More comprehensive fact-checking approaches might need to address the broader context of workplace violence research, media coverage patterns, and the factors that create lasting cultural associations between specific occupations and behavioral characteristics. This would help readers understand not just what the evidence shows about postal worker violence, but why inaccurate beliefs persist despite contrary data.

Future fact-checking on occupational stereotypes and cultural phrases might benefit from interdisciplinary approaches that combine statistical analysis, media studies, and psychological research on belief formation and persistence. The goal would be not just to correct specific misconceptions but to help readers develop better frameworks for evaluating claims about group behavior and occupational characteristics.

The "going postal" case also suggests that fact-checkers might need to develop more sophisticated approaches for addressing claims that are simultaneously historically accurate and statistically misleading. Traditional fact-checking formats may be inadequate for complex claims that involve multiple dimensions of truth and falsehood requiring different types of evidence and analysis.
