# "Power Poses Increase Testosterone and Confidence"


## 1. The Claim

**The Central Hypothesis:** The power posing hypothesis proposes that adopting expansive, open physical postures for approximately two minutes produces measurable physiological and psychological changes that enhance performance and confidence in high-stakes situations.

**Specific Physiological Claims:**

The original research claimed that brief episodes of "high-power" posing—including postures such as standing with hands on hips and feet shoulder-width apart (the "Wonder Woman" pose), sitting with arms stretched wide and feet up on a desk, or standing with arms raised in a "V" shape—produces rapid biochemical changes including:

- **Testosterone increases** of approximately 20% within minutes of posing
- **Cortisol decreases** of approximately 25% following the posing intervention
- **Sustained hormonal changes** that persist beyond the immediate posing period
- **Measurable stress response alterations** that prepare individuals for challenging situations

These hormonal claims formed the core scientific foundation for broader assertions about power posing's effectiveness as a practical intervention for anxiety reduction, confidence enhancement, and performance improvement.

**Psychological and Behavioral Claims:**

Beyond hormonal changes, power posing research claimed to produce measurable psychological effects including:

- **Increased subjective feelings of power and confidence** as measured through validated psychological scales
- **Enhanced risk tolerance** demonstrated through behavioral economic games and decision-making tasks
- **Improved performance** in job interviews, negotiations, and public speaking situations
- **Reduced anxiety and stress** in challenging interpersonal and professional contexts
- **Greater assertiveness and presence** in social and professional interactions

**Cultural and Popular Applications:**

The power posing hypothesis gained extraordinary cultural momentum following Amy Cuddy's 2012 TED talk "Your Body Language May Shape Who You Are," which became one of the most-viewed TED presentations of all time, accumulating over 60 million views across various platforms. The talk transformed academic research into a viral cultural phenomenon with widespread practical applications.

**The presentation popularized specific applications including:**

- **Pre-interview preparation routines** where job candidates would spend two minutes in bathroom stalls or private spaces adopting power poses before important meetings
- **Public speaking confidence techniques** involving expansive postures as preparation for presentations or performances
- **Workplace confidence interventions** where employees would use power posing to prepare for negotiations, difficult conversations, or leadership situations
- **Educational and therapeutic applications** in schools, therapy practices, and self-help contexts

**Theoretical Foundation in Embodied Cognition:**

The claim extends beyond specific postural effects to encompass a broader theory of embodied cognition—the idea that physical states systematically influence psychological states through bidirectional mind-body feedback loops. This theoretical framework positions power posing as scientific validation for intuitive folk psychological beliefs about the relationship between posture, confidence, and social presence.

**The embodied cognition framework suggests that:**

- **Physical expansion translates to psychological empowerment** through unconscious neurological pathways
- **Postural changes can override internal emotional states** by activating evolved dominance and confidence systems
- **Brief interventions can produce lasting changes** by triggering adaptive psychological responses to physical cues
- **Cultural body language patterns reflect biological realities** about the relationship between posture and social hierarchy

**Commercial and Institutional Integration:**

The power posing phenomenon quickly expanded beyond academic research and popular media into commercial and institutional applications:

**Corporate training programs** began incorporating power posing workshops and confidence-building sessions based on the research findings. **Educational institutions** integrated power posing techniques into public speaking curricula and student support services. **Therapeutic and coaching practices** adopted power posing as an evidence-based intervention for anxiety, confidence issues, and performance enhancement.

**Self-help industries** developed products, books, and training programs based on power posing research, creating a substantial commercial ecosystem around the claimed benefits. The scientific veneer provided by peer-reviewed research and TED talk presentation legitimized power posing within professional development and personal improvement contexts.


## 2. What's Actually True

**Original Research Methodology and Findings:**

The foundational Carney, Cuddy, and Yap (2010) study, published in *Psychological Science*, employed a randomized experimental design with 42 participants recruited from Harvard Business School and undergraduate populations. Participants were randomly assigned to hold either "high-power" poses (expansive postures designed to take up space and convey dominance) or "low-power" poses (contractive postures that minimize space occupation) for two minutes each.

**Physiological Measurements:** Researchers collected saliva samples before and approximately 17 minutes after the posing intervention to measure testosterone and cortisol levels using enzyme immunoassay techniques. The timing was designed to capture peak hormonal responses based on existing literature about stress hormone dynamics.

**Psychological Assessments:** The study measured self-reported feelings of power using validated scales, risk tolerance through a gambling task where participants could win or lose money based on their choices, and general psychological state through questionnaires assessing mood and arousal levels.

**Reported Results:** The study found that high-power posers showed approximately 20% increases in testosterone levels, 25% decreases in cortisol levels, significantly higher self-reported feelings of power, and greater risk tolerance compared to low-power posers. Effect sizes were large (Cohen's d > 0.80) across multiple outcome measures.

**Methodological Limitations and Red Flags:**

**Retrospective analysis of the original study reveals several methodological concerns that became apparent as research standards evolved:**

**Sample Size Inadequacy:** With only 42 participants (approximately 21 per condition), the study was severely underpowered for detecting plausible effect sizes in hormonal measures. Post-hoc power analyses suggest the study had insufficient statistical power to reliably detect the claimed effects, making the large observed effects statistically suspicious.

**Multiple Comparisons Problem:** The study examined numerous outcome variables (testosterone, cortisol, subjective power, risk tolerance, mood measures) without appropriate statistical corrections for multiple testing. This approach inflates the probability of finding significant results by chance alone.

**Hormone Measurement Issues:** Salivary hormone levels show substantial individual variation and measurement error. The study's single-timepoint measurements (before and after) cannot distinguish true treatment effects from natural fluctuations, diurnal rhythms, or measurement artifacts.

**Potential Experimenter Bias:** The study was not double-blinded, meaning researchers knew which participants were in which condition during data collection and analysis. This creates opportunities for unconscious bias in participant interaction, data collection, and analysis decisions.

**Ecological Validity Questions:** The laboratory setting with explicit posing instructions may not generalize to real-world situations where power posing would presumably be used. The artificial nature of the experimental context may have enhanced expectation effects or demand characteristics.

**Publication and Statistical Concerns:**

**Effect Size Implausibility:** The reported effect sizes (Cohen's d > 0.80) were unusually large for brief psychological interventions, particularly for physiological measures. Such large effects from simple behavioral interventions are rare in psychology and should have prompted skepticism about the findings' robustness.

**Selective Reporting Potential:** While the published paper reported positive results, it's unclear whether the researchers collected additional measures that showed null effects or whether the analysis strategy was influenced by observed patterns in the data.

**Replication Consideration Absence:** The original study was published without discussion of replication needs or recognition of the extraordinary nature of the claimed effects. The authors presented their findings as robust without acknowledging the preliminary nature of single-study evidence for such dramatic claims.


**Current Scientific Consensus and Evidence Assessment:**

As of 2024, the scientific consensus on power posing has crystallized around several key conclusions based on cumulative evidence from multiple independent replication attempts, meta-analyses, and methodological improvements in the field:

**Hormonal Effects: No Credible Evidence:** The dramatic hormonal claims that formed the core of the original power posing hypothesis—significant increases in testosterone and decreases in cortisol from brief posing—are not supported by high-quality replication studies. Multiple independent attempts using improved methodology, larger samples, and better controls have consistently failed to detect these effects.

**The absence of hormonal effects is because:** these claims were the most objective and measurable aspects of the original hypothesis, they provided the biological plausibility for psychological effects, they formed the basis for therapeutic and commercial applications, and their failure undermines the theoretical foundation of embodied cognition claims about rapid mind-body feedback.

**Subjective Effects: Weak and Contested Evidence:** Some studies continue to report modest effects on self-reported confidence or feelings of power, but these findings are inconsistent, small in magnitude, and potentially explained by non-specific factors including demand characteristics (participants guessing the hypothesis), expectation effects (believing the intervention should work), placebo responses (benefits from engaging in any novel activity), and social desirability bias (reporting what researchers appear to want to hear).

**The subjective effects that persist in some studies may reflect:** temporary mood enhancement from taking a break and engaging in a novel activity, placebo responses to participating in a scientifically-framed intervention, social psychological effects of adopting confident postures in social contexts, or measurement artifacts in self-report assessments of confidence and power.

**Performance Effects: No Reliable Evidence:** Claims that power posing improves actual performance in job interviews, negotiations, public speaking, or other high-stakes situations lack credible empirical support. Studies examining behavioral outcomes typically find no meaningful differences between power posing and control conditions when measured through objective performance criteria.

**Mechanistic Understanding and Theoretical Implications:**

**Embodied Cognition Theory Revision:** The power posing failure has prompted reconsideration of embodied cognition theory more broadly. While some mind-body relationships are well-established (facial feedback effects, gesture and spatial reasoning), the power posing claims represented an overextension of these concepts to dramatic physiological changes from brief postural interventions.

**Current embodied cognition research** emphasizes more modest, well-controlled effects with clear mechanistic understanding rather than dramatic claims about rapid psychological transformation through physical manipulation.

**Methodological Lessons for Psychological Research:**

The power posing case has contributed to substantial improvements in psychological research methodology including:

**Pre-registration Requirements:** Many journals now require researchers to specify their hypotheses, methods, and analysis plans before data collection to prevent selective reporting and analytical flexibility.

**Sample Size Standards:** Funding agencies and journals increasingly require power analyses and adequate sample sizes to detect plausible effect sizes rather than relying on statistical significance from underpowered studies.

**Replication Emphasis:** The field has developed stronger norms around replication, with prestigious journals dedicating space to replication studies and researchers receiving credit for conducting and publishing replication attempts.

**Effect Size Plausibility:** Reviewers and editors are more skeptical of unusually large effect sizes from simple interventions, requiring stronger evidence and theoretical justification for dramatic claims.


## 3. Why People Believe This

**The Replication Crisis and Scientific Self-Correction:**

The power posing literature provides a paradigmatic example of how psychological science has evolved through the "replication crisis"—the recognition that many celebrated research findings from the 2000s and early 2010s failed to replicate under improved methodological standards. This crisis revealed systematic problems including publication bias, underpowered studies, flexible analytic practices, and insufficient attention to effect size plausibility.

**Major Replication Attempts and Failures:**

**Ranehill et al. (2015) - Comprehensive Replication:** Published in *Psychological Science*, this study represented the first major independent replication attempt with substantially improved methodology. The researchers recruited 200 participants—nearly five times the original sample size—and employed identical posing protocols with enhanced experimental controls.

**Key methodological improvements included:** pre-registered analysis plans to prevent selective reporting, improved hormone assay procedures with greater measurement precision, longer baseline periods to establish individual hormonal profiles, and more rigorous randomization and blinding procedures.

**Results:** The study found no significant effects of power posing on testosterone or cortisol levels, with confidence intervals that excluded the effect sizes reported in the original study. Some modest effects on self-reported confidence measures were observed, but these were smaller and less consistent than originally claimed.

**Simmons and Simonsohn (2017) - Meta-Analytic Assessment:** This comprehensive analysis examined over 30 studies with more than 1,000 participants, representing the most thorough quantitative synthesis of power posing research available. The analysis employed sophisticated statistical techniques to assess publication bias, study quality effects, and overall effect size estimates.

**Findings revealed clear patterns:** Studies with larger samples consistently showed smaller or null effects, while positive findings clustered in smaller, methodologically weaker studies. This pattern strongly suggests that early positive results reflected statistical artifacts rather than genuine effects. When analyses were restricted to high-quality studies with adequate sample sizes, effect sizes for hormonal measures were indistinguishable from zero.

**The Original Researcher's Disavowal:**

Perhaps the most significant development was Dana Carney's 2016 position paper explicitly disavowing the hormonal effects she had originally reported as first author of the foundational study. In a candid assessment posted on her academic website, Carney stated: "I do not believe that 'power pose' effects are real" and outlined her evolving concerns about the original methodology and subsequent failures to replicate physiological findings.

**Carney's analysis included several critical points:**

**Statistical Power Recognition:** She acknowledged that the original study was severely underpowered and that the large effect sizes were implausible given the subtle nature of the intervention.

**Methodological Evolution:** She noted that research standards had improved substantially since 2010, making clear that studies meeting current methodological requirements consistently failed to replicate the original findings.

**Researcher Responsibility:** Her statement represented an unusual example of scientific self-correction where researchers publicly acknowledge problems with their influential work, prioritizing scientific integrity over personal reputation.

**Continuing Advocacy Versus Scientific Evidence:**

Amy Cuddy's response to the replication failures created additional controversy within the scientific community. Rather than accepting the null replication results, Cuddy continued advocating for power posing's effectiveness while shifting emphasis from hormonal changes to subjective confidence effects.

**This position created tension between:** scientific evidence consensus (hormonal effects are not reliable), personal investment in research findings (Cuddy's career became closely tied to power posing), public commitments (TED talk viewed by millions), and commercial interests (books, speaking engagements, training programs based on the research).

**Cuddy's continued advocacy highlighted ethical and professional questions about researcher responsibility when initial findings fail to replicate, particularly when those findings have achieved widespread cultural influence and commercial application.**


## 4. Verdict

**Classification: CONTESTED CONSENSUS (C)**
**Gap Category:** Contested-Consensus

**Comprehensive Assessment Framework:**

The power posing hypothesis represents a contested consensus case where initial scientific findings achieved extraordinary cultural influence before subsequent research undermined the original claims. The case demonstrates how preliminary scientific results can become culturally embedded through popular media amplification, creating ongoing tension between scientific evidence and public belief.

**Evidence-Based Component Analysis:**

**Hormonal Claims: DEFINITIVELY FALSE**

The specific assertions that power posing increases testosterone levels by approximately 20% and decreases cortisol levels by approximately 25% are no longer scientifically credible based on multiple independent lines of evidence:

- **Replication Failures:** Multiple high-quality studies with larger samples and improved methodology have consistently failed to detect hormonal effects
- **Original Researcher Disavowal:** Dana Carney, the first author of the foundational study, has explicitly stated she no longer believes the hormonal effects are real
- **Meta-Analytic Evidence:** Comprehensive analyses of all available studies show effect sizes indistinguishable from zero when study quality is high
- **Methodological Retrospective:** The original study is now recognized as severely underpowered with effect sizes that were implausibly large for the proposed intervention

**Subjective Confidence Claims: UNPROVEN/WEAK EVIDENCE**

Claims about increased feelings of power, confidence, and psychological empowerment receive mixed support with important qualifications:

- **Inconsistent Replication:** Some studies find modest subjective effects while others find none, creating an inconsistent pattern that suggests weak or unreliable effects
- **Potential Confounding:** Observed effects may result from non-specific factors (novelty, expectation, demand characteristics) rather than specific power posing mechanisms
- **Effect Size Limitations:** When effects are detected, they are typically small and of questionable practical significance
- **Methodological Concerns:** Subjective measures are particularly vulnerable to bias and expectation effects that may explain observed findings

**Performance and Behavioral Claims: NO CREDIBLE EVIDENCE**

Assertions that power posing improves performance in job interviews, negotiations, public speaking, or other real-world situations lack empirical support:

- **Objective Outcome Studies:** Research examining actual behavioral performance typically finds no meaningful differences between power posing and control conditions
- **Ecological Validity Questions:** Laboratory findings on subjective confidence do not translate to measurable improvements in real-world performance contexts
- **Commercial Application Failure:** Despite widespread adoption in corporate training and educational settings, no credible evidence supports effectiveness for practical applications

**Cultural Impact Versus Scientific Validity:**

**Massive Cultural Penetration:** The power posing concept achieved extraordinary cultural reach through Amy Cuddy's viral TED talk, corporate training programs, educational curricula, therapeutic applications, and self-help industries, creating a substantial gap between public belief and scientific evidence.

**Scientific Community Response:** The research community has largely reached consensus that the original claims were not supported by robust evidence, but this scientific correction has not effectively penetrated public consciousness or commercial applications.

**Ethical and Professional Implications:** The case raises important questions about researcher responsibility when influential findings fail to replicate, the role of science communication in amplifying preliminary results, and institutional obligations to correct misinformation derived from initially credible sources.

**Practical Guidance for Consumers:**

**Evidence-Based Recommendations:** Individuals considering power posing should understand that:
- No credible evidence supports hormonal benefits or measurable performance improvements
- Any subjective confidence effects are likely modest, temporary, and possibly due to placebo responses
- Similar or superior benefits might be achieved through other confidence-building activities, brief relaxation, positive self-talk, or adequate preparation for challenging situations
- The practice is generally harmless but should not be considered a scientifically validated intervention

**Alternative Approaches:** For individuals seeking evidence-based confidence and performance enhancement, research supports interventions including adequate preparation and practice, cognitive behavioral techniques, mindfulness and relaxation training, physical exercise, and social skills development.

**Institutional Implications:** Organizations currently promoting power posing in training or therapeutic contexts should consider whether continued endorsement aligns with current scientific evidence and ethical obligations to provide evidence-based interventions.

**Meta-Commentary on Scientific Knowledge Evolution:**

The power posing case exemplifies both the strengths and limitations of scientific self-correction processes. While the research community has effectively identified and corrected the false positive findings, this correction occurred after substantial cultural embedding and commercial application of the original claims.

**This temporal gap between initial publication and definitive refutation** highlights systemic challenges in science communication, including: the speed of modern media amplification versus the deliberate pace of scientific validation, economic and reputational incentives that can interfere with scientific objectivity, and the difficulty of correcting misinformation once it becomes culturally embedded.

**Future Implications:** The power posing controversy has contributed to improved research standards, greater emphasis on replication, and more cautious science communication practices. It serves as a valuable case study for understanding how preliminary scientific findings should be communicated and applied, emphasizing the importance of distinguishing between promising initial results and robust, well-replicated effects.

**Final Verdict Summary:**

The power posing hypothesis represents a contested consensus where initial promising findings failed to withstand scientific scrutiny but achieved cultural influence that persists despite empirical refutation. The specific hormonal claims are definitively false, subjective effects remain unproven or weak, and performance benefits lack credible support. While the practice is harmless, individuals and institutions should understand that power posing is not a scientifically validated intervention for confidence enhancement or performance improvement.

The case serves as an important reminder of the need for scientific humility, robust replication requirements, and responsible science communication that acknowledges the preliminary nature of initial findings before they achieve widespread cultural adoption.

## 5. The Wider Picture

**The Replication Crisis in Psychology and Broader Scientific Reform:**

The power posing controversy emerged within the broader "replication crisis" that has affected multiple scientific disciplines, particularly psychology and medicine. This crisis revealed systematic problems in how research is conducted, reviewed, and published that inflate the apparent strength of evidence for many scientific claims.

**Institutional and Cultural Factors Contributing to Replication Problems:**

**Publication Bias:** Academic journals preferentially publish positive, novel, and statistically significant results while rejecting negative or null findings, creating systematic bias in the published literature toward false positive results.

**Career Incentives:** Academic promotion and tenure systems reward quantity of publications and media attention rather than research quality or replication, creating incentives for researchers to pursue dramatic but potentially unreliable findings.

**Statistical Misunderstanding:** Many researchers lacked adequate statistical training to recognize problems with small sample sizes, multiple comparisons, and analytical flexibility that inflated false positive rates.

**Media and Cultural Amplification:** Science communication systems that reward simple, dramatic messages led to oversimplification and premature application of preliminary research findings.

**The Broader Context of Self-Help and Positive Psychology:**

Power posing gained traction within the broader positive psychology movement and self-help culture that emphasizes individual agency, simple interventions for complex problems, and scientific validation of intuitive folk psychology beliefs.

**Cultural Appeal Factors:** The power posing claim appealed to widely-held beliefs about the relationship between confidence and success, offered a simple and accessible intervention requiring no special training or equipment, provided scientific legitimacy for folk psychological intuitions about body language, and promised immediate results through brief, private actions.

**Commercial and Therapeutic Applications:** The research findings quickly spawned commercial applications including corporate training programs, therapeutic interventions, educational curricula, and self-help products that created economic interests in maintaining belief in power posing effectiveness.

**These commercial applications created feedback loops** where financial interests reinforced continued promotion of power posing despite accumulating negative evidence, illustrating how scientific claims can become institutionally embedded in ways that resist correction.

**TED Talks and Science Communication Evolution:**

Amy Cuddy's TED talk represents a case study in how modern science communication can amplify preliminary research findings to global audiences before scientific consensus has emerged. The talk's extraordinary reach (over 60 million views) created cultural momentum that outpaced scientific validation.

**The TED format's characteristics—** simplified presentation, inspirational messaging, actionable takeaways, and charismatic delivery—may systematically favor compelling but preliminary findings over cautious, well-established science.

**This dynamic raises questions about:** responsible science communication timing (when should preliminary findings be shared with public audiences), researcher obligations when findings fail to replicate (how should scientists respond to evidence contradicting their popular claims), and institutional responsibilities (how should organizations handle speakers whose research credibility changes after presentation).

**Related Scientific Controversies and Patterns:**

Power posing shares characteristics with other psychological claims that achieved popular success before facing replication problems:

**The "Mozart Effect":** Claims that listening to classical music enhances spatial reasoning, later found to be largely artifactual or due to general arousal effects rather than music-specific benefits.

**Stereotype Threat:** While the basic phenomenon has some support, many specific claims about its mechanisms and ubiquity have failed to replicate or shown much smaller effects than originally reported.

**Priming Effects in Social Psychology:** Numerous dramatic findings about unconscious behavioral priming have failed to replicate, leading to substantial revision of claims about subliminal influence on behavior.

**Brain Training Games:** Claims about cognitive enhancement from computerized training programs have largely failed to show transfer to real-world abilities despite initial promising results.

**These cases share common patterns:** initial dramatic findings with large effect sizes, rapid translation to popular applications and commercial products, media amplification of preliminary results, subsequent replication failures, and ongoing debates about what evidence, if any, remains credible.


## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

**Coverage Gap and Fact-Checking Challenges:**

Snopes has not provided formal fact-checking analysis of the power posing controversy, despite its massive cultural reach, substantial commercial applications, and significant subsequent scientific developments including replication failures and researcher disavowals. This coverage gap represents a notable absence -checking oversight of viral scientific claims that achieve widespread cultural adoption through social media amplification and popular psychology platforms.

**The absence of Snopes coverage reflects several analytical challenges that make power posing claims particularly complex for traditional fact-checking approaches:**

**Scientific Evolution and Timing Issues:** The power posing claim experienced a complete reversal in scientific status between its initial publication (2010) and subsequent replication attempts (2015-2017). Traditional fact-checking typically addresses static claims rather than evolving scientific controversies where the evidential landscape changes substantially over time.

**Methodological Complexity Requirements:** Evaluating power posing claims requires sophisticated understanding of psychological research methodology, hormone measurement techniques, statistical power analysis, and the replication crisis in social psychology. These technical requirements exceed the typical expertise base of general-purpose fact-checking organizations.

**Gradations of Evidence Strength:** Unlike binary factual claims that can be rated "true" or "false," power posing involves multiple components (hormonal effects, subjective confidence, behavioral performance) with different evidence bases and contested interpretations. Different aspects of the overall claim have different evidential status, complicating simple fact-checking classifications.

**Cultural Impact Versus Scientific Validity:** The claim's extraordinary cultural influence and practical applications create contexts where fact-checking must address both scientific accuracy and social consequences. Millions of people adopted power posing practices based on initial research findings, creating complex ethical and practical considerations for fact-checking organizations.

**If Snopes were to address power posing under their current evaluation framework, the assessment would likely require nuanced ratings:**

**"False" for specific hormonal claims** (testosterone increases, cortisol decreases) based on replication failures and original researcher disavowal. **"Unproven" or "Mixed" for psychological effects** (confidence, performance) that remain contested with weak and inconsistent evidence. **"Context Required" for broader embodied cognition claims** that have some support but were oversimplified in popular presentations.
