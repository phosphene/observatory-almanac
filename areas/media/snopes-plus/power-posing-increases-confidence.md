# Adopting expansive "power poses" increases testosterone and feelings of power

## 1. The Claim

Stand like Superman for two minutes — feet wide, hands on hips, chest out — and your body will chemically reward you. Your testosterone will surge by roughly 20 percent, your cortisol (the stress hormone) will drop by about 25 percent, and you will feel more powerful, more confident, and more willing to take risks before a high-stakes moment like a job interview or a performance review. The pose itself is the mechanism: your body's physiology literally changes to match the expansive shape you've assumed.

This is the core claim of "power posing," the theory that body posture causally influences hormonal state and psychological confidence in ways that are large, rapid, and behaviorally consequential. It achieved mainstream cultural saturation through a 2012 TED Talk by social psychologist Amy Cuddy, which became one of the most watched TED Talks of all time, accumulating tens of millions of views and seeding self-help literature worldwide. The underlying premise was thrilling in its simplicity: you could hack your own neuroendocrine system with a free, two-minute exercise, and walk into the room already winning.

The specific claims in their strongest form are:

- Two minutes of expansive posture raises salivary testosterone by 19–20 percent compared to contractive postures.
- Two minutes of expansive posture lowers salivary cortisol by 25 percent compared to contractive postures.
- These hormonal changes are what produce the downstream psychological effects: heightened feelings of power, increased tolerance for risk, and improved performance in evaluative social settings.
- The mechanism generalizes beyond laboratory conditions to real-world high-stakes environments.

The weaker, residual version of the claim — which Cuddy continued to defend after replication failures — is that power posing increases subjective feelings of power and confidence even if the hormonal effects are absent or negligible. The distinction matters enormously for evaluating the science.

## 2. What's Actually True

The replication record tells a complicated story with a clear direction: the hormonal and neuroendocrine claims failed to replicate when tested under more rigorous conditions. The subjective-feeling claim survives, weakly, but carries far less theoretical weight.

**The original study (Carney, Cuddy, and Yap, 2010)**

The foundational paper was published in *Psychological Science* by Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy, and Andy Yap. Forty-two participants were assigned to hold either high-power (expansive) or low-power (contractive) poses for one minute each, across two poses. Saliva was collected before and after. Results showed that high-power posers had higher testosterone and lower cortisol relative to low-power posers, and reported feeling more powerful and more willing to take a gambling risk. The sample size was small — 42 participants — which in social psychology's pre-replication-crisis era was unremarkable for a pilot study. Effect sizes were substantial but variance was wide.

**The Ranehill et al. (2015) replication**

Eva Ranehill and colleagues at the University of Zurich conducted a large pre-registered replication using 200 participants — nearly five times the original sample. They followed the original protocol carefully, measuring testosterone, cortisol, and self-reported feelings of power. Their results: no significant effect on testosterone, no significant effect on cortisol. Subjective feelings of power were nominally higher in the high-power group, but the hormonal hypothesis — the mechanistic core of the original claim — was not supported. The paper was published in *Psychological Science* alongside a commentary from Cuddy disputing the replication's design choices.

**The Credé and Phillips (2017) meta-analysis**

Marcus Credé and Sarah Phillips conducted a comprehensive meta-analysis pooling results from multiple studies examining power posing's effects on hormonal outcomes. Their findings were clear: after correcting for publication bias and small-sample inflation, there was no evidence of a meaningful hormonal effect. The meta-analysis estimated the true effect of power posing on testosterone at approximately zero. A small positive effect on self-reported feelings survived, but at a much reduced magnitude compared to the original study's reported effects.

**Carney's recantation (2016)**

In a particularly significant development, Dana Carney — first author of the original 2010 paper — posted a public statement on her academic website in 2016 explicitly stating that she no longer believed the power posing effect was real. She cited concerns about the original study's small sample, multiple dependent variables, flexible analysis, and the accumulating failure to replicate. Carney stated she did not believe in the effect and would not publish or teach about it as though it were real. This kind of explicit recantation by a primary author is unusual in academic psychology and was widely noted as a significant moment in the replication crisis discourse.

**Cuddy's position**

Cuddy did not recant. She continued to defend the subjective-feeling version of the claim, arguing that the hormonal effects were never the main point and that the experience of feeling more powerful — even without hormonal changes — was meaningful and actionable. She published a book (*Presence*, 2015) and continued speaking publicly about power posing. Her defense narrowed the claim considerably from what the original paper asserted and what the TED Talk communicated, but she maintained that the residual effect was worth defending.

**The current scientific consensus**

The hormonal claims — the ones that powered the viral spread of the idea — are not supported. The testosterone and cortisol effects from the 2010 paper have not held up under replication with larger samples and pre-registration. A modest effect on self-reported confidence may remain, but this is both smaller and more ambiguous than the original findings, and its practical significance in real-world performance settings has not been established. Power posing, in its original formulation, is a case study in how preliminary findings can become cultural facts before the scientific process has time to correct them.

## 3. Why People Believe This

The power posing story spread for reasons that have less to do with scientific rigor and more to do with how ideas find cultural traction. Understanding the spread explains why the correction has been slower and less complete than the original propagation.

**The TED Talk as amplification engine**

Cuddy's 2012 TED Talk was exceptionally well-crafted for emotional resonance. It opened with her own story of a traumatic brain injury that she said had damaged her intelligence, framed power posing as an insight accessible to anyone who feels like an impostor, and concluded with a call to action that was simple and immediately actionable: go to the bathroom before your next interview and stand in a power pose for two minutes. The narrative arc — from vulnerability to transformation through science — was perfectly constructed for viral sharing. TED Talks by design favor stories with clear takeaways and emotional payoff; power posing was built for the format.

**The simplicity of the mechanism**

Theories that propose a simple physical action producing a large, measurable chemical change are inherently satisfying to the human pattern-recognition system. The idea that posture causes hormonal change resembles confirmed phenomena like the physical feedback of smiling affecting mood, or cold water on the face triggering a diving reflex. It fits an existing cognitive template: body and brain are more deeply interconnected than we naively assume. This template is not wrong as a general principle, which made the specific and overclaimed version easier to accept.

**Embodied cognition as a legitimizing frame**

The broader field of embodied cognition — which studies how bodily states influence cognitive and emotional processing — was gaining scientific traction in the early 2010s. Power posing arrived packaged in that legitimate research tradition, borrowing credibility from a real and interesting set of phenomena. The framing made the specific claims feel like natural extensions of established science rather than isolated outliers requiring scrutiny.

**Self-help appeal and confirmation bias**

Millions of people have stood in a power pose before a stressful moment and reported feeling better afterward. This confirms the claim experientially even when placebo effects, expectancy effects, and the general calming benefit of deliberate breathing or posture attention are sufficient to explain the experience. The intervention is pleasant, free, and harmless; when something feels helpful, it tends to survive belief revision more robustly than purely abstract claims.

**Incomplete coverage of the correction**

Science journalism is structurally better at amplifying positive findings than corrections. The 2010 paper generated enormous press coverage. The 2015 replication received coverage in science media but not at remotely comparable scale in general media. Carney's 2016 recantation was covered in science-adjacent outlets but did not reach the hundreds of millions of people who had seen the TED Talk. The cultural correction has been slow and uneven, leaving a large population still operating under the original formulation.

**Motivated reasoning among practitioners**

Life coaches, executive trainers, HR departments, and sports psychologists had incorporated power posing into their practice. Once an idea is embedded in professional protocols and identity, correction faces motivated resistance. Practitioners who had taught power posing as established fact had personal and reputational reasons to interpret ambiguous follow-up findings charitably and to selectively cite the evidence that supported the residual claim.

## 4. Verdict

**The hormonal and neuroendocrine claims are not supported.** The testosterone and cortisol effects reported in Carney, Cuddy, and Yap (2010) have failed to replicate in larger, better-powered, pre-registered studies. A meta-analysis of the hormonal evidence found no meaningful effect after correcting for publication bias. The first author of the original paper has publicly stated she no longer believes the effect is real.

**A modest effect on subjective feelings of power may exist**, but its magnitude is substantially smaller than originally reported, its practical significance in real-world performance is unestablished, and it is consistent with expectancy and placebo effects rather than a specific physiological mechanism.

**The claim as culturally transmitted is false.** The version that became a global self-help phenomenon — two minutes of posing produces measurable hormonal changes that chemically prime you for performance — is not supported by the current evidence base.

**The claim in its weakest surviving form — that power posing makes some people feel more confident — is probably true but trivially so.** It is consistent with general relaxation, intentional breathing, and expectancy effects. It does not require a novel hormonal mechanism or justify the specific protocol of the original study.

**EARC Assessment: C (Contested-consensus)**

The scientific community has largely reached a consensus that the strong hormonal claims are unsupported, but Cuddy and some researchers continue to defend a weaker residual version. The full retraction of the original paper has not occurred. The popular culture version of the claim remains widely circulated. The situation is not "clearly false everywhere" — the weakest surviving claim has not been refuted — but the version that made power posing famous is not supported.

## 5. The Wider Picture

The power posing debate is a window into the replication crisis in social psychology and into the structural conditions that allow scientific overclaiming to propagate at cultural scale before correction can catch up.

**The replication crisis in social psychology**

The early 2010s saw the beginning of a large-scale reckoning with the reliability of published findings in social and experimental psychology. The "crisis" was not a scandal involving fraud (though some fraud was discovered) but a systemic problem with how the incentive structures of academic publishing interacted with human statistical intuition. Small samples, flexible analysis pipelines, publication bias toward positive results, and widespread underpowering of studies had produced a published literature in which many celebrated findings — priming effects, ego depletion, facial feedback, power posing — failed to survive rigorous replication attempts.

Power posing became a prominent case not because it was uniquely problematic but because of its unusual visibility. Most social psychology findings that failed to replicate were known only to specialists. Power posing had a TED Talk with tens of millions of views. Its failure to replicate was therefore unusually public, unusually contested, and unusually consequential for a broader audience's trust in psychological science.

**The role of pre-registration**

One reform that emerged from the replication crisis is pre-registration: researchers publicly commit to their hypotheses, sample sizes, and analysis plans before collecting data, making it impossible to analyze data in multiple ways and report only the significant result. The Ranehill et al. (2015) study was pre-registered; the original Carney et al. (2010) study was not. This difference is methodologically meaningful. Pre-registered studies are more conservative and less likely to produce Type I errors (false positives). The contrast between the exploratory 2010 study and the pre-registered 2015 replication is a clean illustration of why pre-registration became a scientific norm.

**Embodied cognition: what actually holds**

The broader field that power posing drew upon — embodied cognition — contains genuine and replicated findings, though often with smaller effect sizes and more restricted conditions than popular accounts suggest. The facial feedback hypothesis (that the physical act of smiling influences mood) has had its own replication controversies and a large pre-registered multi-laboratory study found no support for the strong version; a more recent large-scale study found a small effect. The general principle that bodily states and cognitive-emotional states interact is well-founded; the specific mechanisms proposed in popularized versions of embodied cognition research are frequently overclaimed.

**Hormones, dominance, and the Testosterone-Dominance hypothesis**

The hormonal mechanism proposed for power posing drew on a real and reasonably well-established finding: in many animal species and some human contexts, testosterone levels respond to competitive outcomes, with winners showing higher testosterone and losers lower. This "winner-loser effect" or testosterone-dominance hypothesis is robust in the animal literature and has human analogues in competitive sports and electoral outcomes. The claim embedded in power posing was an extension of this framework — could you produce the hormonal profile of a winner through posture alone? The answer, given current evidence, appears to be no. The winner effect is a consequence of competitive outcomes, not a product of posture adopted before competition. The logical extension was plausible but incorrect.

**Self-help and the evidence gap**

Power posing's cultural afterlife raises broader questions about the relationship between popular self-help interventions and evidence standards. Millions of people continue to use power posing; many report that it helps them. In the absence of evidence of harm, and given that the residual subjective effect has not been disproven, there is a pragmatic argument that the intervention is acceptable to recommend. But this pragmatic defense is importantly different from the original scientific claim, and conflating them obscures the question of what science actually showed. The distinction matters for how we consume self-help advice derived from psychological research.

**The communication problem**

The power posing episode reveals a structural tension in science communication. Researchers have incentives to communicate their findings compellingly and to emphasize their significance; journalists and TED Talk formats reward simple narratives with large claims; the public has limited bandwidth to track follow-up corrections and revisions. The result is a systematic bias in which positive findings propagate further than corrections. This is not a problem unique to power posing — it appears in nutrition science, cancer screening research, and economics — but power posing provided an unusually clean case study because the original propagation and the subsequent correction both happened in relatively transparent, well-documented ways.

## 6. How Fact-Checkers Handle It

Power posing occupies an unusual position in the fact-checking landscape: it is not addressed by Snopes, PolitiFact, or most mainstream fact-checking organizations because it is primarily a scientific claim rather than a political or viral misinformation claim. Its entry point into the fact-checking ecosystem has been through science journalism, academic commentary, and the broader reporting on the replication crisis rather than through the standard debunking pipeline.

**Snopes and mainstream fact-checkers**

Snopes has not published a dedicated entry on power posing as of the time of this writing. This is consistent with Snopes's general focus on viral social media claims, urban legends, and political misinformation rather than scientific debates about published research. Power posing is not a claim that circulates as a meme or a political talking point — it circulates as a TED Talk, a book, and a self-help practice. This places it outside the standard fact-checker's purview.

**Science journalism**

The most rigorous public documentation of the power posing controversy has come from science journalists and commentators. Ed Yong at *The Atlantic*, Andrew Gelman at *Statistical Modeling, Causal Inference, and Social Science*, and various contributors at *Science* and *Nature* covered the replication failure and Carney's recantation in detail. The coverage in science-adjacent outlets was thorough and clearly concluded that the hormonal claims were unsupported. But this coverage reached a different and smaller audience than the TED Talk.

**The Cuddy-Carney split as a fact-checking complication**

The fact that the two senior authors of the original paper disagree about what the evidence shows is itself a complication for straightforward fact-checking. Cuddy's continued defense of a weaker version of the claim makes it possible for interested parties to frame the debate as ongoing scientific controversy rather than settled failure. Distinguishing "the strong claim is false" from "there is nothing here" requires nuance that flat fact-checking verdicts do not always accommodate. The nuanced verdict — the hormonal mechanism is not supported; a small subjective effect may exist but explains little of the cultural appeal — is harder to communicate concisely than either "true" or "false."

**What fact-checkers should note**

If power posing claims appear in public discourse — in corporate training materials, wellness content, or media coverage — the accurate characterization is:

1. The testosterone and cortisol claims are not supported by current evidence and failed to replicate in larger studies.
2. The first author of the original paper no longer believes the effect is real.
3. A small subjective effect on feelings of confidence may exist, consistent with expectancy effects.
4. The intervention is probably harmless, but recommending it as scientifically validated is not accurate.
5. The TED Talk framing substantially overclaims what the original study showed, and the original study itself was underpowered and exploratory.

**Template for corrections**

When power posing claims appear: note the original study, note the replication failure (Ranehill et al., 2015), note the meta-analysis (Credé & Phillips, 2017), note Carney's recantation, and note that Cuddy's continued defense concerns only the much-reduced subjective claim. This is sufficient to contextualize the claim for any general audience without requiring detailed statistical literacy.
