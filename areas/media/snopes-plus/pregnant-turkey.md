## §1 Claim and Verdict Summary

The claim under investigation is that cooks have stuffed roast turkeys with Cornish game hens in order to convince credulous family members—typically during Thanksgiving dinner—that they had cooked and were about to consume a "pregnant turkey." The prank exploits a surprising but documented gap in common biological knowledge: some people either do not know, or in the moment of surprise forget, that turkeys are birds and therefore lay eggs rather than carrying live young to term. The shock of discovering a small bird inside the cavity of a larger cooked bird produces, in those who accept the deception, a moment of genuine horror at having apparently cooked a pregnant animal and its unborn offspring.

Snopes veteran folklore investigator David Emery (2022) rated this claim **True**. The pregnant turkey prank is a real practice with a traceable internet history extending at least to 1996, documented examples in email folklore from 2000 onward, and extensive contemporary evidence in the form of YouTube and TikTok videos in which the prank is successfully executed and recorded. Emery describes it as a "relatively new tradition"—new in the sense that its documented origins appear to coincide with the internet era and it shows no clear pre-internet antecedents, unlike many Thanksgiving food legends that trace to much older oral tradition.

This entry examines the prank's documented history, the biology of turkey reproduction that underlies the misconception being exploited, the anthropology of holiday pranks as a recurring tradition type, and the epistemics of what Emery calls "scarelore"—a category of humorous internet content designed to provoke fear or disgust before revealing itself as joke.

## §2 Documenting the Prank: From 1996 Forum Post to TikTok Era

The earliest traceable instance of the pregnant turkey prank in digital archives is a brief instructional forum post from 1996. The post's content was: *"Here's one for Thanksgiving: Put a cornish game hen inside the turkey before you cook it. Tell the people at the table that it was pregnant. For the squeamish, a hard-boiled egg is almost as good!"* This text, while brief, establishes all the essential elements of the prank as it continues to be practiced: the Cornish game hen as the false "fetus," the dinner-table moment of revelation, and even the alternative hard-boiled egg variant for scenarios where securing a game hen is impractical.

The 1996 post predates the era of social media, YouTube, and TikTok by a decade or more. Its presence in an internet forum at that date indicates that the prank was either already known and being shared digitally, or was being proposed as an original idea (it is impossible to determine intent from a brief forum post). What the 1996 record establishes is that by the mid-1990s, someone found the concept sufficiently clever and plausible to propose it publicly.

By November 2000, the prank had evolved from an instruction set to a narrative form. Emery cites a forwarded email from November 2000 recounting a specific family incident in first-person prose: a mother, knowing her sister was "gullible," hid a Cornish game hen inside the turkey while her sister was sent to the store on a pretextual errand; at the dinner table, the sister pulled out the game hen and, when told "you killed a pregnant bird," began to cry. The mother spent two hours convincing her sister that turkeys lay eggs. This narrative structure—with specific characters, an emotional arc, and a comedic punchline—represents the transition from prank instruction to folk narrative, suggesting that the practice had by 2000 been performed and discussed sufficiently to generate a body of anecdotal stories.

The contemporary evidence from YouTube and TikTok is extensive. Emery notes that a YouTube search for "pregnant turkey" yields dozens of video examples, documenting successful and unsuccessful pranks across multiple years and regional contexts. The movement onto video platforms represents a further evolution: the prank now generates not just folk narrative but first-person documentary evidence, with the reactions of the pranked parties recorded and shared. This evolution from forum post to email narrative to social video is itself a useful case study in how prank traditions evolve and amplify across platform generations.

## §3 Epistemic Novelty: Scarelore, Biological Ignorance Exploitation, and the Architecture of a Successful Prank

The pregnant turkey prank illuminates a specific and underanalyzed category of humor that David Emery (2022) characterizing as *scarelore*—a portmanteau term used in Snopes's own analytical vocabulary to describe content that uses fear or disgust to produce a comedic effect, typically by revealing, after the initial horror response, that the frightening premise was false or was a prank. The pregnant turkey prank is a particularly clean example of scarelore because its architecture depends on a genuine, if momentary, biological misconception.

The prank's mechanism can be analyzed in three functional stages:

**Stage 1 — Exploitation of knowledge gap:** The prank works because, under conditions of social pressure and surprise, some people fail to immediately access the knowledge that turkeys lay eggs. This is not merely a failure of biological education; it reflects the more general cognitive phenomenon of knowledge accessibility failure under emotional arousal. Tversky and Kahneman's work on availability heuristics (1973, "Availability: A Heuristic for Judging Frequency and Probability," *Cognitive Psychology*) suggests that information is cognitively available not as a function of whether it is known but of how easily it can be retrieved in a given context. The surprise of finding a small bird inside a large cooked bird creates a high-arousal, low-deliberation cognitive environment in which the automatic associative network (small bird inside larger animal ≈ unborn offspring) overrides the more deliberate retrieval of the relevant biological knowledge (birds lay eggs; all turkeys have eggs, not live young). The prank design specifically exploits this override mechanism by staging the revelation at the dinner table—a high-social-salience moment where emotional reactivity is naturally elevated.

**Stage 2 — The persuasion challenge:** The prank succeeds to the extent that the victim can be kept in the dark long enough for the initial horror response to establish itself. One documented variant reinforces the deception by using the fact that game hens are small and pink, consistent with the visual schema of an "unborn" animal, which creates a perceptual confirmation of the false claim. The game hen's size relative to the full turkey is consistent with what an uninformed person might imagine a late-term fetal bird to look like if they had not thought carefully about the biology.

**Stage 3 — The comedic resolution:** The prank's comedy—and its cultural staying power—depends on the resolution being quick and clear. Once the gag is explained (turkeys lay eggs; that's just a Cornish game hen), the horror dissolves, the victim (ideally) laughs, and the social bonding function of shared humor reasserts itself. Pranks that maintain the deception too long, or that produce genuine distress without quick resolution, tend to be retrospectively judged as cruel rather than funny. The pregnant turkey prank, when well-executed, has a natural resolution mechanism: any adult who recovers their reasoning quickly can verify that turkeys lay eggs, which provides an almost immediate self-correction.

The epistemic novelty is the prank's use of **biological category violation** as its humor mechanism. A Cornish game hen is placed inside a turkey, but the conceptual category violation—"pregnant turkey"—requires the audience to temporarily accept a category error (turkeys give live birth) and then correct it. This is related to the broader logic of incongruity theory in humor research (McGhee, 1979, *Humor: Its Origin and Development*, W.H. Freeman)—humor arises from unexpected incongruity that is then resolved—but distinctive in that the incongruity is not initially presented as humor but as a disturbing reality, with the humor only emerging at the resolution stage.

## §4 Turkeys, Cornish Game Hens, and the Biology Behind the Prank

Understanding why the prank is both plausible (in the moment of surprise) and biologically absurd (on reflection) requires a brief look at the relevant avian biology and the specific choice of Cornish game hen as the prank instrument.

**Turkey reproductive biology:** Turkeys (*Meleagris gallopavo*) are, like all birds, oviparous—they reproduce by laying eggs that are then incubated externally. Female turkeys (hens) typically lay clutches of 10–15 eggs per nesting season, beginning in mid-spring, incubating them for approximately 28 days before hatching. Commercial turkey production relies on artificial insemination because the selective breeding of commercial Broad-Breasted White turkeys for extreme breast muscle development has left most commercial tom turkeys physically incapable of natural mating (Hafez, 2014, *Reproduction in Farm Animals*, 7th ed., Wiley-Blackwell). But this reproductive impediment to natural mating does not change the fundamental biology: turkeys lay eggs and are entirely incapable of carrying live young internally, as mammals do.

**The Cornish game hen:** The Cornish game hen is not a wild bird species but a specific hybrid chicken (*Gallus gallus domesticus*) produced by crossing Cornish chickens with a White Plymouth Rock or Cornish White chicken. They are slaughtered at a young age—typically 4–6 weeks—when they weigh approximately 1–2 pounds, which accounts for their small size. Their small, rounded form and pink skin when raw bear a superficial visual resemblance to what an imaginative and uninformed observer might picture as an avian fetus, though this comparison collapses immediately on biological reflection (a fetal or newly hatched turkey would be covered in wet down, not slaughtered and dressed for cooking).

The Cornish game hen's suitability as the prank's instrument is therefore a combination of its small size relative to a full turkey—sufficient to fit inside the turkey's body cavity—and its prepared-food appearance, which makes it plausible as a "found object" rather than an obviously separately prepared food item. A fully feathered, raw game hen would break the prank's credibility immediately; the hairless, dressed commercial product maintains just enough anatomical ambiguity to support the deception during the brief window of initial surprise.

## §5 Holiday Pranks as Anthropological Category

The pregnant turkey prank occupies a recognized slot in the anthropology and folklore of holiday celebrations: the holiday-specific food prank that exploits the emotional and social heightening of the festive occasion to produce a comedic effect that would be flatter in everyday contexts.

Thanksgiving in the United States is a holiday with particularly strong ritual associations centered on the turkey as the meal's focal object. The turkey is not just the main course but the performative center of the Thanksgiving meal—the object that is carved, displayed, and photographed; the container of stuffing; the subject of the morning news segments on proper preparation technique. This heightened symbolic weight, combined with the gathering of extended family (including those unfamiliar with each other's practical knowledge or judgment) and the social pressure of the communal meal, creates ideal conditions for pranks centered on the turkey.

Van Gennep (1909, *Les Rites de Passage*, Paris: Nourry; English translation 1960, University of Chicago Press) described holiday and ritual gatherings as liminal spaces—thresholds between ordinary time and ceremonial time—in which normal social rules are temporarily suspended and unusual behaviors become licensed. Turner (1969, *The Ritual Process: Structure and Anti-Structure*, Aldine) extended this analysis to show how ritual liminality enables forms of communal play, inversion, and rule violation that reinforce social bonds precisely through their departure from ordinary norms. Holiday pranks fit within this framework: the joke is licensed by the liminal context of the holiday gathering, and the shared laughter that follows successful execution serves the social bonding function that Turner identifies as central to ritual anti-structure.

The pregnant turkey prank also connects to a broader tradition of Thanksgiving-specific folk humor documented by Emery (2022) and in the Snopes archive more generally, including the "turkey neck penis prank"—in which children are allegedly deceived into believing that the raw, skinned neck of a turkey is a body part of a different kind—and the associated urban legend ("The Gobbler") in which a sleeping father's pants are visited by a hungry house cat after a turkey neck has been placed there by prankster children. These pranks share the structural feature of using the turkey's body parts as the material for a deception involving misidentification and resultant horror, resolved by the revelation of the actual, innocent explanation.

This cluster of turkey-specific pranks suggests that the holiday turkey has acquired the status of a prank-generative object in American folk culture—a physical prop so rich in psychological and symbolic associations (feast, family, mortality, preparation) that it serves as a ready vehicle for humor involving deception and false identification.

## §6 Summary, Epistemic Classification, and Gap Assessment

The pregnant turkey prank is **True** as a documented practice. David Emery (2022) traced its digital history from a 1996 forum post through email folklore to contemporary videos, establishing its status as a genuine tradition with a traceable origin in the early internet era and robust ongoing practice. The prank's mechanism exploits a real (if transient) biological misconception and uses the liminal social conditions of the holiday dinner table as its stage.

The **EARC gap** classification is **E (Explainer/Epistemic)**: this is not a case of false claims, dangerous misinformation, or contested empirical facts. It is an investigation into whether a folk practice exists and what its documented history looks like. The fact-check affirms the practice's existence and situates it within the broader ecology of holiday prank folklore and internet-era folk tradition diffusion.

The epistemic class is **verified-tradition**: a practice for which documentary evidence exists, which was not previously confirmed as a real practice (rather than merely a proposed or apocryphal one), and which Snopes has traced to a specific digital origin point and documented longitudinal persistence.

Notable methodological features of Emery's investigation:

- Use of internet archive research to establish earliest known instance (1996 forum post) — a form of digital folklore dating not available in pre-internet contexts
- Acknowledgment of pre-internet uncertainty ("as best we can determine, the pregnant turkey prank is no older than the internet itself") — appropriate epistemic modesty about the limits of digital archive coverage
- Reference to the Snopes archive for related pranks ("turkey neck penis prank," "The Gobbler" urban legend) — cross-linking to related documented cases that contextualize this prank within a broader folk tradition

**No research gap.** The folkloric and anthropological frameworks for analyzing holiday pranks are well-established. Emery's investigation is appropriately scoped and its conclusions are supported by documentary evidence.
